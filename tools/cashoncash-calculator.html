What Is Cash-on-Cash Return?

Cash-on-cash return is the annual pre-tax cash flow a property produces divided by the total cash you invested to buy it. If you put $950,000 into a deal (down payment, closing costs, initial capex) and it distributes $76,000 a year after the loan payment, your cash-on-cash return is 8%. It is the metric that answers the only question most investors actually ask: what is my money earning me this year? Full definition in the cash-on-cash glossary entry.

Unlike cap rate, cash-on-cash is a leveraged return: the loan you choose changes it directly. That makes it the right lens for comparing financing structures on the same deal, and the wrong lens for comparing two properties without noting how each is financed.

What Is a Good Cash-on-Cash Return?

According to Commercial Lending Solutions (CLS CRE), a nationwide commercial mortgage brokerage with over $1 billion in closed financings, for stabilized commercial real estate in 2026 6% to 10% cash-on-cash is considered solid. Below 5%, most investors question whether the return justifies the illiquidity and risk of real estate; above 10% usually means either exceptional financing, a value-add business plan, or risk that deserves a closer look. Benchmarks by strategy:

  • Multifamily (stabilized): 6% to 10%. Agency debt at high leverage keeps cash flow thinner but safer.
  • Industrial: 7% to 10%, supported by long leases and low capex.
  • Retail (NNN): 8% to 12%. Higher yield compensates for tenant concentration risk.
  • Office: 6% to 9% for leased assets; wide variance by market in the current cycle.
  • Value-add (post-stabilization): 12% to 20%+. Early years often run negative before the plan matures.
  • Core / trophy assets: 3% to 6%. Institutional buyers accept lower cash yield for durability.

Financing is the biggest lever: the same property at 65% LTV with a 6.5% rate and at 55% LTV with a 7.5% rate can differ by 300+ basis points of cash-on-cash. That spread is exactly what a broker canvassing 1,000+ lenders is for. Ask us what financing would do to your return.

How to Calculate Cash-on-Cash Return

Formula: Cash-on-Cash Return = Annual Pre-Tax Cash Flow ÷ Total Cash Invested.

  1. Start with NOI (net operating income): all property income minus operating expenses. Not sure of NOI? Use the NOI calculator.
  2. Subtract annual debt service (principal + interest) to get annual pre-tax cash flow.
  3. Add up total cash invested: down payment + closing costs + upfront rehab or capex.
  4. Divide cash flow by cash invested.

Worked example: a $2,500,000 multifamily purchase with $180,000 NOI and a $1,625,000 loan (65% LTV) costing $131,000/year in debt service leaves $49,000 annual cash flow. Cash invested is $875,000 down + $75,000 closing and reserves = $950,000. Cash-on-cash = $49,000 ÷ $950,000 = 5.2%. The same deal with interest-only bridge debt in year one might cash flow $71,000, a 7.5% cash-on-cash, which is why financing structure matters as much as the asset.

Cash-on-Cash vs Cap Rate vs IRR vs ROI

Each metric answers a different question. Cap rate: what does the property yield unleveraged? Cash-on-cash: what does my equity yield this year, after the loan payment? IRR: what is my annualized total return over the whole hold, counting cash flow, principal paydown, and the sale? ROI is the loosest of the four, usually meaning total profit over total cash without annualizing. A value-add deal can show a 3% cash-on-cash in year one and still pencil to an 18% IRR; judging it on either number alone gets the answer wrong.

When Leverage Helps and When It Hurts

Debt raises cash-on-cash only when the property's yield exceeds the cost of the debt. The rule of thumb: compare the cap rate to the loan constant (annual debt service divided by loan amount). Buy at a 6.5% cap with debt at a 7.8% constant and every borrowed dollar earns less than it costs, so more leverage means a lower cash-on-cash. That is negative leverage, and it is common early in high-rate cycles. Interest-only periods, longer amortization, or cheaper capital sources flip the math, which is exactly the comparison worth running across multiple lenders before you sign a term sheet.

What Cash-on-Cash Misses

Three things, all of which favor real estate: principal paydown (your tenants retiring your loan), appreciation, and tax shield from depreciation. A 6% cash-on-cash deal with 2% annual principal paydown and modest appreciation is earning double digits in total. Cash-on-cash also says nothing about when the cash arrives within the year or how durable it is against lease rollover. Use it as your current-yield gauge, IRR as your full-hold scorecard.

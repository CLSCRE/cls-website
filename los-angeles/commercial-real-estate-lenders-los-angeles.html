Commercial Real Estate Lenders in Los Angeles: Who Lends on What

Quick answer: Commercial real estate loans in Los Angeles come from six lender groups: banks and credit unions, life insurance companies, agency lenders, CMBS conduits, debt funds and private bridge lenders, and HUD/FHA. Which one fits depends on the property, the business plan, leverage and speed. This page maps each group to the Los Angeles deals it lends on, without naming any firm.
By Trevor Damyan, Commercial Mortgage Broker (Los Angeles) · Updated November 2026

Sponsors comparing Los Angeles lenders usually ask the wrong first question: who is the biggest. The better question is who actually wants this asset, at this leverage, on this timeline. This guide explains the lender landscape from the borrower's side, the way a Los Angeles broker routes a file.

Overview

The Six Lender Groups at a Glance

Every Los Angeles commercial loan is priced by one of a handful of capital sources. They are listed here by type, never by firm, because the right name changes with the property, the sponsor and the quarter.

Construction capital sits across these groups. Banks and debt funds lead on ground-up work at up to 75 percent loan to cost, and HUD-backed programs can reach 85 percent on qualifying projects. Rates for every group come from our current rate table, not from this page.

Matching

Which Lender Group Fits Which Los Angeles Deal

  • Rent-stabilized apartment building: agency, bank and life company lenders can all lend, but each must underwrite the capped rent roll, not market rent. See rent-controlled apartment financing.
  • Value-add apartment with heavy vacancy or deferred capex: private bridge and debt fund lenders, then a refinance into agency or bank debt. Soft-story work is usually priced in at purchase, see soft-story retrofit financing.
  • Stabilized industrial in the South Bay, San Gabriel Valley or Vernon: life companies, banks and CMBS conduits compete. Our closed deals include conduit and credit union industrial refinances.
  • Downtown office: few conventional lenders at any leverage. Capital shows up for conversions, see adaptive reuse financing.
  • 100 percent affordable ground-up under ED1: construction debt layered with tax credit equity and a permanent takeout, see ED1 construction financing.
  • Market-rate or mixed-income ground-up near transit: bank and debt fund construction loans, see TOC density bonus financing.
  • Owner-user building: banks and credit unions first, because the borrower's operating account is part of the deal, see owner-user financing in Los Angeles.
  • Exit planning inside City of Los Angeles limits: the transfer tax can make refinance and hold beat a sale, see Measure ULA and your exit.
Local rules

How Los Angeles Changes the Underwriting

Los Angeles lenders do not price a Los Angeles building like the same building in another metro. Four local facts decide most quotes.

  • Rent regulation. The City of Los Angeles Rent Stabilization Ordinance covers most apartment buildings with a certificate of occupancy on or before October 1, 1978. The Los Angeles Housing Department states the annual increase is 3 percent from July 1, 2025 to June 30, 2027, and that the formula afterward is 90 percent of CPI with a 1 percent floor and 4 percent cap (LAHD). Lenders size on that rent roll.
  • Transfer tax. Measure ULA adds tiered tax on City of Los Angeles sales above indexed thresholds, which is why many sponsors refinance and hold instead of selling.
  • Seismic and capex. Soft-story and non-ductile concrete retrofit mandates change purchase price, reserves and who can lend.
  • Entitlement path. A site with ED1, TOC or adaptive reuse already resolved is quoted by a wider pool of construction lenders than one still in discretionary review.

Due diligence

Questions to Ask Any Los Angeles Lender or Broker

  • Has this lender closed this property type in this submarket in the last 12 months?
  • Will the quote underwrite the actual capped rent roll, or a pro forma that will be re-traded at closing?
  • Is the loan recourse, non-recourse with carve-outs, or full recourse, and who signs?
  • What are the prepayment terms: defeasance, yield maintenance, step-down or open?
  • Is there a deposit or banking relationship requirement, and is it priced into the rate?
  • Which reserves are required: interest, tax and insurance, replacement, seismic?
  • How many lenders will see the deal, and which groups, before a term sheet is chosen?

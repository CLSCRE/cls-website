Prop 1's $11.25 Billion Housing Bond: How an LA Developer Would Use It

Where Prop 1's $11.25B housing bond money goes, how it fits a 4% tax credit stack, when HCD funds could flow, and what LA developers should line up now.

October 24, 2026

Updated October 24, 2026. The vote is November 3.

In August I wrote about the November ballot as a capital event. This is the follow up on mechanics: if Proposition 1 passes, where the money goes, how it sits in a 100% affordable stack, and when an LA deal could draw on it. I am not telling anyone how to vote.

What Prop 1 is

Proposition 1 is the Veterans and Affordable Housing Bond Act of 2026, placed on the ballot by SB 417 (Chapter 16, Statutes of 2026). It authorizes $11.25 billion in state general obligation bonds: $10 billion for state housing programs, repaid from the General Fund, and $1.25 billion for CalVet home loans, repaid by the veterans who borrow. The Legislative Analyst puts the cost of the $10 billion piece at $500 million to $600 million a year for about 25 years.

Where the $10 billion goes

The bill text routes most of it through the Department of Housing and Community Development (HCD). For a rental developer, these are the lines that matter:

  • $5.1 billion, Multifamily Housing Program (MHP). Long term soft loans for new construction and rehab. At least 10% of assisted units in each funded development must be affordable to extremely low income households.
  • $1.15 billion, supportive housing through MHP. Includes capitalized operating subsidy reserves, with $150 million set aside for youth housing.
  • $750 million, Portfolio Reinvestment Program. Recapitalizes existing HCD funded properties.
  • $200 million to buy existing unrestricted units and put long term affordability on them.
  • $500 million, Infill Infrastructure Grant Program. Sewer, water, street and park work that supports high density infill housing.

The LAO counts the four rental lines as $7.2 billion, enough for up to 40,000 rental units. The rest goes to homeownership ($1.1 billion through CalHome and CalHFA), farmworker, student, tribal and local pilot programs.

How MHP money sits in the stack

Under HCD's published MHP terms, the loan is a deferred payment loan at 3% simple interest with a 55 year term, paying 0.42% a year for the first 30 years with the balance due at maturity. It does not make your first mortgage bigger. It fills the hole between total cost and what the rents, the 4% tax credit equity and the local soft money can carry.

The July 2025 federal tax law cut the bond test for 4% credits from 50% to 25% of aggregate basis, if at least 5% is financed with bonds issued after 2025. That stretches the state's bond cap. It does not close the gap. HCD said as much in April with a $240 million MHP Gap Funding NOFA for construction ready projects that held HCD awards but could not move forward because of funding gaps.

The construction lender sees that on day one, which is why the soft commitments get underwritten as hard as the rents. For how the other state and local programs stack, see California's affordable housing capital stack, decoded.

Timing: passage is not money

Certified results post by December 11, 2026, and the act is operative only if voters adopt it. Then the state sells bonds, HCD writes guidelines (exempt from formal rulemaking) and issues NOFAs. The last cycle is my best guide. Voters approved the 2018 housing bond on November 6, 2018. HCD released the first MHP NOFA from it on June 19, 2019 and announced MHP awards on December 20, 2019. On a similar clock, my read is a first round in mid 2027 and awards late in the year. That is an inference, not a schedule.

The act lets HCD disburse during construction, but MHP has historically funded as permanent financing. Underwrite it at conversion until a NOFA says otherwise.

What to line up now

  • Site control. HCD's NOFAs require it and score readiness to proceed.
  • Entitlements. In Los Angeles, 100% affordable near transit can be a ministerial approval. I worked one through in Issue 17.
  • Track record. MHP requires a sponsor that has already developed at least one affordable project. If that is not you yet, partner with someone it is.
  • Local soft money and a bond plan. Gap money follows deals with the rest of the stack in motion.
  • A construction lender who knows layered deals. Subordination, equity pay-ins and conversion conditions are where these closings stall.

How I'd finance it

On a market rate deal I size the construction loan up to 75% of cost, usually limited by 1.20x DSCR at 6.5% on a 30 year amortization, and a perm loan sized the same way takes it out. On a 100% affordable deal the perm is sized that way too, but the construction loan runs larger because tax credit equity and soft loans pay it down at conversion. That only works with a construction lender who is comfortable with three or four layers of soft debt behind it. That is the lender I go find.

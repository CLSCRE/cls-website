Multifamily Underwriting Guide

Multifamily Underwriting: DSCR, Debt Yield and LTV, With a Worked Loan Sizing Example

By Trevor Damyan, Commercial Mortgage Broker at Commercial Lending Solutions · Updated November 2026

Quick answer: Lenders size a multifamily loan to the smallest of three amounts: a maximum loan to value, a minimum debt service coverage ratio (DSCR) and a minimum debt yield. In the worked example below, a 40 unit apartment building with $517,466 of underwritten net operating income (NOI) and a $9,500,000 value could carry a $7,125,000 loan on the leverage test alone, but the 1.25x DSCR test caps it at about $5.46 million, or 57 percent of value. Headline leverage is rarely the real limit. Coverage is, and the NOI the lender underwrites is usually well below the NOI the seller advertises.

The Three Tests

How a Lender Sizes a Multifamily Loan

Every multifamily lender, from an agency seller servicer to a regional bank to a debt fund, runs the same three arithmetic checks on the property's income and value. The loan is the smallest number that comes out. Knowing which test binds on your deal tells you whether to chase a higher appraisal, a lower rate, a longer amortization or a different lender type.

TestFormulaWhere lenders set the lineWhat it protects against
Loan to value (LTV)Loan amount ÷ appraised valueAgency up to 80 percent, CMBS up to 75, banks 65 to 75, life companies 50 to 65, bridge 65 to 75 of as-is valueA falling appraisal
Debt service coverage (DSCR)NOI ÷ annual debt serviceMost permanent lenders 1.25x; life companies often 1.35x or more; bridge lenders may accept under 1.0x going inA rate reset or an NOI miss
Debt yieldNOI ÷ loan amountCMBS 9 to 10 percent or better; life companies similar; bridge 6 to 8 percent going in; agency embeds it in its DSCR and LTV gridWeak appraisals and low rates that flatter LTV and DSCR

Ranges are market norms, not a quote. Each lender sets its own grid by property type, market and sponsor.

Related definitions with their own examples: DSCR, debt yield, LTV, cap rate and amortization.

Step 1

Build the Lender's NOI, Not the Seller's

Our example is an illustrative 40 unit garden apartment building under contract at $9,500,000, or $237,500 per unit. It is a teaching case, not a transaction and not a market quote. The statement below is what an underwriter builds from the rent roll and trailing operating history.

Underwritten NOI is $517,466. At the $9,500,000 price that is a 5.45 percent cap rate on the lender's NOI.

Why the seller's number is higher

The offering memorandum for the same building shows a trailing 12 month NOI of $638,900, a 6.73 percent cap rate. The gap is not an error. It is four routine underwriting adjustments.

The $121,434 difference is 19 percent of the seller's NOI and removes about $1.28 million of loan proceeds at a 1.25x DSCR.

Other adjustments that move NOI on real deals: using in-place rents instead of market rents on units that are below market, capping rent growth on regulated units, normalizing a one time insurance credit or tax abatement, and excluding other income the lender cannot verify. California acquisitions deserve special attention because the sale reassesses property taxes, so the seller's tax line is rarely the buyer's.

Run the Three Tests on $517,466 of NOI

DSCR at 1.25x$517,466 ÷ 1.25 = $413,973 of debt service, at 6.50 percent and 30 year amortization (a 7.585 percent annual constant)$5,457,915
Debt yield at 8 percent$517,466 ÷ 0.08$6,468,325
Debt yield at 9 percent$517,466 ÷ 0.09$5,749,622

The 6.50 percent rate is an illustrative assumption inside the current indicative agency and life company range, not a quote.

Result. The binding test is DSCR at about $5.46 million. That is 57.5 percent LTV, a 9.48 percent debt yield and $4,042,085 of equity before closing costs, nearly 43 percent of the price. A buyer who underwrote 75 percent leverage on the seller's NOI would be short by roughly $1.67 million at closing. If the lender's debt yield floor were 10 percent the loan would fall again to $5,174,660.

When does LTV actually bind?

Only when rates are low or coverage minimums are loose. In this example a 30 year amortizing loan reaches the 75 percent LTV cap before it hits a 1.25x DSCR only if the rate falls to about 4.1 percent. At any higher rate the lender runs out of coverage before it runs out of leverage. That is why the "up to 80 percent LTV" on a program page and the loan you actually receive can be 20 points apart.

DSCR binds for every lender type in this case, so the loan amounts spread by coverage minimum and payment constant, not by headline leverage. Agency executes largest because it pairs a 1.25x minimum with 30 year amortization. The bank loan is smallest because its shorter amortization and higher rate raise the payment constant. A life company lends less than agency or CMBS here but is typically the lowest priced of the four and carries the longest fixed terms. The right answer depends on whether the sponsor values proceeds, rate, recourse, prepayment flexibility or closing speed. Our side by side guides cover those trade offs: agency vs life company, agency vs bank and agency vs CMBS.

HUD insured and affordable programs

HUD 223(f) and 221(d)(4) loans underwrite to lower coverage than conventional lenders and reach higher leverage (up to 85 percent, and more on qualifying affordable deals) over 35 to 40 year fully amortizing terms, in exchange for longer processing and program requirements. Run the same three tests with the program's own coverage minimum. See HUD and FHA multifamily loans.

Value-Add Deals

Underwriting a Bridge Loan: The Takeout Test

A value-add bridge lender looks past in-place coverage. Suppose the buyer plans $1,200,000 of renovation on the same building, for a total cost of $10,700,000. A 75 percent loan to cost bridge loan is $8,025,000, interest-only for two to three years, with renovation funds released against completed work. The in-place NOI cannot service that loan on an amortizing basis, and the lender knows it. The question that decides the deal is whether the stabilized NOI will refinance it.

Takeout test. If renovations lift underwritten NOI to $640,000, a permanent lender at 1.25x DSCR, 6.50 percent and 30 year amortization sizes at about $6,750,328. That is $1,274,672 short of the $8,025,000 bridge balance, so the sponsor must repay the gap with equity, a cash-out from a later phase or a larger NOI. Bridge underwriting is largely the work of making that gap small, or showing where the equity will come from. Compare your options on multifamily bridge loans and bridge vs agency for value-add.
Preparation

What to Have Ready Before You Ask for Terms

  • A current rent roll with unit mix, in-place rents, lease expirations and any regulated units flagged
  • Trailing 12 month and trailing 3 month operating statements, plus two years of history if available
  • The latest property tax bill, an estimate of the reassessed tax on a purchase, and the insurance quote or declarations page
  • A capital improvement scope and budget if the business plan includes renovation
  • Sponsor financial statement and a schedule of real estate owned, since agency lenders look for net worth near the loan amount and meaningful post closing liquidity
  • Your own underwritten NOI built with the lender adjustments above, so you start the conversation at the number the lender will use

Common Mistakes

Five Underwriting Mistakes That Cost Proceeds

  1. Underwriting to the seller's NOI. The reassessment, management fee, vacancy floor and reserves adjustments above are routine and rarely negotiable.
  2. Treating stated LTV as the loan. Coverage and debt yield usually bind first, so size the deal on the tightest test before you set the offer price.
  3. Ignoring the appraisal. On an acquisition most term sheets size to the lesser of price or appraised value, so a low appraisal cuts proceeds even when the purchase price is fair.
  4. Skipping the rate stress. A 50 basis point move changes the example loan by about $273,000 on a 30 year schedule. Model the quote and the quote plus 50 basis points.
  5. Planning a bridge exit on stabilized NOI that is not yet proven. Lenders credit in-place performance at the takeout, not a pro forma.

Multifamily Underwriting FAQ

Most permanent multifamily lenders size to a minimum DSCR of 1.25x. Agency programs center on 1.25x for market rate apartments, banks usually want 1.20x to 1.30x, CMBS conduits 1.25x to 1.35x, and life insurance companies often show effective coverage of 1.35x or more because they lend at lower leverage. Bridge lenders can fund transitional deals with going in coverage below 1.0x by relying on an interest reserve and the stabilized business plan.
DSCR compares NOI to the loan payment, so it moves with the interest rate and amortization schedule. Debt yield compares NOI directly to the loan amount, so it ignores rate and appraisal assumptions. In the example above a 1.25x DSCR at 6.50 percent and 30 year amortization produces a debt yield of about 9.5 percent, so a lender requiring 10 percent debt yield would size the loan lower.
Underwriters rebuild the operating statement. They apply a vacancy floor of at least 5 percent even on a full building, add a 3 percent management fee even when the owner self manages, reassess property taxes to the purchase price on an acquisition, and deduct replacement reserves. In the example those four adjustments reduce a seller NOI of $638,900 to $517,466, which cuts loan proceeds by roughly $1.28 million at a 1.25x DSCR.
At a 1.25x DSCR, a 6.50 percent rate and 30 year amortization, each $10,000 of underwritten NOI supports about $105,500 of additional loan. The ratio changes with rate and amortization, but the rule of thumb is that one dollar of NOI is worth about ten dollars and fifty cents of loan at today's pricing.
At current rates DSCR almost always binds first on stabilized apartments. With a 30 year amortization schedule, a 1.25x DSCR test only gives way to a 75 percent LTV cap when the interest rate falls to roughly 4.1 percent in the example. At rates above that, lenders cannot reach their stated maximum leverage without breaking their own coverage minimum.
A bridge lender sizes to cost or as-is value rather than to in-place coverage, funds renovation costs through a future funding facility, and reserves interest during the business plan. The test that matters is the takeout: whether the stabilized NOI will support a permanent loan large enough to repay the bridge. When it will not, the borrower faces an equity gap at refinance, which the bridge example above shows at about $1.27 million.

A building with a large share of regulated units often appraises on a higher cap rate, which tightens LTV and debt yield.

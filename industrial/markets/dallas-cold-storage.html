Cold Storage Financing in Dallas

By Trevor Damyan, Commercial Mortgage Broker at CLS CRE. Dallas, TX industrial market.

How Cold Storage Financing Works in Dallas

Dallas-Fort Worth's position as a top-three industrial market nationally extends powerfully into the cold storage sector, where the metro's central location and robust food distribution infrastructure create exceptional demand for refrigerated facilities. The region's cold storage activity concentrates primarily in the Great Southwest submarket near Lancaster and DeSoto, where proximity to major transportation arteries supports efficient cold chain logistics, and the DFW Airport corridor, where international food importers and pharmaceutical distributors require temperature-controlled facilities with direct airfreight access.

The fundamentals driving Dallas cold storage development reflect both regional population growth and the metro's role as a central distribution hub for the Southwest. Major food distributors and third-party logistics operators serving Texas, Oklahoma, Arkansas, and Louisiana often anchor new cold storage facilities with long-term leases, creating the cash flow stability that attracts institutional capital. The market has seen particular strength in frozen food distribution and pharmaceutical cold chain facilities, where national operators require the redundant power and backup refrigeration systems that distinguish institutional-grade cold storage from basic refrigerated warehouse space.

Unlike standard industrial distribution, cold storage facilities in Dallas typically require 15 to 25 acres to accommodate the specialized truck courts and temperature-controlled loading necessary for efficient operations. This land requirement, combined with Dallas's relatively affordable industrial land compared to coastal markets, has made the metro attractive for large-scale cold storage development targeting regional and national distribution networks.

Lender Appetite and Capital Stack for Dallas Cold Storage

Life insurance companies represent the most competitive permanent financing source for stabilized Dallas cold storage, particularly those with dedicated industrial specialty desks that understand refrigeration infrastructure and cold chain tenant profiles. These lenders typically structure permanent loans at 70% to 75% loan-to-value with 25 to 30-year amortization schedules, recognizing that cold storage facilities often command premium rents that support slightly higher leverage than standard warehouse properties.

In the current rate environment, with the 10-year Treasury around 4.3% and SOFR near 3.6%, life insurance company pricing for stabilized Dallas cold storage typically ranges from 150 to 200 basis points over the 10-year Treasury, depending on tenant credit and lease term. CMBS execution remains viable for larger stabilized portfolios above $50 million, though CMBS lenders require more extensive third-party engineering reports on refrigeration systems and environmental compliance than life companies.

Construction financing for cold storage development comes primarily from specialty debt funds and regional Texas banks with industrial construction experience, given the complexity of refrigeration system installation and the higher development costs. Construction lenders typically limit loan-to-cost at 65% to 70% for cold storage, reflecting both the specialized nature of the improvements and the smaller pool of potential buyers compared to standard warehouse space. Bridge financing through debt funds serves the value-add repositioning market, where existing refrigerated facilities require updated ammonia systems or conversion from older refrigeration technology.

Prepayment structures for Dallas cold storage financing generally include yield maintenance or defeasance requirements through year seven, with step-down prepayment penalties thereafter. Life insurance lenders often accept supplemental financing restrictions that accommodate future refrigeration system upgrades or tenant improvement requirements without triggering prepayment penalties.

Underwriting Criteria That Matter in Dallas

Dallas cold storage underwriting centers on refrigeration infrastructure quality and tenant creditworthiness, with lenders requiring detailed third-party engineering assessments of ammonia or synthetic refrigeration systems, backup power capabilities, and insulation performance. The temperature consistency required for pharmaceutical cold storage or frozen food distribution demands redundant refrigeration systems that can maintain specified temperature ranges during Texas summer heat, making mechanical system quality a primary underwriting focus.

Tenant credit analysis for Dallas cold storage goes beyond standard rent-to-revenue ratios to examine the operator's cold chain expertise and track record with temperature-sensitive cargo. Lenders prefer tenants with established relationships with major food distributors, grocery wholesalers, or pharmaceutical companies, as these relationships typically translate to longer lease terms and more stable cash flows. National third-party logistics providers specializing in cold chain often receive more favorable underwriting treatment than local or regional operators.

Environmental underwriting in Dallas cold storage requires specific attention to ammonia refrigeration systems, which trigger enhanced environmental disclosures and insurance requirements. Lenders require confirmation that facilities comply with EPA refrigerant regulations and maintain proper leak detection systems, particularly important given Texas environmental enforcement standards. Site location relative to residential areas also factors into environmental risk assessment, as ammonia-based systems require specific setback considerations and emergency response planning.

Building specifications that influence Dallas underwriting include clear height capabilities for high-density storage, truck court configuration for efficient temperature-controlled loading, and electrical capacity to handle refrigeration loads during peak summer demand. The ability to accommodate both refrigerated and frozen storage within the same facility often enhances underwriting appeal by expanding the potential tenant pool.

Typical Deal Profile and Timeline

A representative Dallas cold storage financing typically involves a 200,000 to 400,000 square foot facility with total project costs between $60 million and $120 million, reflecting the $250 to $400 per square foot development cost premium for refrigerated construction. Lenders expect sponsors to have prior cold storage development experience or partnership with established cold storage operators, given the specialized nature of refrigeration system design and installation.

The sponsor profile that attracts competitive financing includes industrial development track records with projects above $25 million total cost and demonstrated relationships with national cold chain tenants. Lenders prefer sponsors who can secure tenant commitments during the development phase, particularly given the limited pool of qualified cold storage operators compared to standard warehouse tenants. Pre-leasing requirements typically range from 50% to 70% for construction financing, higher than standard industrial development.

Timeline from initial lender outreach through closing typically extends 90 to 120 days for permanent financing on stabilized assets, with additional time required for engineering assessments of refrigeration systems and environmental compliance verification. Construction loan timelines often stretch 120 to 150 days due to the complexity of refrigeration system design review and the need for specialized contractor vetting. Bridge financing can often close within 60 to 75 days, assuming clear title and updated environmental assessments.

Deal structures commonly include mechanical system reserve requirements, typically $1 to $3 per square foot annually, to address ongoing refrigeration maintenance and eventual system replacement costs. These reserves reflect lender recognition that refrigeration systems require specialized maintenance and carry higher replacement costs than standard HVAC systems.

Common Execution Pitfalls Specific to Dallas

Electrical capacity constraints in certain Dallas submarkets create significant execution risks for cold storage development, particularly in areas where utility infrastructure was designed for standard warehouse loads rather than the high electrical demands of industrial refrigeration. Sponsors often underestimate the lead time and cost required for electrical service upgrades, which can extend construction timelines and increase development costs beyond initial projections.

Appraisal challenges frequently emerge from the limited comparable sales data for cold storage facilities, as the specialized nature of refrigerated buildings creates a narrow market for valuation analysis. Dallas appraisers may rely on cost approach methodologies that don't fully capture the income premium from cold storage rents, leading to conservative valuations that complicate refinancing or sale execution. Lenders increasingly require appraisers with specific cold storage experience to avoid valuation shortfalls.

Environmental compliance complexity around ammonia refrigeration systems can delay closing timelines when environmental consultants lack specific experience with industrial refrigeration requirements. Phase I environmental assessments must address refrigerant handling and storage protocols, while insurance carriers require specialized coverage for ammonia-based systems that may not be readily available through standard industrial property policies.

The limited pool of qualified cold storage tenants in Dallas can create leasing risk during development or repositioning projects, particularly when multiple cold storage facilities come to market simultaneously. Unlike standard warehouse space where numerous logistics operators provide tenant depth, cold storage requires tenants with specific refrigerated distribution capabilities, making lease-up timelines less predictable and potentially extending stabilization periods beyond initial projections.

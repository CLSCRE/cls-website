What Is a Cap Rate?

A capitalization rate is a property's net operating income divided by its price, expressed as a percentage. It tells you the unleveraged annual yield you would earn if you bought the asset all cash. Because it strips out financing, the cap rate is the cleanest way to compare two properties, two markets, or two points in a cycle. Full definition and worked examples in our cap rate glossary entry.

How to Calculate a Cap Rate

Formula: Cap Rate = Net Operating Income ÷ Purchase Price (or Market Value).

  1. Establish NOI: all property income minus operating expenses, before debt service and capex. Use in-place income, not the broker's pro forma, if you want the number lenders will use. Run it in the NOI calculator.
  2. Confirm the price basis: contract price for an acquisition, current appraised value for a refinance.
  3. Divide. $200,000 NOI on a $3,200,000 purchase is a 6.25% cap rate.

The formula also runs in reverse, which is how most CRE valuation actually works: Value = NOI ÷ Cap Rate. That same $200,000 NOI at a 5.5% market cap rate supports a $3,636,000 value; at 7% it supports $2,857,000. A 150 basis point cap rate move changed the value by $779,000 with no change to the building, which is why cap rate assumptions get argued harder than any other underwriting input.

What Is a Good Cap Rate for Commercial Property?

There is no universally good cap rate, only the right one for the risk. In the current cycle, stabilized assets typically trade around these ranges:

  • Multifamily: 4.5% to 6.5%. The most liquid, most financeable asset class prices tightest.
  • Industrial: 5.0% to 7.0%, with modern logistics at the low end.
  • Retail: 6.0% to 8.5%. Grocery-anchored trades tighter, unanchored strip wider.
  • Office: 6.5% to 9.0%+, with wide variance by market and lease term in the current cycle.
  • Hospitality: 7.0% to 10.0%, reflecting operational intensity.

Within each range, location quality, tenant credit, lease duration, and asset age move a specific deal up or down. A low cap rate is not a bad deal and a high one is not a bargain; each is the market's price for a different bundle of risk.

Cap Rate vs Cash-on-Cash vs Debt Yield

Three yields, three questions. The cap rate asks what the property earns unleveraged. Cash-on-cash asks what your equity earns after the loan payment, so it depends entirely on financing. Debt yield (NOI divided by loan amount) asks what the lender earns if they have to take the keys, which is why life companies and CMBS desks size loans off it. A deal can carry an attractive cap rate and still produce weak cash-on-cash if debt is expensive, and vice versa.

What Moves Cap Rates

Cap rates track three forces: the risk-free rate (Treasury yields set the floor for what any income stream is worth), expected rent growth (markets that price in growth accept lower going-in yields), and risk premium (liquidity, tenant credit, asset condition). When Treasuries rise faster than rents, cap rates expand and values fall. Underwriters also distinguish the going-in cap rate from the exit cap rate assumed at sale; adding 25 to 50 basis points to the exit is standard discipline.

What the Cap Rate Does Not Tell You

A cap rate is a single-year snapshot. It says nothing about capital expenditures ahead, lease rollover risk, below-market rents that could be marked up, or how financing changes your actual return. Use it to screen and compare, then underwrite cash flows year by year before you price a deal on it.

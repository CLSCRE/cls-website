What Is a Good Cash-on-Cash Return?

For stabilized commercial real estate, 6% to 10% cash-on-cash is considered solid. Below 5%, most investors question whether the return justifies the illiquidity and risk of real estate; above 10% usually means either exceptional financing, a value-add business plan, or risk that deserves a closer look. Benchmarks by strategy:

  • Multifamily (stabilized): 6% to 10%. Agency debt at high leverage keeps cash flow thinner but safer.
  • Industrial: 7% to 10%, supported by long leases and low capex.
  • Retail (NNN): 8% to 12%. Higher yield compensates for tenant concentration risk.
  • Office: 6% to 9% for leased assets; wide variance by market in the current cycle.
  • Value-add (post-stabilization): 12% to 20%+. Early years often run negative before the plan matures.
  • Core / trophy assets: 3% to 6%. Institutional buyers accept lower cash yield for durability.

Financing is the biggest lever: the same property at 65% LTV with a 6.5% rate and at 55% LTV with a 7.5% rate can differ by 300+ basis points of cash-on-cash. That spread is exactly what a broker canvassing 1,000+ lenders is for. Ask us what financing would do to your return.

How to Calculate Cash-on-Cash Return

Formula: Cash-on-Cash Return = Annual Pre-Tax Cash Flow ÷ Total Cash Invested.

  1. Start with NOI (net operating income): all property income minus operating expenses. Not sure of NOI? Use the NOI calculator.
  2. Subtract annual debt service (principal + interest) to get annual pre-tax cash flow.
  3. Add up total cash invested: down payment + closing costs + upfront rehab or capex.
  4. Divide cash flow by cash invested.

Worked example: a $2,500,000 multifamily purchase with $180,000 NOI and a $1,625,000 loan (65% LTV) costing $131,000/year in debt service leaves $49,000 annual cash flow. Cash invested is $875,000 down + $75,000 closing and reserves = $950,000. Cash-on-cash = $49,000 ÷ $950,000 = 5.2%. The same deal with interest-only bridge debt in year one might cash flow $71,000, a 7.5% cash-on-cash, which is why financing structure matters as much as the asset.

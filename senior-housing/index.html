Sector Overview

Why Senior Housing Financing Is a Category of Its Own

The demographic case for senior housing is unlike any other commercial real estate sector. Understanding the capital markets requires understanding what is driving the demand.

The Demographic Tailwind That Changes Everything

Approximately 73 million Baby Boomers are aging through their sixties and seventies, and the leading edge of that cohort began crossing age 80 in 2026. The 80-plus population is the primary demand driver for assisted living, memory care, and skilled nursing, and that cohort will grow at a faster rate over the next decade than at any point in American history. This is not a cyclical story. It is a structural demographic shift with a 20-year runway that institutional capital is only beginning to price in.

In 2026, senior housing has become one of the fastest-growing institutional commercial real estate sectors by capital deployment. Life insurance companies that historically focused on multifamily and industrial are now building dedicated senior housing desks. Senior housing debt funds have raised record capital over the past three years. HUD FHA 232 applications are at multi-year highs. The capital is flowing because the fundamentals are undeniable.

HUD FHA 232: The Gold Standard for Senior Housing Capital

For operators who can plan 12 to 18 months in advance, HUD FHA 232 is the most powerful financing tool in senior housing. The program provides fully amortizing fixed-rate loans with terms up to 40 years for new construction and up to 35 years for existing facilities. The loans are non-recourse, meaning the borrower has no personal liability beyond the asset itself. Rates are among the lowest available in the senior housing space because the federal government insures the loan, reducing lender risk to near zero.

The catch is time and complexity. A HUD 232 application requires thorough regulatory review, Davis-Bacon wage compliance for any construction component, and coordination with state health regulators who must certify the facility's licensure status. Operators who have never navigated a HUD submission often underestimate how detail-intensive the process is. Working with a broker who has placed HUD 232 transactions before dramatically reduces the risk of a failed submission or an avoidable delay.

Census-Based Underwriting: How Lenders Actually Think

Senior housing underwriting differs from standard commercial real estate in one fundamental way: cash flow is driven by census (the number of residents currently in care), not simply by signed leases. A skilled nursing facility with 120 licensed beds and 95 percent occupancy has a very different financial profile from the same facility at 75 percent occupancy, and lenders model the facility's net operating income using trailing 12-month census data, not stabilized projections.

For assisted living and memory care, lenders also evaluate revenue per occupied bed, since higher-acuity residents generate meaningfully more revenue than standard AL residents. This is particularly important for memory care underwriting, where private-pay rates can be 30 to 60 percent higher than in adjacent AL units. An operator who understands how to present census data, payor mix, and rate-per-resident trends to a lender will consistently achieve better loan sizing and pricing than one who submits a standard rent roll.

The Bridge-to-HUD Strategy for Value-Add Operators

One of the most effective financing strategies in senior housing is the bridge-to-HUD structure. An operator acquires an assisted living or memory care facility at below-stabilized occupancy, closes on short-term bridge financing from a senior housing debt fund, and simultaneously submits a HUD FHA 232 application. During the 12 to 18 months that the HUD application is under review, the operator stabilizes the facility, improves census, and potentially renovates common areas. When the HUD commitment arrives, the bridge loan is retired with a fully amortizing, non-recourse, fixed-rate HUD loan at a rate that often represents a 200 to 400 basis point improvement over the bridge.

This strategy requires careful execution. The bridge lender must be comfortable with the HUD timeline and the exit certainty. The HUD MAP lender must be engaged early. And the operator must have the capital, management depth, and regulatory relationships to execute on the operational improvement plan during the bridge period. When all three pieces align, bridge-to-HUD is one of the most powerful return amplifiers available to senior housing investors.

Senior Housing Financing Nationwide: What Investors Need to Know

No sector in commercial real estate carries a more powerful long-term demand thesis than senior housing. Seventy-three million Baby Boomers are entering peak senior care years between 2025 and 2040, and the pipeline of new supply built during that same window is nowhere near sufficient to meet the wave. For operators, developers, and investors positioned in independent living, assisted living, memory care, and skilled nursing facilities, the financing environment in 2025 and 2026 has grown meaningfully more sophisticated, and more favorable, than at any point in the post-COVID recovery. At Commercial Lending Solutions, we have structured senior housing transactions from Florida to California, Texas to Pennsylvania, across every major care type and capital structure. Here is what you need to know to finance a senior housing asset the right way.

Why Investors Nationwide Are Prioritizing Senior Housing

The fundamental investment case is demographic and durable. Unlike multifamily or office, senior housing demand is not driven by economic cycles or remote work trends. It is driven by age, and age is not reversible. Occupancy across stabilized senior housing assets nationwide has recovered to the 85 to 90 percent range in most markets after the COVID-era disruption, and rent growth in private-pay assisted living and memory care is outpacing virtually every other property type. Affluent suburban markets across the Sun Belt, Mid-Atlantic, and Pacific Coast corridors are seeing the sharpest compression between demand and available beds. For investors who understand the operational complexity of the sector, the risk-adjusted returns are exceptional. The financing structures available to qualified operators and investors have expanded significantly, with capital available across the full lifecycle from ground-up construction through stabilized permanent debt.

Who Qualifies: Deal Size, Property Types, and Borrower Profile

Senior housing financing is not a commodity product. Lenders underwrite to operator quality as much as they underwrite to real estate. The key qualifying factors include:

  • Loan size: Most institutional lenders require a minimum of $5 million, with the most competitive structures beginning at $10 million and above.
  • Property types: Independent living communities, assisted living facilities, memory care residences, continuing care retirement communities (CCRCs), and licensed skilled nursing facilities all qualify, though capital sources differ meaningfully by care type.
  • Operator experience: Lenders require documented operational experience. For construction financing, most debt funds and banks require the borrower to have stabilized and operated at least two comparable facilities. Bridge and permanent lenders want to see a seasoned management team with verifiable census history.
  • Payor mix: Private-pay dominant assets attract the deepest lender pool. Medicaid-heavy facilities require more specialized lenders and carry tighter EBITDAR coverage requirements.
  • EBITDAR coverage: Stabilized assets targeting HUD or life company financing typically need 1.40x or better coverage at underwritten census levels.

How Senior Housing Financing Is Structured

The capital stack for senior housing looks different from conventional CRE, and understanding the options is critical to maximizing proceeds and minimizing cost of capital.

HUD FHA 232 is the gold standard for stabilized licensed care facilities. With 35 to 40 year fully amortizing fixed-rate terms, non-recourse structure, and the best long-term rates available in the market, HUD is the permanent financing target for most serious operators. The process is longer, typically six to nine months from application to close, but the economics over a 10 or 20 year hold are unmatched. HUD underwrites to licensed bed count, resident census, and payor mix, not to a cap rate on stabilized NOI. That distinction matters enormously.

Bridge-to-HUD is the dominant structure for value-add acquisitions and recently constructed facilities. A specialty debt fund provides a 12 to 24 month bridge loan while the operator stabilizes census to HUD-qualifying levels, then a HUD takeout retires the bridge. This two-step structure is how the vast majority of active senior housing deals are capitalized today.

Life company financing is well suited for stabilized independent living communities with strong private-pay census and institutional-quality sponsorship. Life companies offer competitive fixed rates and are particularly active in the $15 million to $50 million range on low-acuity assets.

Construction financing for new assisted living and memory care development is available through regional banks and specialty debt funds for experienced sponsors in high-barrier suburban markets. Loan-to-cost typically ranges from 55 to 70 percent depending on market and operator track record.

National Market Context That Drives Underwriting

Two property segments are drawing the most aggressive capital right now. Purpose-built memory care facilities in affluent suburban corridors, particularly those in the Southeast and Southwest, are attracting bridge and construction capital at terms that reflect the acute shortage of licensed memory care beds in those communities. Separately, repositioning plays in skilled nursing, specifically converting older SNF licenses to higher-acuity assisted living or pursuing CCRC licensing upgrades, represent some of the most complex but highest-upside transactions in the sector today. Lenders who specialize in senior housing understand that a 78-bed assisted living facility in a supply-constrained coastal suburban market underwrites fundamentally differently than a 120-bed Medicaid-heavy SNF in a rural market. We work with lenders who make that distinction and price accordingly.

When to Choose Senior Housing Financing Over Alternatives

If your asset is licensed for senior care, you should almost certainly be using a senior housing specific lender rather than a generic CRE lender. Generic lenders cap out at lower leverage, apply conventional cap rate underwriting that ignores the operational value of the license and operator, and frequently pass on deals that specialized lenders close comfortably. HUD is the right answer when you have a stabilized asset and a long-term hold horizon. Bridge debt is the right answer when census is building or the asset is in lease-up. Construction debt is the right answer when you have the track record and the market fundamentals to justify a new build. The worst outcome we see in this sector is an experienced operator accepting mediocre terms from a generalist lender because they did not know the specialized market existed.

Common Questions

Senior Housing Financing FAQ

Answers from a broker who closes senior housing deals, not a chatbot or a FAQ template.

What is HUD FHA 232 financing for senior housing?

HUD FHA 232 is a federal mortgage insurance program administered by the Department of Housing and Urban Development specifically for residential care facilities including assisted living, memory care, and skilled nursing. The program provides fixed-rate, fully amortizing loans with terms up to 40 years and is non-recourse to the borrower. Rates are typically among the lowest available for senior housing because the federal government insures the loan, which allows lenders to offer institutional pricing even to smaller operators. The tradeoff is time: HUD 232 applications require 12 to 18 months from submission to closing, thorough regulatory review, and compliance with Davis-Bacon wage requirements for construction loans. For operators who plan ahead, it is the gold standard for long-term permanent financing.

What occupancy do lenders require for senior housing financing?

Occupancy requirements vary by lender type and facility category. Conventional lenders and life insurance companies typically require 85 to 90 percent stabilized occupancy for permanent financing on independent living and assisted living facilities. HUD FHA 232 uses a debt service coverage analysis rather than a hard occupancy floor, but underwriters look for demonstrated stabilization at or above 85 percent over a trailing 12-month period. Memory care and skilled nursing facilities are underwritten using a combination of occupancy and revenue-per-occupied-bed metrics, since Medicaid and Medicare reimbursement rates are as important as physical census. Bridge lenders are more flexible and will lend into lease-up as low as 60 to 70 percent occupancy when the sponsorship is strong and the exit to permanent financing is clear.

Can I get financing for a newly constructed assisted living facility?

Yes. Construction financing for ground-up assisted living is available from senior housing debt funds, regional banks, and certain life insurance companies with construction programs. Loan-to-cost advance rates typically range from 65 to 75 percent, with loan terms of 18 to 36 months covering the construction and lease-up period. Lenders evaluate the sponsor's prior operating experience with assisted living, the local market absorption rate, pre-leasing or deposit activity, and the creditworthiness of the management operator. Once the facility reaches stabilized occupancy, the construction loan is typically refinanced into a permanent loan, either through a conventional lender, a life insurance company, or HUD FHA 232.

How is memory care financing different from assisted living?

Memory care facilities serve residents with Alzheimer's disease and other forms of dementia and operate as a distinct licensed care category in most states. From a financing standpoint, memory care underwriting places greater emphasis on revenue per occupied bed rather than occupancy alone, because memory care commands significantly higher rates per resident than standard assisted living. Lenders also evaluate the operational depth of the memory care management team, the physical design of the secure unit, and state licensure status. Memory care assets that are co-located with assisted living on the same campus are often underwritten as a blended facility, which can simplify the capital stack. Stand-alone memory care facilities have a narrower lender universe but attract interest from specialized senior housing debt funds that understand the operating model.

What is a bridge-to-HUD structure for senior housing?

A bridge-to-HUD structure is a two-phase financing strategy used by senior housing operators to capture the long-term pricing of HUD FHA 232 without waiting 12 to 18 months to close before acquiring or repositioning an asset. In phase one, the operator closes on a short-term bridge loan at a higher rate, typically ranging from 8 to 12 percent, which funds the acquisition or renovation and covers the lease-up period while the HUD application is processed. In phase two, the bridge loan is retired with the proceeds of the HUD 232 permanent loan at a substantially lower fixed rate. This strategy is particularly common for value-add acquisitions of existing assisted living or memory care facilities where the current occupancy is below permanent financing thresholds but the operator has a clear path to stabilization.

Do I need a licensed operator to get a senior housing loan?

Yes. Senior housing lenders, including HUD, life insurance companies, and senior housing debt funds, require that the facility be operated by a licensed and experienced management platform. This is not a formality. Lenders underwrite the operator's track record as carefully as they underwrite the real estate. For assisted living, memory care, and skilled nursing, you will need to demonstrate that the management entity holds the required state operating license, has a history of positive survey results, and has experience managing facilities of comparable size and acuity. For independent living facilities that do not provide licensed care services, the operator underwriting is less stringent but lenders still expect a competent property management platform with demonstrated senior housing experience.

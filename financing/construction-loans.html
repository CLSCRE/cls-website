When it fits

When Construction financing fits.

Construction financing is inherently higher risk than permanent lending, which means lender selection and deal structuring are critical. Experienced borrowers with strong track records can access competitive bank construction loans, while more complex or speculative projects may require debt fund capital. Key considerations include loan-to-cost ratios, interest reserves, completion guarantees, and the exit strategy, whether that's a permanent loan take-out, sale, or refinance.

Structure before pricing: leverage, recourse, reserves, prepayment, and closing certainty can matter as much as the headline coupon. We test those trade-offs before recommending a path.

Terms, draws and timing

Typical commercial construction loan terms.

A commercial construction loan funds up to 75% of total project cost (HUD-backed multifamily up to 85%) over 12 to 36 months, interest only, with proceeds released in draws as inspected work is completed. These are the terms we see most often on loans from $2 million to $100 million and up. Final terms depend on the lender, the sponsor and the project.

How a construction loan is drawn

Unlike a term loan, a construction loan is not funded in one payment. The lender releases money against completed work, which keeps its exposure matched to what has actually been built.

StepWhat happens
1. Equity goes in firstThe sponsor's equity, including land contributed at its appraised value, is spent before the first loan dollar is advanced.
2. Closing drawCovers land purchase or payoff if applicable, initial soft costs, and sometimes a mobilization payment to the general contractor.
3. Monthly draw requestThe general contractor submits a draw request against the line-item budget with invoices, lien waivers and a schedule of work completed.
4. InspectionThe lender's construction inspector visits the site and confirms the work matches the amount requested and the approved budget.
5. FundingAfter a title date-down confirms no new liens, funds are released, typically within 5 to 10 business days of the request. Retainage of 5% to 10% of each draw is held back.
6. CompletionWith the certificate of occupancy and final lien waivers, retainage is released and the loan moves to its exit: a permanent loan, a sale, or a built-in conversion.

Our step-by-step construction loan guide walks through each stage in more detail.

How much equity a construction loan requires

With leverage capped at 75% of total project cost, equity is at least 25% of total cost, and many lenders land between 25% and 40%. Industrial and multifamily projects with experienced sponsors sit at the low end. Office, hospitality and first-time developer projects sit at the high end. Land owned free and clear counts toward equity, usually after a 10% to 20% appraisal discount. Regulated banks also face a higher capital charge on construction loans unless the borrower contributes at least 15% of the as-completed value in cash or unencumbered land, so bank equity rarely falls below that level. Lenders also look for post-closing liquidity of about 10% of the loan amount.

How long a construction loan takes to close

Lender typeTypical time to close
Banks and credit unions45 to 90 days. Third-party reports (appraisal, environmental, plan and cost review) run in parallel and take 4 to 5 weeks.
Debt funds and private lendersUnder 30 days when a complete package is ready and speed matters
HUD 221(d)(4) multifamily12 to 24 months from first lender engagement to construction loan close. See HUD 221(d)(4) vs bank construction.

A permitted project, an executed contractor contract and a reconciled line-item budget on day one typically save 2 to 3 weeks.

Who qualifies for a construction loan

Lenders underwrite four things separately: the project budget and cost certainty, the development team's track record, the equity and completion guarantee, and the exit. The strongest borrowers have completed two or more comparable projects, use a licensed and bonded general contractor on a fixed-price or guaranteed maximum price contract, hold permits or final approvals, have a net worth at least equal to the loan amount, and can show a credible takeout. First-time developers can still qualify by adding an experienced co-sponsor or development manager, more equity or additional guarantees. Construction financing, clarified.

The final structure depends on the asset, sponsorship, and market. These are the starting parameters we discuss most often.

Working out equity? Our commercial property down payment guide covers typical requirements by loan type: 20% to 30% conventional, 10% for SBA owner occupied.

Commercial construction loans are short-term facilities (12-36 months) that fund the development of new buildings or major renovations. The loan is drawn in stages as construction milestones are completed, with interest-only payments during the build period. Upon completion, the borrower refinances into permanent financing or sells the property.

Construction loans fund up to 75% of total project cost (loan-to-cost, or LTC), depending on the lender, borrower experience, and project type. Only HUD-backed multifamily executions go higher, up to 85%. The remaining 25% or more of total project cost must come from borrower equity, which can include land contributed at its appraised value.

Most lenders require borrowers to demonstrate relevant development experience for construction loans. First-time developers may need to partner with an experienced co-sponsor or general contractor, or work with private lenders who are more flexible on experience requirements.

Construction loan rates typically range from 7.75% to 11.75%, depending on the lender type and project risk profile. Bank construction loans for experienced developers are at the lower end, while debt fund and private lender rates are higher but offer more flexibility.

A construction-to-permanent loan (or mini-perm) combines construction financing with a built-in permanent loan conversion upon project completion. This eliminates the need to refinance separately, saving time and closing costs, though terms may be less competitive than standalone permanent financing.

Construction loan draws are released based on completed work verified by an independent inspector. The borrower submits draw requests with supporting documentation, the lender's inspector verifies the work, and funds are released (typically within 5-10 business days). An interest reserve is usually built into the loan to cover debt service during construction.

Draws are released as work is completed. The general contractor submits a draw request with invoices and lien waivers, the lender's inspector confirms the work on site, a title date-down checks for new liens, and funds are typically released within 5 to 10 business days. The lender holds back 5% to 10% of each draw as retainage until completion. Sponsor equity is spent first, and interest accrues only on the amount drawn.

At up to 75% loan-to-cost, equity is at least 25% of total project cost, and most lenders land between 25% and 40% depending on property type, sponsor experience and pre-leasing. Land you own free and clear counts as equity, usually at its appraised value less a 10% to 20% discount. HUD-backed multifamily loans can go up to 85% of cost, which lowers the equity requirement.

Bank and credit union construction loans typically close in 45 to 90 days. Debt funds and private lenders can close in under 30 days when the package is complete and speed matters. HUD 221(d)(4) loans take 12 to 24 months from first lender engagement to construction loan close.

Borrowers with two or more comparable completed projects, a licensed and bonded general contractor on a fixed-price or guaranteed maximum price contract, permits in hand or at final approval, net worth at least equal to the loan amount, and a credible exit qualify most easily. First-time developers can qualify with an experienced co-sponsor, more equity or additional guarantees.

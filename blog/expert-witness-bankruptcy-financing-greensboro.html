Expert Witness: CRE Financing Feasibility in Chapter 11 Proceedings in Greensboro, NC

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Bankruptcy Exit Financing

The Greensboro Market Context

Greensboro sits at the center of the Piedmont Triad, a three-market metro that also encompasses Winston-Salem and High Point. For purposes of commercial real estate financing analysis, Greensboro functions as a secondary market with genuine institutional depth, not simply a tertiary market that lenders approach with reluctance. That distinction matters significantly when a bankruptcy court needs to determine whether exit financing is feasible, because the answer in a true tertiary market and the answer in Greensboro are often materially different.

The industrial sector anchors Greensboro's CRE fundamentals. The Piedmont Triad region has absorbed significant logistics and distribution demand tied to interstate access along I-40, I-85, and I-73. Industrial vacancy in the greater Greensboro submarket has tracked in the 4 to 6 percent range in recent years, with asking rents for bulk distribution product rising from roughly $4.50 to $5.50 per square foot net toward the $6.00 to $7.50 range for newer or recently renovated facilities. Those rent levels may appear modest against coastal gateway markets, but they translate into stable, lender-friendly income streams when backed by creditworthy tenants on multi-year leases.

Multifamily fundamentals in Greensboro have remained relatively resilient. The University of North Carolina at Greensboro, North Carolina A&T State University, and a diversified employer base anchored by Volvo Financial Services, Cone Health, and logistics operators support consistent renter demand. Cap rates for stabilized multifamily in Greensboro have generally traded in the 5.25 to 6.25 percent range depending on vintage, submarket, and unit mix, with value-add assets transacting at 6.50 to 7.50 percent on in-place income. Office fundamentals are weaker, consistent with national trends, and lenders underwrite Greensboro office with particular conservatism given elevated sublease availability and tenant consolidation activity near the downtown core and along the Battleground Avenue corridor.

For a bankruptcy exit financing analysis, Greensboro's market profile matters because it establishes the universe of lenders willing to extend credit, the underwriting parameters they apply, and the realistic loan proceeds available to satisfy a plan. A court cannot simply assume financing is available in the abstract. The analysis must be grounded in what specific lender categories are actually active in this market, what they require, and what the asset's income stream can actually support at current market rates and terms.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by counsel representing a plan proponent in a Chapter 11 proceeding involving a commercial real estate asset located in Greensboro. The asset was a multi-tenant industrial flex property, a category that includes single-story buildings combining warehouse and office space, typically occupied by light manufacturing, distribution, professional services, and contractor tenants. The property had been operating with a mix of occupied and vacant suites, generating meaningful but below-stabilized net operating income at the time the debtor filed for bankruptcy protection.

The core questions presented to us were precise and legally significant. First, could the property support first-mortgage financing in an amount sufficient to satisfy the senior secured creditor's claim in full as part of a confirmed plan? Second, what categories of lenders actively originate loans on assets encumbered by, or recently exiting, a bankruptcy estate? Third, what loan terms, including interest rate, amortization, and loan-to-value constraints, would a reasonable lender apply to this asset in the current market? Fourth, was there a realistic path to closing exit financing within the timeframe contemplated by the proposed plan, or was the financing contingency a structural impediment to confirmation?

These questions cannot be answered by an appraiser, whose scope is limited to value. They require an expert with current, active knowledge of lender underwriting criteria, debt market conditions, and the specific structural requirements lenders impose when financing assets with bankruptcy history. CLS CRE was engaged to provide a written expert opinion and to be available for deposition and, if necessary, testimony at the plan confirmation hearing.

The Financial Analysis

The methodology I applied followed the standard income approach to debt sizing that institutional and commercial lenders use when underwriting income-producing commercial real estate. The analysis works backward from income to maximum supportable debt, a process lenders call debt yield and DSCR underwriting, and it is the correct framework for answering the court's question about financing feasibility.

Step one was establishing stabilized net operating income. The property's actual in-place rent roll was analyzed suite by suite, identifying executed leases, month-to-month tenancies, and vacant suites. Market rents for comparable industrial flex product in Greensboro were established using leasing comparables from the surrounding submarket. For occupied suites, in-place rents were used where they were at or below market; market rents were used where in-place rents exceeded current market levels, because lenders do not credit above-market rents without burnoff analysis. Vacancy and credit loss were applied at 8 percent of gross potential income, consistent with lender underwriting standards for multi-tenant flex product in secondary markets. Management fees were applied at 5 percent of effective gross income. Stabilized NOI was calculated at approximately $310,000 annually.

Step two was applying the DSCR threshold. The market standard for conventional commercial mortgage financing on industrial and flex product is a minimum debt service coverage ratio of 1.25x, meaning net operating income must equal at least 125 percent of annual debt service. Some lenders apply 1.20x for strong assets or sponsorship, and some apply 1.30x for assets with elevated risk characteristics. For a bankruptcy exit scenario on a multi-tenant flex asset in a secondary market, 1.25x is the appropriate benchmark. Applying 1.25x DSCR to $310,000 NOI yields maximum allowable annual debt service of $248,000, which equals $20,667 per month.

Step three was selecting the rate and amortization assumptions. At the time of the analysis, first-mortgage rates on commercial real estate in this risk category were pricing in the range of 7.25 to 8.00 percent on a 25-year amortization schedule for a 5 to 7-year fixed term, with some lenders offering 30-year amortization for borrowers with strong credit profiles. I used 7.625 percent on a 25-year amortization as the base case, representing a mid-range pricing assumption consistent with actual term sheets being issued in this market. At those terms, the monthly debt service on a $2,500,000 loan would be approximately $18,420. At $2,750,000, monthly debt service would be approximately $20,260. At $3,000,000, monthly debt service would reach approximately $22,100, which exceeds the $20,667 maximum and produces a DSCR below 1.25x.

The DSCR-constrained maximum loan was therefore approximately $2,725,000. The appraised value of the property, established in a contemporaneous appraisal, was $3,800,000. The resulting loan-to-value ratio on the DSCR-limited loan was approximately 71.7 percent. This is within the conventional LTV ceiling of 70 to 75 percent that most lenders apply to multi-tenant industrial flex in secondary markets, confirming that the financing was both income-supportable and collateral-supportable at the same time. That alignment is significant: it means neither constraint was the binding limitation, which in turn means the loan is closer to the core of a lender's credit box rather than pushing against its edges.

The senior creditor's claim, including accrued interest and fees through the anticipated payoff date, was approximately $2,400,000. The analysis demonstrated that the property could support first-mortgage financing sufficient to satisfy the senior claim in full with approximately $325,000 of remaining proceeds available to address administrative expenses and junior claims under the plan.

The Complicating Factors

Bankruptcy history and lender seasoning requirements. The most significant structural complication in any bankruptcy exit financing is the seasoning requirement that most conventional lenders impose. Many agency lenders and life company programs require six to twelve months of post-confirmation, clean-title operating history before they will originate a new loan. Some CMBS programs have structural prohibitions on loans to borrowers with recent bankruptcy filings. This does not mean financing is unavailable, but it does narrow the universe. Bridge lenders, community banks with relationship-oriented credit cultures, and credit unions with active CRE portfolios are more likely to originate at or shortly after plan confirmation than institutional permanent lenders. The analysis identified specific lender categories that do not impose seasoning requirements and confirmed their active presence in the Greensboro market.

Title and lien complexity. Assets exiting bankruptcy often carry title complications: mechanics liens filed pre-petition, real property tax arrearages, subordinate deeds of trust that survived the bankruptcy process, or disputes about lien priority that were not fully resolved in the plan. Lenders require clean, insurable title as a condition of loan origination. When title issues remain, they can delay or prevent closing even if the financial analysis supports the loan. In this engagement, I identified specific title conditions that counsel needed to address as a precondition to financing, providing the court with a clear roadmap for what the plan needed to accomplish on the title side.

Occupancy at confirmation versus stabilized occupancy. Lenders underwrite to stabilized NOI, but they also evaluate current occupancy as a measure of execution risk. If the property is 60 percent occupied at confirmation and the plan assumes stabilization to 85 percent within 12 months, a lender providing exit financing must decide whether to underwrite to in-place income, stabilized income, or some blended figure. Most conventional lenders will underwrite to in-place income with a stabilization reserve held back from loan proceeds. Bridge lenders may use a future-funded structure where a portion of the loan is held back and disbursed as lease-up milestones are achieved. Either structure reduces the net proceeds available at closing, which has direct implications for whether the plan can satisfy creditors on the confirmation date versus over time.

Sponsorship and guarantee requirements. Lenders originating loans on properties exiting bankruptcy scrutinize the borrower entity and guarantor profile carefully. If the reorganized debtor is a newly formed entity without operating history, lenders will typically require a creditworthy guarantor, a completion guarantee, or both. If the plan proponent is a third-party acquirer rather than the reorganized debtor, the acquirer's financial strength and CRE operating experience become underwriting factors. Weak or unavailable sponsorship can reduce the lender universe significantly, pushing the deal toward higher-cost bridge or private capital solutions.

The Greensboro Lender Market

Greensboro is served by a meaningful range of commercial real estate lenders, and understanding which categories are active, and at what terms, is essential to a financing feasibility analysis.

Community and regional banks represent the most active originator category for commercial real estate loans in the $1,000,000 to $5,000,000 range in Greensboro. North Carolina has a historically strong community banking sector, and several institutions maintain local Greensboro offices with CRE lending authority. These lenders typically price on a spread over the prime rate or SOFR, with all-in rates in the 7.25 to 8.50 percent range in the current environment. They apply 25-year amortization, 5-year terms, and personal recourse guarantees. Their credit committees can move faster than institutional lenders, and their relationship orientation makes them more willing to engage on nuanced situations like bankruptcy exits. They are generally the most viable primary lender for a plan that requires closing within 90 to 120 days of confirmation.

Credit unions with commercial lending programs are an underutilized source of CRE financing in secondary markets like Greensboro. Several North Carolina-chartered credit unions have active member business lending programs that extend to commercial real estate. Their rates are often competitive with community banks, and their regulatory environment does not include the same seasoning-based prohibitions that apply to CMBS. Field of membership requirements may limit access for some borrowers.

Debt funds and bridge lenders are the appropriate tool when conventional lenders are unavailable at confirmation due to seasoning or occupancy issues. Bridge loans on industrial flex product in Greensboro are currently pricing at 300 to 450 basis points over SOFR on interest-only terms, with origination fees of 1.00 to 2.00 percent, typically for 12 to 36-month terms with extension options. The higher cost is the price of execution certainty. In a plan confirmation context, having a bridge loan commitment in hand is often more valuable than a lower-rate conditional commitment that cannot close on the plan's effective date.

SBA 504 financing is worth noting for owner-occupied industrial flex product. If the reorganized debtor or plan acquirer will occupy at least 51 percent of the building, SBA 504 provides a second-mortgage structure with below-market fixed rates on the CDC portion. The processing timeline is longer, typically 60 to 90 days post-approval, but for the right asset profile it substantially improves loan economics.

What Attorneys Handling Greensboro CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to retain a CRE financing expert. The time to engage a financing expert is before the plan of reorganization is filed, not after a creditor objects that the financing contingency is speculative. If the plan depends on exit financing to fund creditor payments, and if a secured creditor's claim will be paid from loan proceeds, then financing feasibility is a confirmation element that requires evidentiary support. An expert retained early can shape the plan's financing structure to maximize confirmability, not simply defend a structure that was designed without lender input.

What a financing expert can answer that an appraiser cannot. An appraiser determines value. A CRE financing expert determines what a lender will actually lend against that value, under what terms, subject to what conditions, and within what timeframe. These are entirely different questions. A property worth $3,800,000 does not automatically support a $2,850,000 loan. The income stream, occupancy, sponsorship, title condition, and lender market conditions all affect the achievable loan amount. Courts have occasionally conflated appraised value with financing capacity; a qualified financing expert prevents that error.

What makes a CRE financing expert qualified. Qualifications in this field are not determined by academic credentials alone. The expert must have current, active knowledge of commercial mortgage underwriting, meaning they must be engaging with lenders, reviewing term sheets, and closing loans in the present market. A retired bank officer whose experience ended five years ago cannot reliably opine on what lenders require today. CLS CRE is an active commercial mortgage brokerage firm, not a consulting practice. Our opinions are grounded in live market transactions.

Information counsel should gather before the engagement. To conduct a financing feasibility analysis, the expert needs: the current rent roll with lease terms and expiration dates; trailing 12 and 24-month operating statements; the most recent property condition assessment or inspection report; a current title report; the appraised value if available; the secured creditor's claim amount including projected payoff; and the proposed plan timeline including targeted confirmation and effective dates. The more complete this package, the more precise and defensible the expert opinion.

How financing expert testimony is used at confirmation hearings. At a plan confirmation hearing, a CRE financing expert typically provides testimony on two points: first, that financing is feasible as defined under the Bankruptcy Code's feasibility standard; and second, that the specific terms and proceeds assumed in the plan are supported by current market conditions. This testimony is distinct from, and complementary to, valuation testimony. Lenders, appraisers, and restructuring professionals each address different confirmation elements. The financing expert's role is to close the gap between a property's value and a lender's willingness to extend credit against it.

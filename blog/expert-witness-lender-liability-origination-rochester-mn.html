Expert Witness: Commercial Loan Origination Standards in Rochester, MN

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Lender Liability and Loan Origination Standards

The Rochester Market Context

Rochester, Minnesota occupies a unique position in the Upper Midwest commercial real estate landscape. It is not a primary gateway market, yet it consistently defies the volatility that afflicts comparably sized secondary cities. The reason is structural: the Mayo Clinic health system anchors approximately one-third of total employment in the Olmsted County economy, and that anchor generates a demand base for commercial real estate that is remarkably resistant to cyclical downturns. For lender liability and loan origination disputes, that structural stability cuts both ways. It means that a lender who denied financing on creditworthy collateral may have done so without proper market justification. It also means that a borrower who defaulted in a market this stable has questions to answer.

The Destination Medical Center initiative, a public-private development framework that has directed billions in planned investment toward Rochester's downtown and surrounding corridors, has materially altered the risk profile of assets in specific submarkets. Medical office, life sciences-adjacent flex industrial, and multifamily properties within the DMC footprint carry underwriting characteristics that differ meaningfully from comparable assets in secondary markets without an institutional anchor of Mayo's scale. Lenders who failed to account for that distinction in their underwriting, or who applied generic small-market criteria to Rochester collateral, may have deviated from the standard of care applicable to a competent commercial mortgage originator active in this specific market.

On the multifamily side, Rochester vacancy rates have historically tracked below 5%, and effective rents have grown steadily across the post-2015 period, supported by clinical staff, medical students, and a growing biomedical research workforce. Industrial properties serving medical supply chain, healthcare logistics, and biotech manufacturing functions are leased by tenants with creditworthiness profiles more consistent with institutional markets than secondary Midwest markets. Office fundamentals are more mixed, as the broader suburban office correction has reached Rochester, but medical office and MOB product tied to clinic-adjacent demand remains well-occupied. Any expert analysis in a loan origination dispute must be grounded in these submarket-specific fundamentals, not in generic Rochester-as-secondary-market assumptions.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing a borrower in a lender liability action arising from a failed commercial mortgage transaction involving a mixed-use medical office and retail property in the downtown Rochester submarket. The borrower alleged that the originating mortgage broker and the direct lender had materially misrepresented the available loan proceeds, failed to disclose that the loan structure presented at application was not achievable under the lender's own credit guidelines, and had imposed non-standard fee structures that bore no rational relationship to the services rendered or the risk assumed.

Counsel retained CLS CRE to address four core questions. First, whether the loan terms presented in the commitment letter and term sheet were consistent with what a reasonably competent commercial mortgage broker would have represented as achievable for this asset type in this market at the time of origination. Second, whether the lender's underwriting methodology, specifically its treatment of in-place rent versus market rent for partially vacant medical office space, conformed to industry-standard underwriting practice. Third, whether the fees charged, including origination points, processing fees, and rate lock extension fees, were within the range of market-standard compensation for a transaction of this type and complexity. Fourth, what a competent broker, exercising reasonable professional care, would have done differently at each stage of the transaction from initial analysis through closing.

These are questions that require someone who has actually originated, underwritten, and brokered commercial loans across multiple lender categories and market cycles. An appraiser can speak to value. A forensic accountant can speak to cash flow. Neither can speak to what a reasonable broker or lender would have done, what was achievable in the capital markets at that moment, or whether the disclosed terms reflected genuine market practice. That gap is precisely where CRE financing expert testimony is most consequential.

The Financial Analysis

My methodology began with a reconstruction of the property's stabilized net operating income as it should have been underwritten at origination, using the actual rent roll, executed leases, and operating expense history available at the time the commitment letter was issued. The subject property was a three-story mixed-use building with medical office on the upper floors and ground-floor retail, located in the DMC corridor. At origination, the property was approximately 82% occupied, with two medical tenants on long-term leases and one vacancy in a ground-floor retail bay.

The originating broker had calculated NOI by including full contract rents from both medical tenants, applying a 5% vacancy allowance globally, and using the borrower's self-reported operating expenses without independent verification or comparison to market expense benchmarks. That approach overstated NOI materially. A lender applying industry-standard underwriting would have used a vacancy allowance calibrated to the retail component separately from the medical office component, given that retail vacancy in downtown Rochester was running meaningfully higher than medical office vacancy at the time. Blending a 5% vacancy factor across the full rent roll when a retail bay was already dark misrepresented the actual risk profile of the income stream.

Using actual market data from comparable mixed-use assets in the Rochester submarket and applying expense ratios consistent with professionally managed medical office product of this vintage and scale, I reconstructed stabilized NOI at approximately $415,000 annually. The broker had presented NOI of approximately $487,000 to support the loan request. That $72,000 differential was not a minor rounding issue; it was the difference between a fundable loan and one that could not clear standard DSCR thresholds.

On DSCR, the applicable standard for a mixed-use medical office asset at a community bank or regional bank lender in this market was 1.25x minimum, with most lenders in the relevant size range targeting 1.30x to provide covenant headroom. At a 7.25% interest rate and 25-year amortization, consistent with community bank pricing for this asset type and loan size at the time of origination, the annual debt service on the requested loan of $4,200,000 would have been approximately $361,000. Applying the lender's stated 1.25x minimum DSCR against the broker's inflated NOI of $487,000 produced a maximum supportable debt service of approximately $390,000, which appeared to support the loan amount with modest headroom. Against the correctly reconstructed NOI of $415,000, maximum supportable debt service at 1.25x was approximately $332,000, supporting a maximum loan amount of approximately $3,860,000. The requested loan of $4,200,000 was not supportable under proper underwriting, and a reasonably competent broker should have identified that before submitting the application.

On LTV, the as-is appraised value at origination was $5,800,000. The requested loan of $4,200,000 represented 72.4% LTV, which is above the 65% to 70% LTV range that most community and regional bank lenders apply to mixed-use assets with retail vacancy exposure. The loan was structured at a leverage level that required both the inflated NOI and a lender willing to accept above-guideline LTV. Neither condition was disclosed to the borrower as a constraint on execution certainty. The combination of income overstatement and LTV stretch meant that the loan commitment, as presented, was not genuinely executable at the terms disclosed.

The Complicating Factors

Medical office vacancy treatment. Medical office in Rochester is not underwritten the same way as general professional office, and a competent lender or broker should know that distinction. Medical tenants, particularly those affiliated with or serving the Mayo system, carry credit profiles and lease renewal propensities that justify different vacancy and rollover assumptions than speculative office. However, that favorable treatment applies to occupied space under lease, not to pro forma stabilization of vacant space that has not yet been leased. When a broker applies medical office NOI assumptions to vacant retail space within the same building, they are conflating two distinct risk profiles. Lenders who actually close medical office loans in Rochester understand this distinction. Brokers or lenders who do not may produce underwriting that looks supportable on paper but collapses under scrutiny.

DMC corridor premiums and uncertainty. The Destination Medical Center initiative has created genuine value uplift in specific Rochester corridors, and lenders active in this market are aware of it. However, DMC-adjacent premium assumptions must be grounded in actual comparable sales and lease transactions, not in projections or planning documents. In this engagement, the originating broker had applied a market rent assumption that reflected anticipated post-redevelopment rents rather than current achievable rents for comparable existing space. That distinction matters enormously in underwriting. Lenders underwrite to in-place income, with stabilized underwriting reserved for properties where a clear lease-up path exists and where the borrower has demonstrated leasing velocity. Projecting future rents into current underwriting without disclosure constitutes a material misrepresentation of the loan's supportable proceeds.

Fee structure and market standard. The total fees charged in this transaction included a 1.5% origination fee, a $7,500 processing and due diligence fee, and a rate lock extension fee of $21,000 triggered when the closing was delayed. Each of these requires separate analysis against market practice. A 1.5% origination fee is within market range for a transaction of this complexity and size, assuming the broker actually sourced the capital and managed the transaction. Processing fees in the $3,500 to $7,500 range are common for community and regional bank deals. The rate lock extension fee, however, requires scrutiny. Extension fees are legitimate in theory, but they must be disclosed upfront, quantified, and tied to a genuine rate lock cost incurred by the lender. In this matter, the extension fee was assessed without any disclosure in the original term sheet that such a fee could arise, and the lender could not produce documentation that it had actually incurred a hedging cost equal to or approximating the fee charged. That combination of non-disclosure and unsubstantiated cost recovery falls outside the standard of care for a properly run commercial mortgage origination.

The Rochester Lender Market

For mixed-use medical office assets in Rochester at loan sizes between $3,000,000 and $6,000,000, the realistic lender universe at origination included four primary categories, each with distinct credit parameters and pricing behavior.

Community banks and regional banks with Olmsted County presence. These lenders, including institutions headquartered in Rochester and those with active commercial lending offices in the region, represent the deepest source of execution for this asset type at this size. They typically lend to 65% to 70% LTV on mixed-use assets, require 1.25x to 1.30x DSCR, and price at spreads of 175 to 250 basis points over the relevant Treasury index, depending on credit quality and relationship depth. They require full recourse, typically apply 20 to 25-year amortization, and have three to five-year balloon terms. Their credit process is relationship-driven, and borrowers without an existing deposit or operating account relationship may face longer timelines and more conservative initial terms.

Credit unions with commercial lending programs. Several Minnesota-chartered credit unions have expanded commercial real estate lending, and some have been active in Rochester. Their pricing is often more competitive than community banks, but their credit boxes tend to be narrower, with stricter LTV requirements and limited appetite for assets with retail vacancy. They are generally not appropriate execution for a mixed-use property with leasing risk unless the borrower has a strong existing relationship.

Correspondent and conduit lenders operating through national brokers. CMBS execution becomes viable above approximately $5,000,000 for stabilized assets in Rochester, but it requires full occupancy and credit tenant leases to produce execution that is competitive with portfolio lenders. The retail component in the subject transaction, with existing vacancy, would have disqualified the asset from standard CMBS execution without a lease-up reserve and a lender willing to underwrite to stabilized value. Any broker representing CMBS as a likely execution channel for a partially vacant mixed-use asset was misrepresenting the available market.

SBA 504 and USDA programs. For owner-occupied components of mixed-use assets, SBA 504 financing can be relevant in Rochester, and USDA Business and Industry programs have been used for commercial assets in Olmsted County. These programs have longer timelines, specific occupancy requirements, and fee structures that differ materially from conventional commercial mortgages. A competent broker should be able to identify when these programs are applicable and explain their trade-offs clearly to the borrower.

What Attorneys Handling Rochester CRE Disputes Need to Know

The threshold question in any commercial real estate financing dispute is whether you have an expert who can speak specifically to what the capital markets required at the time of the transaction, not what lending generally looks like or what a textbook says underwriting should involve. A retired community banker from Rochester can speak to how one institution operated. I can speak to what the entire lender market for this asset type was doing, what terms were achievable, what was not, and where the specific broker or lender deviated from what a reasonably competent professional would have done.

Attorneys should retain a CRE financing expert early. The most useful expert work happens before depositions, not after. When I am brought in before fact discovery closes, I can help counsel identify the specific documents and data points that will prove or disprove the financial claims at issue. The critical materials in a loan origination dispute include the original loan application and supporting financials, all versions of the term sheet or commitment letter, the lender's internal credit memo, the broker's fee agreements and engagement letters, all correspondence related to underwriting conditions and any changes to loan terms between application and closing, and the final closing documents. Attorneys who wait to retain an expert until trial preparation often discover that critical underwriting documents were not obtained in discovery.

A CRE financing expert can address questions that no other expert can answer. An appraiser can tell the court what the property was worth. I can tell the court whether the loan amount was supportable given market underwriting standards, whether the broker accurately represented what was achievable, whether the lender's credit process deviated from industry norms, whether fees were market-standard or predatory, and what execution was actually available in the Rochester market at the relevant time. Those questions go directly to duty, breach, and causation in a lender liability or professional negligence claim.

At trial or in an arbitration proceeding, CRE financing expert testimony is most effective when grounded in specific, quantified analysis rather than general assertions about industry standards. Juries and arbitrators respond to the calculation that shows exactly how many dollars of loan proceeds were overstated, exactly which DSCR threshold was applicable and why, and exactly what a competent broker should have told the client before the commitment letter was signed. Vague testimony about what is "customary" is far less persuasive than a reconstructed underwriting analysis showing precisely where the actual underwriting diverged from standard practice.

Key Takeaways

  • Rochester is not a generic secondary market. The Mayo Clinic anchor and DMC investment framework create asset-specific underwriting characteristics that a competent broker or lender must understand and apply correctly. Applying generic small-market criteria to Rochester CRE without accounting for these distinctions can itself constitute a deviation from the standard of care.
  • NOI reconstruction is foundational. In nearly every loan origination dispute I have analyzed, the central financial issue traces back to how income and vacancy were treated. A competent underwriter applies component-level vacancy assumptions, uses in-place rents for occupied space, and does not project future rents without a documented leasing basis and appropriate lender disclosure.
  • DSCR thresholds are not negotiable after the fact. The 1.25x minimum DSCR applicable to mixed-use assets at community and regional bank lenders in this market is a credit policy, not a guideline. A commitment letter that appears to satisfy this threshold only because NOI was overstated is not a valid commitment; it is a representation the lender cannot honor under its own credit standards.
  • Fee disclosure is a specific professional obligation. Rate lock extension fees, modification fees, and supplemental processing charges are only market-standard when disclosed upfront and tied to documented cost. Undisclosed fees assessed without a corresponding cost basis are outside the standard of care and may constitute a separate basis for liability.
  • The available lender market defines what was achievable. Expert testimony on what terms were "achievable" must be grounded in what actual lenders active in Rochester were doing for this asset type at this size, not in what terms theoretically exist somewhere in the capital markets. Representing CMBS terms for a partially vacant mixed-use asset, or representing proceeds that no community bank in this market would actually fund, misrepresents the genuine financing universe available to the borrower.
  • Retain the financing expert before depositions close. The most consequential expert work in these cases happens at the document identification and discovery planning stage, not at trial. Early engagement allows counsel to build the financial record needed to support the expert's ultimate testimony.

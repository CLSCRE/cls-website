Expert Witness: CRE Financing Feasibility in Chapter 11 Proceedings in Cincinnati, OH

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Bankruptcy Exit Financing

The Cincinnati Market Context

Cincinnati is not a secondary market in any dismissive sense of that term. With a metropolitan area population exceeding 2.2 million, a diversified economic base anchored by healthcare, advanced manufacturing, logistics, and consumer goods, and a commercial real estate stock that spans every major asset class, Cincinnati generates enough transaction volume and financing activity to support meaningful expert analysis in a Chapter 11 plan confirmation proceeding. That context matters when a court is being asked to evaluate whether a proposed reorganization plan is feasible and whether the financing contemplated by that plan is actually achievable in the current capital markets environment.

The industrial sector has been the strongest performer across Greater Cincinnati over the past several years, driven by the region's position at the intersection of major interstate corridors and its proximity to the DHL global hub at Cincinnati/Northern Kentucky International Airport. Warehouse and distribution vacancy in submarkets such as Sharonville, Blue Ash, and the I-75 corridor has remained in the 3 to 5 percent range, with asking rents for Class A product pushing past $6.00 per square foot net in well-located submarkets. That rent trajectory has been consequential for lenders underwriting in this market because stabilized industrial cash flows in Cincinnati are genuinely creditworthy at conventional leverage levels.

Multifamily fundamentals have been more mixed. Cincinnati's urban core neighborhoods, particularly those in the Over-the-Rhine and Pendleton corridors, experienced significant rent growth in the 2018 through 2022 period as urban reinvestment accelerated. However, new supply delivered across 2023 and 2024 has introduced concessions and moderated effective rents in some submarkets. Cap rates for stabilized multifamily assets in Cincinnati have settled in the 5.25 to 6.25 percent range depending on vintage, location, and occupancy profile, with value-add product trading wider. Office fundamentals remain under pressure, consistent with the national picture, with downtown Cincinnati office vacancy elevated above 20 percent in many submarkets and suburban office facing its own headwinds. For purposes of a bankruptcy exit financing analysis, the asset class matters enormously, because the financing universe, leverage thresholds, and lender appetite are not uniform across property types.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by counsel representing a secured creditor in a Chapter 11 bankruptcy proceeding pending in the Southern District of Ohio. The debtor was the owner of a stabilized multifamily property located in a suburban Cincinnati submarket. The property consisted of a mid-size garden-style apartment community with a physical occupancy rate that had recovered to approximately 91 percent in the months preceding the plan confirmation hearing, following a period of management instability that had contributed to the debtor's financial distress.

The plan of reorganization proposed by the debtor included a refinancing of the property as the mechanism for satisfying the claims of senior secured creditors at confirmation. The plan proponent asserted that the property could support first-mortgage financing sufficient to pay the secured creditor in full. Our retaining counsel needed an independent expert opinion on three specific questions: first, whether the property's stabilized net operating income could actually support a loan of the size contemplated by the plan; second, what categories of lenders actively originate first-mortgage loans on assets held by Chapter 11 debtors, or exiting Chapter 11, in the current market; and third, whether the loan terms assumed by the plan proponent, including the interest rate, amortization, and loan-to-value ratio, were consistent with actual market conditions for this asset type and complexity profile.

These questions required not just financial modeling but an expert with active lender relationships, current market knowledge, and the ability to translate that knowledge into testimony a court can evaluate. CLS CRE maintains relationships with more than 1,000 lenders across 50 states and has closed approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, which provides the empirical foundation necessary to offer reliable opinions about what is and is not achievable in any specific capital markets environment.

The Financial Analysis

The first task in any bankruptcy exit financing analysis is constructing a defensible underwritten net operating income. In a distressed or contested matter, this requires particular care because there are often competing versions of the income story: the debtor's projections, the appraiser's stabilized income estimate, and the actual trailing operations of the property. For this engagement, we reviewed 24 months of operating statements, rent rolls, bank statements, and property management reports. We normalized the income by applying market-rate rents to the occupied units, adjusting vacancy to a stabilized assumption of 7 percent consistent with comparable properties in this Cincinnati submarket, and removing non-recurring income items.

The resulting underwritten effective gross income was approximately $1,580,000 annually. Against that, we applied operating expenses consistent with market norms for garden-style multifamily in suburban Cincinnati, including management fees at 5 percent of effective gross income, maintenance and repair reserves at $350 per unit per year, insurance, real estate taxes based on the current assessed value, and utilities for common areas. Total operating expenses came to approximately $615,000, yielding an underwritten net operating income of $965,000. This NOI figure was critical because it was the foundation of every subsequent calculation.

For a multifamily asset exiting a Chapter 11 proceeding, the applicable debt service coverage ratio threshold among conventional lenders is 1.25x at a minimum, with many agency and life company lenders applying 1.30x in a risk-off environment. We used 1.25x as the market standard minimum for this analysis, reflecting the broader availability of multifamily capital even in a distressed context. At a 1.25x DSCR, the maximum annual debt service the property can support is $772,000, calculated as $965,000 divided by 1.25.

We then applied a stressed coupon rate of 6.75 percent, consistent with agency multifamily pricing available in the market at the time of the analysis for properties with some complexity premium, on a 30-year amortization schedule. This is a conventional Freddie Mac small balance or Fannie Mae multifamily assumption. At those terms, the annual debt service constant for a fully amortizing loan is approximately 7.78 percent. Dividing the maximum supportable annual debt service of $772,000 by the 7.78 percent constant yields a maximum supportable loan of approximately $9,920,000.

The appraised value of the property as stabilized was approximately $13,500,000, implying a loan-to-value ratio of approximately 73.5 percent on the maximum supportable loan. This was within the 75 percent LTV threshold that agency multifamily lenders typically apply for this property type and loan size. The analysis therefore supported the conclusion that first-mortgage financing sufficient to satisfy the senior secured creditor's claim was feasible, subject to the complicating factors described below, and that the plan's financing assumption was not speculative from a capital markets standpoint.

The Complicating Factors

Bankruptcy Estate Status and Lender Underwriting. The single most significant complicating factor in any bankruptcy exit financing is the status of the property as a bankruptcy estate asset. Most conventional lenders, including agency lenders, community banks, and credit unions, require that a property be free and clear of pending bankruptcy proceedings at or before loan closing. They will not close a loan into a bankruptcy estate, and they will not commit to fund a loan that is contingent on a plan confirmation that has not yet occurred. This narrows the practical lender universe considerably. The financing must either close simultaneously with the effective date of the confirmed plan, or the borrower must demonstrate that the property will be transferred to a reorganized entity that is not itself a debtor before the lender will close. Getting this sequencing right is as much a legal structuring problem as a financing problem, and expert testimony on how lenders actually execute around this constraint is genuinely useful to a court evaluating plan feasibility.

Lender Due Diligence on Management History. A property that experienced management instability severe enough to contribute to a bankruptcy filing will face elevated scrutiny during lender due diligence. Lenders will want to see a minimum of six to twelve months of stabilized operations under current management before they commit to funding. If the trailing financials reflect the distressed period rather than the recovered period, the lender will discount the income story. For this engagement, the property's recovery to 91 percent occupancy was a critical fact. Lenders needed to see rent roll documentation, lease abstracts, and bank statements confirming that the recovery was genuine and sustained, not a brief improvement before a new decline. This is not something an appraiser typically evaluates in terms of lender process; it is a capital markets judgment about whether a specific lender's credit committee will approve the loan.

Environmental and Title Complications. Properties that have been in financial distress frequently have deferred title work, unpaid real estate taxes, mechanics liens, or outstanding code enforcement orders that must be resolved before a conventional lender will close. In the Cincinnati market, where real estate tax reassessment cycles can create significant payment obligations on properties with extended owner distress, this is a real issue. We identified delinquent real estate taxes as a potential closing impediment and opined that any workable plan financing structure would need to either reserve for tax payoff at closing or obtain tax lender payoff quotes as part of the plan confirmation record.

Borrower Entity Structure Post-Confirmation. Lenders underwrite the borrower as well as the property. A reorganized entity emerging from Chapter 11 will typically have a damaged credit history, and the principals associated with the debtor may have personal financial issues related to the bankruptcy. Agency lenders require principal creditworthiness in addition to property creditworthiness. If the reorganized entity's principals have materially impaired credit profiles, the practical lender universe shifts toward bridge lenders, debt funds, and hard money sources who underwrite primarily on the asset rather than the sponsor. That shift in lender category typically means higher rates, shorter terms, and lower leverage, all of which must be reflected in the financing feasibility analysis presented to the court.

The Cincinnati Lender Market

For a stabilized multifamily asset in Greater Cincinnati that is exiting Chapter 11 with clean title and a reorganized borrower entity, the applicable lender categories are as follows.

Agency Lenders (Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac Multifamily). Agency executions are the most attractive option for stabilized multifamily in Cincinnati from a rate and leverage standpoint. Freddie Mac's small balance loan program, applicable to properties in the $1 million to $7.5 million loan size range, and Fannie Mae's small loan program cover a substantial portion of Cincinnati's suburban multifamily stock. For larger loans, conventional agency production through Delegated Underwriting and Servicing lenders is applicable. Agency lenders will require the property to be out of bankruptcy, title to be clear, and the borrower entity to meet creditworthiness standards. Pricing at the time of this engagement was in the 6.50 to 7.00 percent range for 5 to 10-year fixed terms with 30-year amortization. LTV caps of 75 to 80 percent apply depending on program and DSCR.

Regional and Community Banks. Cincinnati has a meaningful community banking infrastructure, with institutions including regional banks headquartered in Ohio and Kentucky that are active multifamily lenders in the metro area. These lenders typically offer 3 to 5-year fixed-rate terms with 25-year amortization, floating rate options, and portfolio execution that does not require the same secondary market compliance as agency loans. They are generally more flexible on borrower credit issues than agency lenders but will apply similar DSCR discipline. Community banks in Cincinnati typically price at 25 to 75 basis points over the corresponding treasury or SOFR index, and they typically cap LTV at 70 to 75 percent for multifamily.

Debt Funds and Bridge Lenders. Where the property or borrower does not yet meet agency or bank standards, bridge lenders and debt funds fill the gap. These lenders underwrite primarily on the asset's as-stabilized value and the strength of the business plan to achieve stabilization. For a Cincinnati multifamily property exiting bankruptcy with occupancy in recovery mode, a bridge lender might close a loan at 65 to 70 percent LTV at a rate of SOFR plus 275 to 450 basis points, with a 2 to 3-year term and extension options. The exit strategy for the bridge loan would typically be an agency or bank refinance once the property has demonstrated 12 months of stable operations under new management. This two-step structure, bridge-to-permanent, is a standard and recognized financing structure in the capital markets and a court should understand it as a legitimate path to plan feasibility.

Life Insurance Companies. Life company lenders are present in the Cincinnati market for larger, higher-quality assets. They are not typically the relevant lender category for a multifamily asset emerging from bankruptcy given their conservatism on borrower credit quality and their preference for stabilized, institutional-quality assets with clean operating histories. However, for the right asset profile, a life company execution offers the most attractive fixed-rate terms in the market.

What Attorneys Handling Cincinnati CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to retain a CRE financing expert. In any Chapter 11 plan confirmation proceeding where the plan contemplates a refinancing as the mechanism for creditor satisfaction, financing feasibility is a fact question that requires expert testimony. An appraiser can establish value; a CRE financing expert establishes whether the capital markets will actually lend against that value, at what terms, and under what conditions. These are different questions. A court cannot simply assume that a loan equal to X percent of appraised value is available just because the appraised value supports it. Market conditions, lender credit criteria, borrower qualifications, and asset-specific complications determine actual loan proceeds. That determination requires a capital markets expert, not a valuation expert.

What a CRE financing expert can establish that an appraiser cannot. An appraiser establishes market value. A CRE financing expert establishes the DSCR thresholds lenders apply, the rate and amortization terms available in the current market, which lender categories are realistically available for this asset and borrower profile, how lenders handle bankruptcy estate assets procedurally, what due diligence conditions must be satisfied before a commitment letter is issued, and whether the timeline contemplated by the plan is realistic given lender processing times. These are operational and market-specific conclusions that fall outside appraisal methodology.

Qualifications that matter. The relevant expert is not a retired bank loan officer who underwrote deals in the 1990s. Lender credit standards, pricing benchmarks, and the regulatory environment that governs commercial real estate lending have changed substantially. An expert who is actively originating commercial real estate loans, maintaining current lender relationships, and closing transactions in the market being analyzed is materially more qualified to opine on what lenders will do today than someone whose direct market experience is dated. CLS CRE's active origination platform across 50 states and more than 1,000 lender relationships provides the empirical foundation for reliable current-market testimony.

Information counsel should gather before engaging the expert. At minimum: 24 months of property operating statements; current rent rolls with lease expiration schedules; any existing loan documents and the current loan payoff balance; the appraisal if one has been completed; the proposed plan of reorganization including all financing-related provisions; the list of creditors and their respective claim amounts; any letters of intent or term sheets from potential lenders that the debtor has already obtained; and documentation of the property's ownership history and any pending title, tax, or environmental issues.

How expert testimony is used at a plan confirmation hearing. In a confirmation proceeding, the financing feasibility expert typically provides a written expert report satisfying Rule 26 disclosure requirements, followed by deposition testimony, and then live testimony at the confirmation hearing if the issue is contested. The expert's role is to give the court an accurate picture of what the capital markets will and will not support, independent of what either party's plan document asserts. This is particularly valuable when the plan proponent has obtained a lender letter of intent that opposing creditors believe is unreliable or conditioned on facts that do not exist.

Key Takeaways

  • Cincinnati is a real capital market. Multifamily, industrial, and other asset classes in Greater Cincinnati attract genuine institutional lender interest, and financing feasibility analysis here is grounded in active market evidence, not speculation.
  • NOI construction is the foundation of everything. In a bankruptcy context, the underwritten NOI must be built from documented operating performance, not projected recovery, unless the lender evidence supports forward-looking underwriting for this specific asset type and market.
  • A 1.25x DSCR is the minimum threshold for market analysis. Any plan that assumes financing below this coverage ratio is not supported by conventional market standards and requires explanation of which specific lender category would accept lower coverage and at what cost.

Expert Witness: NNN Lease Rollover Upside and Financing Impact in Tyler, TX

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Below-Market NNN Lease and Rollover Upside Analysis

The Tyler Market Context

Tyler, Texas sits at the intersection of two dynamics that make it particularly relevant for single-tenant NNN disputes: a mid-size regional economy with genuine institutional-grade commercial assets, and a lender market that blends community bank depth with selective CMBS and agency participation. For single-tenant NNN properties specifically, Tyler's fundamentals matter because lenders underwriting these assets care about market rent levels, lease expiration exposure, and re-leasing probability in that specific submarket, not just national retail or industrial benchmarks.

The Tyler metropolitan statistical area has seen consistent population growth tied to healthcare, logistics, and regional retail demand. The medical corridor anchored by major hospital systems has driven above-average demand for medical office and healthcare-adjacent retail, which frequently appears in NNN structures. Industrial vacancy in the Tyler submarket has remained tight by East Texas standards, generally tracking in the 5% to 8% range depending on product type, which supports re-leasing assumptions for well-located industrial NNN assets. Retail vacancy in primary corridors, particularly along the Loop 323 and Broadway Avenue commercial spines, has been resilient relative to national trends, with functional vacancy concentrated in secondary and tertiary strip product rather than freestanding single-tenant buildings.

These market fundamentals are not academic background. When a lender underwrites a single-tenant NNN property in Tyler and evaluates whether the in-place rent is at, above, or below market, they are relying on exactly this kind of submarket data. When that question becomes the subject of litigation, the financing analysis must be grounded in the same market reality that actual lenders use, not theoretical cap rate tables from national research platforms that do not distinguish Tyler from Beaumont or Longview.

The Engagement

Counsel for one of the parties in a commercial real estate financing dispute retained Commercial Lending Solutions to provide a financing feasibility analysis and, ultimately, a Rule 26 expert report on the impact of below-market in-place rents and lease rollover structure on loan sizing, lender eligibility, and refinancing capacity for a single-tenant NNN property in the Tyler market. The asset was a freestanding commercial building leased to a single tenant under a long-term absolute NNN lease, with the in-place rent representing a rate negotiated several years prior and now materially below current market rents for comparable space in the submarket.

The core legal and financial questions presented to CLS CRE were threefold. First, does the below-market lease structure constrain the maximum supportable loan amount in ways that are material to the damages calculation at issue? Second, do lenders give credit for rollover upside, meaning the higher rent achievable upon lease expiration or early termination, in their loan sizing methodology? Third, was refinancing at specific LTV and DSCR thresholds feasible under market conditions at the relevant date, and if not, what were the binding constraints?

These questions sit squarely in the expertise gap between appraisal and financing. An MAI appraisal can establish value, including value under the leased fee interest with a below-market lease and the fee simple value at market rent. What it cannot do is translate that value into a loanable amount, identify which lenders will and will not underwrite the rollover story, or explain how DSCR constraints interact with the lease term remaining to produce a specific maximum loan. That is the financing expert's role, and it requires current, active market knowledge, not theoretical valuation methodology.

The Financial Analysis

The analysis began with a precise separation of two income streams: the contractual NOI under the in-place lease and the market NOI reflective of current asking and executed rents for comparable single-tenant space in Tyler. This distinction is fundamental because virtually every institutional lender, CMBS conduit, and life insurance company underwrites to the contractual rent during the remaining lease term, not the market rent. Lenders are not equity investors. They do not underwrite to the upside; they underwrite to the contractual cash flow available to service the debt during the loan term.

For this engagement, the in-place NNN rent produced an annual NOI of approximately $285,000 after deducting the minimal landlord obligations typical of an absolute NNN structure. Comparable market rents for the submarket and product type indicated a market NOI closer to $390,000, representing a below-market gap of approximately $105,000 annually. That gap is not trivial. It is the difference between the lease telling a credit story and the property telling a credit story.

Using the lender-standard underwriting framework, we applied a 1.25x DSCR requirement, which is the floor used by most CMBS conduits, regional bank portfolio lenders, and life company programs for single-tenant NNN assets with credit-appropriate tenants. Some community bank programs will accept 1.20x, particularly for shorter amortization periods, but 1.25x is the defensible market standard for this product type. We applied a 6.75% all-in interest rate reflecting market pricing for fixed-rate commercial mortgages at the relevant analysis period, with a 25-year amortization schedule, which is standard for NNN single-tenant retail and industrial assets. These assumptions were not selected to favor any analytical outcome; they reflect what the lender market was actually requiring at the time of the relevant events.

At 1.25x DSCR with a 6.75% rate and 25-year amortization, the constant on this structure produces an annual debt service per dollar of loan of approximately 8.47 cents. Dividing the contractual NOI of $285,000 by the 1.25x DSCR requirement yields maximum annual debt service supportable of $228,000. Dividing $228,000 by the loan constant of 0.0847 produces a maximum supportable loan of approximately $2,692,000 based on the in-place lease cash flow.

When the same calculation is run on the market NOI of $390,000, the maximum supportable loan rises to approximately $3,683,000. That $991,000 difference in loan capacity is the quantified financing impact of the below-market lease. At a stabilized value for the property of approximately $5,200,000 based on a market cap rate of 7.5% applied to market NOI, the LTV at the contractual NOI-supported loan is approximately 51.8%. At the market NOI-supported loan, LTV rises to 70.8%, which is within the standard 70% to 75% LTV range most lenders require for this product type. The below-market lease is not just depressing the loan amount; it is the difference between a loan that is comfortably within LTV parameters and one that is not, because DSCR is the binding constraint rather than LTV.

The Complicating Factors

Rollover Upside: What Lenders Will and Will Not Credit

The most common misconception in below-market NNN disputes is that rollover upside offsets the financing impact of a below-market lease. The logic seems intuitive: if the lease expires in three years and market rent is 37% higher, surely a lender will account for that. In practice, the overwhelming majority of lenders will not, and the reasons are structural, not conservative. Lenders underwrite to the cash flow securing the debt during the loan term. If the lease has four years remaining and the loan term is ten years, the lender must underwrite both the contractual period and the speculative post-rollover period. Most CMBS programs and life company lenders will not underwrite to an assumed re-lease at market rent unless the existing lease has 24 months or fewer remaining, the borrower has demonstrated re-leasing execution in the market, or the loan structure includes a cash flow sweep or reserve mechanism that protects the lender during the transition.

Some portfolio lenders, particularly regional banks with deep Tyler market knowledge, will take a blended view, weighting the contractual rent during the remaining term and applying a haircut to market rent for the post-rollover period. But even in these cases, lenders typically apply a 10% to 15% leasing cost deduction, a 6 to 12 month vacancy assumption between tenants, and tenant improvement reserves that reduce the effective post-rollover NOI materially. The rollover upside story does not produce the same loan sizing as in-place market rent. It produces something less, and the delta needs to be quantified, not assumed away.

Lease Term Remaining and Lender Appetite

Single-tenant NNN lenders are acutely sensitive to lease term remaining relative to the proposed loan term. Most institutional programs require the lease to extend at least 5 years beyond the loan maturity date, or for the loan to be structured with a term no longer than the remaining lease. When a below-market lease has, for example, three to five years remaining on a requested ten-year loan, the lender faces a dual problem: below-market cash flow today and lease expiration within the loan term. This combination pushes the asset outside the credit box of virtually all CMBS conduits and most life company programs. It does not necessarily eliminate financing, but it shifts the lender universe to bridge lenders, local bank portfolio programs, and credit tenant lending platforms that require personal guarantees or additional collateral.

Tenant Credit and Market Re-Leasing Risk

For single-tenant NNN assets in Tyler, tenant credit quality interacts directly with the below-market lease problem. If the tenant is an investment-grade national credit, lenders may be more willing to underwrite to contractual cash flow through the lease term, even at below-market rents, because the default probability is lower and the lease itself has credit support value. If the tenant is a regional or local operator, lenders will discount both the contractual cash flow and the re-leasing assumption, applying a higher vacancy period and lower assumed re-leasing rent. In the Tyler context, this distinction matters because many NNN properties in the market are occupied by regional healthcare, logistics, or food-and-beverage operators that do not carry investment-grade ratings. The financing analysis must reflect that reality.

The Tyler Lender Market

Tyler's lender market for single-tenant NNN assets is more stratified than many observers assume. Understanding which lenders are active, what they require, and how they price is essential both for financing execution and for litigation analysis, because the question of whether financing was available at a specific time requires knowledge of what each lender category was actually doing in that market.

Community and Regional Banks: Tyler has meaningful community bank depth, including institutions with genuine local market knowledge and the willingness to underwrite relationship-based commercial loans with less rigid credit box constraints than CMBS conduits. These lenders typically operate at loan amounts from $500,000 to $10,000,000 for NNN assets, price at floating or short-term fixed rates with 3 to 5 year terms, and require 1.20x to 1.25x DSCR with 65% to 75% LTV. They are more likely than institutional lenders to take a blended view on rollover upside if the borrower has local track record. However, they typically require personal guarantees, impose covenant packages that CMBS loans do not, and may not be suitable for transactions requiring non-recourse financing.

CMBS Conduit Lenders: CMBS programs are active in Tyler for single-tenant NNN assets meeting their credit tenant and lease term requirements. They provide non-recourse financing, fixed 10-year terms, and 25 to 30-year amortization, typically pricing at spreads of 175 to 225 basis points over the applicable swap rate depending on credit and LTV. Their credit boxes are rigid: they require investment-grade or near-investment-grade tenants, leases extending beyond loan maturity, and 1.25x DSCR minimum on in-place cash flow without rollover credit. Below-market leases with short remaining terms are frequently disqualifying for CMBS execution unless the loan is sized to the contractual cash flow with significant LTV headroom.

Life Insurance Company Programs: Life company lenders are selectively active in Tyler for institutional-quality NNN assets, particularly those leased to healthcare or essential retail tenants. They offer the lowest available rates for qualifying assets, typically 25 to 50 basis points inside CMBS pricing, with strong preference for long lease terms, conservative LTV at 60% to 65%, and DSCR of 1.30x or better. They will not underwrite rollover upside under any standard structure. Life company programs are effectively unavailable for below-market lease situations unless the loan is sized to a very conservative level that the transaction economics may not support.

Debt Fund and Bridge Lenders: For below-market NNN assets with lease rollover risk, bridge lenders and debt funds represent the execution path when permanent capital is unavailable. These lenders price at 300 to 500 basis points over SOFR on floating rate structures, typically with 2 to 3 year initial terms and extension options. They will underwrite to the rollover story more aggressively than permanent lenders but require realistic exit strategies and typically impose DSCR floors on a trailing basis to trigger sweep or default provisions. In Tyler, several national bridge platforms are active in this space, though pricing and structure vary materially based on the specific lease and tenant profile.

What Attorneys Handling Tyler CRE Disputes Need to Know

The most important thing attorneys handling Tyler commercial real estate litigation need to understand is when a CRE financing expert adds value that an appraiser cannot provide. An appraisal establishes value. A financing expert establishes what that value translates to in debt capacity, which lenders would provide it, under what terms, and what structural or credit factors preclude or enable specific financing outcomes. These are distinct questions, and conflating them leads to gaps in the expert record that opposing counsel will exploit.

When to retain a CRE financing expert: Counsel should engage a financing expert when damages depend on refinancing capacity, when the dispute involves a failed closing or declined loan commitment, when the question is whether a borrower could have obtained financing on specific terms, or when lease structure, credit quality, or property condition allegedly impaired financing availability. Expert testimony on these questions requires active market knowledge, lender relationship access, and transactional experience, not just appraisal methodology.

What a financing expert can answer that an appraiser cannot: An appraiser can opine on value and, to a limited degree, cap rates. A CRE financing expert can opine on the specific loan amount a lender would approve, the DSCR and LTV thresholds actually applied by market participants, whether rollover upside would be credited by lenders at the relevant date, which lender categories were active and willing for the specific asset type and profile, and what the difference in loan proceeds was between two lease structures or two rent levels. These are financing opinions, not valuation opinions, and they require a different credentialing and experiential foundation.

What qualifies a CRE financing expert: A retired loan officer or appraisal-focused consultant is generally not a qualified CRE financing expert. Qualification requires active transactional experience, meaning loans closed in the current market environment, direct lender relationships across multiple capital source categories, and demonstrated experience structuring transactions involving the specific complexities at issue, whether that is below-market leases, rollover risk, credit tenant analysis, or CMBS subordination. At CLS CRE, our qualification derives from approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, active relationships with more than 1,000 lenders across the capital stack, and direct experience in all 50 states with single-tenant NNN financing in markets including Tyler and comparable Texas markets.

Information counsel should gather before engaging a financing expert: At minimum, counsel should have the rent roll or lease abstract, the lease itself including all amendments and options, any loan applications or term sheets in evidence, the appraisal or BPO, the lender's credit file if available through discovery, and any correspondence between the parties regarding financing availability or commitment. The more complete the factual record provided to the financing expert at engagement, the more precise and defensible the expert's analysis will be.

How financing expert testimony is used: In trial, financing expert testimony typically addresses damages quantification, financing feasibility as of a specific date, and lender market conditions. In plan confirmation hearings, financing expert testimony frequently addresses whether a debtor's reorganization plan is feasible given the financing required, and whether proposed financing terms are consistent with market standards. In arbitration, financing experts are retained to establish whether a party's claimed financing expectations were reasonable given market conditions. In all of these contexts, the expert's credibility depends on the specificity and currency of their market knowledge, not just their credentials.

Key Takeaways

  • Below-market NNN leases constrain loan sizing directly and materially. Because lenders underwrite to contractual cash flow, not market rent, a below-market lease reduces the maximum supportable loan regardless of the property's fee simple value. In the Tyler analysis described here, the below-market lease reduced supportable loan proceeds by nearly $1,000,000 relative to a market-rate lease structure.

SBA Loans for Commercial Real Estate: Complete Guide to SBA 7(a) and SBA 504 in 2026

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Commercial Lending Solutions
```html

SBA loans are the most underutilized financing tool in commercial real estate. I work with small business owners every week who either assume they don't qualify, don't know the programs exist, or have been steered toward conventional financing that costs them significantly more out of pocket and over the life of the loan. This guide explains both SBA 7(a) and SBA 504 in plain terms, with the specific numbers and structure you need to evaluate whether an SBA loan is right for your transaction.

SBA 7(a) vs. SBA 504: Understanding the Structural Difference

These are two distinct programs with different structures, rates, and ideal use cases. Conflating them is the most common mistake I see borrowers make when researching SBA financing.

SBA 7(a) is a general-purpose loan guaranteed by the Small Business Administration and funded entirely by an SBA-approved bank or lender. The SBA guarantees up to 85% of the loan amount on loans at or below $150,000, and 75% on loans above that threshold. The lender retains the unguaranteed portion. Because the guarantee reduces lender risk, 7(a) lenders can offer terms that conventional lenders won't: lower down payments, longer amortization, and more flexible underwriting on business cash flow. Maximum loan amount is $5 million.

SBA 504 is a split-structure loan designed specifically for fixed assets: real estate, equipment, and leasehold improvements. It involves three parties:

  • A conventional bank lender provides 50% of the project cost as a first mortgage at market rates.
  • A Certified Development Company (CDC) provides 40% as a second mortgage backed by an SBA-guaranteed debenture.
  • The borrower contributes 10% as a down payment.

The maximum SBA debenture (the CDC piece) is $5.5 million for standard projects and $5.5 million per project for manufacturers and energy-efficient projects, with a total project cost ceiling that varies by program. In practice, most SBA 504 CRE transactions fall in the $1 million to $15 million range.

For commercial real estate, 504 is almost always the better option. Here is why: the CDC piece carries a fixed rate set monthly by the SBA that has historically run 100 to 250 basis points below what a bank would charge on a conventional second mortgage. When you blend that below-market fixed rate against the conventional first, your blended cost of capital drops materially.

Structure Single loan, one lender Split: 50% bank + 40% CDC + 10% borrower
Max loan amount $5 million $5.5M SBA debenture (project size varies)

SBA 504: The Best Owner-Occupant CRE Loan on the Market

For a business purchasing the building it operates from, the 504 program is the most capital-efficient financing structure available in the United States today. Let me walk through the mechanics with actual numbers.

Assume a $3 million office building purchase. The capital stack looks like this:

  • Bank first mortgage: $1,500,000 (50%) at a market rate, typically 6.5–7.5% in 2026 depending on the lender and term
  • CDC debenture (SBA second): $1,200,000 (40%) at the SBA's fixed effective rate, which has ranged from approximately 5.5–6.5% over the past 24 months and is published monthly
  • Borrower equity: $300,000 (10%)

The SBA debenture carries a 25-year fully amortizing term with no balloon. It is a fixed rate for the life of the loan. That is not a teaser. It is not a 5-year fixed that reprices. It is locked for 25 years.

The bank first mortgage terms vary by lender. Some bank lenders will offer a 10-year fixed rate with a 25-year amortization. Others offer shorter fixed periods. Negotiating the bank piece is where a broker adds significant value: the SBA piece is standardized, but the bank piece is not.

One critical SBA requirement that catches borrowers off guard: personal guarantees are mandatory. The SBA requires unlimited personal guarantees from all owners holding 20% or more of the business. Non-recourse is not available under any SBA program. If you are an investor looking for non-recourse debt, you are looking at CMBS or life company, not SBA.

Eligible Properties and Eligible Uses

What Qualifies

The owner-occupancy requirement is the defining eligibility test for SBA CRE. Here are the thresholds:

  • Purchase of existing property: The borrower's business must occupy at least 51% of the rentable square footage.
  • New construction: The borrower's business must occupy at least 60% of the completed building.

The 51% threshold is measured by the business actually using the space, not leasing it to an affiliate or holding it vacant. If a business occupies 52% of a building and leases the remaining 48% to third-party tenants, that qualifies. The rental income from the tenant space actually helps the deal by supporting debt service coverage.

Property types that routinely qualify include:

  • Office buildings (medical, dental, professional services, tech)
  • Industrial and warehouse facilities (manufacturing, distribution, logistics)
  • Retail buildings with owner-operated businesses
  • Mixed-use properties where the business occupies the commercial portion
  • Special purpose properties: hotels, gas stations, car washes, assisted living facilities (subject to higher down payment requirements discussed below)

Eligible Uses of Proceeds

  • Purchase of existing commercial real estate
  • Ground-up construction of a new facility
  • Renovation or leasehold improvements tied to a purchase or construction
  • Refinance of existing CRE debt (subject to specific program rules)
  • Under 7(a): purchase of real estate combined with equipment, working capital, or business acquisition

What Does Not Qualify

SBA loans are explicitly not available for investment real estate. A borrower purchasing a retail strip center to collect rents, an apartment building, a net-lease property, or any property where the borrower will not occupy a material portion of the space does not qualify. Passive investors and real estate holding companies do not qualify. The SBA is in the business of helping operating businesses own their facilities, not building real estate portfolios.

How SBA Loans Are Underwritten: Global Cash Flow

This is where SBA financing becomes genuinely powerful for the right borrower, and where it differs most from conventional investment real estate underwriting.

Conventional lenders underwrite CRE primarily on the property: net operating income divided by debt service to produce a debt service coverage ratio (DSCR). If the property's income alone does not support the debt, the deal fails. That is the entire analysis.

SBA underwriting uses global cash flow analysis. The underwriter combines the business's cash flow with the property's cash flow into a single coverage calculation. A manufacturing company purchasing its facility might show thin or even negative net income from the building itself (especially in the early years with low outside tenant income), but the business operating inside that facility generates strong free cash flow. Under SBA underwriting, that business cash flow counts.

In practice, I have seen properties that would fail DSCR as a conventional investment loan at 1.0x coverage qualify comfortably as SBA transactions at 1.35x or higher when the business income is layered in. This is the mechanism that makes SBA viable for owner-occupants who could never qualify for conventional investor financing on the same building.

Credit and Business Requirements

  • FICO: Minimum 680 across all principals owning 20%+. Most lenders prefer 700+. Below 680 is a hard stop at most banks.
  • Time in business: 2 years of operating history is the standard threshold. Startups or businesses under 2 years face a higher down payment (15% instead of 10% on 504) and more restrictive lender appetite.
  • Profitability: The business must show positive cash flow on tax returns for the past 2 years. Adjusted cash flow (adding back depreciation, owner compensation above market, one-time expenses) is used, but the underlying business must be viable. A business losing money on paper with aggressive tax strategies will need to document the adjustments carefully.
  • No recent bankruptcies or defaults on federal debt: Any prior default on an SBA loan or federal government obligation is disqualifying.

Down Payment and Equity Requirements

The 10% down payment on SBA 504 is the headline advantage. Conventional CRE lenders typically require 25–30% down on owner-occupied properties and 30–35% on investment properties. The SBA's 10% floor allows a business to preserve significantly more capital for operations, equipment, and growth.

However, the 10% figure applies to established businesses purchasing standard property types. The required down payment increases to 15% in two scenarios:

  • Business in operation less than 2 years at the time of application
  • Special purpose property: Properties with limited alternative uses including hotels, motels, gas stations, car washes, funeral homes, golf courses, marinas, and similar facilities. Lenders classify these as higher risk because they are difficult to reposition if the business fails.

Under SBA 7(a), the down payment requirement varies by lender. Most 7(a) lenders require 10–20% equity injection for CRE transactions. The SBA does not mandate a specific number for 7(a) the way it does for 504; instead, it requires that the lender's equity injection policy is consistent and documented.

Acceptable Equity Sources

The SBA is more flexible on equity sourcing than conventional lenders in some respects. Acceptable equity contributions include:

  • Cash from business or personal accounts
  • Seller financing on full standby (seller agrees to no payments for a specified period, typically the life of the SBA loan) -- this is a powerful tool when negotiating with motivated sellers
  • Gift of equity from a family member, properly documented
  • Equity from the sale of another asset

What is not acceptable: cash-out of the subject property to manufacture equity, borrowed equity injection without SBA approval, or equity that the lender cannot verify the source of.

The SBA Loan Process and Realistic Timelines

SBA loans take longer than conventional loans. Building that into your purchase contract timeline is critical.

SBA 504 Timeline

A complete 504 transaction involves two parallel approval processes: the bank approves the first mortgage, and the CDC packages and submits the debenture application to the SBA. When both are moving simultaneously with a complete application package, you are looking at 45 to 75 days from complete application to close in most cases. Complex transactions, environmental issues, or appraisal disputes can push that to 90 days.

The CDC is your primary SBA contact on a 504 transaction. CDCs are regional nonprofit organizations authorized by the SBA to originate and service 504 debentures. A good CDC with an established relationship with the SBA can move the government approval piece in 10–15 business days.

SBA 7(a) Timeline

The 7(a) program has two processing tracks. Standard processing runs through the SBA's central loan processing centers and takes 5–10 business days for SBA approval after the lender's internal credit approval (which takes 1–4 weeks). Total timeline is typically 30 to 60 days.

Preferred Lender Program (PLP) lenders have delegated authority to approve 7(a) loans without prior SBA review. Working with a PLP lender can compress the timeline to 20 to 40 days for straightforward transactions. Not every bank is a PLP lender. Asking your broker which lenders have PLP status for your transaction type is the fastest way to shorten the timeline.

What Slows Down SBA Closings

  • Incomplete application packages: Missing tax returns, incomplete financial statements, or organizational documents that don't match the borrowing entity create back-and-forth that adds weeks.
  • Phase I environmental issues: Any identified concern triggers a Phase II environmental assessment. Budget 4–8 additional weeks and potentially tens of thousands of dollars.
  • Appraisal disputes: The SBA requires an independent appraisal. If the appraised value comes in below the purchase price, the entire capital stack is recalculated on the appraised value, not the contract price. This is a common deal killer on aggressively priced properties.
  • Franchise approval: Franchise businesses must have their franchise agreement reviewed and approved by the SBA. Major franchises are on the SBA's pre-approved franchise registry; minor or emerging franchises require case-by-case review.

I accelerate closings by submitting complete packages the first time, front-loading environmental review, and having established relationships with CDCs and PLP lenders who move quickly. On a well-prepared SBA 504 deal, 50 days to close is realistic.

Refinancing Existing CRE Debt with SBA 504

The SBA 504 refinance program is one of the most underutilized opportunities in the market right now. Many business owners financed their buildings in 2018–2022 with variable-rate conventional loans or 5-year fixed mortgages that have since repriced. Rates that were 4–5% in 2020 are now 7–8.5% on renewal. The 504 refinance program was designed for exactly this situation.

How the 504 Refinance Works

The structure mirrors the purchase program: the bank provides a 50% first mortgage, the CDC provides a 40% SBA-backed debenture at the fixed below-market rate, and the borrower brings equity (or it comes from existing equity in the property). The borrower does not need to bring new cash to close if there is sufficient existing equity.

Eligibility Requirements for 504 Refinance

  • Rate reduction: The new blended rate must be at least 10% below the existing rate being refinanced. This is calculated on the total debt service, not just the new note rate.
  • Current on existing loan: No delinquency on the debt being refinanced for the prior 12 months.
  • Owner-occupancy: Same 51% occupancy requirement as a purchase.
  • Eligible debt: The existing debt being refinanced must be on the eligible property. Personal loans, lines of credit, and unrelated business debt cannot be rolled in.
  • Loan seasoning: The existing loan must have been in place for at least 6 months (some lenders require 12 months).

The 504 refinance program also allows "business expenses" to be included: up to 20% of the eligible fixed assets can be used to refinance existing business debt or pull out working capital. This means a refinance can simultaneously lower your building payment and consolidate higher-rate business debt.

For business owners sitting on buildings with variable-rate debt or maturing balloon payments in 2026 and 2027, this program deserves an immediate conversation. The 25-year fixed rate on the SBA debenture is a hedge against future rate volatility that you cannot get from a conventional lender at this leverage level.

SBA Loan Costs: What to Budget

SBA loans carry fees that conventional loans do not. Understanding them upfront prevents surprises.

SBA 504 Fees

  • SBA guarantee fee: Approximately 0.5% of the debenture amount, paid upfront or financed into the loan
  • CDC processing fee: Approximately 1.5% of the debenture amount
  • Funding fee: Approximately 0.25% of the debenture
  • Third-party fees: Appraisal ($3,000–$8,000 for commercial), Phase I environmental ($2,000–$4,000), title, recording

Total fees on the SBA piece typically run 2.5–3% of the debenture, much of which can be financed into the debenture itself rather than paid in cash at closing.

SBA 7(a) Fees

The SBA charges a guarantee fee on 7(a) loans based on the guaranteed portion: 0% for loans under $150,000, and a sliding scale from 2% to 3.75% for larger loans. The guarantee fee can be financed into the loan. Additionally, lenders charge origination fees (0.5–2% typically) and standard third-party costs.

Is an SBA Loan Right for Your Transaction?

SBA financing is not the right tool for every deal. Here is a quick self-assessment:

Strong candidate for SBA 504 if:

  • You are an operating business purchasing or building the facility you will use
  • You will occupy 51%+ of the square footage
  • You want to preserve cash (10% down vs. 25–30% conventional)
  • You want a fixed-rate component for 25 years
  • Your business has 2+ years of profitable operating history
  • FICO is 680+

Consider SBA 7(a) instead of 504 if:

  • You need to combine CRE with equipment purchase, working capital, or a business acquisition in a single loan
  • The transaction involves a franchise or business purchase
  • The property is under $1 million (504 fees can make the program less efficient at lower loan sizes)

SBA is likely not the right tool if:

  • The property is purely investment (no owner occupancy)
  • Speed is paramount (you need to close in 30 days or fewer)
  • You need non-recourse debt
  • The business has less than 2 years of history and the property is a special purpose type (stacking two 15% triggers)
```

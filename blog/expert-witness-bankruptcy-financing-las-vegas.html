Expert Witness: CRE Financing Feasibility in Chapter 11 Proceedings in Las Vegas, NV

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Bankruptcy Exit Financing

The Las Vegas Market Context

Las Vegas occupies a unique position in western commercial real estate. It is simultaneously one of the most volatile markets in the country and one of the most resilient, driven by tourism, logistics, data center expansion, and a sustained in-migration wave that has fundamentally altered the market's economic profile over the past decade. For attorneys and financing professionals involved in commercial real estate disputes, that duality matters enormously. A property that looked distressed at the time a bankruptcy petition was filed may look very different by the time a Plan of Reorganization reaches a confirmation hearing, and the inverse is equally true.

The Las Vegas industrial market has been one of the strongest performing asset classes in the region. Net absorption has remained positive for multiple consecutive years, vacancy rates in the core distribution corridors along the I-15 and I-215 have consistently tracked below 5%, and asking rents per square foot have increased materially as Southern California industrial users have migrated to Nevada to access cheaper land, favorable tax treatment, and proximity to a major population center without California's cost structure. For creditors asserting that an industrial asset in a Las Vegas submarket can support institutional first-mortgage financing, that supply-demand backdrop is directly relevant to the income analysis.

Multifamily in Las Vegas has undergone a more complicated trajectory. Rent growth that peaked during the 2021 to 2022 period has moderated, and certain submarkets have absorbed significant new supply. Cap rates, which compressed to the low-to-mid 4% range during peak pricing, have moved out toward the 5.5% to 6.5% range in many transactions as interest rates increased, creating a meaningful gap between in-place debt and current market value in properties that were financed at peak. That repricing dynamic is frequently at the center of Chapter 11 filings involving multifamily assets. Office in Las Vegas is a more challenged story, with hybrid work patterns suppressing absorption along the suburban office corridors of Henderson and Summerlin, making conventional first-mortgage financing on non-stabilized office assets difficult to source regardless of bankruptcy history.

Retail in Las Vegas presents a bifurcated picture. Grocery-anchored neighborhood centers and experiential retail adjacent to the resort corridor have maintained strong occupancy. Strip retail and secondary-anchored product without a traffic-driving anchor has faced sustained leasing headwinds. The type of retail asset matters significantly in any financing feasibility analysis, and any expert opinion that treats Las Vegas retail as a monolithic category is analytically deficient.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by counsel representing a secured creditor holding a first-priority deed of trust on a multi-tenant industrial flex property located in the north Las Vegas submarket. The debtor had filed a Chapter 11 petition following a maturity default on senior debt, and the debtor's proposed Plan of Reorganization included a restructured payoff to the senior creditor through proceeds of a new first-mortgage loan to be originated post-confirmation. The creditor's counsel retained CLS CRE to provide a Rule 26 expert report addressing two core questions that the court needed resolved before confirming the plan: first, whether the property in its current physical and financial condition could support first-mortgage financing sufficient in amount to satisfy the senior creditor's allowed claim with interest; and second, what categories of lenders actively originate loans on real property assets that are or recently were part of a bankruptcy estate, and on what terms.

The asset was a multi-tenant flex industrial property of approximately 85,000 square feet, partially leased to small-bay industrial users, with occupancy at the time of the expert engagement running at approximately 78%. The debtor's plan projected that post-confirmation lease-up would occur within 12 months, and the proposed refinancing was structured as a take-out loan to be placed within 90 days of plan confirmation. Our engagement required us to analyze whether the property's income stream, at both current and stabilized occupancy, could support a loan sufficient to pay the creditor's claim, and to opine on whether the lender market would realistically provide such financing on the timeline proposed.

The Financial Analysis

The methodology we applied follows the same underwriting logic that institutional lenders use when evaluating a commercial mortgage request, which is the appropriate benchmark for a feasibility opinion. The opinion is not about what any single lender would do; it is about what the competent, active lender market would do based on current standards and conditions.

Net Operating Income Analysis. We began with the rent roll as of the petition date, adjusted for lease expiration risk, vacancy, and credit quality of the tenants in place. Gross potential rent across the occupied units was annualized at approximately $1,020,000 based on in-place lease rates, which were slightly below market for comparable north Las Vegas flex product. We applied a vacancy and credit loss factor of 10%, consistent with lender underwriting practice for multi-tenant flex where no single tenant represents more than 20% of gross revenues. After operating expenses inclusive of property taxes, insurance, management fees at 4% of effective gross income, and a capital reserve of $0.15 per square foot, the stabilized NOI supportable for debt service coverage purposes was calculated at approximately $740,000 annually.

DSCR Threshold. For multi-tenant industrial product in a secondary market like Las Vegas, conventional lenders apply a minimum debt service coverage ratio of 1.25x and many institutional lenders underwrite to 1.30x for assets with any occupancy or tenancy complexity. We applied 1.25x as the market standard threshold, which is the appropriate benchmark for this asset class and market. That threshold is not arbitrary; it represents the lender's cushion against income volatility, and for a property coming out of bankruptcy, many lenders will apply a stress haircut on top of their standard DSCR requirement, effectively underwriting to 1.30x or higher.

Rate and Amortization Assumptions. At the time of the engagement, commercial mortgage rates for non-agency stabilized industrial were pricing at approximately 7.00% to 7.50% for 5-year fixed terms with 25-year amortization schedules. For a bankruptcy exit scenario with less than 24 months of seasoning post-confirmation, lenders in this category typically add a risk premium of 50 to 100 basis points. We used a rate assumption of 7.75% on a 25-year amortization as a conservative market mid-point, consistent with what bridge lenders and CMBS conduits were quoting for comparable risk profiles.

Maximum Supportable Debt. Working backward from the NOI and DSCR floor, the maximum annual debt service supportable at 1.25x coverage is $592,000 ($740,000 divided by 1.25). At a 7.75% rate on a 25-year amortization schedule, the monthly payment per $1,000,000 of loan balance is approximately $7,575, or $90,900 annually. Dividing $592,000 of available annual debt service by $90,900 per million yields a maximum supportable loan balance of approximately $6,512,000 based on cash flow alone.

LTV Constraint Check. For institutional first-mortgage lending on industrial flex product in Las Vegas, maximum LTV at the time of the engagement ranged from 65% for bank portfolio loans to 70% for CMBS conduit executions on stabilized product. The appraised value of the property, based on the most recent court-ordered appraisal, was $9,800,000. Applying a 65% LTV cap produces a maximum loan of $6,370,000; at 70% LTV, $6,860,000. The cash-flow-derived maximum of $6,512,000 fell within both LTV parameters, confirming that the binding constraint was income coverage, not collateral value. The creditor's allowed claim, inclusive of accrued interest and fees, was approximately $5,900,000. Our analysis concluded that a loan sufficient to satisfy the allowed claim was financeable, but with a narrow margin of approximately $600,000 of debt service headroom, the plan had limited tolerance for income deterioration during the lease-up period.

The Complicating Factors

Bankruptcy Seasoning Requirements. The single most significant complicating factor in any bankruptcy exit financing is lender seasoning. Most conventional lenders, including bank portfolio lenders, life companies, and CMBS conduit originators, have internal credit policies that prohibit originating first-mortgage loans on properties that emerged from bankruptcy within the prior 12 to 36 months, depending on the lender type. This is not a legal prohibition; it is a credit policy response to the risk of plan failure, fraudulent conveyance challenges, and the reputational exposure that comes from participating in a transaction that returns to distress. Life companies are among the most restrictive, frequently requiring 36 months of clean operating history post-confirmation. CMBS conduits vary by deal sponsor, with some accepting 12-month seasoning for stabilized product in primary markets and 24 months for secondary markets like Las Vegas. Bridge lenders and debt funds represent the most flexible category, frequently willing to originate without seasoning requirements, though at higher rates and with more protective loan structure.

Partial Occupancy at Confirmation. The debtor's plan proposed that a refinancing loan would be placed 90 days after confirmation at 78% occupancy, with the projection that stabilized occupancy would be achieved in the subsequent 12 months. Lenders do not underwrite to projected occupancy; they underwrite to in-place, verified, and documented cash flow. A 78% occupancy rate on a multi-tenant flex property in Las Vegas is technically sufficient to clear a 1.25x DSCR at the loan amount required, but only at the top of the market rate curve and without any expense surprises. Any lender reviewing this structure will apply a more conservative stabilized vacancy assumption, typically 10% to 15% on flex product, which compresses the usable NOI. Counsel for the creditor needed to understand that the 90-day refinancing timeline in the debtor's plan was unrealistic for conventional lenders and feasible only through a bridge lending execution, which carries a different rate, fee, and term structure than the debtor's plan contemplated.

Title and Lien Resolution. Properties exiting bankruptcy carry a confirmation order that is intended to function as a lien-stripping mechanism for junior creditors whose claims are addressed in the plan. In practice, institutional lenders, particularly those whose loans will be securitized, require clean title insurance with no exceptions related to the prior bankruptcy proceeding. Title underwriters in Nevada have become more sophisticated about insuring over confirmed plan orders, but the process requires a detailed review of the confirmation order, proof of plan consummation, and in some cases a post-confirmation quiet title proceeding. Lenders expecting a 90-day closing from confirmation need to factor this title resolution work into the timeline. Our report flagged this as a structural impediment to the debtor's proposed schedule that the court should weigh in assessing plan feasibility.

Recourse Requirements for Exit Financing. Most bridge lenders and debt funds willing to extend credit on a bankruptcy exit will require full recourse or a completion guarantee from the sponsoring principal. In a Chapter 11 context where the reorganized debtor's principal may have limited financial wherewithal post-reorganization, sourcing a creditworthy guarantor can be as difficult as meeting the property-level underwriting criteria. This is a factor that courts frequently overlook when evaluating plan feasibility on the basis of collateral value alone.

The Las Vegas Lender Market

Bridge Lenders and Debt Funds. This is the most active category for bankruptcy exit financing on Las Vegas commercial assets. Debt funds and non-bank bridge lenders, many of which maintain Nevada-specific origination activity, will lend on properties emerging from bankruptcy without seasoning restrictions, provided the collateral is in a recoverable submarket, the in-place cash flow is documentable, and the sponsor can provide adequate recourse support. Rates typically range from 8.50% to 11.00% for first-mortgage bridge positions on stabilized or near-stabilized industrial product in Las Vegas, with origination fees of 1.00% to 2.00% and terms of 12 to 36 months with extension options. These lenders underwrite to current LTV and in-place DSCR, and they value speed and flexibility over rate optimization. For a plan requiring a 90-day refinancing, this is the only realistic lender category at the moment of emergence.

Regional and Community Banks. Nevada has a functional community banking market, with institutions headquartered in Las Vegas and Reno that maintain CRE portfolios in the state. These lenders are relationship-driven, portfolio-hold oriented, and typically require 12 to 24 months of post-bankruptcy operating history before they will engage on a new origination. Their rates for industrial product are competitive with agency-adjacent pricing, typically in the 7.00% to 8.00% range on 5 to 7-year terms, with 25-year amortization. They are appropriate for take-out financing after a bridge period, not for immediate bankruptcy exit.

CMBS Conduit Lenders. Several active CMBS originators cover the Las Vegas market for industrial and retail product. They require stabilized occupancy, typically defined as 90% or above for 90 days, and most have internal policies requiring a minimum of 12 months of post-confirmation operating history for properties that exited bankruptcy. CMBS execution offers non-recourse financing, which is attractive to reorganized debtors, but the structural requirements, including cash management, reserve funding, and lockbox arrangements, add operational complexity. For a well-stabilized asset with seasoned occupancy, CMBS is a viable permanent financing path, typically at 65% to 70% LTV with DSCR floors of 1.25x to 1.30x.

Life Insurance Companies. Life companies are present in Las Vegas for large industrial, retail, and multifamily assets above $15,000,000, but they are essentially unavailable for bankruptcy exit financing regardless of asset quality. Their investment mandates require clean, unencumbered title history, and their internal approval processes are incompatible with the timelines typical of plan confirmation proceedings.

What Attorneys Handling Las Vegas CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to Retain a CRE Financing Expert. If the feasibility of a Plan of Reorganization depends on the debtor's ability to refinance real property, you need a financing expert retained before the plan is filed, not after objections are raised. The debtor's plan proponent will frequently include a financing commitment letter or term sheet as evidence of feasibility. A creditor's expert can evaluate whether that commitment letter is from an active lender, whether the terms are commercially realistic, and whether the property-level metrics are sufficient to close the loan that the commitment contemplates. These are questions an appraiser cannot answer; an appraiser establishes value, not financing capacity or lender market access.

What a CRE Financing Expert Provides That an Appraiser Cannot. Appraisers determine market value under USPAP standards. A financing expert analyzes the debt capital markets: which lender categories are active for a specific asset type and risk profile, what underwriting criteria they apply, what the maximum supportable loan is based on current market pricing, and whether the timeline and terms proposed in a plan are achievable in the real lender market. In a confirmation hearing, a court needs both opinions, but they are not interchangeable.

Qualifications That Matter. A qualified CRE financing expert should have current, active origination experience, not merely historical experience as a banker or investor. The lender market changes materially with every rate cycle, credit cycle, and regulatory environment. An expert who originated loans in 2015 but has not been active since cannot reliably opine on what a lender would do in today's market. Look for experts with verifiable current transaction volume, documented lender relationships across multiple categories, and experience preparing expert reports that have withstood cross-examination in federal bankruptcy court or state court proceedings.

Information Counsel Should Gather Before the Engagement. Prior to retaining a financing expert, counsel should compile the rent roll and lease abstracts, trailing 12 to 24 months of operating statements, the most recent appraisal, the existing loan documents and any modification history, the proposed plan term sheet or term loan commitment, and any lender correspondence from the period preceding the filing. The expert will also need to understand the timeline the plan proposes for refinancing, who the reorganized debtor's principal will be, and whether any recourse guaranty is contemplated. The more complete this package, the more defensible and specific the expert opinion will be.

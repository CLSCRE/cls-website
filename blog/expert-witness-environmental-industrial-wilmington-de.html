Expert Witness: Financing a Contaminated Industrial Property in Wilmington, DE

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Environmental Contamination Analysis

The Wilmington Market Context

Wilmington, Delaware occupies a unique position in the Mid-Atlantic industrial corridor that makes it both strategically valuable and analytically complex when contaminated properties become the subject of litigation. The city sits at the convergence of I-95, I-495, and the Port of Wilmington, one of the largest auto-import terminals on the East Coast, which has historically driven dense concentrations of heavy industrial, chemical manufacturing, and logistics users along the Christina River waterfront and in the northern Delaware industrial submarkets. That industrial legacy is precisely why chlorinated volatile organic compound (VOC) contamination and 1,4-dioxane plume cases appear with notable frequency in Delaware Superior Court and in Chapter 11 proceedings before the Bankruptcy Court for the District of Delaware, one of the most active commercial bankruptcy venues in the country.

The Wilmington industrial market itself has tightened considerably over the past several years. Vacancy in the broader New Castle County industrial submarket has traded in the 4% to 7% range, supported by e-commerce distribution demand and last-mile logistics users seeking proximity to the Philadelphia and Baltimore metro populations. Average asking rents for Class B warehouse product in the Wilmington submarket have been tracking in the $7.00 to $9.50 per square foot triple-net range, though newer Class A distribution product in northern Delaware can command $10.00 to $12.00 NNN. Legacy industrial properties, particularly those carrying any environmental stigma, tend to cluster in the lower tier of that range or below it. Cap rates for stabilized industrial assets in Wilmington trade roughly in the 6.25% to 7.50% range depending on credit quality, lease term, and whether any environmental disclosure is attached to the asset. That spread matters enormously in a financing feasibility analysis: a 100-basis-point difference in cap rate on a $3 million NOI asset translates to roughly $2 million in implied value, which can be the difference between a loan that retires senior debt and one that falls short.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by counsel representing the senior secured creditor group in a regulatory enforcement matter involving an industrial warehouse property in the Wilmington submarket. The asset was a mid-size single-tenant warehouse facility, fully occupied on a long-term NNN lease at below-market rents, with a prior industrial use history that had generated a documented subsurface contamination condition. The Delaware Department of Natural Resources and Environmental Control (DNREC) had issued a formal notice of violation, and the responsible party determination was being litigated concurrent with the creditor's effort to understand whether the property could support conventional mortgage financing sufficient to satisfy the outstanding senior debt.

The core legal and financial questions presented to CLS CRE were specific and consequential. First, does the documented environmental condition, standing alone, preclude institutional first-mortgage financing in the current market? Second, if financing is available, what is the maximum supportable loan amount under current market underwriting standards? Third, how does the below-market lease structure affect the lender's underwriting of income, and what options, if any, exist to bridge the gap between contract rent and market rent for financing purposes? The creditor's litigation position depended in part on demonstrating that the property retained sufficient financing capacity to support a reorganization plan or a negotiated payoff, and that the environmental condition did not render the collateral effectively unfinanceable.

The Financial Analysis

The income analysis began with the existing lease, which is the correct starting point for any institutional lender underwriting a NNN-leased industrial asset. The property generated gross NNN rent of approximately $620,000 annually. Because the lease was structured as true triple-net with the tenant responsible for taxes, insurance, and all maintenance obligations, the NNN rent translated directly to net operating income with minimal leakage. We conservatively applied a 2% management and administrative reserve to arrive at an underwritten NOI of approximately $607,600. This is consistent with how CMBS servicers and balance sheet lenders approach NNN industrial: they credit the full NNN income stream but retain a nominal haircut for vacancy risk upon lease rollover and administrative costs.

The below-market rent issue required separate treatment. Market NNN rents for comparable warehouse product in the Wilmington submarket at the time of our analysis supported a market-rate NOI of approximately $810,000 to $850,000 on the same footprint. The gap between contract NOI ($607,600) and market NOI (midpoint approximately $830,000) is substantial, representing roughly a 37% discount. Lenders underwrite to in-place income, not market income, with narrow exceptions for short remaining lease terms where rollover to market is imminent. The remaining lease term in this case extended beyond five years, meaning no institutional lender would gross up income to market. The financing analysis had to be built entirely on the $607,600 underwritten NOI figure.

For debt service coverage ratio, we applied a 1.25x DSCR threshold as the base case, which reflects the market standard for industrial collateral carrying any credit complexity. Many conventional lenders will clear industrial at 1.20x DSCR for stabilized, clean assets with strong tenancy. The presence of documented environmental impairment pushes that requirement to 1.25x at minimum, and some lenders in the environmental-sensitive credit box require 1.30x or higher. Using 1.25x as the operative standard is defensible as the midpoint of market practice and is consistent with CMBS underwriting guidelines published by major rating agencies for comparable collateral.

For the interest rate assumption, we modeled a 7.25% fixed rate on a 25-year amortization schedule, which reflects the pricing environment for environmental-impaired industrial collateral through non-agency balance sheet lenders and certain CMBS conduits willing to underwrite subject-to environmental indemnity structures. This rate carries a 75-to-100 basis point premium over clean industrial collateral, reflecting the environmental risk premium lenders price into this category. Using these parameters, annual debt service on a 25-year amortization at 7.25% on a $1.00 of loan principal produces a constant of approximately 0.08683. Working backward from the DSCR constraint: $607,600 divided by 1.25x equals maximum allowable annual debt service of $486,080. Dividing $486,080 by the annual mortgage constant of 0.08683 produces a maximum supportable loan of approximately $5,598,000, which we rounded to $5.6 million for presentation purposes.

The LTV constraint provides an independent ceiling. For environmental-impaired industrial collateral in Wilmington, institutional lenders apply LTV limits in the 55% to 65% range, compared to 70% to 75% for clean institutional industrial. Using a capitalized value derived from the in-place NOI at a 7.25% cap rate (reflecting environmental stigma), implied value was approximately $8.38 million. Applying a 65% LTV ceiling produces a maximum loan of approximately $5.45 million. The two tests, DSCR and LTV, converged in the $5.4 to $5.6 million range, providing the creditor's counsel with a defensible financing capacity figure supported by two independent analytical methodologies. Whether that figure was sufficient to retire the existing senior debt was a question of comparing this output against the payoff demand, which was within the scope of the broader insolvency analysis.

The Complicating Factors

1,4-Dioxane and Chlorinated VOC Contamination as a Lender Risk Event. The specific contaminants at issue here, 1,4-dioxane and chlorinated VOCs such as trichloroethylene or perchloroethylene, represent a particularly difficult underwriting scenario for lenders. Unlike petroleum hydrocarbon contamination, which tends to be relatively localized and well-understood in terms of remediation cost and timeline, chlorinated VOC plumes are highly mobile in groundwater, can migrate significant distances from the source area, and have regulatory thresholds measured in parts per billion. DNREC has adopted EPA's maximum contaminant level of 0.35 micrograms per liter for 1,4-dioxane in groundwater, and standard chlorinated VOC MCLs are similarly low. Lenders who have environmental counsel review Phase II reports on these assets often conclude that the remediation timeline is indeterminate, which is the single most damaging characteristic from a financing standpoint. A lender can price a defined remediation cost; an open-ended remediation obligation with no cap is frequently a deal killer at the institutional level.

The DNREC Enforcement Action and Responsible Party Determination. An active regulatory enforcement proceeding creates lender liability exposure under CERCLA and under the Delaware Hazardous Substance Cleanup Act that most institutional lenders are not willing to accept without specific structural protections. The secured creditor exemption under CERCLA provides some insulation, but it applies only so long as the lender does not participate in management of the facility. A lender who takes title through foreclosure and then exercises any operational control risks losing that exemption. This means conventional lenders require an environmental indemnity from a creditworthy indemnitor, an owner-controlled environmental insurance policy with specific limits and a remediation cost cap endorsement, or ideally both, before they will close a loan on a property with an open enforcement action. If neither is available, institutional financing is effectively foreclosed regardless of the income stream.

The Below-Market Lease as a Dual-Edged Problem. The below-market rent creates two compounding problems. In the short term, it suppresses underwritten NOI and reduces loan capacity as demonstrated in the financial analysis above. In the longer term, it creates a lease rollover risk that lenders underwrite conservatively: if the tenant vacates at lease expiration rather than renewing at market, the lender is left with an environmentally impaired vacant industrial building. That residual risk profile causes lenders to further tighten LTV constraints and to require larger debt service reserves or lease rollover reserves at closing. We modeled a required lease rollover reserve of $150,000 held in escrow as a condition of closing, which effectively reduces net loan proceeds available to the borrower and was relevant to the creditor's analysis of whether proceeds were sufficient to retire the senior debt position.

Title Insurance Complications. Environmental enforcement actions frequently generate liens or notices of potential liability that create title insurance exceptions. Major title underwriters will not insure over an active DNREC enforcement lien without an indemnity arrangement or a consent agreement that defines the scope of the obligation. Lenders require clean lender's title insurance as a closing condition, and a title policy with broad environmental exceptions is typically rejected by the lender's counsel. This creates a closing mechanics problem that can be fatal to the transaction even when the economic analysis supports the loan amount.

The Wilmington Lender Market

The lender universe for environmentally impaired industrial collateral in Wilmington is narrower than for clean product, but it is not empty. Understanding which categories of lenders actually operate in this space is essential both for financing execution and for expert testimony on financing feasibility.

Balance Sheet Regional and Community Banks. Delaware's community banking sector is relatively deep for a small state, anchored by several institutions with significant commercial real estate portfolios concentrated in New Castle County. These lenders are the most likely source of financing for impaired industrial collateral at the sub-$10 million loan size because they can apply portfolio-level credit discretion that CMBS conduits cannot. They will typically require a documented remediation plan with a cost estimate and timeline, a Phase II environmental site assessment from a DNREC-approved qualified environmental professional, a fully executed owner-controlled environmental insurance policy, and personal guarantees from principals with meaningful net worth. Pricing for this category of lender on impaired industrial in Wilmington runs 150 to 250 basis points over the applicable index rate, and amortization is typically 20 to 25 years on a 5 to 7 year fixed-rate term.

Specialty Finance and Bridge Lenders. For properties where the environmental condition precludes conventional bank financing entirely, specialty environmental bridge lenders represent an alternative capital source. These lenders specifically underwrite regulatory risk and structure loans around remediation milestones. Loan-to-value for this category is typically lower, in the 50% to 60% range, and rates are materially higher, often in the 9% to 12% range for industrial collateral with active enforcement proceedings. The practical role of this lender category in a creditor litigation context is important: their existence demonstrates that the property is not completely unfinanceable, but their pricing and terms may produce net proceeds insufficient to retire senior debt, which is itself a relevant finding for the court.

CMBS Conduits. Conventional CMBS execution is effectively unavailable for this asset type while the DNREC enforcement action is open. CMBS rating agency criteria for environmental impairment require, at minimum, a no-further-action letter or an equivalent regulatory closure document confirming that no additional remediation is required. Without that, the loan will not achieve an investment-grade rating and will not be securitized. Some single-asset single-borrower CMBS structures have closed on environmentally impaired assets, but only with full environmental insurance wraps at the trust level, and this execution is not practical at the Wilmington sub-$10 million loan size.

Debt Funds. Commercial real estate debt funds with a value-add or transitional credit mandate are a legitimate capital source for this asset type, particularly when the environmental condition is defined and the remediation is underway. Debt fund pricing on impaired Wilmington industrial collateral is typically in the 8% to 10.5% range with 1.0 to 1.5 points of origination fee, and loan terms are typically shorter, 24 to 36 months with extension options tied to remediation milestones. This category of lender underwrites the exit more aggressively than the entry, meaning their primary credit question is whether the asset can refinance into conventional debt at loan maturity after remediation is complete.

What Attorneys Handling Wilmington CRE Disputes Need to Know

The District of Delaware and Delaware Superior Court both see a disproportionate volume of complex CRE financing disputes relative to the state's physical size, largely because of Delaware's corporate domicile concentration and its active Chapter 11 docket. Attorneys in these matters frequently need to establish or challenge a specific proposition: that a given property, at a given point in time, could or could not support financing of a given amount. An appraiser can establish value; a CRE financing expert establishes financiability, and those are not the same question.

An appraiser applies a capitalization methodology or a comparable sales analysis to derive a market value conclusion. A CRE financing expert applies lender underwriting criteria, which are more restrictive and more condition-specific than appraisal methodology, to answer the question: what would an actual lender, operating in the current market, actually lend on this asset? The difference matters in practice. An appraiser might conclude that an impaired industrial property in Wilmington has a market value of $8.5 million, while a CRE financing expert might conclude that the maximum conventional loan is $5.4 million because of environmental risk adjustments to LTV caps and DSCR thresholds that no appraiser is trained to apply.

A qualified CRE financing expert is not simply a retired banker or a former loan officer. The expert must have current, active market knowledge: relationships with lenders who are actually quoting and closing loans on comparable collateral today, familiarity with current CMBS underwriting criteria, current balance sheet lender credit boxes, and current environmental insurance market terms. Stale market knowledge produces testimony that opposing counsel can effectively undermine by asking whether the expert has closed any loans on this type of collateral in the past 24 months. At CLS CRE, our approximately $1 billion in transaction volume across 1,000-plus lender relationships and all 50 states provides the active market foundation that sustains that cross-examination.

Counsel preparing to retain a CRE financing expert for a Wilmington industrial contamination matter should gather the following before the engagement commences: the current rent roll and all lease documents including NNN provisions; the most recent Phase I and Phase II environmental site assessment reports; all DNREC correspondence including the notice of violation, any consent agreement, and any remediation plan submissions; the current first-mortgage loan documents including the original note, any modifications, and the current payoff statement; any existing appraisals; and the property's operating history including historical NOI statements. The earlier this material is assembled, the more defensible the expert's methodology becomes under Daubert scrutiny.

CRE financing expert testimony is used in several distinct procedural postures in Wilmington: at confirmation hearings to establish that a proposed Plan of Reorganization is financially feasible and consistent with market financing standards; in lender liability litigation to establish whether a lender's refusal to advance funds or extend a loan was within or outside market standard practice; in guaranty enforcement actions to establish the property's financing capacity as a component of the collateral's value to the creditor; and in regulatory takings or environmental damage claims to establish how contamination has impaired the financing capacity of the property independent of its appraised value.

Expert Witness: Commercial Loan Modification Standards in Albany, NY

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Loan Modification and Workout Dispute

The Albany Market Context

Albany's commercial real estate market occupies a structural position that makes it genuinely distinct from other mid-size Northeast markets, and that distinction matters considerably when a loan modification or workout dispute lands in litigation. The capital of New York State, Albany benefits from a large, stable base of government employment, anchor healthcare institutions, and a university presence that collectively insulate the market from the cyclical volatility that whipsaws purely private-sector driven metros. That stability, however, cuts both ways in workout disputes: lenders and borrowers frequently disagree about whether a property's underperformance reflects a temporary, market-correctable problem or a structural impairment that calls for fundamentally different loan terms.

On the multifamily side, Albany has seen vacancy rates in stabilized Class B and Class C product tighten into the 4% to 6% range across submarkets including the South End, Center Square, and the Pine Hills corridor, while effective rents on two-bedroom units have moved from the mid-$1,100 range into the $1,300 to $1,600 range depending on condition and submarket. Cap rates for stabilized workforce housing have compressed to the 6.25% to 7.25% range for well-located product, reflecting continued investor demand despite rising debt costs. These figures are material to workout disputes because they establish the baseline against which a troubled property's performance must be measured: a lender arguing that a property is permanently impaired needs to account for a market that has, in fact, been improving.

Office fundamentals tell a different story. Albany's downtown and suburban office inventory, particularly older Class B product along the Washington Avenue corridor and in suburban nodes like Colonie and Latham, has experienced vacancy pressures consistent with national trends but amplified by the state government's hybrid work adoption. Direct vacancy in Albany's overall office market has been running in the 14% to 18% range for Class B product, with effective net rents under pressure. For workout disputes involving office collateral, this matters because it directly affects the argument over whether modified loan terms are commercially reasonable given forward-looking cash flow assumptions. A lender who locked in pre-pandemic stabilized occupancy projections without adjusting for realistic re-leasing timelines and rent concessions is exposed to exactly the kind of expert challenge that generates litigation.

Industrial and flex product in Albany has been the market's strongest performer. The Capital Region's logistics position between New York City, Boston, and Montreal has driven industrial vacancy below 5% in well-located submarkets, with asking rents on warehouse and distribution product moving upward. This context is relevant in workout scenarios involving industrial collateral where a borrower argues that a lender's workout terms were unreasonably punitive given the underlying asset's marketability and income stability.

The Engagement

In this matter, counsel retained Commercial Lending Solutions to serve as a commercial real estate financing expert witness in a dispute arising from a contested loan modification and workout process. Counsel represented the borrower, a commercial real estate owner whose mixed-use asset in an Albany urban submarket had experienced material cash flow disruption during a period of elevated vacancy. The lender had initiated workout discussions, and the parties had exchanged multiple rounds of modification proposals before the relationship deteriorated into litigation.

The asset involved ground-floor retail with upper-floor residential units, a property type that presents specific underwriting challenges because institutional lenders frequently disagree about whether to analyze it as a retail asset, a multifamily asset, or a blended collateral type. The retail component had lost its anchor tenant, and the borrower had been operating with a partially stabilized residential component while pursuing replacement retail tenants.

The core legal and financial questions our engagement was retained to address were as follows. First, did the lender's conduct during the modification process, specifically its income and vacancy assumptions, meet commercial real estate industry standards of care for workout analysis? Second, were the loan modification terms ultimately proposed by the lender commercially reasonable given the property's documented cash flow and the prevailing Albany market conditions at the time of the negotiations? Third, how did the lender's handling of the modification request compare to typical commercial real estate workout practices at peer institutions? Fourth, was the borrower's proposed alternative workout structure within the range of what a reasonable commercial lender would consider for this asset type, location, and loan size? These questions required both quantitative financial modeling and a qualitative assessment of lender conduct against market norms.

The Financial Analysis

The foundation of our analysis was a reconstruction of the property's stabilized and as-is net operating income, using the borrower's actual rent rolls, operating expense histories, and the lender's internal credit memoranda obtained through discovery. This dual-track approach is essential in workout disputes because the gap between what a lender assumes in its internal analysis and what the property is actually producing is frequently where the dispute lives.

For the residential component, we established actual gross potential rent based on current in-place leases and compared that against market rent as supported by comparable lease transactions within the same Albany submarket. Residential units were achieving rents consistent with the broader market range described above, and the property was operating at approximately 88% residential occupancy at the time of the modification negotiations. We applied a stabilized vacancy assumption of 7%, which is supportable given Albany market conditions, rather than the 10% to 15% vacancy load the lender had applied in its own workout underwriting. That difference alone had a meaningful effect on underwritten NOI.

For the retail component, we underwrote the dark space at a market re-leasing assumption rather than treating it as permanently impaired. Given comparable retail lease transactions in Albany urban submarkets, a replacement tenant at a market rent of approximately $14 to $18 per square foot net was supportable, with a 12 to 18 month absorption timeline and a standard tenant improvement and free rent concession package. We capitalized the lease-up costs and discounted the income stream accordingly, rather than simply zeroing out the retail income as the lender had done.

The resulting as-stabilized NOI we calculated was approximately $310,000 annually, compared to the lender's workout underwriting figure of approximately $238,000, a differential of roughly $72,000 that had compounding effects on every other metric in the analysis.

We applied a debt service coverage ratio threshold of 1.25x, which represents the lower bound of what a conventional commercial real estate lender requires for mixed-use collateral of this type and risk profile. Some community banks in the Albany market will consider 1.20x for well-located, low-leverage deals, but 1.25x is the appropriate standard for a loan of this size and complexity. At a 7.25% note rate on a 25-year amortization schedule, which reflected prevailing terms for Albany workout modifications at the time of the dispute, a DSCR of 1.25x supported maximum annual debt service of approximately $248,000, equating to a maximum supportable loan amount of approximately $2.87 million.

The outstanding loan balance subject to the modification was approximately $2.65 million, meaning that our analysis demonstrated the property, underwritten correctly against current market conditions, could support the existing debt load at a modified interest rate. The lender's own figures, using its inflated vacancy assumptions, produced a DSCR below 1.0x on the existing balance, which is precisely what drove its position that the borrower needed to make a principal paydown as a condition of modification.

On the LTV side, we used a capitalization rate of 7.0% applied to our stabilized NOI to derive an independent value estimate of approximately $4.43 million, yielding a loan-to-value ratio of approximately 60% on the existing balance. For mixed-use collateral in an Albany urban submarket, conventional lenders typically require LTV at or below 70% to 75% for a modification, with agency or insurance company capital coming in tighter at 65%. At 60% LTV, the collateral coverage was well within the range that a reasonable commercial lender would consider adequate for a workout modification without requiring additional equity contribution.

The Complicating Factors

Mixed-Use Collateral Classification: The single most technically complex aspect of this engagement was the lender's inconsistent treatment of the asset's collateral classification. During original underwriting, the lender had treated the property as multifamily-dominant for purposes of setting its DSCR requirement and stress-testing vacancy. During the workout, the lender shifted its classification to treat the property as retail-dominant, applying a higher DSCR threshold and a more conservative vacancy load. This reclassification had no basis in the property's actual income composition, where residential rent constituted approximately 68% of gross potential revenue, and it had the practical effect of making the modification terms considerably more onerous than the original loan terms would have required. In my experience, lenders handling workouts on mixed-use collateral should apply a blended underwriting standard that reflects the actual income composition of the asset, not a classification that happens to produce the most conservative outcome for the lender's credit exposure.

Dark Retail Permanent Impairment Assumption: The lender's workout underwriting treated the vacant retail space as permanently dark, assigning it zero income contribution in perpetuity. This is not how a commercially reasonable lender analyzes retail vacancy in an urban Albany submarket with documented leasing activity. The appropriate methodology is to underwrite re-leasing at market rent with a realistic absorption timeline, net of lease-up costs, and to capitalize or discount that income stream. Treating dark retail as permanent impairment without market evidence to support that conclusion overstates the property's distress and understates the borrower's equity position.

Interest Rate Environment and Modification Terms: The lender proposed a modification that included a step-up interest rate structure, beginning at 7.5% in year one and stepping to 8.5% by year three. Applied to the lender's own depressed NOI figure, this structure produced DSCR projections that fell below 1.0x by year two, which meant the modification, as proposed, was designed to fail. A commercially reasonable workout structure for this asset, given market conditions, would have involved a fixed or modestly stepped rate that the stabilized NOI could actually cover. Proposing modification terms that the lender's own underwriting showed the borrower could not service is not a commercially reasonable workout practice; it is a credit decision dressed up as a modification offer.

Market Timing and Negotiations Timeline: The modification negotiations extended over approximately 14 months. During that period, Albany market rents moved upward and vacancy tightened, which our analysis documented with quarterly data. The lender did not update its income assumptions during this period, continuing to rely on vacancy and rent figures from the initial workout submission. A commercially reasonable lender, conducting a workout over a multi-quarter period in an improving market, updates its underwriting. Failure to do so produced an analysis that grew progressively more disconnected from market reality as negotiations continued.

The Albany Lender Market

Albany's commercial real estate lending market is more institutionally diverse than many borrowers and their counsel appreciate, which is relevant both to workout disputes and to the question of what constitutes a commercially reasonable modification offer in this market.

Community and regional banks headquartered in or with significant Capital Region operations, including institutions with New York State charters as well as federally chartered institutions active in the market, represent a deep source of commercial real estate capital for assets in the $1 million to $10 million range. These lenders typically require 1.20x to 1.25x DSCR, will consider LTV up to 75% for stabilized assets, and price at spreads of 175 to 275 basis points over benchmark on 5-year fixed terms. For workout and modification situations, community banks in Albany have historically been relationship-oriented and willing to extend, modify, and restructure loans rather than accelerate, provided the borrower can demonstrate a credible path to stabilization.

Credit unions with commercial real estate programs, several of which are active in the Albany MSA, have emerged as meaningful lenders for smaller mixed-use and multifamily assets. Their credit criteria are similar to community banks but their cost of funds advantages sometimes allow them to offer more favorable modification terms. In a workout dispute context, the question of whether the lender's proposed terms were commercially reasonable must account for what comparable lenders in the Albany market were offering during the same period.

The Small Business Administration's 504 program, administered through Certified Development Companies active in the Capital Region, is relevant for owner-occupied commercial properties. Debt service terms under SBA structures are fixed for long terms and fully amortizing, which affects workout analysis differently than a balloon-maturity conventional loan.

For larger assets and portfolios, CMBS conduit lenders, life insurance companies, and debt funds are present in the Albany market but typically at loan sizes above $5 million and for institutional-quality assets. These lenders have defined, inflexible modification protocols, and their workout conduct is frequently governed by PSA documents and servicer guidelines rather than individual lender discretion, a distinction that matters significantly in litigation over modification conduct.

What Attorneys Handling Albany CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to retain a CRE financing expert: Any dispute involving a contested loan modification, forbearance agreement, or workout where the terms or process are at issue warrants early retention of a financing expert. The earlier in the case, the better: a financing expert can help counsel understand whether the financial record supports a viable theory, identify what documents to request in discovery, and flag inconsistencies in lender underwriting before depositions occur.

What a financing expert provides that an appraiser does not: Appraisers establish value. A commercial real estate financing expert establishes what a lender would actually do with an asset of a given value, income profile, and risk characteristic. The expert can opine on whether the lender's underwriting methodology met industry standards, whether the modification terms were within the range of what a reasonable commercial lender would offer, and whether the lender's conduct during negotiations was consistent with typical workout practices. These are capital markets and credit process opinions, not valuation opinions, and they require a fundamentally different expertise.

What makes a CRE financing expert qualified: Active market participation is the critical differentiator. A retired banker who left the market years ago cannot credibly testify about current lender standards of care, current DSCR thresholds, or current pricing in a specific submarket. A qualified CRE financing expert has current, active deal experience across multiple lender types and loan structures. At Commercial Lending Solutions, our approximately $1 billion in transaction volume and relationships with more than 1,000 lenders active across all 50 states provide the market depth required to give credible, current opinions on lender conduct and modification reasonableness.

Documents counsel should gather before the expert engagement: The original loan application and underwriting package; the lender's internal credit approval memoranda for both the original loan and the workout; all modification proposals exchanged by both parties; the property's rent rolls, operating statements, and lease documents for the period surrounding the workout; any appraisals or broker opinions of value obtained during the workout period; all correspondence between the parties regarding the modification; and the lender's internal servicing notes if obtainable through discovery. The gap between a lender's internal documents and its external communications to the borrower is frequently where the expert's most important opinions are formed.

How financing expert testimony is used at trial or in a plan hearing: In trial, a financing expert typically provides opinion testimony on whether the lender's conduct met the commercial real estate industry standard of care, whether the proposed modification terms were commercially reasonable, and how the lender's approach compared to what a similarly situated lender would have done. In a Chapter 11 plan confirmation hearing, financing expert testimony is frequently used to establish that a proposed plan of reorganization is feasible given the debtor's financing alternatives, or to challenge a creditor's assertion that no market exists for refinancing a particular asset.

Key Takeaways

  • NOI reconstruction is the foundation of every workout dispute analysis. The gap between a lender's workout underwriting and a correctly computed NOI is frequently the central financial issue, and quantifying that gap with documented, market-supported assumptions is the expert's primary task.
  • DSCR thresholds of 1.20x to 1.25x represent the market standard for Albany commercial real estate modification analysis. Lenders applying materially higher thresholds without documented credit policy justification are exposed to a commercially unreasonable standard-of-care challenge.
  • Dark space must be analyzed at market re-leasing assumptions, not as permanent impairment, unless the expert can document with market evidence that no reasonable absorption timeline exists for the subject space in the subject submarket.
  • Albany's mixed-use and multifamily markets have been appreciating, which means workout terms negotiated against stale, pre-improvement market data may be objectively unreasonable when measured against conditions at the time of final modification offers.
  • Collateral reclassification by a lender during the workout period, without a documented basis in the asset's actual income composition, is a significant indicator of commercially unreasonable workout conduct and one of the strongest bases for expert challenge.
  • Modification terms that a lender's own underwriting shows the borrower cannot service are not commercially reasonable workout proposals.

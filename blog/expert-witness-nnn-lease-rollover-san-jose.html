Expert Witness: NNN Lease Rollover Upside and Financing Impact in San Jose, CA

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Below-Market NNN Lease and Rollover Upside Analysis

The San Jose Market Context

San Jose is one of the most technically complex commercial real estate markets in the United States, and that complexity creates a specific category of financing and litigation risk that practitioners in other markets rarely encounter. As the economic core of Silicon Valley, San Jose's commercial property fundamentals are deeply intertwined with the technology sector's hiring cycles, sublease activity, and occupancy decisions. That interdependence creates pronounced volatility in the very metrics that lenders use to size commercial mortgage debt: in-place rent levels, market rent, vacancy, and lease term.

For single-tenant NNN assets in San Jose, the relevant market conditions as of late 2024 and into 2025 include the following. Industrial and flex-industrial vacancy across the San Jose submarket has risen to approximately 8% to 10% after a prolonged period of sub-4% vacancy during 2021 and 2022. Asking rents for industrial product in nodes like North San Jose, Alviso, and the Brokaw Road corridor have softened 10% to 15% from peak, though well-located last-mile and life-science-adjacent industrial assets continue to command premium rents. Class A office vacancy in downtown San Jose and North First Street corridors exceeds 20%, with effective rents under downward pressure from an elevated sublease supply overhang driven by technology sector contractions. Retail NNN assets, particularly those occupied by credit tenants in established corridors like Stevens Creek, Almaden, and Berryessa, have held value better, but single-tenant retail properties leased to mid-credit tenants remain subject to heightened lender scrutiny.

The relevance of these conditions to a below-market lease dispute is direct. When in-place rents are below market in a rising-rent environment, lenders historically gave partial credit to rollover upside. In a market where rents have plateaued or declined from recent highs, the same rollover upside argument becomes considerably harder to make to a credit committee. Counsel and their clients need to understand that lender underwriting in San Jose is not static; it reflects where the market is today, not where it was when the lease was signed.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing a party in a dispute involving a single-tenant NNN industrial/flex property in a core San Jose submarket. The asset was leased in its entirety to a single occupant under a long-term NNN lease at a rent that had been set several years prior, during a period of moderately lower market rents. By the time the dispute arose, market rents had moved upward significantly from the lease's inception, meaning the in-place rent was measurably below prevailing market rates. However, the lease still carried meaningful remaining term, creating a period during which the below-market income stream would be locked in before any rollover opportunity arose.

The core legal and financial questions before CLS CRE were as follows. First, how does a commercial lender actually underwrite a single-tenant NNN property when the in-place lease rent is below current market? Second, does the existence of rollover upside, meaning the prospect of re-leasing at market rates upon expiration, improve the borrower's ability to obtain financing, and if so, by how much and under what conditions? Third, what is the maximum supportable loan amount on this asset using the in-place NOI, and does that loan amount provide sufficient proceeds to achieve a commercially reasonable refinancing outcome? Fourth, does the lender's requirement to underwrite to contractual rents, rather than market rents, constrain the loan to an LTV and DSCR profile that would be considered commercially unreasonable or damaging to the borrower's equity position?

These are precisely the kinds of questions that sit at the intersection of financing practice and legal liability, and they require a practitioner with active lender relationships and current underwriting knowledge to answer with credibility.

The Financial Analysis

Our analysis began with the in-place NOI. In a NNN lease, the landlord's income is essentially the base rent, net of any non-reimbursed expenses. For this engagement, the subject property generated an in-place annual base rent of approximately $2.1 million. After adjusting for a small management fee reserve (even NNN structures warrant a modest management allocation of 1% to 2% for lender underwriting purposes) and a vacancy credit that most lenders apply even to single-tenant NNN deals (typically 3% to 5% for credit tenants, higher for non-investment-grade occupants), the underwritten NOI applied in our analysis was approximately $2.0 million.

Market rent for comparable industrial/flex space in the subject's San Jose submarket, based on current leasing comps, was running approximately 22% above the in-place contractual rent. That gap, roughly $462,000 in annualized rent, represents the rollover upside. Market NOI at rollover would therefore be approximately $2.45 million, adjusting for the same vacancy and expense factors.

DSCR Methodology. Commercial lenders underwriting single-tenant NNN assets in today's market apply a minimum DSCR of 1.20x to 1.25x as a standard credit threshold. Life companies and CMBS conduits more commonly require 1.25x; bank and credit union portfolio lenders frequently require 1.20x as a floor but may require 1.25x for assets with single-tenant concentration or below-investment-grade tenancy. For this analysis, we applied a 1.25x DSCR floor, consistent with what a CMBS lender or life company, the most likely permanent financing source for a stabilized NNN asset of this type, would require.

Rate and Amortization Assumptions. Based on the prevailing rate environment at the time of the relevant financing window, we applied a mortgage constant reflecting a 7.25% all-in fixed rate (consistent with 10-year Treasury yields in the 4.40% to 4.60% range plus a 250 to 275 basis point spread typical for single-tenant NNN industrial at 60% to 65% LTV) and a 30-year amortization schedule. The resulting mortgage constant is approximately 0.0818 (annual debt service per dollar of loan balance).

Maximum Supportable Loan Calculation, In-Place NOI. At 1.25x DSCR, the maximum annual debt service the in-place NOI of $2.0 million can support is $2.0 million divided by 1.25, equaling $1.6 million. Dividing $1.6 million by the mortgage constant of 0.0818 yields a maximum supportable loan of approximately $19.56 million.

LTV Cross-Check. Assuming a lender-appraised value for the subject property based on the in-place NOI capitalized at a market cap rate for single-tenant NNN industrial in San Jose of approximately 5.50% to 5.75%, the income-approach value was approximately $34.8 million to $36.4 million. A 65% LTV ceiling, standard for this product type in a CMBS or life company execution, yields a maximum loan of approximately $22.6 million to $23.7 million. The DSCR constraint at $19.56 million was the binding constraint, not LTV, which is the more common outcome when in-place rents are below market. The borrower was effectively leaving approximately $3 million to $4 million of LTV-implied loan proceeds on the table because the debt service coverage test, applied to contractual rather than market rents, was the ceiling.

Rollover Upside Scenario. At market NOI of $2.45 million, the DSCR-constrained loan rises to approximately $23.96 million, and the LTV test becomes the binding constraint. The gap between what the in-place lease supports and what the market-rate lease would support, approximately $4.4 million in loan proceeds, is the quantified financing impact of the below-market rent structure. This figure was central to the damages analysis in the engagement.

The Complicating Factors

Lenders Underwrite Contractual Rents, Not Market Rents. This is the single most important principle in this type of dispute, and it is frequently misunderstood by parties who are not active in the lending market. With very limited exceptions, commercial lenders underwrite to the in-place contractual rent for the remaining lease term. Rollover upside is not a credit-positive in the near term; it is a credit-neutral at best and a risk factor at worst, because it requires the lender to assume that the space will be successfully re-tenanted at market rates at the end of the lease. For a single-tenant property with lease term remaining, the lender's focus is entirely on the current tenant's credit and the contractual rent. There is no mechanism in standard CMBS or life company underwriting to gross up NOI for projected rollover rents.

Remaining Lease Term and the Re-Leasing Risk Window. Lenders analyze single-tenant NNN deals in the context of lease term relative to loan term. If the loan term is 10 years and the lease expires in year 6, the lender is effectively underwriting 4 years of rollover risk within the loan term. Most CMBS lenders will discount the as-leveraged cash flow analysis for this exposure, and some will require a cash management or reserve structure triggered by the approach of lease expiration. Life companies are generally more conservative, preferring leases that extend at least co-terminus with the loan term or beyond. A below-market lease with significant remaining term but a rollover event inside the loan term creates a structural underwriting problem that no amount of rollover upside argument resolves at the lender level.

Tenant Credit and the NNN Structure's Dependency on It. NNN financing is fundamentally a credit play. The lender is not really underwriting real estate; the lender is underwriting the tenant's obligation to pay rent. When the tenant is investment-grade, the pricing, LTV, and DSCR requirements all move favorably. When the tenant is non-investment-grade, the lender is underwriting the real estate as a fallback, and the below-market lease complicates even the fallback analysis, because in-place rent is below what re-tenanting would achieve, which means the lender's worst-case scenario (vacancy after tenant departure) actually improves the income picture. This inversion creates a perverse underwriting dynamic that lenders address by pricing up and constraining LTV further.

Interest Rate Sensitivity and the DSCR Constraint. In a higher-rate environment, the DSCR constraint tightens for any given NOI. A property that would have supported a $24 million loan at a 5.25% rate environment may only support a $19.5 million loan at a 7.25% rate, with the same NOI. This rate sensitivity amplifies the impact of below-market rents, because the combination of contractual rents below market and a higher mortgage constant creates a compounding suppression of loan proceeds. Counsel should understand that damages analyses in rate-sensitive environments must account for the prevailing rate at the relevant financing date, not the rate at signing or the rate today.

The San Jose Lender Market

San Jose benefits from one of the deepest and most diverse institutional lending markets in the Western United States. The presence of major technology-sector corporate real estate demand has historically attracted a broad range of capital sources. For a single-tenant NNN industrial or flex property in a core San Jose submarket, the following lender categories are the primary execution paths.

Life Insurance Companies. Life companies including major institutional allocators active in the Bay Area are the preferred execution for stabilized NNN assets with investment-grade tenancy and long lease terms. They offer the best pricing (typically 20 to 50 basis points inside CMBS), 10-year fixed terms, and 25 to 30 year amortization or interest-only structures for well-qualified assets. Their constraints: investment-grade tenant preference, minimum 10-year lease term remaining at origination, maximum 60% to 65% LTV, and 1.25x DSCR. Below-market leases are not per se disqualifying for life companies, but the credit committee will scrutinize rollover risk and may require lease-term-aligned sizing.

CMBS Conduit Lenders. CMBS lenders are active in San Jose across asset types and will consider non-investment-grade tenants that life companies avoid. The trade-off is pricing (spreads typically 50 to 100 basis points wider than life companies), tighter prepayment structures (defeasance or yield maintenance), and more rigid covenant packages. CMBS underwriting for single-tenant NNN with below-market rents will apply contractual rent only and may require a special servicer trigger tied to lease rollover risk. CMBS is the most common execution for single-tenant NNN assets in the $15 million to $40 million range in San Jose.

Regional and Community Banks. Several regional banks and community banks with strong Bay Area commercial real estate platforms, including institutions headquartered in Northern California, maintain active single-tenant and NNN lending programs. Their credit boxes tend to be more flexible on tenant credit quality but stricter on LTV (frequently capped at 60%) and DSCR (often 1.25x to 1.30x). Their advantage in disputed-financing scenarios is flexibility: they can structure around certain complications that CMBS cannot. Their limitation is balance-sheet capacity; a $25 million single-tenant NNN loan may require syndication or stretch the concentration limits of a community bank.

Debt Funds and Bridge Lenders. For below-market lease scenarios where the borrower is attempting to bridge to rollover and re-leasing at market rates, debt funds offer floating-rate bridge structures at higher costs (SOFR plus 350 to 500 basis points) and shorter terms (2 to 3 years with extension options). These are appropriate for a value-add NNN strategy but are not appropriate as a refinancing benchmark for a stabilized asset; counsel should be cautious about opposing parties characterizing bridge debt terms as the financing standard for a property that could qualify for permanent financing absent the lease structure complications.

What Attorneys Handling San Jose CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to Retain a CRE Financing Expert. Retain a CRE financing expert, not just an appraiser, in any dispute where the ability to finance or refinance a property is at issue. Appraisers determine value; financing experts determine what the debt market will actually do with that value. In a below-market lease dispute, the question is not just what the property is worth but what loan a lender will make against that value given the lease structure. These are distinct questions with distinct methodologies, and conflating them is one of the most common errors in CRE litigation.

What a CRE Financing Expert Answers That an Appraiser Cannot. Appraisers do not opine on DSCR calculations, lender credit box requirements, interest rate assumptions for underwriting, or whether a specific loan structure is achievable in the current capital markets. A CRE financing expert addresses all of these, and can further opine on the universe of available lenders for a specific asset, the realistic proceeds range across execution alternatives, and the impact of specific lease terms on financing feasibility at defined LTV and DSCR thresholds. These are exactly the issues at the heart of a below-market lease financing dispute.

Qualifications: Active Practice vs. Retired Banking Experience. An expert who has not been an active commercial mortgage broker or lender in the past three to five years is not positioned to reliably opine on current lender underwriting standards, current spreads, or current credit box requirements. The commercial lending market changes rapidly, and what was true in 2019 is materially different from what is true in 2024 and 2025. Counsel should prioritize experts with demonstrated current transaction volume, active lender relationships across multiple capital categories, and recent experience in the relevant market and asset class.

Information Counsel Should Gather Before the Engagement. Before retaining a CRE financing expert, counsel should gather: the full lease agreement including all amendments, rent schedules, and renewal option terms; the rent roll as of the relevant date; any existing loan documents including appraisals, lender correspondence, and term sheets; any broker opinions of value or financing feasibility letters; market rent surveys or comparable lease data; and any communications between the parties regarding financing expectations or commitments. The more complete the documentary record at engagement inception, the more precise and defensible the expert's conclusions will be.

How CRE Financing Expert Testimony Is Used. In trial, CRE financing expert testimony establishes the nexus between a lease structure or transaction dispute and a quantifiable financing impact. It converts an abstract argument about lease terms into a specific dollar figure: how many loan proceeds were lost, what LTV headroom was forfeited, or what refinancing alternatives were foreclosed by a specific lease or contractual condition.

Expert Witness: CRE Financing Feasibility in Chapter 11 Proceedings in Johnson City, TN

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Bankruptcy Exit Financing

The Johnson City Market Context

Johnson City, Tennessee sits at the geographic center of the Tri-Cities metropolitan statistical area, which encompasses Kingsport and Bristol alongside Johnson City and collectively represents approximately 500,000 residents across northeast Tennessee and southwest Virginia. For purposes of commercial real estate financing analysis, what matters is not the population figure itself but the economic composition underlying it: a regional economy anchored by healthcare, higher education, advanced manufacturing, and a growing logistics sector tied to Interstate 26. East Tennessee State University and its affiliated academic medical center function as stabilizing economic engines in a way that insulates the Johnson City market from some of the cyclicality that affects purely industrial or purely retail-driven secondary markets. That insulation has practical consequences when a lender underwrites a commercial property here: tenant diversification risk is lower than it would be in a single-industry market, and demand for medical office, multifamily, and neighborhood retail has demonstrated resilience through rate cycles.

The Johnson City multifamily market has absorbed consistent rent growth through 2022 and 2023, with effective rents in stabilized Class B garden-style product running in the $950 to $1,200 per unit per month range depending on vintage and location relative to ETSU and the medical corridor. Cap rates on stabilized multifamily transactions here have generally cleared in the 5.75% to 6.75% range, which is meaningfully higher than Nashville or Knoxville but reflects a liquidity discount appropriate to a market of this size. Industrial vacancy in the Johnson City submarket has remained below 6% for most of the post-pandemic period, driven in part by healthcare supply chain tenants and light manufacturing activity. Retail fundamentals are bifurcated: grocery-anchored and necessity-based retail has performed well, while older big-box-dependent strip centers have faced persistent occupancy pressure. Office fundamentals reflect the national trend but are complicated locally by a medical office segment that has remained relatively stable. All of these characteristics matter when a financing expert is asked to assess whether a specific asset type in this market can support first-mortgage debt in a bankruptcy context.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by counsel representing a plan proponent in a Chapter 11 proceeding involving a commercial income-producing property located in the Johnson City submarket. The asset was a multifamily apartment complex, a stabilized garden-style property that had been owned by the debtor for several years prior to the filing. The debtor's financial distress arose not from occupancy failure but from a capital structure problem: the original acquisition was financed with short-term debt that matured into a rate environment the property's cash flow could not service at the original principal balance without refinancing into unfavorable terms. That scenario, a fundamentally sound asset trapped in a broken capital structure, is exactly the type of situation where financing feasibility analysis becomes dispositive in a Plan confirmation proceeding.

Counsel retained CLS CRE to address two primary questions that the court would need to resolve in evaluating Plan feasibility under 11 U.S.C. Section 1129(a)(11): first, whether the property, based on its current and reasonably projected income, could support first-mortgage financing in an amount sufficient to satisfy the senior secured creditor and fund any required reserve accounts; and second, whether an active and identifiable category of lenders actually originates loans on commercial real estate assets that are exiting Chapter 11 bankruptcy, and if so, on what terms and conditions. The second question is often underappreciated by counsel: it is not enough to show that the math works in the abstract. A plan proponent must be able to demonstrate that a lender willing to make that loan actually exists in the current market and will engage with a bankruptcy estate exit scenario.

Our engagement required us to produce a written expert report under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 26, including a full description of our methodology, the data sources we relied upon, and the conclusions we reached. We were prepared to testify at the confirmation hearing and to be cross-examined on each element of the analysis.

The Financial Analysis

The analytical starting point in any financing feasibility opinion is stabilized net operating income. For this property, that required us to construct a trailing twelve-month income and expense summary from the debtor's operating records and then stress-test it against Johnson City market comparables. The property was running at approximately 94% physical occupancy, which is broadly consistent with the broader Tri-Cities market for stabilized Class B garden-style product. Gross potential rent was calculated at the in-place lease rates, and we applied a vacancy and credit loss factor of 6% based on current market conditions. Effective gross income after ancillary revenue was approximately $1,150,000 annually. Operating expenses, including property management at 8% of EGI, real estate taxes at the assessed rate applicable in Washington County, insurance, maintenance, and replacement reserves at $300 per unit per year, produced a total expense load of approximately $460,000. Stabilized NOI for financing analysis purposes was therefore approximately $690,000.

Debt service coverage ratio is the primary constraint a lender applies to size a commercial real estate loan. The market standard for conventional agency-eligible multifamily financing, including Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac programs, is a minimum DSCR of 1.25x, meaning the property's NOI must be at least 25% greater than annual debt service. For bank and CMBS lenders on non-agency multifamily, a 1.20x DSCR floor is common, though certain community and regional banks may underwrite to 1.25x as a credit policy requirement. We applied the 1.25x threshold as our conservative underwriting floor given the bankruptcy exit context, which typically causes lenders to tighten their credit parameters relative to clean-title transactions.

On the rate and amortization side, we analyzed financing terms available in the market at the time of the engagement. A 30-year amortization schedule is standard for agency multifamily executions. We assumed a note rate of 6.75%, which reflected prevailing 10-year Treasury yields plus appropriate spread for this asset quality and market. At 6.75% on a 30-year amortization schedule, the monthly mortgage constant is approximately 0.6485%, producing an annual debt service constant of 7.782%. Dividing the maximum supportable annual debt service (NOI divided by DSCR minimum) by the annual mortgage constant produces the maximum loan amount supportable by the income stream.

Maximum supportable annual debt service: $690,000 divided by 1.25 equals $552,000. Maximum supportable loan: $552,000 divided by 0.07782 equals approximately $7,093,000. We then applied a loan-to-value constraint. For conventional multifamily financing, maximum LTV is typically 80% on agency programs and 70% to 75% for bank and CMBS executions on assets exiting bankruptcy. The property's as-stabilized appraised value, based on a concurrent appraisal using a capitalization rate of 6.25% applied to our stabilized NOI figure, was approximately $11,040,000. At 75% LTV, the maximum loan supported by collateral value alone would be approximately $8,280,000. The income-based constraint of $7,093,000 was the binding constraint, which is the proper result in a well-underwritten deal: the income stream, not leverage, limits the loan amount. This analysis produced a conclusion that the property could support approximately $7.0 million in first-mortgage financing, which was sufficient to satisfy the senior secured creditor's allowed claim and fund required bankruptcy exit reserves.

The Complicating Factors

Bankruptcy Estate Title and Lender Credit Policy. The single most significant complicating factor in exit financing of any type is that many conventional lenders, particularly agency lenders and community banks with conservative credit policies, will not originate a loan to a borrower who is currently in, or has recently exited, a Chapter 11 proceeding. Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac delegated underwriting guidelines impose seasoning requirements on bankruptcies: typically a borrower must demonstrate a multi-year period of clean credit history post-discharge before becoming eligible. This does not mean financing is impossible; it means the lender universe narrows, and counsel and the plan proponent must understand that narrowing in advance. The practical solution is a two-step capital structure: bridge financing from a non-agency lender willing to close into or immediately out of the bankruptcy estate, followed by a permanent agency refinance once the seasoning clock has run.

Identifying the Borrowing Entity Post-Confirmation. Lenders do not make loans to bankruptcy estates. They make loans to legal entities that own real property. A critical element of plan feasibility analysis is confirming that the confirmed plan creates or identifies a creditworthy post-confirmation entity capable of serving as borrower. If the reorganized debtor is the same legal entity that filed for bankruptcy, lenders will treat it as a recently-bankrupted borrower and apply seasoning constraints accordingly. If the plan contemplates a sale to a third-party purchaser using exit financing, the purchaser's creditworthiness, liquidity, and real estate ownership experience become part of the underwriting analysis. Attorneys drafting a reorganization plan need to address this entity structure question explicitly, and a financing expert can advise on which structures are more likely to satisfy a lender's credit requirements.

Title and Bankruptcy Court Approval Timing. Commercial lenders require clear, insurable title at closing. In a bankruptcy exit context, the Plan confirmation order and related findings under 11 U.S.C. Section 1141 are the mechanism by which title is cleared of pre-petition liens. Lenders and their counsel need time to review the confirmation order, the Plan itself, and the vesting language. Most lenders also require that any appeal period has run or that appropriate protections against appeal-based delay are in place. A realistic timeline from confirmation to loan closing in a bankruptcy exit scenario is 60 to 90 days at minimum, and counsel should factor that into Plan timelines and any cash collateral or post-confirmation operating agreements.

Reserve and Escrow Requirements. Exit financing lenders typically impose reserve requirements that are more robust than those applied to clean-title transactions. Expect lenders to require funded debt service reserves of three to six months, replacement reserves, and possibly a deferred maintenance reserve if the property has any physical condition issues documented in a Property Condition Assessment. These funded reserves reduce the net proceeds available at closing, which must be factored into the feasibility analysis. If the gross loan amount is $7,000,000 and the lender requires $500,000 in funded reserves, the net available to pay creditors and costs is $6,500,000. That distinction is material in a plan confirmation feasibility analysis and is sometimes overlooked when the analysis focuses only on the gross loan amount.

The Johnson City Lender Market

Despite being a secondary market, Johnson City and the broader Tri-Cities MSA have a meaningful depth of commercial real estate lender activity that is often underappreciated by attorneys and plan proponents based outside the region. Understanding which lender categories are active and what each requires is essential to a financing feasibility opinion that will withstand cross-examination.

Agency Lenders (Fannie Mae/Freddie Mac): Both agencies are active in the Tri-Cities market through delegated underwriting lenders, primarily for stabilized multifamily of 5+ units. The credit box requires strong sponsorship, minimum DSCR of 1.25x, maximum LTV of 80% (typically lower in practice), and post-bankruptcy seasoning. Rates are tied to 10-year Treasury benchmarks with spreads in the 150 to 200 basis point range for this market and quality tier. Not available for direct bankruptcy estate closings without restructuring.

Regional and Community Banks: This is the most active lender category for Johnson City commercial real estate in the sub-$15 million loan size range. Multiple regional banks based in Tennessee, Virginia, and the Carolinas have active CRE lending programs in the Tri-Cities. Community banks with local deposit relationships and knowledge of the Washington County market are often the most flexible on structure, including bridge scenarios and post-confirmation situations. They typically underwrite to 1.20x to 1.25x DSCR, 70% to 75% LTV, and price at Prime or SOFR plus 200 to 300 basis points depending on credit quality. They can close on shorter timelines than agency lenders and are more willing to engage with complex situations if the asset quality is strong.

Debt Funds and Bridge Lenders: For assets exiting bankruptcy where conventional lender access is constrained by seasoning requirements, debt funds and bridge lenders are often the appropriate first-step capital source. These lenders price at higher rates, typically SOFR plus 350 to 600 basis points with origination fees of 1% to 2%, but they underwrite asset quality and cash flow rather than borrower credit history in the traditional sense. They accept shorter-term loans (12 to 36 months) structured around a refinance exit. CLS CRE maintains active relationships with debt funds that specifically understand bankruptcy exit executions and can close with appropriate speed.

Life Insurance Companies: Generally not active in the Tri-Cities for this loan size or complexity level. Life company allocations to secondary and tertiary markets tend to require larger loan sizes, lower leverage, and pristine sponsorship. For most Johnson City bankruptcy exit scenarios, life company financing is not a relevant option and should not be cited as an available execution in a feasibility analysis.

What Attorneys Handling Johnson City CRE Disputes Need to Know

A commercial real estate financing expert is not an appraiser, and the distinction matters enormously in a Plan confirmation proceeding. An appraiser values the property. A financing expert opines on whether the property can be financed, by whom, on what terms, and whether those terms are consistent with what is available in the actual market at the time of the proceeding. A court evaluating Plan feasibility under the Bankruptcy Code needs both, and confusing the two roles produces gaps in the evidentiary record that opposing counsel will exploit.

Attorneys should retain a CRE financing expert rather than, or in addition to, an appraiser when the following questions are at issue: whether projected exit financing is realistically available; whether proposed loan terms in the Plan are consistent with actual market conditions; whether a creditor's claim that refinancing is impossible is accurate; whether proposed interest rates, LTV ratios, or DSCR assumptions in a feasibility analysis are supported by the current lending environment; or whether the proposed lender category actually originates this type of loan in this market.

What distinguishes a qualified CRE financing expert from a retired banker is active market engagement. A retired banker can describe how a bank would have underwritten a loan five years ago. An active commercial mortgage broker who closes transactions in this asset class and market today can testify to what lenders are actually requiring right now, what they are pricing, and whether they will engage with the specific complications presented by the case. CLS CRE has closed transactions in 50 states, maintains relationships with more than 1,000 lenders across all categories, and has produced expert reports in contested Plan proceedings. That combination of current market knowledge and expert witness experience is what makes testimony credible under cross-examination.

Before retaining a financing expert, counsel should gather the following: the trailing twelve-month operating statement for the property; the current rent roll with lease expiration dates; the most recent property condition assessment and environmental report if available; the current property tax bill; any existing appraisal, even if contested; the full capital structure of the estate including all secured and unsecured claims; and the proposed Plan treatment of secured debt. The more complete the operating history, the stronger the financing feasibility opinion will be, because lenders underwrite to actual trailing income, not pro forma projections.

In a Plan confirmation hearing, financing expert testimony is typically used to establish that the Plan meets the feasibility standard under 11 U.S.C. Section 1129(a)(11), which requires that confirmation is not likely to be followed by liquidation or further reorganization. Demonstrating that viable exit financing exists, on terms that the property's income can service, is central to meeting that standard. Expert testimony on this point should be specific enough to withstand cross-examination: not "financing is generally available for multifamily properties" but "properties of this type, in this market, at this LTV and DSCR, can be financed by the following categories of lenders at rates in the following range, and we have identified willing lenders in each category."

Key Takeaways

  • The Johnson City market supports first-mortgage financing feasibility analysis for stabilized income-producing assets. The market has sufficient lender depth, transaction volume, and economic fundamentals to produce a credible financing opinion for Plan confirmation purposes, and the asset class matters less than the quality of the income stream and the structure of the exit.
  • Financing feasibility analysis begins with stabilized NOI, not appraised value. The income-based constraint is almost always the binding limit in a well-underwritten deal. A property can be worth more than enough to secure a loan that the income stream cannot service, and a plan premised on collateral value alone without income analysis will not survive competent cross-examination.

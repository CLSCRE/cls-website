Case Study: $3.24M Build-to-Suit Mezzanine Loan Exiting Into a Sale-Leaseback in Fort Myers

By Trevor Damyan July 27, 2026
Quick answer: Commercial Lending Solutions arranged a $3,237,400 mezzanine construction facility through a national bank for a build-to-suit entertainment venue in Fort Myers, Florida. The one-year facility financed the development phase behind a first-lien conventional loan from the same lender, structured so the developer could exit through a sale-leaseback to a national net-lease real estate investment trust once construction was complete.

The Deal That Walked in the Door

We are Commercial Lending Solutions, a commercial mortgage brokerage based in Los Angeles, and this financing brought us a Florida-based development group building a ground-up entertainment venue in Fort Myers, Florida (FL) under a long-term lease with a national entertainment operator. The developer needed a bank lender comfortable providing construction capital sized to bridge the build-out, structured from the outset with a planned sale-leaseback exit to a national net-lease REIT once the property was completed and stabilized. Commercial Lending Solutions specializes in construction financing and complex mezzanine capital structures for developers across Florida and nationwide.

Build-to-suit entertainment venues in markets like Fort Myers carry a specific financing logic: the construction lender is underwriting to the tenant's credit and lease terms as much as the developer's own balance sheet, and the exit is typically a sale to an institutional net-lease buyer rather than a long-term hold. Structuring the capital stack correctly from day one, with an eye toward that eventual sale, shaped how this financing came together.

What Made It Hard

  • Layering mezzanine debt behind a first-lien construction loan. The $3,237,400 mezzanine facility had to be properly subordinated to the senior conventional loan from the same lender, with clean subordination language covering both the debt itself and any security interests behind it.
  • Coordinating tenant protections ahead of a future sale. With the eventual buyer expecting a clean, bankable lease, the developer needed an executed Subordination, Non-Disturbance, and Attornment Agreement with the tenant in place well before the sale-leaseback closing, protecting the tenant's occupancy rights through the transition from construction lender to permanent REIT owner.
  • Sizing a one-year facility to a real construction and sale timeline. A mezzanine loan this short only works if the construction schedule and the sale-leaseback closing are realistically aligned, leaving little room for delays on either the build-out or the REIT's own closing process.

How We Ran the Process

Trevor Damyan, a California-licensed real estate broker (DRE #02244836), arranged the mezzanine construction facility through a national bank, with the loan resolution dated March 14, 2022:

  • $3,237,400 in mezzanine loan proceeds, structured as subordinated debt behind a first-lien conventional construction loan from the same lender
  • A one-year term, running from March 14, 2022 to March 14, 2023, sized to the anticipated construction and stabilization period ahead of the planned sale-leaseback exit
  • UCC-1 financing statement collateral covering fixtures and improvements on the property, filed against both the operating borrower entity and its affiliated guarantor entity
  • Formal subordination authority built into the loan resolution, allowing the lender to subordinate the mezzanine debt and any related security interests on terms to be agreed with the senior lender
  • A negotiated exit structure: a purchase and sale agreement between the development entity, as seller, and a national net-lease real estate investment trust, as purchaser, for the completed, tenant-occupied property
  • An executed Subordination, Non-Disturbance, and Attornment Agreement with the entertainment tenant, protecting the tenant's lease rights through the transition from construction financing to the REIT's permanent ownership

The facility matured on schedule in March 2023, with the development completed and the sale-leaseback to the net-lease REIT structured to retire the construction capital stack.

Why We Won This One

Structuring the mezzanine debt correctly behind the first-lien construction loan, with clean subordination terms agreed upfront rather than negotiated under time pressure later, is what let this capital stack function as intended through the full construction period. Getting both loans properly documented and collateralized from the same lender simplified what could have been a more contentious intercreditor negotiation with two separate lenders.

Getting the tenant's SNDA executed well ahead of the sale-leaseback closing also mattered. A net-lease REIT buyer underwrites almost entirely to the strength and enforceability of the in-place lease, and having that tenant protection agreement finalized before the sale process began kept the eventual buyer's due diligence from becoming a bottleneck at the finish line.

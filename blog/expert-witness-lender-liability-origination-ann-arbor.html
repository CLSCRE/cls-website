Expert Witness: Commercial Loan Origination Standards in Ann Arbor, MI

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Lender Liability and Loan Origination Standards

The Ann Arbor Market Context

Ann Arbor occupies a unique position in Michigan commercial real estate. Unlike Detroit's urban core or the suburban office corridors of Troy and Southfield, Ann Arbor operates on its own economic gravity, driven by the University of Michigan's 47,000-student enrollment, a mature life sciences and technology sector anchored by major research institutions, and a professional services economy that has insulated the submarket from the cyclical swings that have hammered other Michigan metros. For attorneys and investors navigating commercial real estate disputes here, that distinctiveness matters enormously, because what constitutes a market-standard underwriting decision in Ann Arbor is not necessarily what a regional bank underwriter in Grand Rapids or a national lender accustomed to Chicago or Columbus would apply.

The Ann Arbor multifamily market has sustained strong fundamentals through the rate cycle. Average market rents in Ann Arbor have tracked materially above the statewide Michigan average, with Class A multifamily assets near the university core and the South State Street corridor consistently achieving occupancies above 95 percent during the academic calendar. Cap rates on stabilized multifamily product have compressed into the 5.0 to 5.75 percent range for well-located assets, though secondary locations farther from campus have traded in the 6.0 to 6.75 percent range. Office is a more complicated story: Ann Arbor's office vacancy has climbed post-pandemic, with some submarkets showing vacancy rates in the 15 to 20 percent range as tech tenants rationalized hybrid work footprints, even as life sciences and research-adjacent flex space has remained comparatively tight. Industrial and flex product in the Ann Arbor and Ypsilanti corridor has benefited from proximity to automotive technology suppliers and university spin-out firms, with vacancy below 5 percent in functional modern product and asking rents trending upward. These market-specific conditions are not background noise in a lender liability engagement; they are the foundation upon which any standard-of-care analysis must be built.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing the defendant in a lender liability matter arising from a commercial loan origination on a mixed-use property in Ann Arbor. The asset was a mid-size building combining ground-floor retail and professional office space with upper-floor residential units, located in a walkable urban submarket with direct proximity to the university demand base. The borrower, a local operating entity, alleged that the originating mortgage broker and the funding lender had failed to disclose material loan terms accurately, had misrepresented the achievable loan amount during the marketing and commitment phase, and had placed the borrower into a loan structure that was inconsistent with market standards, ultimately resulting in a loan that the borrower claimed was neither properly sized to the property's income nor consistent with the terms represented during the engagement period.

Our engagement covered three distinct analytical tracks. First, counsel needed an independent assessment of whether the loan as originated was supportable by the property's income stream at the underwritten figures, and whether the debt service coverage ratio and loan-to-value constraints applied were consistent with what a reasonably competent lender or mortgage broker would have used for this asset type in this market. Second, counsel needed testimony on whether the disclosed loan terms, including the interest rate, index, spread, prepayment structure, and recourse provisions, fell within the range of what the Ann Arbor lender market was actually offering for comparable transactions at the time of origination. Third, we were asked to opine on professional conduct standards: what a reasonably competent commercial mortgage broker operating in this market should have done at each stage of the engagement, from initial feasibility analysis through commitment, closing, and post-close communication.

The Financial Analysis

The foundation of any lender liability analysis involving loan sizing is the income underwriting. For a mixed-use asset like this one, that means disaggregating the income stream into its components, residential units, commercial office, and retail, and applying asset-class-specific underwriting conventions to each. It is not appropriate to simply blend gross revenues and apply a single vacancy and expense assumption. Lenders, and the brokers who prepare packages for them, are expected to underwrite each component on its own merits.

For the residential component, market rents were supported by comparables within a half-mile radius. A stabilized vacancy of 5 percent was applied, consistent with the sub-5 percent physical vacancy observed in comparable Ann Arbor urban residential product during the relevant period. For the commercial component, the office and retail space required a more conservative approach: a 10 to 12 percent economic vacancy assumption was appropriate given both the submarket conditions and the shorter weighted average lease term on the in-place tenancy. Operating expenses for a mixed-use asset of this configuration in Ann Arbor were validated against third-party property management cost data for comparable buildings, running approximately 35 to 38 percent of effective gross income when real estate taxes, insurance, utilities, management, and reserves were properly captured.

The resulting stabilized net operating income available for debt service was approximately $310,000 annually. Applying the institutional standard of a 1.25x minimum debt service coverage ratio, which is the threshold most bank and non-bank portfolio lenders require for mixed-use assets with commercial exposure, the maximum supportable annual debt service was $248,000. At a then-prevailing rate environment of 7.25 percent on a 30-year amortization schedule, that debt service ceiling supported a maximum loan of approximately $3.05 million. Applying a 65 percent loan-to-value constraint, which is market standard for mixed-use commercial product with meaningful office and retail exposure, the appraised value would need to support a loan of at least that magnitude for the LTV test not to be the binding constraint. If the appraised value came in at, for example, $4.4 million, the 65 percent LTV ceiling would produce a maximum loan of $2.86 million, making LTV rather than DSCR the binding constraint and reducing the achievable loan below what the income alone would have supported.

This is where the professional conduct question becomes legally material. A reasonably competent commercial mortgage broker, at the preliminary sizing stage, should have communicated both constraints to the borrower, run a sensitivity analysis on both the DSCR and LTV tests, and presented a range of achievable loan proceeds tied to appraised value outcomes. Representing a loan amount as achievable based solely on income, without flagging the LTV constraint, is a material omission. That is precisely the kind of omission that becomes the factual predicate for a negligent misrepresentation claim.

The Complicating Factors

Mixed-Use Collateral Classification: Lenders are not uniform in how they classify mixed-use assets for underwriting and pricing purposes. A property that is 60 percent residential by revenue but 40 percent commercial by square footage may be treated as a multifamily loan by some institutions and as a commercial real estate loan by others. That classification difference is not academic. It affects the regulatory capital treatment for a bank lender, the available leverage, the pricing spread, and the applicable reserve requirements. In this engagement, the asset straddled that classification boundary, and a competent broker was obligated to understand which lenders would treat it as multifamily, unlocking potentially more favorable leverage under agency or HUD guidelines, and which would treat it as commercial, imposing tighter LTV and DSCR constraints. Failure to navigate that classification issue, or to disclose it to the borrower, is a recognized deficiency in the standard of care.

University-Proximate Demand and Seasonal Cash Flow: Ann Arbor's residential market is heavily influenced by the academic calendar. Leases in university-adjacent buildings frequently turn over in May and August, creating short windows of potential vacancy exposure that a national lender unfamiliar with the market may underwrite more conservatively than local conditions warrant. Conversely, a broker who represented the asset's stabilized occupancy as perpetually near-100 percent without disclosing the seasonal lease renewal concentration risk would have overstated the income reliability in the underwriting narrative. Both failure modes appear in Ann Arbor lender liability cases, and the expert witness must be able to articulate what the market-standard treatment of academic-calendar-driven tenancy actually looks like in a commercial loan underwriting package.

Recourse and Guaranty Structure: For commercial assets in this size range in Ann Arbor, the question of full recourse versus non-recourse with carve-outs is a material economic term. Many regional and community banks in Michigan require full recourse for loans below $5 million, particularly on mixed-use collateral or for sponsorships without a deep track record in commercial investment. Non-recourse execution is available but requires life insurance company or CMBS execution, or a debt fund platform, each of which brings its own pricing, leverage, and structural requirements. A broker who represented a non-recourse execution as the expected outcome for a borrower who would only qualify for full recourse at the available lender pool has materially misrepresented the transaction's terms. That representation, when documented in email communications or term sheets, can be determinative in a negligence or fraudulent inducement analysis.

Prepayment Penalty Disclosure: Bank portfolio lenders in this market commonly impose step-down prepayment penalties or yield maintenance provisions on fixed-rate commercial loans. The difference between a 3-2-1 step-down and a yield maintenance provision at a 7 percent coupon rate can represent a six-figure difference in prepayment cost to the borrower within the first five years. Proper disclosure of the prepayment structure, including worked examples for the borrower's intended hold period, is part of the standard of care for a commercial mortgage broker. In disputes where the borrower refinanced or sold the property earlier than anticipated, the prepayment penalty becomes a damages element, and the question of what was disclosed and when becomes central to the liability analysis.

The Ann Arbor Lender Market

Ann Arbor benefits from meaningful lender depth for well-structured commercial transactions, though the market is not as liquid as Chicago or Detroit's inner suburbs for deals with complexity or credit haircuts. Understanding which lenders are active and what their credit boxes look like is essential both to the financing analysis and to the standard-of-care opinion.

Regional and Community Banks: Several Michigan-based regional banks and community banks maintain active commercial real estate portfolios in the Ann Arbor and Washtenaw County market. These institutions typically lend up to 70 to 75 percent LTV on stabilized multifamily, 60 to 65 percent on mixed-use with commercial income, and require personal guaranties from principals on loans below approximately $5 to $7 million. Rates are typically indexed to the prime rate or to Treasuries with spreads of 175 to 275 basis points depending on term length, credit quality, and deposit relationship. For borrowers with existing depository relationships, these lenders offer competitive execution and meaningful flexibility on structure. They are often the right lender for Ann Arbor middle-market borrowers, but they are not equipped for higher-leverage requests or complex assets with value-add business plans.

Life Insurance Companies and Correspondent Lenders: For stabilized commercial assets above roughly $5 million with clean tenancy and strong sponsorship, life insurance company correspondents and conduit lenders offer non-recourse fixed-rate execution. These lenders are active in Ann Arbor for institutional-quality multifamily, net-leased retail, and creditworthy office. They require DSCR minimums at or above 1.25x, debt yield minimums of 8.0 to 9.0 percent, and generally limit proceeds on mixed-use assets to 60 to 65 percent LTV. Their pricing advantage over bank debt can be meaningful on larger transactions, but their underwriting timelines and due diligence requirements are substantial.

Bridge and Debt Fund Lenders: For value-add acquisitions, transitional assets, or situations where the in-place NOI does not yet support conventional bank sizing, short-term bridge debt from private debt funds and mortgage REITs is available in the Ann Arbor market. These lenders price at higher spreads, typically SOFR plus 300 to 500 basis points, but will lend on a stabilized-NOI-forward basis, meaning they underwrite to the projected income rather than the current income. That flexibility comes at a cost, and a competent broker must model the all-in cost of bridge financing alongside the conventional permanent loan sizing to demonstrate whether the execution actually pencils for the borrower's business plan.

That breadth is directly relevant to expert testimony: an expert who can only speak to one lender category has an incomplete picture of what the market would have offered at the time of the disputed transaction.

What Attorneys Handling Ann Arbor CRE Disputes Need to Know

The most important threshold question for counsel considering a CRE financing expert is this: an appraiser tells you what the property was worth. A CRE financing expert tells you whether the loan was appropriate for that value, whether the terms were consistent with market practice, and whether the broker or lender acted competently. Those are entirely different questions, and a licensed appraiser is not qualified to answer the financing standards questions. The expert you need for a lender liability or professional negligence matter is an active commercial mortgage professional with current market knowledge, not a retired banker who last closed a deal a decade ago and whose sense of "market standard" is frozen in a prior rate environment.

Counsel should retain a CRE financing expert early, ideally before discovery is fully closed. The expert can help identify which documents to request, including loan origination files, underwriting worksheets, rate lock confirmations, broker fee agreements, and internal lender credit memoranda. These documents often reveal the gap between what was represented to the borrower during marketing and what the lender's internal credit team actually underwrote.

When evaluating expert qualifications, counsel should look for active deal experience in the asset type and geographic market at issue, demonstrated ability to work across multiple lender categories, and prior Rule 26 report experience. A practitioner who closes transactions today understands current market conditions and can speak credibly about what was achievable in the market at the relevant time. Theoretical knowledge without transactional depth will not withstand cross-examination on market pricing, lender appetite, or underwriting conventions.

At trial or in an arbitration, CRE financing expert testimony is most powerful when it moves from the abstract to the quantitative. Jurors and arbitrators understand numbers. Showing that the maximum supportable loan at market-standard DSCR and LTV was $2.86 million, while the broker represented that $3.5 million was achievable, makes the alleged misrepresentation tangible. Showing that the prepayment penalty disclosed at closing differed from what was represented in the broker's engagement letter converts a conceptual dispute about "terms" into a calculable damages figure.

Attorneys should gather the following before engaging an expert: the original broker engagement or listing agreement; all term sheets and commitment letters issued; the final loan documents including note, mortgage, and any guaranty; the broker's underwriting package as submitted to the lender; the lender's internal credit memorandum; any email correspondence between the broker, borrower, and lender regarding loan sizing, rate, or terms; and the appraisal and environmental reports relied upon in underwriting. These materials allow the expert to reconstruct the underwriting as it actually occurred and compare it against market standards at the time.

Key Takeaways

  • Ann Arbor is a distinct submarket with university-driven demand characteristics, life sciences sector stability, and post-pandemic office softness that require market-specific underwriting assumptions. Generic Midwest underwriting conventions are not an adequate substitute.
  • Mixed-use collateral classification is a material underwriting decision. Whether a property is treated as multifamily or commercial determines leverage, pricing, and available lender universe. A competent broker must navigate this classification proactively and disclose its implications to the borrower.
  • Both DSCR and LTV constraints must be tested independently. Representing a loan amount based solely on income without running the LTV sensitivity analysis is a recognized deficiency in the standard of care and a common factual predicate for negligent misrepresentation claims.
  • Recourse structure and prepayment provisions are material economic terms that must be accurately represented during the marketing and commitment phase. Post-closing surprises on either of these terms are the basis for many Ann Arbor lender liability claims.
  • The Ann Arbor lender market has genuine depth across community banks, regional banks, life insurance correspondents, and bridge lenders. An expert who can quantify what each category would have offered for the specific asset at the specific time provides the most credible testimony on whether the executed transaction was consistent with market options.

Expert Witness: Financing a Contaminated Industrial Property in Myrtle Beach, SC

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Environmental Contamination Analysis

The Myrtle Beach Market Context

Myrtle Beach is not a secondary market that attorneys and investors can afford to underestimate. The Grand Strand corridor, anchored by Horry County, has evolved from a seasonal tourism economy into a diversified commercial real estate market with meaningful industrial, retail, and multifamily fundamentals. Population growth in Horry County has consistently ranked among the fastest in South Carolina and the Southeast broadly, driven by retiree migration, remote worker relocation, and expansion of the logistics and light manufacturing base that serves the coastal Carolina supply chain. That growth has consequences for industrial real estate specifically.

Industrial vacancy in the Myrtle Beach submarket has tightened materially over the past several years. Functional warehouse and distribution space along the US-501 and US-17 Business corridors, near the Conway area, and in the industrial pockets adjacent to Myrtle Beach International Airport has absorbed steadily, with vacancy rates in the 4% to 7% range depending on vintage and configuration. Asking rents for bulk warehouse product have climbed into the $6.50 to $9.00 per square foot NNN range for newer construction, with older vintage product and properties with environmental complexity trading at significant discounts to that range. That spread between market rent and below-market contract rent is the exact dynamic at the center of the dispute this article addresses.

For secured creditors and their counsel, the Myrtle Beach industrial market presents a specific underwriting challenge: the lender universe for contaminated collateral is thinner here than in Charlotte, Columbia, or Greenville. Myrtle Beach lacks the deep institutional lender bench of South Carolina's larger inland metros. Regional and community banks dominate the construction and permanent lending landscape, and most of them have environmental policy restrictions that are stricter than what CMBS or bridge lenders will tolerate on a national basis. That dynamic directly shapes the maximum supportable loan analysis in a regulatory enforcement context.

The Engagement

Counsel for a group of secured creditors retained Commercial Lending Solutions to provide a Rule 26 expert report in connection with a regulatory enforcement matter involving an industrial warehouse property in the Myrtle Beach metropolitan statistical area. The property was a single-tenant, triple-net leased warehouse facility of approximately 85,000 square feet, situated on a site with documented soil and groundwater contamination attributable to prior industrial operations by a previous owner. The contaminants of concern included 1,4-dioxane and chlorinated volatile organic compounds, specifically trichloroethylene and its degradation byproducts. Regulatory oversight was active, with a corrective action plan under review by the South Carolina Department of Environmental Services.

The core legal and financial questions our engagement required us to address were the following. First, does the presence of documented environmental contamination under active regulatory oversight categorically preclude first-mortgage financing in the current market, or does a viable financing path exist? Second, assuming financing is available, what is the maximum supportable loan amount under current market underwriting standards, and is that amount sufficient to retire the existing senior debt? Third, what is the appropriate methodology for quantifying any financing constraint attributable to the environmental condition versus constraints attributable to the below-market lease structure?

These are not questions an MAI appraiser is trained to answer, and they are not questions a retired banker can answer reliably without current, active market relationships and deal-level experience. They require someone who is actively placing commercial mortgage debt in the current market, across multiple lender categories, on properties with exactly this kind of complexity. That is the qualification set our firm brought to this engagement.

The Financial Analysis

The income analysis began with the in-place lease, which was the only contractual cash flow available to service debt. The property was NNN-leased to a single tenant at a contract rent of $5.25 per square foot, generating gross contract rent of approximately $446,250 annually. Under a true triple-net structure, landlord expenses are minimal. We underwrote a vacancy and credit loss reserve of 5% even on the in-place tenancy, reflecting single-tenant concentration risk, and applied an annual management fee of 2% of effective gross income, producing a stabilized NOI of approximately $419,478.

Market rent for comparable functional warehouse space without environmental stigma in the Myrtle Beach submarket was analyzed at $7.25 per square foot NNN, yielding a market NOI approximately 38% above the in-place figure. This gap is critical because lenders underwrite to the lesser of in-place income and stabilized market income, and in this case, the in-place lease was the binding constraint. No lender will underwrite to mark-to-market assumptions on a NNN lease with multiple years remaining at below-market rent. The lease term and any extension options must be fully mapped before the income analysis is complete.

On the debt service coverage ratio, the market standard for industrial collateral is 1.20x at most regional and community banks, and 1.25x at agency-adjacent and institutional lenders. For contaminated collateral with active regulatory oversight, the appropriate standard to apply is 1.30x, reflecting the additional risk premium lenders assign to properties with unresolved environmental contingencies. This is not a theoretical construct; it reflects actual credit committee policy at the lender categories most likely to engage with this type of collateral in the Southeast.

Applying a 1.30x DSCR to the in-place NOI of $419,478, the maximum annual debt service supportable by the property is $322,675. At a market interest rate of 7.25% on a 25-year amortization schedule, which reflects current pricing for stabilized industrial collateral with environmental complexity at community and regional banks in this market, $322,675 in annual debt service supports a loan of approximately $3,870,000. At a 65% LTV constraint, which is the appropriate ceiling for contaminated industrial collateral, the loan ceiling based on value would be approximately $3,640,000 at a market cap rate of 7.50% applied to the in-place NOI. The binding constraint in this case was the income-based limit, not the LTV ceiling.

The existing senior debt position was materially higher than either constraint. That gap between maximum supportable financing and the existing debt load is the central financial fact the secured creditors' counsel needed to establish for purposes of the enforcement proceeding. Our analysis demonstrated that the environmental condition contributed to the financing shortfall but was not the sole cause; the below-market lease structure independently produced a financing constraint of substantial magnitude even on a clean property at the same in-place rent.

The Complicating Factors

Active regulatory oversight and corrective action status. The presence of an active corrective action plan under SCDES oversight is the single most significant lender deterrent in this fact pattern. Most community and regional banks in South Carolina will not originate a first mortgage on a property with an open regulatory file, regardless of the indemnity structure or insurance coverage available. The lenders that will engage with open-file contamination are primarily national bridge lenders and certain specialty finance platforms with dedicated environmental risk underwriting teams. Those platforms price the environmental risk into the rate and structure, typically through higher margins, shorter loan terms, and personal guaranty requirements that may not be available in a litigation or enforcement context.

1,4-Dioxane as a contaminant of concern. Chlorinated VOCs have an established remediation track record and a well-developed insurance and indemnity market. 1,4-Dioxane is a materially different problem. It is a probable human carcinogen under EPA classification, it does not respond to conventional pump-and-treat remediation, and its plume behavior in sandy coastal soils of the type prevalent in Horry County is difficult to model and contain. Lenders with environmental policy experience understand that 1,4-dioxane contamination carries longer remediation timelines and higher cost uncertainty than chlorinated VOC cases. That uncertainty translates directly into financing unavailability at the conventional bank level and aggressive pricing at the specialty lender level.

Single-tenant concentration risk compounding the environmental issue. A single-tenant NNN lease provides clean cash flow predictability in normal circumstances, but it creates binary occupancy risk in an environmental enforcement context. If the tenant vacates at lease expiration or exercises a termination right triggered by the environmental condition, the property generates zero income. Lenders underwriting single-tenant industrial collateral always stress the go-dark scenario, and the environmental overhang materially reduces the probability that a replacement tenant leases contaminated space at market rents without significant landlord concessions.

Below-market rent and refinancing risk. The lease in this matter was structured at rents well below current market. That creates a compounding problem for the financing analysis. The in-place rent produces NOI that cannot support the existing debt load. If the tenant vacates at expiration, the new lease will be at market rents, but the environmental condition will require significant landlord concessions, a rent-free period, or a tenant improvement allowance that lenders will treat as a carrying cost during the re-leasing period. The path from current NOI to stabilized market NOI runs directly through a period of income disruption that no construction or bridge lender will ignore.

The Myrtle Beach Lender Market

Understanding which lenders are realistically active in Myrtle Beach for this asset type and this complexity level is not an academic exercise. It is the foundation of any credible financing feasibility opinion.

Community banks. Horry County and the broader Grand Strand market are served by a meaningful community banking sector, including several institutions headquartered in the Carolinas with active commercial real estate portfolios. These lenders are relationship-driven, hold loans on balance sheet, and can exercise credit judgment that goes beyond formulaic policy. However, virtually all of them will require a clean Phase II and evidence of regulatory closure or no further action status before originating a first mortgage. For an open-file contamination case, community banks are largely not available regardless of the income analysis.

Regional banks. The larger regional bank platforms operating in South Carolina have more formalized environmental credit policy, which in most cases is more restrictive than what community banks exercise case by case. Regional banks in this market will typically require either a closed regulatory file, environmental liability transfer insurance with the bank named as additional insured, or both. Pricing on approved contaminated-collateral loans at regional banks, when they do approve them, runs 50 to 100 basis points above their standard industrial pricing, which was approximately 6.75% to 7.25% at the time of this analysis.

CMBS and conduit lenders. CMBS is not a realistic execution path for contaminated collateral with an open regulatory file. The rating agency requirements and loan-level representations and warranties that CMBS securitization requires are incompatible with unresolved environmental contingencies. This is not a pricing issue; it is a structural disqualifier.

Bridge and specialty lenders. The most realistic financing path for this collateral, if financing is available at all, runs through national bridge lenders and specialty environmental finance platforms. These lenders underwrite contaminated collateral regularly, with interest rates in the 9.50% to 12.00% range, loan terms of two to three years with extension options, LTV constraints at 55% to 65% of as-is value, and origination fees of 1.00% to 2.00%. They require a credible remediation timeline, a qualified environmental professional on the remediation program, and often a funded remediation reserve held in escrow. In the Myrtle Beach market, borrower access to these platforms is typically through a national mortgage banking relationship rather than a local banking relationship, because most local brokers and bankers do not maintain active correspondent relationships with specialty environmental lenders.

What Attorneys Handling Myrtle Beach CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to retain a CRE financing expert. Any commercial real estate litigation matter in which the availability or terms of financing is a material fact requires a CRE financing expert. This includes plan feasibility disputes in bankruptcy, lender liability cases, secured creditor enforcement actions, borrower claims of wrongful foreclosure, and regulatory enforcement proceedings where the property's debt capacity is at issue. If the case turns on whether a property can be refinanced, what it would cost to refinance, or whether the existing debt load is serviceable from the property's income, an appraiser cannot answer those questions and a retired banker's opinion will be challenged as lacking current market knowledge.

What a CRE financing expert can answer that an appraiser cannot. An MAI appraiser opines on value. A CRE financing expert opines on the debt markets: what lenders will actually lend, at what terms, on what collateral, under what conditions, and for what reasons. Those are different questions. In a contaminated collateral dispute, the appraiser tells you what the property is worth. The financing expert tells you whether that value supports a loan sufficient to retire the senior debt, which lenders are realistically available for that loan, and what structural conditions those lenders would impose. Courts and plan confirmation hearings require both opinions, not one.

Qualification standards for a CRE financing expert. The expert should be an active commercial mortgage broker or capital markets professional, not a retired banker or former lender. Active market participation means current lender relationships, recent deal closings on comparable collateral, and direct knowledge of current credit policy at the relevant lender categories. A broker who closed contaminated industrial financings in 2015 and has since moved to consulting or teaching is not qualified to opine on current market conditions. Curriculum vitae credentials matter less than demonstrable current deal activity.

Information counsel should gather before the expert engagement. At minimum, the expert needs the current rent roll and all lease documents, the Phase I and Phase II environmental site assessments, the current regulatory file and any corrective action correspondence with SCDES, the existing loan documents and current payoff statement, any recent appraisal or broker price opinion, and the property's historical operating statements for at least three years. The environmental documents are particularly critical because the financing analysis cannot be completed without understanding the contaminant profile, the remediation status, and the regulatory pathway to closure.

How CRE financing expert testimony is used. In a plan confirmation hearing, the financing expert testifies on whether the debtor's proposed refinancing is feasible and whether the plan's financial projections are supported by current market conditions. In a secured creditor enforcement action, the expert establishes the maximum supportable loan as evidence of the property's debt capacity, which is relevant to whether the existing debt is overlevered relative to the income stream. In a lender liability matter, the expert can opine on whether the lender's origination standards at the time of the loan complied with prevailing market practice.

Key Takeaways

  • Environmental contamination does not categorically preclude financing, but it dramatically narrows the lender universe. For open-file 1,4-dioxane or chlorinated VOC contamination in the Myrtle Beach market, the available lenders are primarily bridge and specialty environmental platforms, not community or regional banks.
  • The maximum supportable loan is determined by the lesser of the income-based limit and the LTV ceiling. In below-market lease scenarios, the income constraint typically binds first, and the gap between the maximum supportable loan and the existing debt load is the central financial fact in most secured creditor disputes.
  • 1,4-Dioxane contamination carries specific lender risk concerns that are different from and more severe than chlorinated VOC contamination alone. Any financing feasibility analysis in a 1,4-dioxane case must address remediation timeline uncertainty, treatment technology limitations, and the risk premium those factors impose on available debt pricing.
  • Below-market rent and environmental contamination are compounding constraints, not interchangeable ones. Courts and plan hearing officers need expert testimony that disaggregates the two constraints and quantifies each independently.
  • CMBS and agency execution are not available for contaminated collateral with open regulatory files. Any feasibility opinion that relies on CMBS or bank execution for this type of collateral is not credible and will not withstand cross-examination.
  • The Myrtle Beach market's community banking depth provides relationship lending options for clean collateral, but does not meaningfully expand the financing universe for contaminated industrial property. Local lender relationships do not overcome environmental credit policy.
  • A DSCR of 1.30x is the appropriate underwriting standard for contaminated collateral, not the 1.20x standard applied to clean industrial assets. This distinction is material to the maximum supportable loan calculation and should be documented with supporting market evidence in any expert report.

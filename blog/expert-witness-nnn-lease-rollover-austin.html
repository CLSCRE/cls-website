Expert Witness: NNN Lease Rollover Upside and Financing Impact in Austin, TX

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Below-Market NNN Lease and Rollover Upside Analysis

The Austin Market Context

Austin's commercial real estate market has undergone a structural transformation over the past decade that makes it one of the more analytically complex markets in the country for single-tenant net lease disputes. The city is no longer a secondary Sunbelt market. It is a primary institutional capital destination with corresponding volatility in rent levels, cap rates, and lender appetite. Understanding that evolution is essential to any expert analysis of a financing dispute rooted in lease economics.

For single-tenant NNN properties specifically, Austin has seen meaningful bifurcation between asset quality tiers. Core retail corridors in submarkets such as the Domain, South Congress, and Mueller have commanded cap rates in the 5.0% to 6.25% range for well-leased, credit-quality tenants, while secondary suburban locations with shorter lease terms have traded at 6.5% to 8.0% or wider, depending on tenant credit and lease structure. That spread matters enormously in a financing dispute because it directly affects how a lender sizes debt against the in-place income versus the market rent potential.

Industrial in Austin has followed a trajectory common to high-growth markets: vacancy compressed aggressively through 2021 and 2022 as demand from tech, logistics, and light manufacturing grew faster than supply. By late 2023 and into 2024, new deliveries in submarkets such as Southeast Austin and the 130 Corridor pushed vacancy back toward the 8% to 11% range, moderating rent growth from the near-double-digit annual gains seen at the cycle peak. For a NNN industrial asset under lease dispute, this means the gap between a below-market in-place rent and current market rent can be substantial, but it also means that the durability of that rollover upside is not guaranteed. A lender underwrites the current lease, not the projected market rent.

Office fundamentals in Austin tell a more cautionary story. Direct vacancy in Class A downtown Austin has exceeded 20% in recent reporting periods, with sublease space adding another several percentage points of effective availability. For any single-tenant NNN office dispute, this context is not background noise. It is central to the financing analysis, because lenders have materially tightened their credit standards for office-anchored collateral across the board, and Austin is no exception regardless of its overall market strength.

The broader interest rate environment since 2022 has compressed debt proceeds across all NNN asset types. A property that could support a 70% LTV loan at a 4.5% coupon in 2021 may only support a 55% to 60% LTV loan at a 7.0% to 7.5% coupon today, even if the property's value has not changed. That mechanical compression of loan proceeds is a critical input in any dispute where financing feasibility is at issue.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing a party in a commercial real estate financing dispute involving a single-tenant NNN property in the Austin metropolitan area. The asset was a freestanding commercial building leased to a single occupant under a long-form net lease with below-market in-place rent relative to current Austin submarket conditions. The remaining lease term and the structure of rent escalations were both at issue.

Counsel's core questions to CLS CRE were precisely the kind that require a capital markets practitioner rather than a generalist appraiser or retired bank officer. First, how do institutional and regional lenders actually underwrite a property whose in-place NOI is materially below current market rent? Second, does the existence of rollover upside, meaning the prospect of re-leasing at market rent when the current lease expires, improve a borrower's ability to secure financing, and if so, how and under what conditions? Third, given the current in-place income, what maximum loan amount could the property support at market-standard DSCR and LTV thresholds, and how does that compare to the loan amounts that were at issue in the dispute? Fourth, would a reasonable lender in the Austin market have closed a loan on this collateral at the parameters being contested, or does the lease structure itself create an underwriting obstacle that closes off financing from most lender categories?

These are not questions an appraisal report answers. An appraisal establishes value. A capital markets expert witness establishes what the financing market will actually do with that value, and why. Those are fundamentally different analytical exercises, and the distinction matters at trial and in plan confirmation hearings alike.

The Financial Analysis

The income analysis began with the in-place lease. The tenant was paying a base rent that, when converted to a per-square-foot NNN equivalent, was approximately 18% to 22% below the current market asking rent for comparable space in the same Austin submarket. The lease included fixed annual bumps of 2.0%, which is a common escalation structure in NNN deals but one that does not keep pace with Austin's historical rent growth over the prior cycle. The result was a growing gap between in-place rent and market rent as the lease aged.

The in-place NOI for the property, after deducting landlord-controlled operating expenses and reserves appropriate for a NNN structure, was approximately $620,000 annually. This was the number a lender would use as the underwritten NOI. Lenders do not underwrite to projected rollover rent. They underwrite to the contractual income stream that exists on the day of loan origination, with very limited exceptions discussed below.

Applying a market-standard DSCR requirement of 1.25x, which is the floor most conventional commercial mortgage lenders require on single-tenant NNN deals with non-investment-grade tenants, the maximum annual debt service this property could support was $496,000 ($620,000 divided by 1.25). At a 7.25% interest rate on a 25-year amortization schedule, which reflected prevailing market conditions for a stabilized but below-market-leased NNN asset in Austin at the time of the analysis, $496,000 in annual debt service corresponds to a maximum supportable loan of approximately $5,950,000.

The appraised value of the property, reflective of both the in-place below-market income and the market rent upside, was approximately $9,200,000. A 65% LTV constraint, typical for this collateral and lender type, would have permitted a loan of approximately $5,980,000. The DSCR constraint and the LTV constraint produced nearly identical maximum loan amounts in this instance, which is analytically significant. It means the property was not a case where the borrower was simply equity-short. The income constraint was independently binding, and any attempt to stretch the LTV would have failed on DSCR grounds regardless.

The market-rent scenario was also modeled. If the tenant were paying current market rent, estimated at $32.00 per square foot NNN on a 28,000 square foot building, the gross potential rent would be approximately $896,000. After reserves and minor landlord obligations, the market-rent NOI would approach $850,000. At the same 1.25x DSCR and 7.25% rate, the maximum supportable loan on market-rent income would be approximately $8,150,000, representing a 37% increase in debt proceeds. This quantified the financing impact of the below-market lease structure in concrete terms that could be presented to a trier of fact without ambiguity.

The Complicating Factors

Rollover Upside Does Not Improve Today's Loan Sizing. The single most important financing principle in this type of dispute is that anticipated rollover upside is not underwritten by conventional commercial mortgage lenders. A lender advancing money today is collateralized by the in-place income stream. The borrower's belief, or even strong market evidence, that rent will increase at lease expiration does not move the underwriting needle for a conventional loan. Bridge lenders and value-add debt funds are the exception, and their pricing reflects the risk premium: spreads of 300 to 500 basis points over SOFR are common, loan terms are typically 24 to 36 months with extensions, and origination fees and exit fees add further to the cost of capital. Representing that rollover upside enables conventional financing at conventional pricing is analytically incorrect and would not withstand scrutiny from an informed lender or expert witness.

Lease Expiration Timing Relative to Loan Maturity. When a NNN lease expires inside the loan term, the lender faces the prospect of a vacant single-tenant building at maturity with no income to support refinancing. This is a severe underwriting concern. Most lenders require that the lease term extend at least 12 to 18 months beyond the loan maturity date. A 10-year lease with three years remaining is a fundamentally different underwriting proposition than the same building with nine years remaining on the lease, even if the NOI is identical today. In the Austin context, where office and some retail lease renewals have been contested, this constraint has caused deals to fall out of conventional lending and into the bridge or hard-money category, with materially higher pricing and lower leverage.

Tenant Credit and Lease Enforceability. Single-tenant NNN lenders underwrite not just the income stream but the credit quality of the entity obligated to pay it. An investment-grade tenant obligor allows a lender to treat the lease as near-bond-equivalent collateral. A non-rated, single-location tenant obligor requires the lender to underwrite the business risk of the tenant independently, often resulting in lower LTV and higher DSCR requirements. In disputes involving below-market leases, the question of whether the lease is enforceable as written, particularly whether there are conditions or disputes that could allow the tenant to terminate or modify rent obligations, is directly material to the lender's assessment. A lease under active litigation is, for financing purposes, a distressed asset regardless of its face terms.

Lender Interpretations of Market Rent Evidence. In underwriting, lenders use third-party market rent analyses or internal comp databases to stress-test a below-market lease. If market rents have declined since the lease was executed, the lender may view the in-place below-market rent as not actually being below-market anymore. Conversely, if market rents have risen sharply, as occurred in Austin's industrial and select retail submarkets from 2019 through 2022, the below-market discount widens and the refinancing constraint tightens proportionally. Expert witnesses in these disputes must be grounded in the specific submarket rent data, not citywide averages, because Austin's submarket-level variance is significant.

The Austin Lender Market

The lender universe for single-tenant NNN assets in Austin is stratified by asset quality, lease term, tenant credit, and deal size. Understanding which categories of lenders are actually accessible for a given collateral profile is essential to any financing feasibility analysis.

Life Insurance Companies and CMBS. For NNN assets with investment-grade tenants, long remaining lease terms (10-plus years), and clean title and environmental, life companies and conduit CMBS lenders are the pricing leaders in Austin. They will lend at 60% to 65% LTV with DSCR requirements of 1.20x to 1.25x and offer fixed rates with 10-year terms. They do not accommodate below-market leases gracefully. Their credit boxes are largely formulaic, and a lease with under five years of remaining term, or a non-rated tenant, typically disqualifies the deal from this category entirely.

Regional and Community Banks. Austin benefits from a deep community and regional bank presence, including several Texas-domiciled institutions with meaningful commercial real estate portfolios and local market knowledge. These lenders will accommodate shorter lease terms and non-rated tenants, but they price accordingly. Expect rates 50 to 100 basis points higher than life company pricing, lower LTV ceilings in the 55% to 65% range, and recourse or partial recourse requirements that CMBS lenders do not impose. They are relationship-driven, which means deal history and sponsorship quality matter as much as the asset's fundamentals.

Debt Funds and Bridge Lenders. For below-market-leased assets where the business plan is to re-lease at market and then refinance into permanent debt, bridge lenders and debt funds fill the gap. In Austin, multiple national debt funds have established relationships with local sponsors and will underwrite to a value-add thesis. Leverage is typically 65% to 70% of cost or as-is value, rates are floating and substantially higher than conventional products, and the lender will require a clearly defined exit strategy, usually a lease-up or sale. These are not permanent financing solutions, and expert analysis should clearly distinguish bridge-financed assumptions from conventional-financed assumptions when presenting alternative scenarios.

SBA 504 and Conventional SBA Programs. For owner-occupant scenarios where the NNN tenant dispute involves a business that also owns the real estate, SBA lending programs are relevant. The 504 program allows up to 90% combined LTV through a first mortgage and a Certified Development Company second, making it one of the few paths to high-leverage owner-occupied commercial real estate financing in Austin regardless of below-market rent issues. However, this product is only available for owner-occupants, and it is not a refinancing solution for an investor-owned single-tenant NNN property.

What Attorneys Handling Austin CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to Retain a CRE Financing Expert. Counsel should consider retaining a CRE financing expert any time the dispute involves a question about what a borrower could or could not have borrowed against a specific property, whether financing was feasible at specific terms, what damages flow from a financing failure, or how a property's lease structure affects its refinancing prospects. These questions arise in lender liability cases, landlord-tenant disputes involving lease enforcement and property value, partnership dissolution proceedings, bankruptcy plan confirmation, and post-closing disputes over representations made about a property's financing capacity.

What a CRE Financing Expert Answers That an Appraiser Cannot. An appraiser determines value. A CRE financing expert determines what the debt markets will actually do with that value. These are distinct disciplines. An appraisal of $9,200,000 tells you what a buyer might pay. It does not tell you whether a lender will lend $6,000,000 or $4,500,000 against that property, why one lender category prices differently from another, what DSCR threshold applies to this asset class and why, or whether the lease structure itself creates an underwriting obstacle that no LTV ratio can overcome. In a financing feasibility dispute, the appraiser's report is often the starting point, not the answer.

What Qualifies a CRE Financing Expert. The relevant qualification is active, current market practice, not a historical title. A retired bank credit officer who last originated loans in a prior rate cycle may not have current knowledge of how debt funds price, how CMBS conduit underwriting has evolved post-COVID, or how Austin's specific lender relationships and market dynamics affect deal execution. The most credible CRE financing expert witnesses are professionals who are currently active in placing commercial mortgage debt, who maintain broad lender relationships across multiple capital stack tiers, and who can testify from current market practice rather than historical experience alone. CLS CRE places commercial mortgages across all 50 states with over 1,000 active lender relationships and approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, providing the market depth that credible expert testimony requires.

Information Counsel Should Gather Before the Engagement. The financing expert will need the full executed lease and all amendments, the current rent roll, historical operating statements for at least three years, any existing appraisals or broker opinions of value, the current or proposed loan term sheet or commitment letter (if financing was attempted and failed), any lender rejection or credit committee memoranda, the title report, and the environmental phase history. For a below-market lease dispute, the expert will also need access to comparable lease transaction data and, where available, the tenant's financial statements if tenant credit is a material underwriting variable.

How CRE Financing Expert Testimony Is Used. At trial, CRE financing expert testimony is used to establish the outer boundaries of what was financially achievable on a given property at a given time, to rebut opposing party claims that financing was available on terms it was not, and to quantify the economic harm caused by a financing failure or a lease structure that precluded refinancing. In bankruptcy plan confirmation proceedings, it is used to establish whether a proposed plan's financing assumptions are feasible under market conditions. In both contexts, the expert must be prepared to defend methodology under cross-examination by opposing counsel who may retain their own financing expert.

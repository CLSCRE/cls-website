Expert Witness: How Construction Defects Affect CRE Financing in Huntsville, AL

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Construction Defect Impact on Financing

The Huntsville Market Context

Huntsville, Alabama has undergone a structural transformation over the past decade that most national capital markets professionals are only beginning to fully appreciate. What was once a mid-tier Sun Belt market anchored by federal defense spending has evolved into one of the Southeast's most dynamic commercial real estate environments. The presence of Redstone Arsenal, the U.S. Army Materiel Command, NASA's Marshall Space Flight Center, and a rapidly expanding private aerospace and defense contractor ecosystem has created demand patterns that diverge sharply from conventional Sun Belt CRE assumptions. That distinction matters enormously when a financing dispute lands in litigation.

Industrial and flex product is where Huntsville's story is most compelling for lenders and litigants alike. Vacancy in the Cummings Research Park corridor and the broader Madison County industrial submarket has remained in the low single digits, with net absorption driven by defense contractors, logistics operators serving the I-565 corridor, and advanced manufacturing tenants. Class A industrial rents in prime Huntsville submarkets have pushed toward $8.00 to $10.00 per square foot on a triple-net basis, a level that supports meaningful loan amounts even at conservative underwriting. Multifamily fundamentals are equally strong: effective rents have climbed steadily, concessions have compressed, and institutional capital has been acquiring stabilized apartment assets at cap rates in the 5.00 to 5.75 percent range, with value-add plays trading somewhat wider. Office is more bifurcated, with government-adjacent Class A product holding its own while suburban Class B faces the same secular headwinds visible nationally.

This context matters for construction defect disputes because the strength of the underlying market creates a false sense of security. Lenders see strong rent rolls, low vacancy, and a favorable economic backdrop and may initially appear willing to proceed. But when construction defects enter the picture, the underwriting calculus shifts fundamentally, and the gap between a defect-free asset and a defective one can represent millions of dollars in maximum supportable loan proceeds. Understanding that gap, and quantifying it with precision, is exactly what a commercial real estate financing expert is retained to do.

The Engagement

Counsel for a plaintiff in a Huntsville commercial real estate dispute retained Commercial Lending Solutions to provide an expert analysis of financing feasibility and loan value impact in connection with construction defect litigation involving a mid-size commercial property in the Huntsville metropolitan area. The asset was a recently completed mixed-use commercial building incorporating both ground-floor retail and upper-floor office space, located within a high-visibility Huntsville submarket characterized by strong tenant demand and limited Class A availability. The property had been placed in service and partially leased when the construction defects at issue were discovered and litigation commenced.

Counsel's core questions were threefold. First, would a commercially reasonable lender have funded the originally anticipated permanent financing on this asset once the alleged defects became known, and if so, on what terms? Second, how did the defects affect the three primary financing metrics that drive loan sizing: net operating income, debt service coverage ratio, and loan-to-value? Third, what was the quantifiable difference between the maximum supportable loan on a defect-free asset of equivalent type and the maximum supportable loan on the subject property in its defective condition? The answers to these questions directly informed the damages analysis, because the inability to secure anticipated financing, or the requirement to accept materially inferior financing terms, constitutes a quantifiable economic harm that courts and juries can evaluate.

CLS CRE was retained based on our firm's active transaction experience across approximately $1 billion in commercial real estate financing volume, relationships with over 1,000 institutional and regional lenders nationwide, and prior expert witness engagements involving financing feasibility and lender market standards. My own background encompasses both origination and structuring of commercial loans across asset classes in Alabama and across all 50 states, which allows me to speak to both what the written underwriting guidelines require and how credit committees actually behave when defects are in the picture.

The Financial Analysis

The analytical framework I applied follows the methodology used by institutional commercial mortgage lenders when underwriting permanent debt on stabilized or near-stabilized assets. This is not a theoretical framework. It is the actual underwriting process that a credit officer at a life insurance company, CMBS conduit, regional bank, or debt fund would apply when evaluating a loan application on this property type in this market.

Step One: Establish Underwritten NOI. The starting point is a stabilized net operating income figure, derived from in-place leases adjusted for any above-market or below-market terms, market rents for vacant space at Huntsville submarket absorption assumptions, and a deduction for stabilized vacancy and credit loss (typically 5 to 10 percent for mixed-use retail and office in Huntsville), management fees (4 to 5 percent of effective gross income is standard for third-party managed assets), and a replacement reserve allowance. For a hypothetical Huntsville mixed-use asset of approximately 35,000 square feet with blended gross revenues of $1,150,000 annually at stabilization, a lender-underwritten NOI in the range of $750,000 to $800,000 is a reasonable baseline for a well-leased, defect-free asset in the current market.

Step Two: Apply DSCR Constraints. Institutional lenders underwriting permanent commercial mortgage debt require a minimum debt service coverage ratio, calculated as underwritten NOI divided by annual debt service on the proposed loan. The market standard for mixed-use retail and office product is a minimum DSCR of 1.25x, meaning the property must generate at least $1.25 of NOI for every $1.00 of debt service. Life companies and CMBS lenders frequently apply 1.30x or higher on office-heavy assets given current office market sentiment nationally, even in strong markets like Huntsville. Regional banks may accept 1.20x for well-located properties with strong tenancy, but this represents the floor, not the standard.

Step Three: Size the Loan to NOI and DSCR. Using an interest rate assumption of 6.75 percent (reflective of current permanent lending rates for this product type in secondary markets like Huntsville, with a 25-year amortization schedule), annual debt service on a $9,000,000 loan would be approximately $746,000. At a DSCR of 1.25x, the maximum loan supportable from an NOI of $800,000 is approximately $8,600,000 (calculated as: $800,000 divided by 1.25x = $640,000 maximum allowable debt service; $640,000 in annual debt service at 6.75 percent over 25 years supports a loan of approximately $8,550,000 to $8,600,000). This is the income-constrained loan ceiling.

Step Four: Apply LTV Constraints. The DSCR constraint operates in parallel with, not in substitution for, the loan-to-value constraint. For stabilized mixed-use commercial assets, institutional lenders in current market conditions are holding LTV at or below 65 percent. Some life companies have tightened further to 60 percent on office-heavy assets. If an appraiser values the defect-free subject property at $13,500,000, the LTV-constrained maximum loan is $8,775,000 at 65 percent LTV. In this example, DSCR is the binding constraint, and the maximum supportable loan is approximately $8,550,000.

Step Five: Quantify the Defect Impact. Construction defects affect the financing in two simultaneous ways. First, they reduce NOI directly by requiring reserves for remediation, creating uncertainty that lenders model as an additional deduction from underwritten income, and causing or prolonging vacancy as tenants delay occupancy or exercise early termination rights. If the alleged defects in this engagement created a conservative NOI impairment of $120,000 annually (through documented vacancy extension, tenant rent abatement provisions triggered by the defects, and a lender-required remediation reserve of $75,000 per year), the underwritten NOI falls to approximately $680,000. At 1.25x DSCR with the same rate and amortization assumptions, the maximum supportable loan drops to approximately $7,275,000. Second, the defects reduce the appraised value of the property, which tightens the LTV constraint simultaneously. A value impairment of $1,500,000 from the defect-free baseline of $13,500,000 reduces the LTV-constrained maximum loan from $8,775,000 to $7,800,000 at 65 percent LTV. The combined effect is a financing gap of approximately $1,275,000 to $1,500,000 relative to what the owner could have borrowed on a defect-free asset, before accounting for the pricing premium lenders charge when defects are known.

The Complicating Factors

Lender Disclosure Obligations and Known-Defect Contamination. Once construction defects are a matter of public record, specifically once litigation has been filed, lenders are on notice. Any lender that funds a permanent loan on a property with pending construction defect litigation without adequately addressing the defect in its underwriting has a regulatory and credit quality problem. In practice, this means lenders either decline to fund entirely, require the defect litigation to be resolved before closing, or require the borrower to escrow a remediation holdback equal to the estimated repair cost plus a contingency, typically 15 to 25 percent. That holdback comes directly out of loan proceeds and reduces the net cash the borrower receives, which is a quantifiable harm distinct from the loan sizing reduction described above.

Title Insurance Complications. Commercial title insurers will frequently add exceptions or endorsement requirements when pending construction litigation involving the subject property is identified. Lenders require title insurance as a condition of funding, and a title policy with broad exceptions related to the construction defect litigation may not satisfy the lender's title requirements. This can effectively prevent the transaction from closing even if the borrower and lender have agreed on other terms, creating a total financing unavailability scenario rather than a mere pricing or proceeds adjustment.

Tenant Estoppel and SNDA Issues. Mixed-use assets require tenants to execute estoppel certificates and subordination, non-disturbance, and attornment agreements as a condition of permanent loan closing. If tenants are aware of construction defects and have asserted claims or defenses arising from those defects, they may refuse to deliver clean estoppels, which halts the loan closing. Lenders will not fund without clean estoppels from anchor and major tenants, and a tenant who has documented a defect claim in writing has both the leverage and the legal basis to condition or decline the estoppel. This scenario converts a financing feasibility issue into a financing impossibility issue for at least the portion of the leased space affected by the defect.

Rate and Pricing Premium. Even in scenarios where a lender is willing to proceed with full knowledge of the defect dispute, they price the additional risk. A property with pending litigation that clouds title, creates uncertain income, and raises questions about long-term structural integrity will carry a credit spread premium of 50 to 150 basis points over a comparable defect-free asset. On an $8,000,000 loan, 100 basis points in additional interest rate cost represents $80,000 per year in additional debt service, which further compresses DSCR and further reduces the maximum supportable loan, creating a compounding adverse effect on the financing structure.

The Huntsville Lender Market

Huntsville benefits from a relatively deep and diverse commercial lending ecosystem for a market of its size, which reflects both the economic stability of the defense and aerospace base and the growing attention of institutional capital to Alabama's stronger markets. Understanding which lenders are active and what each category requires is essential to evaluating financing feasibility accurately.

Regional and Community Banks. Huntsville and the broader Madison County market are well-served by regionally based commercial banks with active construction and permanent lending programs. These institutions generally operate with relationship-based credit criteria, meaning they have more flexibility in deal structuring than CMBS lenders, but they also have legal and regulatory constraints that prevent them from knowingly funding loans on properties with unresolved material defect litigation. Their typical permanent lending parameters for mixed-use commercial product are LTV up to 70 percent with demonstrated cash flow, DSCR of 1.20x minimum, and amortization of 20 to 25 years. In a construction defect context, these lenders will typically condition any commitment on resolution of the litigation or a fully funded escrow arrangement.

CMBS Conduit Lenders. Several national CMBS lenders are active in the Huntsville market for stabilized assets at loan amounts above $5,000,000. CMBS lenders apply entirely standardized underwriting criteria with no relationship-based exceptions. If pending litigation appears in the title search or is disclosed on the loan application, a CMBS conduit will decline the deal at origination or pull back from commitment. There is no path to a CMBS permanent loan on a property with active construction defect litigation unless the litigation is resolved and the title is cleared.

Debt Funds and Bridge Lenders. This category is relevant because a borrower who cannot access conventional permanent financing due to the defect dispute may be forced into a bridge loan from a private debt fund. Bridge lenders will accept the litigation risk at a price: rates of 9.00 to 11.00 percent, interest-only structures, origination fees of 1.00 to 2.00 percent, and short terms of 12 to 24 months with extension options. The differential between a 6.75 percent conventional permanent loan and a 10.00 percent bridge loan represents directly quantifiable additional interest expense that is attributable to the defect. That cost differential is a component of economic damages in the right litigation context.

Life Insurance Companies. Life companies are active in Huntsville on larger, higher-quality stabilized assets, typically with loan amounts above $7,500,000 and best-in-class tenancy. They represent the lowest-cost permanent capital available in the market, with all-in rates at or below conventional bank pricing. Life companies have zero tolerance for pending litigation on collateral. They will not issue a term sheet on a defective asset, and their participation represents the upper bound of financing quality that is eliminated entirely when defects are alleged.

What Attorneys Handling Huntsville CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to Retain a CRE Financing Expert. A commercial real estate financing expert should be retained whenever the damages theory in a CRE dispute involves lost financing proceeds, inability to refinance or exit a position, increased borrowing costs, or a claim that financing was unavailable. These scenarios arise in construction defect cases, partnership disputes involving refinancing deadlocks, lender liability cases, and loan modification disputes. The earlier the engagement, the more useful the expert can be in shaping the damages theory and identifying the specific lender market evidence that supports it.

What a Financing Expert Can Answer That an Appraiser Cannot. An appraisal establishes value. A financing expert establishes what a commercially reasonable lender would have done with that value under current and historical market conditions. These are different questions. An appraiser does not opine on DSCR constraints, lender credit boxes, CMBS conduit guidelines, escrow holdback requirements, or the market standard for known-defect disclosure. A financing expert does. In many CRE damages analyses, the financing gap is larger than the value gap, because the loan sizing constraints amplify the economic impact of a given value reduction. Attorneys who rely solely on appraisal testimony miss this leverage.

What Makes a Financing Expert Qualified. Relevant qualifications are active deal origination experience, not merely historical advisory or academic background. A retired banker with 10-year-old market experience cannot credibly testify about current CMBS guidelines, current debt fund pricing, or the specific lender market conditions in Huntsville in the relevant time period. The expert should have verifiable transaction volume, documented lender relationships, and experience with the specific asset type and market at issue. At CLS CRE, our approximately $1 billion in transaction volume across 50 states with more than 1,000 active lender relationships provides that foundation.

Information Counsel Should Gather Before the Expert Engagement. To support a financing analysis, counsel should preserve and produce: the original loan application and lender commitment letter if one was issued; all lender correspondence following discovery of the defects; any appraisals, whether lender-ordered or otherwise; the property's rent roll and operating statements for the trailing 24 months; all tenant correspondence related to the defects, including estoppel negotiations; the title commitment and any exceptions related to the defect litigation; and any competing financing quotes obtained after the defects were discovered. This documentary record allows the expert to reconstruct the actual financing timeline and demonstrate precisely when and how the defects caused financing harm.

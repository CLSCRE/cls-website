Commercial Mortgage Rates: Week of August 31, 2026

Trevor Damyan Founder & Principal Broker
CA DRE #02244836

Former CBRE and Marcus & Millichap Capital Corporation. $1B+ in closed commercial real estate financing across bridge, construction, permanent, and structured debt.

August 31, 2026
Quick answer: Commercial mortgage rates held in a narrow range during the week of August 31 through September 4, 2026, as bond markets digested mixed labor data and Fed officials offered cautious commentary ahead of the fall policy cycle. Here is what borrowers and investors need to know heading into Q4.

Rate Summary: Week of August 31 to September 4, 2026

The final week of August carried its usual late-summer quiet into a holiday-shortened trading stretch, but the underlying dynamics were anything but sleepy. Treasury yields nudged modestly higher early in the week after a stronger-than-expected ADP private payrolls print on Wednesday, only to give back a portion of those gains on Friday when the Bureau of Labor Statistics released a headline nonfarm payrolls number that landed just below consensus expectations. The net effect was a modest steepening of the yield curve, with the 2-year Treasury ending the week roughly flat while the 10-year drifted higher by approximately 8 to 10 basis points from where it closed the prior Friday.

For commercial real estate borrowers, that movement matters in very practical terms. Agency and CMBS lenders price their fixed-rate products off Treasury benchmarks, so even a single-digit basis point move in the 10-year can shift a permanent loan coupon by a meaningful amount on a multi-million-dollar transaction. Bridge and construction borrowers using floating-rate debt are watching SOFR, which remained relatively stable in the 4.80 to 4.90 percent range during the week, consistent with market expectations that the Federal Reserve will hold the federal funds rate steady at its September meeting before potentially resuming cuts later in Q4.

The broad takeaway: rate conditions remain challenging by historical standards but have stabilized enough that well-structured deals with strong sponsorship are clearing the market. Spreads on most product types are neither widening dramatically nor compressing aggressively. It is a pricing environment that rewards preparation and precision more than timing.

Treasury Yields: The Benchmark That Drives Fixed-Rate Pricing

The 10-year U.S. Treasury yield, the primary benchmark for most commercial mortgage fixed-rate products, closed the week of September 4, 2026, in the range of approximately 4.45 to 4.55 percent. The 5-year Treasury, which influences shorter fixed-rate terms and some agency loan pricing, finished near 4.20 to 4.30 percent. The 2-year Treasury held around 4.35 to 4.45 percent, reflecting a yield curve that has largely returned to a normal upward slope after its prolonged inversion.

Key drivers for the week included the following:

  • ADP payrolls for August came in above estimates, initially pushing yields higher as investors dialed back rate-cut expectations for September.
  • The official BLS jobs report on Friday showed headline job creation slightly below the consensus forecast, partially reversing the earlier yield spike and reinforcing a "hold steady" narrative for the Fed.
  • Fed Governor commentary released mid-week underscored that policymakers remain data-dependent and are in no hurry to resume cutting until inflation readings give them additional confidence.
  • Global demand for U.S. Treasuries at the week's auction was described as solid, providing a floor under prices and a ceiling on yields.

The current yield environment sits meaningfully above the 2020 to 2021 lows that many borrowers still reference mentally, but it is also well below the October 2023 peak above 5 percent on the 10-year. Lenders and borrowers who accept today's benchmark as the operating reality, rather than waiting for a return to pandemic-era rates, are the ones closing transactions.

What This Means for CRE Borrowers Right Now

A steeper yield curve, stable SOFR, and cautious Fed rhetoric create a specific set of conditions that affect each borrower category differently.

For permanent loan seekers, the slight drift higher in the 10-year means all-in fixed coupons on agency and CMBS product edged up modestly from the prior week. Borrowers who locked rate applications in mid-August may find their quotes under minor pressure if they have not yet executed a rate lock. If you are approaching a lock decision, this week's data argues for moving sooner rather than later, given the residual upside risk in yields if September economic data continues to print above consensus.

For floating-rate borrowers on bridge and construction loans, the week provided a measure of relief. SOFR stability means your current pay rate is not moving, and the market still prices in at least one additional Fed cut before year-end, which would directly reduce carry costs on open-ended bridge positions. That said, lenders continue to underwrite to conservative exit cap rates and scrutinize debt service coverage at stabilization with care. The spread environment for bridge loans has not compressed materially, so the all-in cost of capital for transitional deals remains elevated.

Refinance borrowers facing loan maturities in Q4 2026 are the group most affected by this week's yield movement. If you have a loan maturing in October or November and have not yet engaged a lender or broker, the window to engineer a smooth transition is closing. Lenders processing volume ahead of year-end are prioritizing deals they have been working on, not new submissions arriving in October.

CRE Loan Rate Ranges by Loan Type

The following ranges are indicative market rates for the week ending September 4, 2026. They reflect mid-market conditions for creditworthy sponsors on stabilized or near-stabilized assets in major and secondary markets. Individual deal pricing will vary based on asset quality, market, leverage, loan structure, and sponsorship.

  • Agency Multifamily (Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, 7- to 10-year fixed): All-in approximately 5.65 to 6.10 percent, representing spreads of roughly 115 to 160 basis points over the corresponding Treasury benchmark. Lower leverage and high-quality assets in primary markets access the tighter end of that range.
  • HUD/FHA Multifamily (223(f) refinance and 221(d)(4) construction-to-permanent): All-in approximately 5.40 to 5.80 percent on a fully amortizing 35- to 40-year term. HUD paper remains the lowest fixed-rate coupon available in the market, though execution timelines of 6 to 12 months limit its applicability for time-sensitive situations.
  • CMBS (conduit, 10-year fixed, non-recourse): All-in approximately 6.25 to 7.00 percent, with spreads of approximately 165 to 245 basis points over the 10-year Treasury. Retail, office, and hospitality assets carry spread premiums toward the wider end. Industrial and multifamily CMBS executions are tighter.
  • Life Company (permanent, 5- to 15-year fixed, lower leverage): All-in approximately 5.85 to 6.50 percent, typically representing spreads of 135 to 200 basis points over the comparable Treasury. Life companies remain highly selective on asset type and market, favoring multifamily, industrial, grocery-anchored retail, and Class A office in gateway cities.
  • Bank Permanent and Portfolio Loans (5-year fixed, recourse): All-in approximately 6.00 to 6.75 percent on stabilized assets, with pricing varying significantly by institution appetite, relationship, and geography. Shorter fixed terms with floating-rate resets are also available in this channel.
  • Bridge Loans (floating, 1- to 3-year term, transitional assets): SOFR plus 275 to 425 basis points, resulting in all-in rates of approximately 7.55 to 9.05 percent at current SOFR levels. Deals with clear value-add business plans, experienced sponsors, and pre-identified exit strategies access the tighter end of the spread range.
  • Construction Loans (floating, bank and debt fund): SOFR plus 300 to 450 basis points for bank-originated construction, resulting in all-in rates of approximately 7.80 to 9.30 percent. Debt fund construction capital is available at SOFR plus 400 to 575 basis points for projects that do not meet traditional bank criteria.
  • Mezzanine and Preferred Equity (debt fund and family office sources): All-in approximately 10.50 to 13.50 percent, depending on position in the capital stack, loan-to-cost, and deal complexity. This capital is priced as a fixed or floating coupon depending on the provider and deal structure.

Market Outlook: What to Watch in September

The Fed's September meeting on September 16 and 17 is the single most important scheduled event for CRE capital markets in the near term. Futures markets heading into this week assign roughly 80 to 85 percent probability to a hold, with the remaining probability weighted toward a 25 basis point cut. The bond market has largely priced in the hold scenario, which means a surprise cut would push Treasury yields lower and create a favorable window for borrowers pursuing rate locks in the days that follow. A hold, by contrast, is unlikely to move markets significantly on its own.

Beyond the Fed, the September CPI report scheduled for mid-month and the September jobs report in early October will significantly influence whether the market reprices additional cuts into the Q4 and Q1 2027 forward curve. Borrowers on floating-rate loans benefit if that repricing moves in a dovish direction.

On the credit side, lender appetite in the CMBS market has been consistent through mid-year, and execution volumes are running ahead of 2025 pace. Agency multifamily lenders continue to compete aggressively on lower-leverage refinances, particularly in sunbelt and mountain west markets where rent growth is stabilizing after the 2023 to 2024 correction. Bridge lenders are active but disciplined, and the gap between what sponsors expect in bridge pricing and what lenders are willing to offer has narrowed compared to late 2024, though it has not closed entirely.

Action Items for Borrowers This Week

Given current market conditions, here is where borrowers should focus their energy:

  • If you have a Q4 2026 loan maturity, engage your capital markets advisor immediately. The September and October closing calendar is filling rapidly.
  • If you are pursuing an agency permanent loan and your application is in process, discuss rate lock timing with your broker. The 10-year has room to move in either direction in September, and waiting carries risk.
  • If you are in the early stages of a bridge loan on a transitional asset, stress-test your underwriting at SOFR plus 400 basis points rather than at today's SOFR. Extension risk is real, and lenders are modeling it carefully.
  • If you are considering a construction start in Q4, explore construction-to-permanent structures through agency and life company channels before defaulting to bank floating-rate construction debt. Locking in a permanent exit today can meaningfully reduce project risk.
  • For any deal where you are evaluating fixed versus floating, run the break-even analysis with your advisor. The carry differential between a fixed 6.25 percent CMBS loan and a floating bridge at SOFR plus 350 basis points is smaller than it has been in prior cycles, making fixed-rate permanent debt comparatively more attractive than it was 18 months ago.

SOFR, the index underlying most floating-rate commercial real estate loans originated since 2023, was running in the 4.80 to 4.90 percent range during this week. That is meaningful but not transformative, which is why bridge borrowers should focus primarily on the business plan execution and exit timeline rather than banking on rate-cut relief to solve a tight debt service situation. Always stress-test your bridge loan at a rate scenario where SOFR stays flat or moves higher before committing to a floating-rate structure.
Both agency loans (Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac) and CMBS conduit loans are fixed-rate, non-recourse permanent financing options for stabilized commercial real estate, but they differ in important ways that affect pricing, flexibility, and fit. Agency loans are available exclusively for multifamily properties (apartment buildings, manufactured housing, student housing, and affordable housing). They currently offer all-in coupons of approximately 5.65 to 6.10 percent, which is meaningfully lower than CMBS pricing for comparable multifamily assets. Agency loans also offer supplemental financing options, flexible prepayment structures, and strong long-term lender relationships. CMBS conduit loans cover a broader set of asset types including retail, office, industrial, hospitality, and mixed-use, in addition to multifamily. CMBS all-in coupons are currently in the range of approximately 6.25 to 7.00 percent for most asset types. For a stabilized apartment building specifically, agency financing will almost always produce a lower coupon, better terms, and greater flexibility than CMBS, assuming the property meets agency eligibility requirements. CMBS becomes the more relevant tool for non-multifamily assets, or for multifamily situations where agency programs cannot accommodate the loan structure, such as very short amortization periods or certain mixed-use configurations.

Expert Witness: CRE Partnership and Waterfall Disputes in Roanoke, VA

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Joint Venture and Partnership Dispute

The Roanoke Market Context

Roanoke, Virginia sits at the intersection of regional economic resilience and genuine commercial real estate complexity. The Roanoke Metropolitan Statistical Area, anchored by the Roanoke Valley and encompassing Salem, Vinton, and Botetourt County, has historically attracted joint venture capital for exactly the reasons that also generate disputes: moderate deal sizes, compressed cap rates relative to primary markets, and a commercial lending environment where regional banks and credit unions carry outsized influence compared to institutional sources. For attorneys handling commercial real estate litigation in this market, understanding the financing fundamentals is inseparable from understanding why waterfall disputes arise here in the first place.

Multifamily has been the dominant institutional asset class in the Roanoke market over the past five years. Garden-style and mid-rise apartment communities in submarkets like South Roanoke, Williamson Road, and the Hollins corridor have traded at cap rates ranging from approximately 5.25% to 6.75% depending on vintage, occupancy, and unit mix. Those cap rate spreads matter enormously in joint venture waterfall mechanics because they directly determine the stabilized value that triggers promote calculations. Industrial has also seen meaningful activity, with vacancy in the Roanoke Valley's flex and light industrial corridor compressing below 5% in recent cycles, driving rent growth in the $4.50 to $7.50 per square foot range for older warehouse product and pushing rents higher for modern logistics facilities. Retail is more bifurcated: grocery-anchored strip centers in the Tanglewood and Valley View corridors have held occupancy well, while unanchored neighborhood retail has faced the same structural headwinds seen nationally. Office, particularly suburban Class B product, remains challenged, with effective rents and occupancy levels that create real tension when sponsors and equity partners disagree about whether a refinance or disposition should have been executed earlier in the hold period.

What makes Roanoke particularly fertile ground for joint venture disputes is the deal size. Most institutional JV structures in this market involve equity contributions in the $3 million to $15 million range, with total capitalization rarely exceeding $30 to $40 million. That size band is large enough to justify sophisticated partnership agreements with tiered waterfall structures, preferred returns, and promoted interest provisions, but small enough that the sponsors executing those agreements are often regional operators who may not have had the same level of institutional guidance that larger coastal sponsors receive. The result is partnership agreements that borrow language from institutional term sheets without always capturing the operational precision required to implement that language cleanly. When distributions fall short of expectations and the deal is in or approaching litigation, the capital stack structure and the financing terms embedded in that structure are almost always central to the dispute.

The Engagement

Counsel representing the limited equity partner in a Roanoke multifamily joint venture retained Commercial Lending Solutions to provide expert analysis and, ultimately, a Rule 26 expert report addressing three core questions. The asset was a garden-style apartment community in the Roanoke Valley, stabilized at the time of the dispute, with a refinance that had been executed approximately 24 months after the original acquisition. The dispute centered on how the refinance proceeds had been distributed, whether the sponsor's preferred return calculation was correct under the governing operating agreement, and whether the promoted interest claimed by the managing member had been properly triggered given the actual returns delivered to the equity investor.

The retaining party's theory was that the refinance had been structured in a way that artificially inflated the sponsor's promoted interest by treating refinance proceeds as a return of capital before the preferred return hurdle had been fully satisfied, thereby pulling the promoted interest trigger prematurely. The opposing position was that the operating agreement's waterfall language supported the distribution methodology the managing member had employed. Our engagement required us to do three things: analyze the capital stack as it existed at closing and post-refinance, render an opinion on what market-standard JV waterfall mechanics would produce under the same facts, and assess whether the financing terms obtained in the refinance were consistent with what an arm's-length, market-responsive process would have generated, or whether the loan structure itself had been engineered in a way that served one party's interests at the expense of the other.

CLS CRE was retained specifically because counsel needed an expert who could address the financing mechanics, not just the accounting. An appraiser can opine on value. A CPA can trace distributions. What counsel needed was someone who could explain how lenders actually size debt on stabilized multifamily assets, how that debt sizing interacts with the capital stack, and whether the refinance proceeds available for distribution were consistent with what the market would have supported at the time.

The Financial Analysis

Our analysis began with a reconstruction of the property's net operating income at the time of the refinance. Working from the rent rolls, operating statements, and property management reports produced in discovery, we determined a trailing 12-month NOI and a forward-looking stabilized NOI, which is the figure that agency lenders and most conventional lenders would have used to size the refinance loan. For a stabilized garden-style multifamily asset in the Roanoke submarket at the relevant time, effective gross income was approximately $1.42 million, with operating expenses at approximately 42% of effective gross income, producing a stabilized NOI of approximately $823,000. That figure was the foundation for every loan sizing calculation in our report.

Agency lenders, specifically Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac small balance and conventional programs, represent the most competitive refinance execution for stabilized multifamily assets in markets like Roanoke. At the time of the subject refinance, agency lenders were pricing 10-year fixed-rate loans in the 5.25% to 5.75% range with 30-year amortization, subject to minimum DSCR requirements of 1.25x for conventional execution and 1.20x for certain affordable or structured products. We applied the 1.25x DSCR threshold as the market-standard constraint for market-rate multifamily, which is consistent with agency guidelines and with the underwriting standards applied by the regional bank lenders who also compete for this product type in the Roanoke market.

At a 5.50% note rate with 30-year amortization, the annual debt service on a hypothetical loan amount of $10.5 million would be approximately $715,000, producing a DSCR of approximately 1.15x. That falls below the 1.25x threshold, meaning the loan would not clear the DSCR test at that amount. Walking back the calculation, the maximum loan amount supportable at 1.25x DSCR was approximately $9.65 million, producing annual debt service of approximately $658,000 against the $823,000 NOI. We cross-checked that figure against the LTV constraint: at a capitalization rate of 5.75% applied to the stabilized NOI, the indicated value was approximately $14.3 million, and 65% LTV produced a constraint of approximately $9.3 million, which was the binding constraint in this case. The maximum supportable loan was therefore approximately $9.3 million, limited by the LTV test rather than the DSCR test.

The actual refinance loan obtained by the managing member was $10.1 million. Our analysis demonstrated that this loan amount was not supportable under the underwriting standards that would have applied to an arm's-length agency refinance. To achieve a $10.1 million loan, either the lender used an inflated NOI figure, accepted a cap rate below market, or structured the transaction through a lender with looser underwriting standards and commensurately higher pricing. Each of those scenarios has different implications for the waterfall: if the NOI was overstated to achieve a larger loan, the distributions flowing from that refinance were based on a financing that misrepresented the property's income to the lender; if a non-agency lender with above-market pricing was used, the equity investors absorbed higher carrying costs that reduced cash flow available for preferred return satisfaction. Our report addressed each scenario quantitatively.

The Complicating Factors

Preferred return accrual methodology. The most technically complex issue in this engagement was the operating agreement's treatment of preferred return accrual during the period between acquisition and the refinance event. The agreement specified an 8% preferred return on invested capital, but it did not clearly specify whether that return was simple or compounding, whether it accrued on the original equity contribution only or on the unreturned capital balance after any interim distributions, and how the quarterly cash flow distributions made during the operating period were applied, whether against the preferred return first or against return of capital first. These are not abstract drafting questions. In a deal with $6.5 million of LP equity at an 8% preferred return accruing over 26 months, the difference between simple and compound accrual is approximately $87,000. The difference in the waterfall position is material, and it directly determines whether the promoted interest trigger was met at the time of the refinance distribution.

Refinance proceeds as a capital event. Many operating agreements treat a refinance as a capital event that triggers the waterfall, while others treat it as a non-event for purposes of the preferred return and promote calculation, distributing only operating cash flow until a sale. The Roanoke agreement was ambiguous on this point, which is common in mid-market deals. From a financing standpoint, the consequence of that ambiguity is significant: a managing member who controls the refinance process can, intentionally or not, structure the timing and sizing of a refinance to influence whether the promote triggers before or after the LP's preferred return is satisfied. Our analysis quantified the financial impact of each interpretation and provided the court with the market-standard framework that institutional JV sponsors use to address this issue.

Lender selection and its effect on available proceeds. The managing member's decision to use a particular lender in the refinance, rather than pursuing competitive agency execution, resulted in a loan with higher fees and above-market pricing, which reduced the net proceeds available for distribution. A borrower in Roanoke pursuing a $9 to $10 million multifamily refinance at the relevant time had access to Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, FHA, and a competitive regional bank market. The difference in all-in cost between agency execution and the execution obtained was approximately 45 basis points in rate plus 1.5 points in origination fees, which reduced the equity investor's net distribution by approximately $210,000 on a net present value basis over the remaining hold period. The managing member had a fiduciary obligation under the operating agreement to pursue financing in the best interest of the joint venture. Whether the lender selection breached that obligation was a legal question for the court, but the financial quantification of the cost was within our scope.

Catch-up provisions and their interaction with debt sizing. The operating agreement contained a GP catch-up provision that, once the LP's preferred return was satisfied, allocated 100% of distributions to the GP until the GP had received a specified percentage of total profits. The sizing of the refinance loan directly determined how much cash was available to satisfy the preferred return at the refinance event. If the loan was undersized, less cash was available to clear the hurdle, the catch-up was deferred, and the economic allocation between the parties shifted materially. Our analysis modeled the waterfall under three loan sizing scenarios: the actual loan, the maximum agency-supportable loan, and the managing member's own underwriting model, demonstrating that the choice of loan size was not financially neutral with respect to the GP/LP economic split.

The Roanoke Lender Market

Attorneys and investors working in the Roanoke market need to understand that the lending landscape here is meaningfully different from Northern Virginia or Richmond. Roanoke is a regional market where community and regional banks carry real weight alongside institutional capital sources, and that dynamic affects both the financing options available in a deal and the standards against which a financing decision should be judged in litigation.

Agency lenders are active in Roanoke for multifamily assets above approximately $1 million in loan size. Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac small balance programs are competitive for properties in the $1 million to $7.5 million range, while conventional agency execution applies above that threshold. Both programs offer fixed and floating rate structures with standard 30-year amortization, LTV limits of 65% to 80% depending on the execution, and DSCR floors of 1.20x to 1.25x. Freddie Mac's small balance program in particular has been active in Virginia secondary markets and offers competitive pricing relative to the larger conventional execution. For a Roanoke multifamily asset seeking a market-standard refinance, agency execution should generally be the first call.

CMBS lenders are less active in Roanoke due to deal size constraints. Most CMBS programs require minimum loan amounts of $5 million to $10 million and prefer assets in markets with sufficient comparable transaction velocity to support the securitization underwriting. Roanoke multifamily and retail assets can qualify, but the CMBS execution is typically not the most competitive for assets under $15 million in total capitalization in this market.

Regional and community banks are perhaps the most important lender category in Roanoke for joint venture assets. Institutions headquartered in Virginia and the Mid-Atlantic region maintain active portfolios in this market across multifamily, industrial, office, and retail. These lenders typically offer recourse or partial recourse structures, floating or short-term fixed rate pricing, DSCR requirements of 1.20x to 1.30x, and LTV limits of 65% to 75%. Relationship lending still matters here, which means a borrower with an existing bank relationship may access better pricing or proceeds than a new-to-bank borrower, a distinction that matters when evaluating whether a managing member obtained competitive financing terms.

Debt funds and bridge lenders operate in Roanoke for transitional or value-add assets that do not yet meet agency or bank stabilization requirements. Pricing for bridge debt in this market is typically in the SOFR plus 275 to 450 basis point range, with 60% to 70% LTV limits and interest-only periods during the business plan execution phase. Bridge lenders evaluating Roanoke assets will underwrite market rent growth, absorption assumptions, and stabilization timelines specific to the submarket, and their willingness to lend is sensitive to the same supply-demand dynamics that affect cap rates and values.

What Attorneys Handling Roanoke CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to retain a CRE financing expert. If the dispute involves a capital event, a refinance, a forced sale, a capital call, a waterfall distribution, or a claim that a party failed to obtain market-standard financing, you need a CRE financing expert. An appraiser will tell you what the property was worth. A CRE financing expert will tell you how much debt the property could support, what terms the market would have offered, what the proceeds of that debt would have been, and how those proceeds would have flowed through a market-standard waterfall. These are different questions with different answers, and conflating them is a common and expensive mistake in commercial real estate litigation.

What a CRE financing expert can answer that an appraiser cannot. Appraisers are trained to opine on value using income, sales, and cost approaches. They are not trained, and are not typically qualified, to opine on lender underwriting standards, maximum supportable loan amounts, market-standard DSCR and LTV constraints, the difference between agency and non-agency execution, or how a capital stack interacts with a waterfall structure. A CRE financing expert with active market experience can address all of those questions and translate them into quantitative opinions that are directly relevant to the economic claims at issue.

What qualifies a CRE financing expert versus a retired banker. The distinction matters. A retired community banker can speak to how one institution underwrites, but cannot testify credibly about the national lender market, agency programs, CMBS execution, or the range of financing options available to a borrower in a specific submarket. A qualified CRE financing expert should have current, active engagement in commercial mortgage origination or brokerage across multiple lender types and deal structures, experience with the specific deal type and market at issue, and a track record of retained expert engagements in which their methodology has withstood scrutiny. At CLS CRE, we are active originators with approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, relationships with more than 1,000 lenders, and engagement in transactions across all 50 states. That market presence is not a credential to be listed; it is the foundation for opinions that a court can rely on.

What information counsel should gather before the engagement. To enable a rigorous expert analysis in a joint venture waterfall dispute, counsel should assemble the operating agreement and all amendments, the closing pro forma and actual sources and uses, rent rolls and operating statements for each year of the hold period, the loan documents from each financing event, the lender's underwriting file if obtainable, all distribution notices and supporting calculations provided by the managing member, and any communications between the parties regarding the refinance, capital calls, or distribution disputes. The more complete the financial record, the more precise and defensible the expert opinion.

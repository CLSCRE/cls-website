Expert Witness: CRE Partnership and Waterfall Disputes in Helena, MT

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Joint Venture and Partnership Dispute

The Helena Market Context

Helena, Montana occupies a distinctive position in the intermountain West commercial real estate landscape. As the state capital, the market is anchored by government employment, healthcare, and professional services, which creates a demand profile unlike the commodity-driven markets in Billings or the tourism-concentrated dynamics of Missoula and Bozeman. That stability has historically attracted joint venture capital into multifamily and mixed-use assets, precisely because government-sector tenancy insulates income streams from the boom-bust cycles that affect other Montana markets.

On the multifamily side, Helena has seen consistent rent growth in the 4% to 6% annual range over the recent cycle, with vacancy rates hovering in the 3% to 5% range across Class B and Class C product. Cap rates on stabilized multifamily assets have compressed into the 5.5% to 6.5% range depending on vintage and location within the submarket, reflecting institutional appetite for the income stability the capital city provides. Office fundamentals remain bifurcated: government-leased office space trades at tighter cap rates and commands premium debt terms, while private-sector office has softened, with vacancy in legacy product running above 12% in some corridors. Industrial and flex space near the airport and along the Interstate 15 corridor has attracted light manufacturing and logistics users, with vacancy tightening below 6% as regional supply chains have reorganized post-pandemic.

For joint venture investors, this market profile matters because the financing environment in Helena directly shapes what waterfall structures are viable. A preferred return of 7% to 8% is achievable when senior debt is priced correctly and the asset performs to underwriting. When debt terms are mispriced in the original JV agreement, or when the capital stack was structured with assumptions that did not reflect Helena market lending reality, the downstream effect on waterfall distributions and promoted interest calculations can be significant and is often the source of the litigation I am retained to analyze.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing the limited partner in a Helena joint venture dispute involving a multifamily asset in the central Helena submarket. The asset was a value-add apartment complex that had been acquired, renovated, and stabilized over a hold period of approximately four years before the joint venture reached an impasse over distribution calculations at a refinance event. The general partner had calculated and distributed proceeds using a waterfall methodology that counsel for the limited partner believed did not reflect the terms of the operative partnership agreement, specifically around the application of the preferred return, the calculation of the cumulative preferred return shortfall, and the threshold at which the promoted interest should have been triggered.

CLS CRE was engaged to answer three distinct financial questions. First, did the capital stack as originally structured, including the senior loan terms, leave sufficient net proceeds at refinance to fund a correct preferred return payout before the promote was calculated? Second, were the debt service obligations under the senior loan consistent with market-standard terms for a Helena multifamily asset of this profile, or had the loan been structured in a way that materially compressed available cash flow during the hold period, thereby artificially suppressing the cumulative preferred return funded through operations? Third, what would a market-standard lender have offered at refinance for an asset at the stabilized performance metrics in evidence, and how would that maximum loan proceed have affected the distribution waterfall relative to what the general partner actually calculated?

These are not appraisal questions. An appraiser can opine on value. A CRE financing expert is the appropriate professional to address how lenders would have underwritten the asset, what loan proceeds were achievable, and whether the financing decisions made by the general partner were consistent with market standards or reflected choices that advantaged one party over the other in the waterfall.

The Financial Analysis

The analysis began with a reconstruction of the asset's stabilized net operating income at the time of the refinance event. Based on rent rolls, historical operating statements, and Helena submarket rental data, I determined that the stabilized NOI for the subject asset was approximately $485,000 annually, after applying a market-standard vacancy allowance of 5% and underwriting management fees at 8% of effective gross income, which is consistent with property management costs in the Helena market for this asset type and size.

Using that NOI baseline, I applied standard debt service coverage ratio analysis to determine the maximum loan proceeds supportable at the time of the refinance. For stabilized multifamily assets in secondary and tertiary markets like Helena, institutional lenders and agency programs typically require a minimum DSCR of 1.25x at the qualifying rate. Some regional lenders will underwrite to 1.20x on strong assets with demonstrated occupancy history, but 1.25x is the appropriate market-standard floor for this analysis because it represents the conservative end of the GSE underwriting parameters that would have been applicable to a stabilized Helena multifamily asset at that time.

At a then-prevailing rate of approximately 6.75% on a 30-year amortization schedule, a 1.25x DSCR constraint on $485,000 of NOI yields maximum supportable annual debt service of $388,000. At that debt service level, the maximum loan supportable is approximately $4,850,000. I also applied an LTV constraint of 75%, which is the standard ceiling for conventional multifamily lending on a non-agency basis for this asset class in a market with Helena's liquidity characteristics. If the appraised value at refinance was $6,800,000, the 75% LTV ceiling would have produced a maximum loan of $5,100,000. The binding constraint is the DSCR test, which limits the loan to $4,850,000, representing an effective LTV of approximately 71.3%.

The significance of this analysis to the waterfall dispute is direct. The general partner had executed a refinance at $4,200,000, which was $650,000 below the maximum supportable loan under market-standard underwriting. That $650,000 gap in proceeds was material because the waterfall in the partnership agreement required that the preferred return shortfall accumulated during the hold period be satisfied from refinance proceeds before the promoted interest was calculated. A larger loan would have funded a larger preferred return payoff to the limited partner, reducing or potentially eliminating the promote distribution that the general partner had taken. Demonstrating the financing gap, and establishing through market evidence that a larger loan was achievable, was central to the limited partner's damages claim.

The Complicating Factors

Cumulative Preferred Return Calculation Methodology. Many JV agreements in the Helena market, particularly those drafted outside the state by national private equity sponsors, use preferred return language that is facially clear but operationally ambiguous. The dispute in this engagement centered on whether the preferred return was calculated on a simple return basis or a compounding basis, and whether the preferred return clock ran from the date of each capital contribution or from the closing date of the joint venture. These distinctions are not academic. On a $3,000,000 limited partner equity contribution with a 7% preferred return, the difference between simple and compound accrual over four years, and the difference between a contribution-date start versus a closing-date start, can represent $150,000 to $250,000 in cumulative preferred return shortfall. That delta directly affects when the waterfall crosses the promote threshold.

As a financing expert, my role is not to interpret contract language, but to establish what is standard in the market, so the trier of fact can evaluate whether the general partner's calculation methodology was consistent with how sophisticated JV participants in the Helena and broader Montana market structure preferred return mechanics. In my experience reviewing JV agreements across this region, compounding preferred returns with contribution-date accrual triggers are the market standard among institutional sponsors, while simpler structures appear more frequently in locally originated deals. The asset type and the sponsor profile matter when establishing the applicable market standard.

The Value-Add Renovation Period and Its Effect on Cash Flow Distributions. The asset was acquired as a value-add play, meaning a significant portion of the hold period involved reduced occupancy and capital expenditure rather than distributable cash flow. In Helena's multifamily market, value-add renovation timelines have run longer than originally projected for a number of assets due to contractor availability and supply chain constraints that affected the region through 2021 and 2022. When the renovation period extends, the preferred return accrues without being funded by operating cash flow. At refinance, the accumulated preferred return shortfall is larger than originally projected, and the question of how that shortfall is treated in the waterfall becomes the center of the dispute.

Lenders look at this dynamic from the other direction. A lender evaluating a refinance on a just-stabilized value-add asset in Helena will require seasoning of the stabilized occupancy, typically 90 days at 90% or better occupancy before they will underwrite to the stabilized NOI. If the asset has not yet demonstrated that seasoning, the lender will haircut the NOI to a trailing-period average, which reduces the supportable loan amount. I was asked to opine on whether the refinance timing chosen by the general partner was consistent with the optimal financing window, or whether a delay of six to nine months would have produced materially better loan proceeds and therefore a materially different waterfall outcome.

Construction Loan-to-Perm Conversion and Interest Reserve Treatment. Several JV agreements in this market type include an interest reserve funded from LP capital during the renovation period, which is treated as an additional capital contribution for preferred return purposes. In this engagement, the parties disputed whether draws against the interest reserve that were used to cover debt service during renovation constituted capital contributions that earned a preferred return, or whether they were simply a mechanism for funding the carry cost of the construction loan without preferred return eligibility. The answer to that question, applied across a 14-month renovation period, produced a six-figure difference in the cumulative preferred return owed to the LP at the time of the refinance distribution.

The Helena Lender Market

Understanding which lenders are active in Helena and what their credit parameters look like is essential both to the financing analysis in a JV dispute and to the broader question of what terms were achievable at any given point in the transaction lifecycle.

Agency Lenders. Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac multifamily programs are accessible for Helena apartment assets that meet minimum unit counts, typically 5 units or more for small balance programs. Agency pricing has historically been the most competitive rate available to Helena multifamily borrowers, and agency underwriting at 1.25x DSCR and 80% LTV on acquisition or 75% on cash-out refinance represents the ceiling of available leverage for qualifying assets. CLS CRE maintains active relationships with multiple agency correspondents who will consider Helena assets, though the secondary market liquidity in Montana can require slightly wider spreads than primary markets like Denver or Salt Lake City.

USDA Business and Industry Programs. For mixed-use or community-oriented commercial assets in Helena, USDA B&I programs represent a legitimate alternative debt source that is not always considered in JV underwriting. These programs can reach LTVs of up to 80% with government backing and are particularly relevant for assets with job creation or rural development characteristics. Their underwriting timeline is longer, which affects the timing analysis in a dispute context.

Regional and Community Banks. Helena has meaningful community bank depth, including institutions with strong Montana market presence and local credit decision authority. These lenders typically underwrite to 70% to 75% LTV on multifamily and commercial assets, price at spreads over the applicable treasury or prime index, and require personal guarantees from the principals in a JV structure. For a JV dispute, the question of which principal provided the guarantee and whether that guarantee obligation affects the equity distribution analysis is often relevant.

Debt Funds and Bridge Lenders. For value-add assets in renovation, non-bank bridge lenders and debt funds are the primary capital source. These lenders underwrite to as-complete and stabilized values, advance loan proceeds in draws tied to renovation milestones, and price at rates substantially above permanent financing, often in the range of 300 to 500 basis points over SOFR. The higher cost of bridge capital during the renovation period is a direct input to preferred return shortfall calculations and cash flow waterfall analysis.

What Attorneys Handling Helena CRE Disputes Need to Know

When a JV dispute in Helena involves waterfall calculations, preferred return mechanics, or claims that one party manipulated financing decisions to advantage themselves in the promote structure, you need a CRE financing expert, not just an appraiser. An appraiser tells you what the property was worth. A CRE financing expert tells you what loan was achievable, whether the capital stack was structured correctly, whether the debt terms were consistent with market standards, and whether the financing decisions made by the managing partner were in the range of reasonable business judgment or represented a departure from market practice that damaged the other party's economic position.

The following questions fall squarely within the scope of a qualified CRE financing expert and outside the scope of an appraisal:

  • What was the maximum loan proceeds achievable on this asset at the time of the refinance, given market conditions and lender underwriting standards in Helena?
  • Were the debt service assumptions embedded in the JV pro forma consistent with what lenders were actually offering for this asset type in this market at the time the JV was formed?
  • Did the senior loan terms, including the interest rate, amortization schedule, and prepayment structure, materially restrict available cash flow and thereby affect the cumulative preferred return shortfall?
  • Was the refinance timing optimal from a financing market standpoint, and if not, what loan proceeds would a market-standard refinance have generated?
  • Is the preferred return calculation methodology used by the GP consistent with market standard JV practice for this asset class and sponsor type?

A qualified CRE financing expert is not simply a retired banker or former loan officer. The qualification standard for Rule 26 expert work requires demonstrated active market participation, current lender relationship access, and the ability to reconstruct the financing market as it existed at the relevant time using contemporaneous rate data, lender underwriting criteria, and transaction comparables. At CLS CRE, our approximately $1 billion in transaction volume and 1,000-plus active lender relationships across 50 states allow us to provide that level of current and historical market grounding, including specific knowledge of Montana and Helena market lending conditions.

Before retaining a financing expert, counsel should gather the original JV agreement with all amendments, the executed loan documents for the senior debt, all draw requests and interest reserve activity, the property's operating statements for each year of the hold period, the rent roll at the time of any refinance event, and the general partner's waterfall calculation with supporting workpapers. The more complete the financial record, the more precise the expert analysis and the more defensible the expert's testimony under cross-examination.

Key Takeaways

  • Waterfall mechanics and promoted interest calculations are directly affected by the capital stack. A financing expert can quantify the relationship between debt terms, available cash flow, and cumulative preferred return shortfalls in ways that make the dispute numerically precise rather than conceptually abstract.
  • Helena multifamily fundamentals support institutional debt at 70% to 75% LTV and 1.25x DSCR. Any refinance analysis in a Helena JV dispute should be benchmarked against these parameters using contemporaneous market rate and term data, not hindsight assumptions.
  • The maximum supportable loan, not the loan actually placed, is the correct measure of financing capacity. When a managing partner refinances below the market-supportable loan amount, the downstream effect on waterfall distributions is a quantifiable damages question that a CRE financing expert is positioned to answer.
  • Preferred return calculation methodology is a market-standard question, not just a contract interpretation question. Expert testimony on how sophisticated JV participants in this asset class and market structure preferred return accrual provides critical context for the trier of fact.
  • Bridge loan terms during the renovation period are a direct input to cumulative preferred return calculations. The cost of carry during value-add renovation, and how that carry cost is treated under the JV agreement, requires expertise in both construction lending and equity waterfall mechanics.
  • Community bank depth in Helena means multiple financing paths existed for most asset types. An expert who can demonstrate that alternative lenders would have offered better terms, and that the managing partner's financing decisions were not market-constrained, strengthens the limited partner's position that damages resulted from discretionary choices rather than market limitations.
  • The timing of a refinance event is a financing market question, not just a business judgment question. A CRE financing expert can establish the optimal market window and the loan proceeds available at different points in the asset's performance trajectory, providing the factual foundation for arguments about whether the managing partner acted in the best interest of the venture.
  • Rule 26 expert qualification in CRE financing requires active market participation and demonstrable lender access. Counsel evaluating expert candidates should probe current transaction volume, lender relationships, and the expert's specific experience with Montana market conditions and Helena asset types.

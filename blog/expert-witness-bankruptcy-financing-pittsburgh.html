Expert Witness: CRE Financing Feasibility in Chapter 11 Proceedings in Pittsburgh, PA

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Bankruptcy Exit Financing

The Pittsburgh Market Context

Pittsburgh is not a secondary market in the dismissive sense that phrase sometimes carries. It is a structurally distinct market with its own capital stack dynamics, lender preferences, and asset class fundamentals that bear little resemblance to Philadelphia, Cleveland, or Columbus. For attorneys and courts handling Chapter 11 reorganization proceedings involving Pittsburgh commercial real estate, that distinction matters enormously, because financing feasibility analysis grounded in generic national benchmarks will produce conclusions that are either too optimistic or too pessimistic to survive cross-examination.

The Pittsburgh MSA has undergone a well-documented economic transition over the past three decades, shifting from a steel-dependent industrial base to a diversified economy anchored by healthcare, higher education, financial services, and an expanding technology sector concentrated in the East End and Oakland submarkets. That transition has created a commercial real estate landscape with sharply uneven fundamentals depending on asset class and submarket. Understanding those fundamentals is not optional context in a financing feasibility analysis; it is the analytical foundation on which every underwriting conclusion rests.

On the industrial side, Pittsburgh continues to benefit from regional logistics demand driven by its position within a day's drive of roughly 70 percent of U.S. population. Flex industrial and warehouse product in the Findlay Township corridor, Robinson Township, and Cranberry Township submarkets has maintained strong occupancy, with functional vacancy in institutional-grade facilities running below ten percent in most of those locations. Net rents on well-located distribution and flex product have trended upward modestly, and lender appetite for stabilized industrial assets in greater Pittsburgh has remained competitive through recent rate cycles.

The office market presents a materially different picture. Pittsburgh's CBD office vacancy has been elevated, with Class A availability exceeding twenty percent in certain blocks of the central business district following pandemic-driven demand contraction and several significant tenant contractions among major employers. Class B and Class C office in secondary Pittsburgh submarkets faces a structural demand problem that is not simply cyclical. Lenders pricing Pittsburgh office loans in the current environment are applying significant haircuts to in-place revenue, questioning lease duration, and in many cases declining the asset class entirely regardless of sponsorship quality.

Multifamily fundamentals in Pittsburgh have been more resilient. Rent growth has moderated from its 2021 to 2022 peak, but occupancy in well-located urban and suburban product has held. Cap rates for stabilized multifamily in the Pittsburgh market have generally ranged from the low fives to the mid-sixes depending on submarket, vintage, and unit mix, with Oakland and Shadyside commanding tighter pricing than outlying communities. This cap rate environment, combined with current debt costs, has compressed returns but has not eliminated financing availability for assets with clean operating histories.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by counsel representing the plan proponent in a Chapter 11 reorganization proceeding involving a commercial real estate asset located in the Pittsburgh metropolitan area. The asset was an income-producing property, specifically a mid-sized multifamily residential complex with ancillary retail space on the ground floor, situated in an established Pittsburgh submarket with demonstrated rental demand history.

The debtor had filed a voluntary Chapter 11 petition following a dispute with its senior secured creditor over a loan maturity and the inability to refinance in a rising rate environment. The Plan of Reorganization proposed by our client's principal would satisfy the senior secured claim at a negotiated payoff figure, fund cure costs on executory contracts, and provide a distribution to unsecured creditors, all funded through new first-mortgage exit financing. The confirmation hearing presented a contested feasibility question: could the property, at its current stabilized income level, support sufficient mortgage debt to execute the plan as proposed?

We were retained to deliver a written expert opinion addressing three core questions. First, what is the maximum first-mortgage loan amount that the property can support under current market underwriting standards? Second, what categories of lenders actively originate loans on assets held by Chapter 11 debtors, and what are their material requirements? Third, is the financing component of the proposed Plan feasible, not merely speculative, within the meaning applied in confirmation proceedings?

Our engagement produced a Rule 26-compliant expert report setting out our methodology, the data sources we relied upon, the calculations we performed, and our opinions. We were subsequently designated as a testifying expert and prepared for deposition and hearing testimony.

The Financial Analysis

The analytical starting point in any CRE financing feasibility opinion is the property's stabilized net operating income, and that calculation requires more rigor in a bankruptcy context than in a conventional refinancing because the court will scrutinize every assumption. We do not simply adopt the debtor's rent rolls or the creditor's appraiser's income projection without independent verification. We build the NOI from documented lease abstracts, rent comparable surveys in the relevant Pittsburgh submarket, historical operating statements obtained through discovery, and our own knowledge of expense ratios for comparable asset types in this market.

For this engagement, the subject property's gross potential rental income from residential units was calculated at approximately $1.42 million annually based on in-place leases and a market rent analysis for comparable units in the submarket. Ancillary retail income contributed an additional $84,000 annually under a stabilized occupancy assumption. We applied a vacancy and credit loss factor of seven percent to residential income and ten percent to retail income, consistent with what Pittsburgh-market lenders apply to mixed-use assets of this vintage in their underwriting models. We deducted operating expenses including property taxes, insurance, utilities in common areas, management fees at five percent of effective gross income, maintenance, and capital reserve contributions at $300 per residential unit annually. The result was a stabilized NOI of approximately $1.07 million.

From that NOI figure, we applied the debt service coverage ratio thresholds that define the current institutional lending standard. The market standard for conventional first-mortgage financing on stabilized multifamily with retail is 1.25x DSCR at the minimum, with most agency-aligned and CMBS lenders requiring 1.25x on the stressed or underwritten rate rather than the note rate. Some balance sheet lenders in the Pittsburgh market will close at 1.20x for well-located product with institutional sponsorship, but that is a ceiling, not a floor, and it requires compensating factors. We used 1.25x as our primary threshold and ran a sensitivity at 1.20x to establish a range.

At a 1.25x DSCR, the maximum annual debt service supportable from $1.07 million in NOI is $856,000. At a 6.75 percent note rate and a thirty-year amortization schedule, a figure consistent with the terms being offered by balance sheet lenders and life company correspondents for this asset type and market during the relevant period, $856,000 in annual debt service supports a loan of approximately $11.1 million. Applying the LTV constraint that governs conventional first-mortgage lending on this asset type, generally sixty-five percent for mixed-use and up to seventy percent for pure multifamily, and using the appraised value of $16.5 million established by a court-ordered appraisal, the LTV ceiling produces a maximum loan of $10.7 million to $11.55 million depending on asset classification. The DSCR-constrained figure of $11.1 million fell within that LTV band, confirming that debt service coverage, not value, was the binding constraint. That is the typical result in a Pittsburgh multifamily deal at current rates.

The Plan proposed a senior creditor payoff of $9.8 million inclusive of principal, default interest, fees, and costs. The analysis demonstrated meaningful headroom: the maximum supportable loan exceeded the required payoff by approximately $1.3 million before transaction costs, providing sufficient cushion to fund origination fees, legal costs, and partial reserves from loan proceeds without compromising feasibility. We documented this conclusion with a full sensitivity table varying rate assumptions from 6.25 percent to 7.50 percent and DSCR thresholds from 1.20x to 1.30x, showing that the plan remained executable across most scenarios and identifying the specific combination of assumptions under which it would fail.

The Complicating Factors

Bankruptcy estate status of the collateral. This is the single most significant complicating factor in any exit financing engagement, and it is the one that most sharply distinguishes this work from conventional loan brokerage. Many lenders will not originate a loan on a property that is in active bankruptcy proceedings, full stop. Their loan committees view the collateral as impaired by the automatic stay, the unpredictability of plan confirmation timelines, and the risk that confirmation fails and the asset reverts to contested status. This is not a negotiating position; it is a genuine credit underwriting concern grounded in past losses. The practical implication is that the lender universe for bankruptcy exit financing is materially smaller than the universe for conventional refinancing of the same asset. Identifying which lenders will actually engage, quote, and close on a bankruptcy estate asset is a core component of the expert's work, not a secondary consideration.

Condition of operating statements. Chapter 11 debtors frequently have operating statements that are unreliable, incomplete, or intentionally structured in ways that obscure actual property performance. Deferred maintenance, management disruptions during the case, tenant attrition related to uncertainty about ownership, and professional fee accruals that distort expense lines all create noise in the historical financials. Lenders underwriting exit financing will not simply accept trailing twelve-month or trailing twenty-four-month figures without significant adjustment. The expert must work through each line of the operating history with the same skepticism a lender's credit officer will apply, or the feasibility conclusions will not survive underwriting scrutiny.

Deferred capital expenditure. Properties in Chapter 11 frequently have deferred maintenance that accumulated before and during the case. In Pittsburgh's older building stock, this is particularly common: properties in neighborhoods like Lawrenceville, the South Side, or Bloomfield often carry brick repointing, roof, mechanical, and elevator capital needs that would require a lender to either reduce proceeds or require an escrow holdback. We addressed this by quantifying identified deferred capital items based on a property condition assessment and adjusting our loan sizing analysis to reflect the probability that a lender would require a capital reserve holdback of between $400,000 and $600,000 at closing.

Title and lien complexity. Bankruptcy proceedings generate layers of claims, liens, and encumbrances that create title insurance challenges. A lender providing exit financing needs a clear first-lien position, which means the court's confirmation order, the plan's lien release mechanism, and the title insurance underwriting must all align. In our experience, the title insurance component is often the last structural problem to get solved and can delay closing in ways that stress the financing commitment timeline. Attorneys handling these matters should engage title counsel early in the process, in parallel with the financing expert engagement, not sequentially.

The Pittsburgh Lender Market

The lender market for bankruptcy exit financing on Pittsburgh commercial real estate is narrower than the general Pittsburgh lending market but is not as thin as many attorneys and debtors assume. Identifying the right lender category for a specific asset, at a specific loan size, with a specific plan structure, requires current market intelligence that a generalist appraiser or accountant cannot provide.

Regional and community banks with Pittsburgh market presence. Pittsburgh has a deep community and regional banking infrastructure that is materially more active in local commercial real estate lending than the banking markets of many comparable-sized metros. Several regional banks headquartered in or with significant Pittsburgh operations have portfolio lending programs that can accommodate exit financing structures, provided the asset is stabilized, the plan is confirmed or near confirmation, and the loan sizing is within their hold limits. These lenders typically require 1.20x to 1.25x DSCR, price between 50 and 150 basis points over the relevant index depending on credit quality, and want recourse or a partial guarantee from the reorganized entity's principals. Their maximum hold exposure for a single credit is generally in the $10 million to $20 million range, which covers a significant portion of the Pittsburgh commercial real estate market by deal size.

Debt funds and bridge lenders. For assets where the operating history is too short, too disrupted, or too uncertain to satisfy a balance sheet lender's income underwriting, private debt funds filling the bridge lending role are the most likely execution path. These lenders price at materially higher rates, often in the eight to eleven percent range in the current environment, and at lower LTVs, typically fifty-five to sixty-five percent of as-stabilized value. However, they underwrite the business plan, not just trailing income, and they are structurally equipped to close on bankruptcy estate assets because their documents and their credit committees are designed for higher-complexity situations. The tradeoff is cost: the higher debt service reduces the plan's cash flow and may reduce distributions to unsecured creditors.

Life company correspondents and CMBS conduits. These execution channels are generally not viable for bankruptcy exit financing until the plan is confirmed and the property is fully stabilized, but they are relevant to the feasibility analysis as a refinancing path that the reorganized entity can pursue post-confirmation to replace higher-cost bridge debt. Including a credible permanent financing path in the feasibility opinion strengthens the plan's presentation to the court.

What Attorneys Handling Pittsburgh CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to retain a CRE financing expert. Retain a CRE financing expert, not after you have drafted the plan, but before you structure the financing mechanics. The financing feasibility section of a Chapter 11 plan is not boilerplate. If the financing assumptions embedded in the plan cannot survive scrutiny by the creditor's expert or the U.S. Trustee, the plan will fail confirmation, and the damage may be irreversible. Early expert involvement allows counsel to structure the plan around financing that is actually executable, not financing that looks plausible on a spreadsheet but cannot be placed in the market.

What a CRE financing expert can do that an appraiser cannot. An appraiser establishes value. A CRE financing expert establishes what a lender will actually do with that value. These are not the same question. An appraiser's capitalized value does not tell you how much debt a specific lender, using its own underwriting model and credit policy, will lend against that asset. It does not tell you whether any lender will originate on a bankruptcy estate asset. It does not tell you what rate, term, amortization, recourse structure, or reserves that lender will require. It does not tell you whether the loan can close within the plan's timeline. A CRE financing expert answers those questions, and in a confirmation proceeding, those are the questions that determine whether the plan is feasible.

What makes a CRE financing expert qualified. The relevant qualification is not a license or an academic credential; it is current, active participation in placing commercial mortgage loans in the relevant market. A retired banker who has not originated a loan in five years cannot reliably testify about current lender credit boxes, current pricing, or which specific lenders will engage on bankruptcy estate assets. An expert who actively brokers commercial mortgages, maintains current relationships with institutional and non-institutional lenders, and has closed transactions in the relevant market within the past twelve to twenty-four months can give testimony that reflects how the market actually behaves, not how it behaved in a prior interest rate regime.

What counsel should gather before the engagement. Provide the expert with the complete rent roll with lease expiration dates, three years of operating statements, the existing loan documents, the appraisal if one has been ordered, any property condition assessment, the proposed plan term sheet, and the creditor's proof of claim with supporting calculations. The earlier the expert has this information, the more useful the engagement will be. Experts retained three weeks before the confirmation hearing to validate conclusions that counsel has already committed to in the plan documents are in an impossible position.

Key Takeaways

  • Financing feasibility is a market opinion, not a mathematical exercise. The question is not whether a loan is theoretically possible given a capitalized value; it is whether specific lender types, at current pricing and underwriting standards, will actually commit to and close on this asset in this legal context.
  • Pittsburgh's lender market has genuine depth for the right asset types. Stabilized multifamily, well-located industrial, and mixed-use assets with demonstrated income can access institutional capital in Pittsburgh; distressed office, vacant retail, and speculative development cannot, regardless of what a plan proponent's projections show.
  • Bankruptcy estate status materially narrows the lender universe and should be treated as a threshold filter, not an afterthought. Any financing feasibility opinion that does not identify specifically which lender categories will engage on a bankruptcy estate asset is incomplete.
  • DSCR, not LTV, is the binding constraint in most Pittsburgh CRE exit financing situations at current interest rate levels. Plans structured around LTV capacity without DSCR validation are likely to fail underwriting.
  • Deferred maintenance and operating statement quality issues specific to assets in Chapter 11 require explicit quantification and adjustment in the expert's analysis; lenders will identify and penalize these issues,

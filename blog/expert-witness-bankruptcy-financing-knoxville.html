Expert Witness: CRE Financing Feasibility in Chapter 11 Proceedings in Knoxville, TN

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Bankruptcy Exit Financing

The Knoxville Market Context

Knoxville sits at an interesting inflection point in the Southeast regional economy. It is not Nashville. It is not Atlanta. But that distinction works in favor of lenders and borrowers who understand it, and it creates genuine complexity in financing disputes precisely because capital markets participants who do not know this market well tend to mischaracterize it as either a secondary market with thin liquidity or a tertiary market with lender caution baked in. Neither framing is accurate, and both can be misleading in a Plan of Reorganization proceeding where the question is whether the asset can support institutional debt.

Knoxville's commercial real estate fundamentals are meaningfully differentiated by asset class. The industrial sector has been the strongest performer, driven by proximity to Interstate 40, the Norfolk Southern rail corridor, and a manufacturing base that includes automotive supply chain and distribution activity tied to the broader Tennessee Valley industrial economy. Industrial vacancy in the Knoxville MSA has consistently tracked below 5%, and net asking rents for warehouse and distribution product have moved upward steadily, reflecting both demand from regional distributors and limited new speculative supply relative to larger Sun Belt markets. For a bankruptcy exit financing involving an industrial asset, this is a favorable starting point: a lender evaluating income stability will find a market with real occupancy support.

Multifamily fundamentals in Knoxville have remained broadly healthy, supported by enrollment-driven demand from the University of Tennessee and a growing healthcare and professional services employment base anchored by the University of Tennessee Medical Center and Oak Ridge National Laboratory's influence on the broader metro workforce. Cap rates for stabilized multifamily in Knoxville have generally ranged from the high 5% to mid-6% range depending on vintage, submarket, and unit mix, though transaction volume moderated through 2023 and into 2024 as rate increases compressed leverage. Retail and office are more nuanced. Suburban office in Knoxville, particularly single-tenant or credit-tenanted medical office, has held value better than the national office narrative suggests, while multi-tenant suburban office is impaired. Any expert engagement involving office collateral in Knoxville requires a granular submarket and tenancy analysis, not a top-down national commentary.

For purposes of a Chapter 11 exit financing analysis, understanding these market dynamics matters because lenders will price risk based on what they know, or think they know, about Knoxville. Part of what a qualified financing expert provides is a corrective to lender generalizations that could otherwise result in an underfinanced or unfeasible exit plan.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by counsel representing the plan proponent in a Chapter 11 reorganization proceeding involving a commercial income-producing property in the Knoxville market. The asset was a stabilized, multi-tenant retail and light service commercial property with an established rent roll, located in a suburban submarket of the Knoxville MSA that benefits from strong traffic counts and retail co-tenancy. The debtor had operated the property through the bankruptcy period, and the property had continued generating income, though some lease modifications had occurred during the case.

The core legal and financial questions presented to CLS CRE were the following. First, does the property's current income stream support first-mortgage debt sufficient to pay the secured creditor's allowed claim in full at confirmation? Second, what categories of lenders actively extend credit on assets encumbered by a bankruptcy estate, and what are their underwriting requirements? Third, what loan terms are commercially reasonable to assume in structuring the proposed plan, including rate, amortization, and loan-to-value? And fourth, is the financing structure described in the proposed Plan of Reorganization feasible under current capital market conditions?

These questions go directly to plan confirmation under 11 U.S.C. Section 1129, specifically the feasibility standard, which requires the court to find that confirmation is not likely to be followed by liquidation or further financial reorganization. A financing expert's analysis provides the factual and market foundation for that finding when the exit requires debt capital.

The Financial Analysis

The methodology begins with the income stream. For this engagement, we analyzed the trailing twelve-month and annualized forward net operating income using actual rent roll data, verified lease abstracts, and operating expense statements from the debtor's schedules and monthly operating reports filed with the bankruptcy court. NOI was calculated on a stabilized basis, meaning we excluded non-recurring items such as lease termination fees and applied a market vacancy and credit loss factor appropriate for this submarket and asset type, which we set at 7% based on comparable Knoxville retail properties with similar tenant profiles.

For this property, stabilized gross potential rent was approximately $780,000 annually. After applying a 7% vacancy and credit loss adjustment and deducting verified operating expenses including property taxes, insurance, management fees at 5% of effective gross income, maintenance reserves, and non-reimbursable operating costs, the stabilized NOI was determined to be approximately $520,000.

Debt service coverage ratio is the governing constraint in underwriting this type of asset. Market standard for conventional commercial real estate lending is a minimum DSCR of 1.25x for retail and mixed-use assets, though some lenders will underwrite to 1.20x for well-located, stabilized properties with credit tenancy. For this analysis, we applied the 1.25x threshold as the market-standard floor, which is appropriate and defensible for courtroom testimony. At 1.25x, the maximum allowable annual debt service is $416,000, which is $520,000 NOI divided by 1.25.

Rate and amortization assumptions are critical and must be grounded in the actual lender market at the time of the analysis, not rate conditions at loan origination or at the petition date. For this engagement, we assumed a fixed rate of 7.25% based on prevailing spreads over the 10-year Treasury for comparable non-agency commercial mortgage lending on secondary-market retail assets with this profile. We assumed a 25-year amortization, which is standard for this asset class. Using those inputs, the maximum loan amount that generates annual debt service of $416,000 at 7.25% on a 25-year schedule is approximately $5,400,000.

LTV provides the second constraint. For stabilized retail with a Knoxville-market tenant profile of this type, conventional and CMBS lenders generally cap proceeds at 65% to 70% LTV. The appraisal of record established a market value for the property that, when tested against a 65% LTV cap, yielded maximum loan proceeds of approximately $5,525,000. The income-based maximum of $5,400,000 was therefore the binding constraint, which is the expected result: DSCR governs when income is the tighter limiter, and LTV governs when value headroom is the tighter limiter. Here, the DSCR constraint was marginally tighter, but both tests confirmed that first-mortgage financing in the range of $5,400,000 was supportable, which was sufficient to satisfy the secured creditor's allowed claim under the proposed plan.

The Complicating Factors

Bankruptcy estate title and lender perception. The single most significant complicating factor in any bankruptcy exit financing is lender willingness to extend credit when the borrower is a debtor-in-possession or when the transaction requires court approval. Many conventional lenders, including regional banks and credit unions that might otherwise be competitive on a stabilized retail asset in Knoxville, will not originate loans on bankruptcy estate assets as a matter of policy. This is not a legal prohibition; it is a credit culture decision. Lenders perceive bankruptcy-connected originations as carrying litigation risk, title complexity, and reputational exposure. This materially narrows the financing universe, and an expert must be prepared to explain this to the court honestly, not defensively.

The practical resolution is to structure the plan financing as a post-confirmation closing, where the debtor has emerged and title transfers to a reorganized entity or to a purchaser under Section 363 or a plan of reorganization. Lenders who understand the mechanics of bankruptcy plan financing can close in this structure, but they require confirmation of the plan order, a clean title commitment, and often a 30-to-60-day seasoning period between plan confirmation and loan closing. The timeline must be built into the plan.

Lease modifications and rent roll integrity during the case. In this engagement, certain leases had been modified during the Chapter 11 period, including one rent deferral arrangement and one lease amendment that reduced base rent in exchange for a lease extension. Both modifications are legally permissible, but they create underwriting complications. Lenders will scrutinize whether the current rent roll reflects sustainable market rents or artificially maintained occupancy. For this property, market rent comparables in the submarket supported the conclusion that the modified rents were at or modestly below market, which was actually favorable: lenders are more comfortable with below-market rents than above-market rents because the former suggests occupancy stability rather than risk of rollover. The expert analysis documented this rent comparison explicitly.

Anchor tenant or major tenant rollover risk. A significant component of the property's income was attributable to a single larger-format tenant whose lease was scheduled to expire within 36 months of the proposed confirmation date. This single-tenant concentration created a near-term rollover risk that would be a primary concern for any conventional or CMBS lender. CMBS lenders in particular will typically require that a major tenant's lease extend at least three to five years beyond the loan maturity date. This structural issue required the expert to address two sub-questions: whether the tenant was likely to renew (supported by analysis of submarket retail vacancy, the tenant's operating history at this location, and comparable renewal activity in the Knoxville retail market), and what lender categories would accept this rollover risk. The conclusion was that CMBS execution was unavailable for this reason, and that the appropriate lender universe was limited to balance-sheet lenders, specifically community and regional banks and certain debt fund lenders who price rollover risk into the rate rather than declining to lend.

Operating history disruption. Bankruptcy proceedings, by their nature, create gaps and irregularities in operating performance. Lenders evaluate trailing income, and if the trailing period includes bankruptcy-related vacancies, legal expenses run through operations, or deferred maintenance expenditures, the underwritten NOI may be distorted. Our analysis normalized the operating history by separating non-recurring bankruptcy-related expenses from ongoing operational costs, and by documenting the pre-petition and petition-period NOI trajectories separately. This normalization methodology is standard in workout and distressed asset lending, and the expert report explained it clearly so that the court could understand why stabilized NOI, rather than raw trailing NOI, was the appropriate underwriting input.

The Knoxville Lender Market

Knoxville has a reasonably deep community and regional bank market, which is relevant to bankruptcy exit financing because these institutions are more likely than institutional lenders to make credit decisions on a case-by-case basis rather than through rigid programmatic credit boxes. Tennessee-chartered community banks and regionals with Knoxville presences have historically been active originators on retail, industrial, and multifamily assets in the $3,000,000 to $15,000,000 range. Their pricing for non-distressed, stabilized assets typically tracks at 150 to 250 basis points over the corresponding Treasury or swap index, and they generally require personal guarantees, a minimum DSCR of 1.20x to 1.25x, and LTV not exceeding 70% for retail. For bankruptcy exit financing, their principal concern is title cleanliness and post-confirmation borrower creditworthiness. When those conditions are satisfied, they are genuine participants in this market.

CMBS lenders, through conduit origination channels, are active in Knoxville for stabilized, long-tenured assets with credit-quality tenancy and lease terms extending well beyond loan maturity. For exit financing, CMBS execution is generally only available where the asset fully clears the rollover and credit concerns described above. When it is available, CMBS pricing in this market has ranged from 175 to 300 basis points over the 10-year swap rate depending on LTV and asset quality. CMBS typically does not require personal guaranty, which can be relevant in a plan context where the reorganized entity may not have sponsor creditworthiness.

Debt funds and bridge lenders are the most flexible category for bankruptcy-adjacent transactions. They will lend on assets with near-term lease rollover, transitional income, or title complexity, and they will accept a faster close timeline. Their pricing in the Knoxville market for this asset type ranges from 300 to 500 basis points over SOFR, with origination fees of 1 to 2 points, and they typically require a shorter loan term of 2 to 3 years with extension options. They are not a long-term financing solution, but they serve as a bridge to conventional refinancing once the asset stabilizes post-emergence.

Life insurance companies and agency lenders (Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac multifamily programs) are relevant for specific asset types. Life companies are active in Knoxville for high-quality industrial and retail assets in the $10,000,000-plus range. Agency execution is available for multifamily assets meeting program guidelines, and agency lenders are generally agnostic to bankruptcy estate history as long as the post-confirmation borrower entity is clean and the asset meets stabilization criteria.

What Attorneys Handling Knoxville CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to retain a CRE financing expert. If your client's position in a Chapter 11 proceeding turns on whether a Plan of Reorganization is feasible, and the plan requires new debt financing, you need a financing expert before the disclosure statement hearing, not after. The feasibility analysis informs the plan structure itself. Waiting until confirmation to address the financing question is a strategic error that leaves the plan vulnerable to objection.

What a financing expert provides that an appraiser cannot. An appraisal establishes value. It does not establish financibility. A property can appraise at a value that supports a particular loan amount on paper, while simultaneously being unlendable in the current market due to lease rollover risk, capital markets conditions, or asset-specific credit issues. The financing expert's role is to translate the appraised value and income stream into a specific, market-grounded conclusion about what lenders will actually do with this asset, at what terms, on what timeline, and from what universe of capital sources. That analysis is not contained in any appraisal, and the court cannot infer it from one.

What qualifies a CRE financing expert. The relevant qualification is active transactional experience in the current capital markets, not historical banking experience or academic credentials. A retired community banker who last originated loans in 2018 cannot offer a credible opinion about current CMBS spreads, debt fund pricing, or the current DSCR underwriting standards of institutional lenders. CLS CRE has approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, relationships with over 1,000 lenders, and active origination across 50 states. Our opinions are grounded in live market data and current lender dialogue, not historical memory.

Information counsel should gather before the engagement. Provide the rent roll with lease abstracts, trailing 12-month and year-to-date operating statements, property tax records, the appraisal, any existing loan documents, monthly operating reports from the bankruptcy filing, the proposed plan term sheet, and any lender correspondence already received. The more complete the data package, the more defensible the expert opinion.

How financing expert testimony is used in a Plan hearing. Expert testimony on financing feasibility is typically submitted via a written expert report under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 26, incorporated into the confirmation record. The expert may be deposed by objecting parties and may testify at the confirmation hearing. The testimony directly addresses the Section 1129(a)(11) feasibility finding. A well-constructed financing feasibility opinion that is factually supported and methodologically transparent will carry significant weight, particularly when it is the only direct evidence in the record addressing the question of whether the proposed plan financing is achievable in the current market.

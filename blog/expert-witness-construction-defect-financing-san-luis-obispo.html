Expert Witness: How Construction Defects Affect CRE Financing in San Luis Obispo, CA

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Construction Defect Impact on Financing

The San Luis Obispo Market Context

San Luis Obispo sits at an unusual intersection in California commercial real estate: it is a supply-constrained coastal market with a major university anchor, a robust tourism economy, and one of the most restrictive entitlement environments in the state. That combination produces CRE dynamics that differ meaningfully from inland Central Valley markets and even from adjacent Santa Barbara County. For attorneys and borrowers dealing with construction defect litigation on a commercial asset here, those dynamics matter enormously, because the financing feasibility analysis is not simply a function of national lending standards applied to a local asset. It is a function of what lenders actually think about San Luis Obispo's demand drivers, its limited competitive supply pipeline, and the constraints that affect both value and liquidity when a property carries legal and physical uncertainty.

Multifamily assets in San Luis Obispo proper trade at cap rates generally in the 4.25% to 5.25% range depending on vintage, unit mix, and proximity to Cal Poly. Vacancy in stabilized multifamily has historically run below 4%, though the student housing segment is more cyclical. Retail is a tale of two cities: ground-floor retail on Higuera Street or in the downtown core commands strong rents and low vacancy, while secondary strip retail further from the core faces more pressure. Industrial is the quiet outperformer in this market. With essentially no developable land left for new industrial product within the city limits and very limited inventory in the broader county, vacancy in San Luis Obispo industrial sits below 3%, and rents have moved materially over the past three years. Net asking rents on small-bay and flex industrial in this market now routinely exceed $1.50 per square foot per month on a triple-net basis for functional product. Office fundamentals are more challenged, as they are nearly everywhere in California, but medical office tied to the regional hospital system maintains demand. For construction defect disputes, this market context matters because a defect-impaired asset in San Luis Obispo loses value against a backdrop where the underlying demand fundamentals are strong. The financing damage is not just about the cost to cure. It is about the market's intolerance for uncertainty when quality, lender-ready inventory is already scarce and lenders know it.

The Engagement

Counsel for one of the parties in active construction defect litigation retained Commercial Lending Solutions to provide an expert analysis addressing a discrete but financially consequential question: how does the existence of alleged, documented, and disputed construction defects affect the commercial financing feasibility of the subject property? The asset in this matter was a mid-size mixed-use commercial building in the San Luis Obispo submarket, comprising ground-floor retail or light commercial space with upper-floor income-producing units. The property had been the subject of construction defect claims involving structural components, waterproofing systems, and mechanical infrastructure, with disputed repair cost estimates ranging across a wide band depending on the expert retained by each side.

Our engagement required us to answer four specific questions that sit outside the scope of a standard appraisal. First, what maximum supportable loan could a qualified borrower obtain on this property in its current alleged-defect condition versus a defect-cured, stabilized condition? Second, what conditions would lenders impose before agreeing to fund any loan at all on a property with known construction defects, active litigation, and disputed repair costs? Third, how do the defects affect the property's DSCR and LTV in the current lending environment, and what is the financing gap created by the defect condition? Fourth, what categories of lenders remain available to a borrower in this situation, and at what cost of capital? These are capital markets and underwriting questions, not valuation questions, and they require a different analytical framework and a different expert than a licensed appraiser provides.

The Financial Analysis

The analytical starting point was the property's stabilized net operating income. In the as-repaired, defect-free condition, the property's gross potential income was underwritten based on current market rents supported by comparable lease data in the San Luis Obispo submarket. For the mixed-use configuration in this matter, that produced a gross potential income figure of approximately $420,000 annually. Applying a vacancy and credit loss allowance of 7%, consistent with market conditions for this asset type and location, and accounting for stabilized operating expenses including taxes, insurance, management, and reserves, the defect-free stabilized NOI was underwritten at approximately $310,000 annually.

The construction defects affected that NOI in two compounding ways. First, certain portions of the building were not leaseable in defect condition, either because tenants had vacated citing habitability issues, or because the space could not be marketed while litigation was pending and physical conditions remained unresolved. This reduced effective gross income by approximately 18% from stabilized levels. Second, insurance costs on the property had increased materially following the defect claim being placed on record, and the property required above-normal maintenance expenditure to prevent further deterioration of affected systems. Those factors combined to reduce actual current NOI to approximately $218,000 annually against the stabilized benchmark of $310,000. That $92,000 NOI impairment is not a hypothetical. It is a real reduction in income that flows directly into the lending analysis.

For a conventional commercial real estate loan on a mixed-use asset in this size range, lenders apply a minimum debt service coverage ratio of 1.25x on the underwritten NOI. That is the market standard for this asset type with a senior bank or credit union lender in the current rate environment. Some SBA 504 structures drop to 1.20x on the project as a whole, but senior lien commercial real estate lenders on stabilized income property in California are generally at 1.25x for assets without complications. At an assumed interest rate of 7.25% on a 25-year amortization schedule, the constant for a fully amortizing loan is approximately 0.0869 annually per dollar of loan balance.

Working backward from the defect-free NOI of $310,000, the maximum supportable debt service is $310,000 divided by 1.25, which equals $248,000 annually. Dividing $248,000 by the annual loan constant of 0.0869 produces a maximum supportable loan of approximately $2,854,000, which we round to $2.85 million for the defect-free scenario. Applying a market LTV cap of 65% for this asset type and complexity level in the current lending environment, the implied stabilized value required to fully support that loan is approximately $4.38 million. That is consistent with cap rate analysis of the stabilized NOI at prevailing market cap rates for this submarket.

Now apply the defect-impaired NOI of $218,000. Maximum debt service at 1.25x coverage becomes $174,400 annually. Dividing by the same loan constant of 0.0869 produces a maximum supportable loan of approximately $2,007,000. That is a financing reduction of approximately $843,000 attributable directly to the NOI impairment created by the defect condition. That number does not include any haircut lenders apply to value because of the litigation cloud, the cost-to-cure uncertainty, or the construction defect escrow hold-back requirements described below. It is purely the income-side impact on debt capacity. When those additional lender constraints are layered in, the financing gap widens further.

The Complicating Factors

Active Litigation as a Lender Disqualifier: When a commercial property is the subject of active construction defect litigation, the overwhelming majority of conventional lenders will not originate a new loan or permit an assumption of an existing loan without the litigation being resolved or specifically structured around. This is not a matter of lender preference. It is a function of secondary market requirements and internal credit policy. Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, HUD, and most institutional conduit lenders have explicit policies that preclude loan origination on properties with pending material litigation affecting title, condition, or occupancy. Regional banks and credit unions operating on portfolio basis have more flexibility, but their credit committees will require a detailed litigation disclosure, outside counsel opinion on exposure, and typically some form of reserve or escrow holdback. The financing universe on an actively litigated property in San Luis Obispo narrows to portfolio lenders, community banks with local market knowledge, and in some cases private bridge capital, all of which carry pricing premiums of 75 to 200 basis points above equivalent clean-title loans.

Cost-to-Cure Uncertainty and Lender Holdbacks: Lenders do not fund against disputed repair costs. When defect experts on opposite sides of a case project repair costs ranging from, for example, $350,000 to $1.2 million for the same scope of work, a lender cannot underwrite to either number with confidence. The institutional response is either to decline the loan outright or to require a funded repair escrow equal to 125% to 150% of the high-side repair estimate, held in trust as a condition of loan funding. In this matter, a funded repair holdback at 125% of the high-side estimate would have reduced net loan proceeds to the borrower by approximately $1.5 million relative to what the same lender would advance on a defect-free asset. That holdback reduces borrower liquidity, increases the effective cost of capital, and in many cases makes the transaction economically unworkable at the loan amounts available.

Title Insurance and Endorsement Availability: Lenders require lender's title insurance as a condition of funding any commercial mortgage. Title underwriters in California become very cautious when a property is subject to construction defect litigation, particularly where mechanic's lien exposure, contractor cross-claims, or subcontractor disputes create subordinate lien risk. Several national title underwriters will issue a lender's policy subject to specific exceptions for the construction defect claims, which many lenders will not accept. Others will require affirmative coverage endorsements that may not be available until litigation is resolved. The absence of clean, exception-free title insurance is an independent lender disqualifier for most institutional financing sources, further compressing the available lending universe.

Insurance Carrier Scrutiny: Commercial property insurance on a building with documented construction defects in waterproofing, structural, or mechanical systems is subject to underwriter exclusions for known defect conditions. When a lender's insurance review reveals that the hazard policy excludes water intrusion damage because of known waterproofing defects, or excludes structural collapse risk because of disclosed structural concerns, the lender's collateral protection is materially compromised. Lenders require confirmation that insurance covers the collateral without carve-outs for known defect conditions, and that requirement frequently cannot be satisfied until after construction defects are repaired and re-inspected.

The San Luis Obispo Lender Market

San Luis Obispo is not a major institutional lending market in the way that Los Angeles or San Francisco are. That is both a constraint and, in some respects, a structural feature that benefits borrowers who have established relationships with local capital sources. The institutional conduit market, meaning CMBS lenders and agency executions, is active for stabilized, unencumbered assets that meet size and quality thresholds, generally above $5 million for CMBS and with strong occupancy and lease term requirements. Those lenders are entirely unavailable for defect-impaired or litigated assets. For those assets, the realistic lending universe consists of three categories.

Community and Regional Banks: Several community banks and regional institutions with Central Coast presences maintain commercial real estate lending programs that are portfolio-funded, meaning they make credit decisions internally without selling into the secondary market. These lenders understand the San Luis Obispo market, have comfort with local asset types, and can structure around complexities that a national lender cannot accommodate. Their credit boxes typically require 1.20x to 1.25x DSCR, 60% to 65% LTV on mixed-use or retail product, and personal guaranty for most borrowers outside of large institutional sponsors. For defect-impaired assets, they will typically require litigation disclosure, legal opinion, and repair escrows, but they will not categorically decline the way an agency lender would. Their pricing on a defect-complexity loan runs 50 to 100 basis points above their standard commercial real estate rate.

Credit Unions with Commercial Lending Programs: Several credit unions operating in the Central Coast region offer commercial real estate loans on an owner-occupied or investor basis. Their programs are relationship-driven and tend to be more flexible on structure than commercial bank competitors, though their credit appetite for defect-impaired assets varies significantly by institution. They are worth engaging in the right situation but are not a reliable primary source for complex defect scenarios.

Private and Bridge Capital: For assets that cannot qualify for institutional financing because of active litigation, title issues, or occupancy impairment, private bridge lenders provide a last-resort capital source. In the San Luis Obispo market, bridge capital currently prices between 10% and 13% interest-only, with origination fees of 1.5 to 3 points and loan terms of 12 to 24 months. LTV on bridge loans for impaired assets in this market is typically capped at 55% to 60% of as-is value. Bridge capital is an expensive, short-duration solution designed to allow a borrower to resolve the defect condition, stabilize occupancy, and refinance into conventional financing. The spread between conventional and bridge pricing represents a quantifiable cost of the defect condition that is relevant to damages calculations.

What Attorneys Handling San Luis Obispo CRE Disputes Need to Know

The question I am most frequently asked by litigation counsel is: when do I need a CRE financing expert as opposed to a commercial appraiser? The answer is straightforward in theory but often missed in practice. An appraiser tells you what a property is worth. A CRE financing expert tells you what a property can borrow, from whom, at what cost, and on what terms. Those are different questions, and they produce different testimony. In a construction defect case where damages include diminished financing capacity, lost refinancing proceeds, or forced recourse to bridge capital, the financing expert is the one who can quantify those impacts with precision. An appraiser who offers opinions on loan availability is exceeding their area of expertise, and opposing counsel should be prepared to challenge that testimony accordingly.

A qualified CRE financing expert should have current, active experience placing commercial loans across multiple lender categories. Expert testimony from someone whose lending experience ended a decade ago is not reliable testimony about what lenders require today. Underwriting standards, DSCR thresholds, LTV limits, reserve requirements, and lender attitudes toward litigation-impaired collateral have all evolved materially since 2020. The expert must be able to describe what lenders actually do in the current market, not what they did before the rate environment changed. At Commercial Lending Solutions, we maintain active relationships with over 1,000 lenders across all categories nationally, we close transactions in all 50 states, and we have approximately $1 billion in transaction volume that informs our opinions about what the market will and will not support at any given time. That is the type of active market engagement that produces credible, current expert testimony.

Attorneys should gather the following before an expert engagement: rent rolls and lease abstracts, operating statements for the trailing two to three years, the property's existing financing documents including any loan agreement covenants, the defect expert reports from both sides identifying specific systems affected and cost-to-cure estimates, any insurance claims filed, title reports showing recorded liens or lis pendens, and any lender communications if the property is already subject to an existing loan that may be in default or special servicing. The earlier in the litigation that financing expert analysis is engaged, the more useful it becomes for both discovery and settlement valuation.

At trial or in a binding arbitration, CRE financing expert testimony most commonly addresses three things: the quantification of financing impairment as a component of economic damages, the rebuttal of opposing expert opinions that understate or overstate financing impact, and the explanation to a judge or jury of how commercial lending actually works so that the damages analysis has proper foundation. In San Luis Obispo County litigation, where the commercial real estate market is specialized and the lender market is relatively concentrated, that market-specific knowledge is particularly valuable.

Key Takeaways

  • Construction defects reduce financing capacity through two compounding mechanisms: NOI impairment reduces maximum supportable loan on a DSCR basis, and lender credit policy restrictions on litigated or defect-impaired collateral further compress the available loan amount and expand required reserves.
  • The financing gap between a defect-impaired and defect-free asset in this analysis exceeded $843,000 on the income side alone, before accounting for lender holdback requirements, litigation premiums in pricing, or bridge capital cost differentials.
  • Active construction defect litigation disqualifies a property from agency, CMBS, and most institutional lending, forcing borrowers to community banks or private bridge capital at materially higher cost, and that cost differential is a quantifiable, compensable component of economic damages.
  • Lenders require funded repair escrows at 125% to 150% of the high-side cost-to-cure estimate, meaning that uncertainty in repair cost projections does not benefit the borrower. Lenders underwrite to the worst-case disclosed

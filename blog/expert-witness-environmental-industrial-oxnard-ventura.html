Expert Witness: Financing a Contaminated Industrial Property in Oxnard-Ventura, CA

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Environmental Contamination Analysis

The Oxnard-Ventura Market Context

The Oxnard-Ventura corridor occupies a distinctive position in Southern California's industrial real estate landscape. Situated in Ventura County between the Port of Hueneme, one of only two public commercial ports on the West Coast between Los Angeles and San Francisco, and the agricultural processing infrastructure of the Oxnard Plain, this submarket has developed a concentrated base of light-to-heavy industrial users including food processing, cold storage, aerospace components, chemical distribution, and agricultural supply chain operations. That industrial heritage is precisely why contamination disputes involving chlorinated solvents and emerging contaminants like 1,4-dioxane arise here with some regularity: these properties have histories of intensive chemical use going back decades.

Industrial vacancy in the Oxnard-Ventura submarket has hovered in the 3% to 5% range in recent years, reflecting the same supply-constrained dynamic that has tightened industrial fundamentals across coastal Southern California. New industrial deliveries have been limited by land scarcity, coastal zone regulations, and the competitive cost of ground-up development in Ventura County. Asking rents for functional warehouse and distribution product in Oxnard have ranged from approximately $1.00 to $1.35 per square foot per month on a NNN basis, with Class A logistics-oriented product at the higher end. Older industrial buildings, particularly those with deferred maintenance or functional obsolescence associated with prior chemical or food processing use, trade at a meaningful discount to that range. These rent dynamics matter enormously in an expert witness engagement because below-market leases in a tight submarket create both a financing constraint and a latent value argument.

From a capital markets perspective, Ventura County industrial assets are not primary gateway product. Institutional buyers and lenders who are aggressively active in the Inland Empire or the South Bay will sometimes step back to a more selective posture in Oxnard-Ventura, which means the effective lender universe for a complicated deal here is narrower than pure market fundamentals would suggest. That nuance matters when opining on financing feasibility in a contested matter.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by counsel representing a class of secured creditors in a regulatory enforcement proceeding involving an industrial warehouse property in the Oxnard submarket of Ventura County. The asset was a single-tenant, NNN-leased industrial building of approximately 85,000 square feet on a site of roughly five acres, occupied by a long-term industrial tenant under a lease with approximately seven years of term remaining. The in-place rent was meaningfully below current market, a condition that arose because the lease had been executed during a period of softer industrial demand and contained below-market annual escalators that had not kept pace with the surge in coastal Southern California industrial rents.

The property was the subject of a state regulatory enforcement action related to confirmed soil and groundwater contamination, specifically the presence of chlorinated volatile organic compounds and 1,4-dioxane, a probable human carcinogen that has drawn increasing regulatory scrutiny from the California Regional Water Quality Control Board and the State Water Resources Control Board. An active remediation program had been initiated under a regulatory oversight agreement, but the cleanup timeline was indeterminate and the final remediation cost estimate carried a wide range of uncertainty.

The central questions we were retained to answer were precise and practical. First, does the confirmed environmental contamination, in conjunction with the regulatory enforcement posture, categorically preclude first-mortgage financing on this asset in the current market? Second, if financing is available, what is the maximum supportable loan amount at prevailing market underwriting standards? Third, is that maximum supportable loan sufficient, on its own, to retire the existing senior secured debt in full? Counsel needed these conclusions expressed as expert opinions that could withstand cross-examination and, if necessary, support a cramdown or plan confirmation argument in a restructuring proceeding.

The Financial Analysis

The income analysis began with the in-place NNN lease. Because the tenant is responsible for taxes, insurance, and operating expenses under a true triple-net structure, the effective gross income and net operating income are substantially equivalent, subject only to a management fee deduction and a credit loss reserve. At an in-place base rent of approximately $0.92 per square foot per month on 85,000 square feet, gross annual rent was approximately $937,000. After applying a 3% management fee of approximately $28,000 and a 2% credit loss reserve of approximately $18,700 to account for lease rollover risk and re-tenanting exposure, we arrived at a stabilized NOI of approximately $890,000.

The environmental condition and the regulatory enforcement posture independently justified an additional lender-imposed risk adjustment. In our experience placing environmentally impaired industrial loans, lenders who will consider these transactions typically apply a haircut to NOI in their underwriting model, ranging from 5% to 15% depending on the severity of contamination and the certainty of the remediation timeline. For this engagement, we applied a conservative 10% environmental risk adjustment, reducing the underwriting NOI to approximately $801,000. This adjustment reflects how lenders actually think about income reliability on an impaired asset, not a theoretical construct.

On the debt service coverage ratio, the market standard for conventional first-mortgage financing on industrial collateral in California is a minimum of 1.25x DSCR for standard product. For environmentally impaired collateral with regulatory enforcement exposure, lenders who will engage at all typically require a minimum of 1.30x to 1.35x DSCR to compensate for the additional risk layers. We underwrote to 1.30x as the baseline coverage threshold.

For rate and amortization assumptions, we modeled a 7.25% note rate reflecting current market pricing for a 5-year fixed, 25-year amortization loan on a Ventura County industrial asset with environmental impairment. The 25-year amortization schedule is consistent with what institutional and regional bank lenders apply to industrial collateral of this vintage, and the note rate reflects a spread of approximately 200 to 225 basis points over the corresponding Treasury, which is representative of where environmentally flagged industrial transactions price when lenders are willing to engage at all.

The maximum annual debt service supportable at 1.30x DSCR on a $801,000 underwriting NOI is approximately $616,000 per year. At a 7.25% note rate on a 25-year amortization schedule, a monthly constant of approximately 0.7181% implies a maximum supportable loan of approximately $7,140,000. Applying a standard 60% LTV constraint (discussed further below) and testing against an estimated as-is market value of $10,500,000 for a below-market NNN industrial asset in this submarket, the LTV constraint produces a maximum of $6,300,000, which is the binding constraint. The DSCR-derived maximum of $7,140,000 exceeds the LTV limit, meaning the loan size in this scenario is LTV-constrained, not income-constrained. That is an important analytical distinction because it tells the court or creditors' committee that the income stream is adequate to service more debt than the collateral value alone will support, which is a relevant data point in evaluating the lender's risk posture and the creditors' recovery prospects.

The Complicating Factors

Environmental Impairment and Phase II Findings

Confirmed chlorinated VOC contamination, particularly when 1,4-dioxane is present, creates a specific set of lender concerns that go beyond simple stigma. 1,4-dioxane is not effectively remediated by conventional pump-and-treat or soil vapor extraction systems that handle chlorinated solvents like trichloroethylene or perchloroethylene. It is highly mobile in groundwater, resistant to biodegradation, and expensive to address using advanced oxidation processes. Remediation timelines for 1,4-dioxane plumes are frequently measured in decades, and final cost estimates carry uncertainty ranges that can be multiples of the preliminary estimate. Lenders who will consider financing an environmentally impaired property are generally willing to engage when there is a defined remediation program with a responsible party, a regulatory oversight agreement in place, and a credible cost-to-complete estimate. When the contaminant is 1,4-dioxane with an indeterminate remediation timeline, the pool of willing lenders contracts significantly.

Regulatory Enforcement Posture

An active regulatory enforcement action is categorically different from a voluntary site investigation or a pre-enforcement notice of violation. The California Regional Water Quality Control Board has authority to issue cleanup and abatement orders that can impose financial obligations senior to or competitive with a first mortgage lien. Institutional lenders underwriting to secondary market standards treat a property subject to an active enforcement action as essentially unfinanceable until the regulatory posture resolves to a monitoring-only or case closure status. Portfolio lenders, including some regional banks and credit unions, have more flexibility but require environmental indemnities, specialized environmental insurance, and often a funded remediation escrow that reduces proceeds to the borrower.

Below-Market Lease and Rollover Risk

The below-market NNN lease creates a compounding problem. The in-place NOI understates the economic potential of the asset, which is favorable from an income perspective, but lenders underwrite to the contractual in-place income stream, not to a pro forma market rent. The gap between in-place rent of approximately $0.92 per square foot per month and market rent of approximately $1.10 to $1.20 per square foot per month represents approximately $160,000 to $230,000 of annual NOI upside that no conventional lender will credit. Additionally, the question of whether the existing tenant will renew at market rates, accept an assignment to a new owner following a foreclosure or sale, or vacate and expose the property to the re-leasing market with a contamination cloud hanging over the site, is a genuine credit risk that lenders price into their LTV and coverage requirements.

LTV Constraints on Impaired Industrial Collateral

Standard LTV for industrial collateral in California is 65% to 70% for a clean asset with a creditworthy tenant. Confirmed environmental impairment with active regulatory enforcement drives that ceiling down to 55% to 60% in the best case, and some lenders impose a hard 50% limit regardless of income coverage. The valuation baseline for LTV purposes is also contested terrain: a lender appraisal on a contaminated site will typically reflect an as-impaired value net of estimated remediation costs, which can reduce the appraised value by 20% to 40% relative to a clean comparable. That appraisal dynamic directly compresses the maximum supportable loan and is a specific area where expert testimony from a financing professional adds precision that an appraiser's report alone cannot provide.

The Oxnard-Ventura Lender Market

The lender universe for this type of asset in this submarket is genuinely specialized. National CMBS conduit lenders are not a viable execution channel for environmentally impaired industrial collateral: CMBS pooling and servicing agreements prohibit loans secured by properties with known environmental conditions that have not been fully remediated and closed, and the securitization disclosure requirements make conduit execution a non-starter in a regulatory enforcement scenario.

Life insurance companies, which are active lenders on high-quality industrial product in coastal California, apply environmental screens in their preliminary underwriting that eliminate contaminated properties before the application stage. A property with active chlorinated VOC and 1,4-dioxane contamination under regulatory oversight will not get past a life company's environmental due diligence checklist.

The realistic lender categories for this scenario are debt funds specializing in transitional and impaired commercial real estate, certain regional and community banks with Ventura County market presence and portfolio lending flexibility, and occasionally credit unions with California industrial CRE programs. Debt funds active in Southern California impaired industrial lending will typically price at 300 to 450 basis points over SOFR on a floating-rate structure, require personal recourse or environmental indemnification from the sponsoring entity, mandate a funded environmental remediation reserve, and apply 55% to 60% LTV constraints. Their execution timelines are faster than bank alternatives, but their pricing reflects the risk premium. Regional bank lenders with Ventura County industrial experience will price more favorably, typically at 200 to 275 basis points over an index on a 5-year fixed structure, but their credit committee approval process for an impaired asset with active enforcement will be lengthy and require extensive environmental documentation, including a lender-commissioned Phase II, a remediation cost estimate from a certified environmental professional, and evidence of an approved remediation work plan.

Community banks with Ventura County deposit franchises have financed industrial properties in the Oxnard submarket but generally lack the specialized environmental underwriting capacity for a complex contaminated site. They are not a reliable execution channel for this type of transaction but may be relevant in evaluating the broader lender market for comparative purposes in an expert report.

What Attorneys Handling Oxnard-Ventura CRE Disputes Need to Know

A CRE financing expert and a commercial real estate appraiser answer fundamentally different questions, and conflating the two roles is a common and costly error in complex CRE litigation. An appraiser determines market value. A CRE financing expert determines what the debt markets will actually do with that asset at that value, under current market conditions, for a borrower with a defined credit profile. In a cramdown, plan confirmation, or secured creditor adequacy of protection dispute, the relevant question is often not what the property is worth, but whether a hypothetical refinancing transaction could generate loan proceeds sufficient to satisfy a specific debt obligation. That is a capital markets question, not a valuation question.

Attorneys should retain a CRE financing expert when the dispute involves any of the following: whether refinancing proceeds are sufficient to pay off existing debt; whether a proposed plan of reorganization is feasible based on assumed refinancing; whether a lender acted within market standards in denying a loan modification or extension; what standard market DSCR, LTV, and underwriting terms apply to a given property type; or whether an environmental condition categorically precludes financing versus merely constraining it.

A qualified CRE financing expert is not simply a retired bank officer or an appraiser with capital markets commentary bolted on. The relevant qualification is active, current engagement in originating or placing commercial mortgage debt across multiple lender types, in the relevant asset class and geography, at the relevant complexity level. Expert testimony that reflects what underwriting standards looked like five years ago, or that is drawn entirely from CMBS guidelines without accounting for portfolio lender flexibility, will not survive cross-examination by counsel who has done the work.

Before engaging a CRE financing expert in an Oxnard-Ventura industrial contamination matter, counsel should gather the following: the current rent roll and lease abstracts including all NNN provisions and renewal options; the most recent Phase I and Phase II environmental site assessments; any regulatory correspondence, cleanup and abatement orders, or oversight agreements; the current loan documents including any environmental representations; the property's operating history and any existing appraisal; and any prior financing term sheets or lender correspondence regarding environmental conditions. This documentation allows the expert to render specific, well-supported opinions rather than general market commentary, which is the difference between testimony that influences the outcome and testimony that the court discounts.

At trial or in a plan confirmation hearing, CRE financing expert testimony is most effective when it is anchored to specific underwriting calculations, identified lender categories with described credit parameters, and clear reasoning that connects market standards to the specific facts of the asset. Judges and arbitrators handling complex commercial matters are sophisticated enough to distinguish between an expert who has actually placed loans of this type and one who is offering theoretical commentary. The specificity of the financial analysis, including the NOI derivation, the DSCR test, and the LTV constraint calculation, is what gives the opinion its evidentiary weight.

Key Takeaways

  • Environmental contamination does not categorically preclude first-mortgage financing, but it radically narrows the lender universe. Confirmed chlorinated VOC and 1,4-dioxane contamination under active regulatory enforcement eliminates CMBS, life companies, and most conventional bank lenders. The viable channel contracts to debt funds and selective portfolio lenders with specialized environmental underwriting capacity.
  • The maximum supportable loan on an environmentally impaired industrial asset in Oxnard-Ventura is constrained by both DSCR and LTV, and LTV is typically the binding constraint. At 55% to 60% LTV on an as-impaired appraised value, the loan ceiling can be substantially lower than income coverage alone would support, a distinction with direct implications for creditor recovery analysis.
  • Below-market NNN leases compound the financing impairment. Lenders underwrite to in-place contractual income, not to market rent upside. The income gap between in-place and market rent is invisible to conventional underwriting, which understates the asset's economic potential while simultaneously depressing the supportable loan amount.
  • 1,4-dioxane contamination is a category-specific problem that most lenders will not underwrite around.

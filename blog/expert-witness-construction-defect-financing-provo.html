Expert Witness: How Construction Defects Affect CRE Financing in Provo, UT

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Construction Defect Impact on Financing

The Provo Market Context

Provo's commercial real estate market is not a secondary market in the dismissive sense that term is sometimes used. The Provo-Orem metropolitan statistical area has undergone a structural transformation over the past decade, driven by the emergence of what is commonly called "Silicon Slopes," the technology and software corridor that runs along the Wasatch Front from Salt Lake City south through Lehi, American Fork, and into Provo itself. That transformation has created genuine institutional-grade demand for commercial product, including multifamily, flex-industrial, office, and mixed-use assets, that did not exist in the same volume fifteen years ago. Understanding that context is essential before any analysis of construction defect financing impact can be credible in this market.

On the industrial side, Utah County's vacancy rates have remained tight relative to national benchmarks, generally tracking in the 4% to 7% range for well-located flex and light-industrial product, with asking rents for industrial space in the Provo submarket running between $10.00 and $15.00 per square foot NNN depending on clear height, dock configuration, and proximity to I-15 interchange access. New construction deliveries have added inventory, but net absorption has kept pace with supply in most quarters through the recent cycle. For multifamily, cap rates in the Provo core and immediately surrounding submarkets have compressed into the 4.75% to 5.75% range for stabilized product, reflecting both institutional capital interest and the enrollment-driven demand generated by Brigham Young University, one of the largest private universities in the United States with enrollment exceeding 33,000 students. Office fundamentals are more nuanced: tech-tenanted creative office has outperformed, while traditional professional office has experienced the same softening visible nationally, with vacancy in some Provo office corridors reaching the low to mid-teens.

What makes this market particularly relevant in a construction defect financing dispute is the fact that Provo has attracted an increasingly sophisticated lender base. Regional banks, credit unions with commercial lending authority, life companies, debt funds, and even some agency lenders active through the multifamily programs all compete for well-underwritten Provo deals. That means the financing feasibility analysis is not a simple binary question of whether money is available. The real question is which capital sources remain viable when a defect is disclosed, at what cost, under what structural requirements, and with what reduction in maximum loan proceeds. Those are the questions that drive damages and settlement value in construction defect litigation.

The Engagement

Counsel retained Commercial Lending Solutions to provide expert financing analysis in connection with a construction defect dispute involving a recently completed commercial property in the Provo submarket. The asset was a mid-size mixed-use development combining ground-floor retail and restaurant space with upper-floor multifamily residential units, a product type that has become increasingly common in the Provo core as the city pursues transit-oriented and walkable commercial development consistent with its general plan objectives. The alleged defects involved structural and waterproofing issues at the building envelope, reported water intrusion affecting multiple residential units and the shared parking structure, and HVAC system deficiencies affecting the commercial tenant spaces.

Counsel's core questions were threefold. First, what would a fully stabilized, defect-free version of this asset support in terms of permanent financing, both in loan amount and loan-to-value, given current Provo lender market conditions? Second, how do the disclosed defects actually affect a lender's underwriting, and what specific adjustments do lenders make to income, value, and loan sizing when known construction defects are present at origination? Third, what is the measurable economic difference between the financing available to a defect-free asset and the financing available to the subject property in its impaired condition, expressed as a dollar-amount reduction in maximum supportable loan proceeds? That third question is where the damages analysis lives, and it is precisely the question that an appraiser, operating within the scope of a standard USPAP-compliant appraisal, is not positioned to fully answer.

CLS CRE was retained by counsel representing the property owner asserting damages against the contractor and its surety. Our role was to develop an independent, methodology-driven analysis of financing feasibility and financing impairment, suitable for use as a Rule 26 expert report and potential trial testimony.

The Financial Analysis

The analysis began with the property's stabilized income stream. For a mixed-use asset of this type in Provo, stabilized occupancy assumptions were built from market comparables rather than from the subject property's actual in-place occupancy, because the defects had materially depressed leasing velocity. For the residential component, market rents for comparable Provo urban-core multifamily units were applied at a 95% economic occupancy, consistent with the submarket's demonstrated absorption characteristics. The commercial ground-floor component was underwritten at a rent-per-square-foot consistent with recent Provo retail and food-and-beverage leases in comparable mixed-use settings, at a more conservative 92% economic occupancy to reflect the retail market's current softness and the additional leasing risk associated with a building with known disclosed defect history.

For this illustrative analysis, assume a property generating stabilized gross potential rent of approximately $1,650,000 annually across both components. After applying vacancy and credit loss at a blended 7%, effective gross income lands at approximately $1,534,500. Operating expenses, including management, insurance, reserves, utilities for common areas, and real estate taxes based on Utah County assessor values, were modeled at approximately $540,000, producing a stabilized net operating income of roughly $994,500 for the defect-free scenario.

Lenders underwriting permanent loans on stabilized mixed-use assets in Provo today are generally pricing at spreads of 175 to 250 basis points over the corresponding Treasury or SOFR benchmark, depending on lender type and credit quality. For a 10-year fixed-rate loan with a 30-year amortization schedule, an all-in rate assumption of 7.00% to 7.25% is defensible as of the relevant analysis period. At 7.125% on a 30-year amortization, the annual debt service constant is approximately 0.08076, meaning each $1,000,000 of loan generates roughly $80,760 of annual debt service.

The debt service coverage ratio threshold is the critical gating mechanism. Industry standard for mixed-use assets with a commercial component is 1.25x DSCR at the floor, with many conventional lenders and life companies requiring 1.30x for assets with meaningful retail exposure given current retail credit uncertainty. At 1.25x DSCR applied to the $994,500 NOI, the maximum annual debt service supportable is $795,600. Dividing by the 0.08076 constant yields a maximum supportable loan of approximately $9,850,000 on an income basis for the defect-free asset.

Simultaneously, LTV constrains the loan independently. For mixed-use assets in Provo, conventional lenders are currently underwriting at 65% to 70% LTV for stabilized product. If the defect-free appraised value is $15,500,000 reflecting a 6.4% cap rate applied to the stabilized NOI, a 65% LTV ceiling produces a maximum loan of $10,075,000. The income constraint at $9,850,000 governs, and this becomes the defect-free maximum supportable loan for purposes of the damages analysis.

Now apply the defect adjustments. Lenders who are willing to underwrite a property with disclosed waterproofing and structural envelope defects will make several simultaneous adjustments. First, they will apply a vacancy and credit loss haircut to both components, typically adding 300 to 500 basis points of additional vacancy above market to reflect lease-up risk and tenant retention risk associated with the known defect history. On this asset, that adjustment reduces stabilized EGI by approximately $75,000 to $95,000. Second, lenders will require a funded reserve held in escrow to cover estimated remediation costs, typically sized at 110% to 125% of the engineer's repair estimate. That reserve is funded from loan proceeds, reducing net proceeds available to the borrower by the full reserve amount. Third, lenders will apply a stressed DSCR floor, often 1.35x to 1.40x rather than 1.25x, to compensate for the perceived income uncertainty. Applying a 1.35x DSCR to the adjusted NOI of approximately $890,000 (reflecting the haircut), the maximum supportable debt service drops to $659,000, producing a maximum supportable loan of approximately $8,160,000 before the reserve holdback. If the remediation reserve is sized at $750,000 (reflecting a $600,000 engineer estimate at 125%), net loan proceeds to the borrower are approximately $7,410,000. The financing impairment is therefore approximately $2,440,000 in net proceeds, representing a measurable, quantifiable economic loss directly attributable to the defects' impact on financing feasibility.

The Complicating Factors

Lender disclosure requirements and "known defect" flags. Once construction defect litigation is filed and a lis pendens is recorded, every institutional lender's pre-application due diligence will surface the pending litigation through title search. At that point, the lender's credit committee is required to flag the loan as involving litigation risk. Most conventional lenders and all life companies have credit policy provisions that either prohibit originating loans on properties subject to active construction defect litigation or require full senior credit officer sign-off with litigation counsel opinion letters as a condition of commitment. The practical effect is that a large portion of the conventional lender universe is simply unavailable until the litigation resolves, is settled, or is dismissed. This dramatically concentrates the available lender market into debt funds, private bridge lenders, and a subset of relationship-driven community and regional banks, all of which price at materially higher rates than conventional lenders.

Insurance gap risk. Lenders on any commercial asset require evidence of property and casualty insurance at levels sufficient to cover replacement cost. When construction defects involve the building envelope, structural systems, or mechanical systems, property insurers frequently either exclude the defect-related systems from coverage, impose higher deductibles, or decline to renew coverage at prior terms. A lender reviewing a commitment for a property with active coverage exclusions related to the defect will require gap insurance or a funded reserve to cover the excluded exposure. In Provo, where the construction insurance market for complex mixed-use products is thinner than in Salt Lake City, obtaining gap coverage at a reasonable premium is itself a transaction execution risk that affects financing certainty.

Appraisal subjectivity on impaired assets. Lenders order their own appraisals; they do not rely on the borrower's or the plaintiff's appraiser. When a property has known construction defects and active litigation, the appraiser is required under USPAP to address the "as-is" value with the defects in place, the "as-repaired" value assuming successful remediation, and the impact of the pending litigation cloud on marketability. Lenders will underwrite to the lower of the two values in most credit policies. The spread between as-is and as-repaired values in our analysis was significant enough to create an LTV ceiling that was more constraining than the income-based limit under the defect scenario, compressing the maximum supportable loan further than the income analysis alone would suggest.

Remediation timeline and construction completion risk. If the remediation has not yet commenced, a lender considering a loan on the property is effectively also underwriting a construction risk on top of the permanent loan credit risk. Lenders treat unremediated defects similarly to incomplete construction: they will require a completion guarantee, a funded holdback sized to cover remediation plus a contingency, and a mechanism for releasing the holdback upon satisfactory third-party inspection and sign-off. Negotiating those mechanics adds cost, complexity, and closing timeline, all of which are quantifiable impairments to the financing that a defect-free asset would not face.

The Provo Lender Market

Provo benefits from a genuinely diverse lender ecosystem, which makes the financing impairment analysis more nuanced than it would be in a smaller or more isolated market. For stabilized, defect-free commercial assets in Provo, the market includes regional and national banks with Utah commercial lending operations, several large credit unions domiciled in Utah County that have robust commercial real estate lending programs and competitive rates on well-underwritten deals, life insurance company correspondents who are active in Utah for stabilized multifamily and industrial product meeting their quality thresholds, CMBS conduit lenders for deals in the $5,000,000 and above range with sufficient NOI stability, and agency lenders for the multifamily component of qualifying mixed-use deals.

For a property with disclosed construction defects and active litigation, that lender universe collapses significantly. Life companies will not originate. CMBS conduit lenders cannot securitize a loan on a property with pending construction defect litigation because the litigation represents an unresolvable exception to title that makes the loan unsaleable on the secondary market. Many regional banks will require litigation resolution as a pre-condition to commitment. What remains is the community bank and credit union segment, for borrowers with existing relationship depth at those institutions, and the private debt fund market.

Debt funds active in the Intermountain West and accessible to Provo borrowers will price bridge loans on impaired assets at rates ranging from 9.50% to 12.00% or higher depending on leverage, complexity, and sponsor strength, compared to the 7.00% to 7.25% conventional permanent rate range. At a 10.50% bridge rate on a 3-year term with a 2-year extension option, the annual debt constant increases substantially, the maximum supportable loan under the same DSCR test shrinks, and the all-in cost of capital to the borrower rises in a way that is directly quantifiable as an economic impairment attributable to the defect-driven loss of conventional financing access.

What Attorneys Handling Provo CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to retain a CRE financing expert. Retain a financing expert early, not after the appraisal is complete. The appraiser will establish value. The financing expert establishes what the market will actually lend against that value, and those are different questions. In construction defect cases, breach of contract disputes, partnership dissolution matters, and bankruptcy plan confirmation hearings, the financing feasibility analysis frequently controls the damages number, the reorganization plan's confirmability, or the settlement range. Waiting until the eve of trial to engage a financing expert means the expert does not have sufficient time to develop a defensible, methodology-grounded report.

What a financing expert answers that an appraiser cannot. An appraiser under USPAP is tasked with estimating value. A CRE financing expert is tasked with answering operational capital markets questions: what would a lender actually commit to on this asset, at what terms, under these specific conditions, and how does that change when the defect is present? The expert draws on active lender relationships, current credit box knowledge, term sheet experience, and underwriting methodology to quantify financing feasibility. That testimony is not replicable from an appraisal report, and courts have recognized this distinction in allowing both categories of experts to testify without finding one cumulative of the other.

Qualifying a CRE financing expert versus a retired banker. A retired banker testifying about lending standards is testifying about historical institutional practice at a single institution. A qualified CRE financing expert should have active, current engagement across multiple lender types, documented transaction volume (our firm has placed approximately $1 billion in transaction volume across 50 states, maintaining relationships with 1,000 or more active lenders), and demonstrated familiarity with current underwriting criteria across the full capital stack. The expert should also have experience preparing Rule 26-compliant reports and withstanding Daubert scrutiny on methodology.

Information counsel should gather before engaging the expert. Provide the expert with the following at the outset: the engineer's or architect's defect report with scope and repair cost estimate; the current rent roll and operating statements for at least 24 months; any existing appraisal or broker opinion of value; copies of any lender communications referencing the defect or litigation; the lis pendens or litigation filing date; any insurance reservation of rights letters or coverage denials; and the property's current debt structure if any encumbrance exists. The financing analysis is only as reliable as the factual foundation underlying it.

Use of financing expert testimony at trial and in plan hearings. At trial, the financing expert typically provides quantification of the financing impairment as a component of economic damages. In bankruptcy plan confirmation hearings, the financing expert may testify to the feasibility of the debtor's proposed exit financing as part of demonstrating that the plan is not likely to be followed by further reorganization. In both contexts, the expert's opinion must be grounded in a reproducible methodology, not judgment alone.

Expert Witness: Commercial Loan Modification Standards in Albuquerque, NM

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Loan Modification and Workout Dispute

The Albuquerque Market Context

Albuquerque occupies a distinct position in the Southwest commercial real estate landscape. It is not a primary gateway market, but it is not a tertiary backwater either. With roughly 900,000 residents in the metro area, a major research university, Kirtland Air Force Base, Sandia National Laboratories, and a growing advanced manufacturing corridor along the I-25 corridor, the market generates genuine, recurring demand for commercial real estate across multiple asset classes. That institutional employment base matters enormously in workout disputes because it creates a floor beneath certain asset values that a purely demographic analysis might miss.

For loan modification and workout disputes, the asset class in question shapes everything. Albuquerque's multifamily market has held up relatively well, with vacancy rates in the 5 to 7 percent range across most submarkets and effective rent growth that has moderated but remained positive since 2022 after a period of strong pandemic-era gains. The northeast heights and downtown-adjacent corridors have absorbed new supply without catastrophic rent compression, which matters when a court needs to assess whether a lender's workout terms reflected actual market deterioration or manufactured distress. Office is a different story: Class B and Class C office in Albuquerque has struggled with vacancy rates exceeding 15 to 18 percent in some submarkets, and net absorption has been negative in multiple quarters. A lender who made a modification decision based on pre-2020 office fundamentals and then blamed the borrower for underperformance deserves scrutiny. Industrial, by contrast, has benefited from regional supply chain repositioning, with vacancy sitting below 5 percent in major corridors, making industrial-secured disputes particularly sensitive to valuation questions. Retail is bifurcated: grocery-anchored centers have maintained occupancy above 92 percent on average, while unanchored strip centers face structural headwinds that a lender's workout team may not have modeled correctly.

One Albuquerque-specific dynamic that consistently appears in modification disputes is the limited depth of the local investment sales market. Cap rate discovery is harder here than in Denver or Phoenix, which means lender internal valuations used to justify workout terms are more susceptible to challenge. When the comparable sales base is thin, the methodology behind a lender's BPO or internal valuation becomes a litigation target. That is precisely the kind of analysis a qualified CRE financing expert can deconstruct.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing a commercial borrower in a dispute arising from a lender's administration of a loan modification and forbearance agreement on a mixed-use income-producing property in Albuquerque's central submarket. The asset included ground-floor retail with upper-floor office tenancy, a combination that placed it squarely in one of the more challenged property type categories in the post-pandemic environment. The loan had originated in 2019 at underwriting metrics that reflected the market conditions of that moment. By 2022, occupancy had declined materially, NOI had contracted, and the borrower had entered into forbearance negotiations with the lender.

Counsel retained CLS CRE to address three distinct but interrelated questions. First, did the lender's conduct during the modification negotiation process conform to industry standards of care for commercial real estate workout administration? Second, were the modification terms ultimately proposed by the lender commercially reasonable given the property's actual cash flow, current market conditions, and the realistic universe of refinancing alternatives available to the borrower? Third, how did the lender's actions compare to the documented practices of institutional lenders operating workout programs in comparable secondary markets during the same time period?

These are precisely the questions that appraisers cannot answer. Appraisers establish value. CRE financing experts establish what the market for capital actually looked like, how lenders actually behave in workouts, and whether the terms imposed on a borrower were within or outside the range of what a commercially reasonable lender would have required. Those are distinct analytical frameworks, and confusing them is a common error in CRE litigation strategy.

The Financial Analysis

Our methodology began with a reconstruction of the property's actual net operating income at three points in time: the original underwriting date, the date forbearance was first requested, and the date the lender made its final modification proposal. This longitudinal NOI analysis is critical because it separates genuine cash flow deterioration from what I would call lender-amplified distress, which occurs when a lender's own conduct during the workout period accelerates borrower default rather than facilitating cure.

For this property type in Albuquerque, we normalized income using in-place leases, market rent surveys for comparable mixed-use space in the central submarket, and vacancy assumptions grounded in actual submarket data rather than national averages. A lender's workout team applying a 10 percent vacancy assumption to a property in a submarket experiencing 18 percent effective vacancy is using a number that flatters the asset. Conversely, applying a 25 percent vacancy assumption to a property with signed leases and creditworthy tenants is a number that punishes the borrower. Both errors appear in lender workout files, and both are provable with submarket-specific data.

In this engagement, the stabilized NOI we calculated at the time of the lender's modification proposal was approximately $420,000 annually, net of management fees, reserves, and normalized expenses. The property's gross potential rent was approximately $580,000, with vacancy and credit loss of approximately $87,000 and operating expenses of approximately $73,000, yielding the $420,000 NOI figure. The lender's internal underwriting used a figure closer to $360,000, which we challenged as incorporating a vacancy penalty inconsistent with the actual lease roll and market conditions at that specific moment.

Applying the industry-standard DSCR threshold of 1.25x, which is the conventional minimum for conventional commercial real estate loans on mixed-use assets and is consistent with the underwriting guidelines published by the major banking regulators and followed by institutional lenders nationwide, we calculated maximum supportable annual debt service of $336,000. At the lender's proposed modification rate of 7.25 percent on a 25-year amortization schedule, that debt service figure supported a loan balance of approximately $3.95 million. The original loan balance at the time of the modification demand was approximately $4.3 million. At a current market cap rate of 7.0 to 7.5 percent for this asset class and submarket, the property's indicated value was approximately $5.6 to $6.0 million, yielding an LTV in the range of 72 to 77 percent.

This calculation was central to our opinion. The lender's modification proposal required the borrower to make an immediate principal paydown of $600,000 to bring the loan to what the lender characterized as an appropriate LTV, and the lender simultaneously refused to extend the forbearance period to allow the borrower time to raise that capital. Our analysis demonstrated that the lender's own NOI assumptions were understated, that the resulting LTV calculation was therefore overstated, and that even under the lender's terms, a commercially reasonable lender would have provided at least a 90-day extension to facilitate the paydown. The combination of compressed NOI assumptions, an accelerated paydown demand, and a refusal to extend the cure period formed the core of the lender conduct analysis.

The Complicating Factors

Mixed-use asset complexity. Lenders in workout situations frequently apply single-asset-class underwriting to mixed-use properties, which distorts both the income analysis and the comparable valuation. A property with ground-floor retail and upper-floor office requires separate lease-by-lease analysis, separate vacancy benchmarks for each component, and a blended cap rate that reflects the actual income composition rather than a generic category assignment. When a lender's workout team applies a pure office cap rate to a property that generates 40 percent of its income from retail, the resulting valuation is structurally incorrect, and that error is not always obvious without expert deconstruction.

The Albuquerque appraisal lag. Secondary markets like Albuquerque experience a documented lag between economic events and their reflection in formal appraisals, particularly for property types with thin transaction volume. A lender who commissioned an appraisal in mid-2022 and used that appraisal to justify 2023 modification terms may have been working from stale data, particularly if the income approach was anchored to pre-renegotiation leases that had since been modified or surrendered. Expert analysis of the appraisal methodology, timing, and comparables is directly relevant to whether the lender's collateral assessment was commercially reasonable.

Workout timeline and good faith obligations. Industry standards for commercial loan workouts, as reflected in the published guidance from the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency, the Federal Reserve, and the FDIC, as well as the workout practices of major institutional lenders, contemplate a process of genuine information exchange, realistic cure period structuring, and term proposals that bear a rational relationship to the property's actual income capacity. A lender who makes modification demands that cannot be satisfied under any commercially realistic scenario, while simultaneously running the clock on a forbearance agreement, is not conducting a workout; it is conducting a foreclosure by another name. This distinction is demonstrable through comparative analysis of documented workout practices.

Interest rate environment timing. The 2022 to 2024 rate environment created a specific and identifiable set of challenges for borrowers seeking to refinance out of troubled loans. A lender who used the unavailability of refinancing as evidence of borrower weakness, while simultaneously knowing that market-wide financing had contracted sharply, was exploiting a market condition rather than responding to borrower-specific credit deterioration. Documenting the refinancing market at the specific moment of modification demands is a core expert function that the lender's characterization of borrower failure requires answering.

The Albuquerque Lender Market

Understanding which lenders are actually active in Albuquerque for distressed or modified commercial assets is essential context for a workout dispute. The lender market here is meaningfully different from what it looks like in Denver or Dallas, and that difference has direct bearing on what modification terms are commercially reasonable.

Community and regional banks. New Mexico's community banking sector is active and relationship-oriented. Several regional banks headquartered in Albuquerque and Santa Fe maintain genuine commercial real estate portfolios and conduct workouts with meaningful borrower engagement. These lenders typically require DSCR minimums of 1.20x to 1.25x, prefer LTV at or below 70 to 75 percent at stabilization, and operate with loan-to-value policies that reflect the limited secondary market liquidity in the region. Their workout practices tend to be less formulaic than larger institutional lenders, which can be either a benefit or a liability depending on the disposition of the workout officer assigned to the file.

National and super-regional banks. Larger institutional lenders with Albuquerque exposure typically manage workout files from centralized special assets departments in Dallas, Phoenix, or Atlanta. This geographic distance from the market creates systematic risks of analytical error. Workout officers applying Phoenix or Dallas comps to Albuquerque assets, or using national vacancy benchmarks rather than submarket-specific data, produce modification proposals that can be shown to deviate from what a lender with actual market knowledge would have required. CLS CRE has relationships with more than 1,000 lenders nationwide and has documented the underwriting criteria of the major institutional players active in secondary Southwest markets, which provides direct comparison benchmarks for expert analysis.

Debt funds and bridge lenders. For borrowers who needed to refinance out of troubled bank loans during the 2022 to 2024 period, the bridge lending market in Albuquerque was available but expensive. Rates of SOFR plus 350 to 500 basis points were typical for stabilizing assets, with origination fees of 1.0 to 2.0 points. Maximum proceeds were typically limited to 65 to 70 percent LTV on as-is value, with future funding components available for value-add execution. Understanding this market is essential for assessing whether a borrower had commercially realistic refinancing alternatives at the time a lender made modification demands, and if so, at what cost.

Life companies and CMBS. Neither life company nor CMBS execution is readily available for troubled or non-stabilized assets in Albuquerque's secondary submarket tier. Life companies require stabilized occupancy above 90 percent and seasoned income, making them irrelevant to most workout scenarios. CMBS is possible for stabilized assets above approximately $5 million but is not a viable exit for assets in active workout. Knowing the boundaries of institutional capital availability is directly relevant to whether a lender's modification demands were calibrated to market reality.

What Attorneys Handling Albuquerque CRE Disputes Need to Know

If you are handling a commercial real estate loan modification or workout dispute in Albuquerque, the question of whether to retain a CRE financing expert is not a close call. The questions at the center of these disputes require specialized knowledge that neither appraisers nor general banking experts typically possess. Here is what you need to understand before your expert engagement begins.

When to retain a CRE financing expert. Retain us early. The expert's analysis of the lender's workout file, internal credit memos, modification term sheets, and valuation methodology will directly shape your discovery strategy. If you wait until the eve of trial to engage an expert, you will have missed the opportunity to use the expert's analytical framework to identify the documents that prove your case. Loan modification disputes typically turn on three evidentiary categories: the lender's internal analysis of the property's income, the lender's assessment of market conditions, and the lender's adherence to or deviation from its own internal workout policies. An expert who understands commercial lending underwriting can tell you exactly which documents to request and what deviations from those documents to look for.

What a CRE financing expert answers that an appraiser cannot. An appraiser tells you what the property was worth. A CRE financing expert tells you what the market for capital on that property actually looked like, what a commercially reasonable lender would have required as modification terms, whether the lender's internal NOI and valuation methodology was within or outside market standards, and whether refinancing alternatives that the lender claimed were unavailable were actually accessible to the borrower at the relevant time. These are financing market questions, not valuation questions. The distinction matters because courts and arbitrators expect the expert's testimony to match the question being answered.

What qualifies a CRE financing expert. The qualification that matters most in this context is active, current engagement in commercial real estate capital markets. A retired bank credit officer knows how one institution underwrites. A CRE broker who has placed debt with dozens of lenders across multiple asset classes in multiple markets knows how the entire lender universe behaves and can testify to industry-wide standards rather than one institution's internal practices. At CLS CRE, our approximately $1 billion in transaction volume and active relationships with more than 1,000 lenders across 50 states provide a market intelligence base that supports defensible, data-grounded opinions on industry standards.

Information to gather before the engagement. Counsel should secure the full loan origination file including the original appraisal and credit memorandum, all forbearance and modification correspondence, the lender's internal workout file including any BPOs or desk reviews commissioned during the workout period, the lender's internal policies governing special assets and workout administration, all communication between the borrower and the lender's workout team during the forbearance period, and any third-party reports or market analyses the lender relied upon in setting modification terms.

How expert testimony is used. In trial, CRE financing expert testimony most commonly supports claims of lender bad faith, breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing in workout administration, and damages quantification where the lender's conduct forced a borrower into a worse outcome than a commercially reasonable workout would have produced. In bankruptcy plan confirmation hearings, expert testimony on financing feasibility directly supports or attacks plan projections and the creditor's valuation of collateral.

Key Takeaways

  • NOI reconstruction is foundational. The lender's income assumptions used during workout negotiations are frequently understated, and the methodology behind those assumptions is always subject to expert challenge. A longitudinal NOI analysis comparing original underwriting, borrower-requested period, and modification demand date is the analytical core of most lender conduct opinions.
  • DSCR and LTV standards are documented and defensible. Industry-standard DSCR minimums of 1.20x to 1.25x and LTV ceilings of 65 to 75 percent for most commercial asset types are consistently applied across the lender market.

Expert Witness: How Construction Defects Affect CRE Financing in Fort Worth, TX

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Construction Defect Impact on Financing

The Fort Worth Market Context

Fort Worth has evolved from a secondary Texas market into a legitimately institutional commercial real estate destination, and that transition matters enormously when construction defect litigation intersects with financing feasibility. The Dallas-Fort Worth metroplex as a whole absorbed more industrial square footage than virtually any other market in the country over the 2020 to 2023 cycle, and Fort Worth captured a meaningful share of that demand, particularly in the Alliance Corridor, the I-35W industrial corridor, and the infill pockets west of downtown. Industrial vacancy in Fort Worth's core submarkets was running in the 5 to 7 percent range through most of 2023, though new deliveries in late 2023 and into 2024 pushed that figure modestly higher in select nodes, with some bulk distribution corridors approaching 9 to 11 percent vacancy as speculative completions outpaced absorption.

Multifamily in Fort Worth has followed a parallel arc. Rent growth that was running 12 to 15 percent annually in 2021 and 2022 decelerated sharply through 2023, with effective rents in many Fort Worth submarkets either flat or marginally declining as supply caught up with pandemic-era demand. Cap rates for stabilized multifamily product in Fort Worth's stronger submarkets, including the Near Southside, Cultural District, and West 7th corridors, were trading in the 4.75 to 5.50 percent range in 2022 but have since drifted toward 5.50 to 6.25 percent as interest rates repriced the capital stack. That cap rate expansion is not academic in litigation: it directly affects supportable loan proceeds and the financing gap that a construction defect can create or widen.

Office fundamentals in Fort Worth are more nuanced than the national narrative suggests. Unlike downtown Dallas, Fort Worth's central business district maintained relatively stronger occupancy, driven by energy sector tenants, professional services firms, and Tarrant County-related government and legal tenants. Still, suburban office in Fort Worth faces meaningful headwinds, with vacancy in some suburban nodes exceeding 18 to 22 percent. When construction defect litigation involves an office building, this market backdrop creates a difficult financing environment even before the defect is factored in. Lenders were already stress-testing Fort Worth suburban office at conservative rent and occupancy assumptions; introduce known structural or envelope defects and the financing universe narrows dramatically. Understanding this baseline is the first step in any credible expert analysis.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing a party in a construction defect dispute involving a newly constructed commercial property in the Fort Worth market. The asset involved was a mid-size multifamily community, a property type where construction defect claims are particularly consequential because the income stream, the physical condition, and the lender's perception of risk are deeply interconnected. The alleged defects included waterproofing failures, moisture infiltration affecting multiple building envelopes, and structural concerns related to podium deck construction, each of which carries specific implications for how institutional and regional lenders evaluate the asset.

Counsel retained CLS CRE to address a defined set of financial and market questions that an appraiser, while competent to estimate value, is not positioned to answer with the same depth or credibility as an active capital markets professional. Those questions included: What financing, if any, was available to a prospective purchaser or refinancing borrower on this property given the known and alleged defects? How do the defects affect the debt service coverage ratio and maximum supportable loan amount? What would a lender require, in terms of reserves, repairs, escrows, and representations, before closing a loan on this asset? And critically, how does the financing gap between a defect-free comparable asset and this encumbered asset translate into quantifiable economic harm?

These questions are not answered in an appraisal report. An appraiser estimates market value. A financing expert analyzes the debt capital that can be placed against that value under current lender market conditions, and that distinction matters enormously in damages modeling, in settlement negotiations, and at trial.

The Financial Analysis

The methodology began with a reconstruction of the property's stabilized net operating income, both as-is given the defect conditions and as it would appear on a defect-free asset of equivalent type, location, and vintage. For a 200-unit multifamily asset in a Fort Worth submarket where effective rents were running approximately $1,450 to $1,600 per month for a comparable product mix, a stabilized gross potential rent of roughly $3.5 million annually is a reasonable baseline. After applying a market vacancy and credit loss assumption of 7 percent, which is consistent with current Fort Worth multifamily market conditions, and deducting operating expenses including management, maintenance, insurance, real estate taxes, and reserves for replacement at approximately 42 to 45 percent of effective gross income, a stabilized NOI of approximately $1.9 million to $2.1 million represents a credible defect-free underwriting baseline.

Against that NOI, lender underwriting in the current environment for a conventionally financed multifamily asset in Fort Worth applies a debt service coverage ratio floor of 1.25x for agency execution, which is the Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac standard as of this writing, and 1.20x for balance sheet and CMBS lenders, though many balance sheet lenders are currently stress-testing at 1.30x or higher given the interest rate environment. Assuming a 30-year amortization schedule and an interest rate of 6.75 to 7.25 percent on a 10-year fixed execution, the maximum annual debt service supportable by a $1.95 million NOI at a 1.25x DSCR is approximately $1.56 million. At a 7.00 percent interest rate on a 30-year amortization, that debt service figure supports a loan of approximately $23.5 million to $24.0 million. If the property would trade at a 5.75 percent cap rate in defect-free condition, a $1.95 million NOI implies a value of approximately $33.9 million, and a $23.5 million loan represents a loan-to-value ratio of approximately 69 percent, which is within the 65 to 75 percent LTV range typical for multifamily in this market.

Now introduce the defects. Waterproofing failures affecting multiple building envelopes create two categories of underwriting impairment. First, they generate documented repair costs that lenders require to be either completed prior to closing or escrowed at a holdback that typically equals 100 to 125 percent of the engineer-estimated remediation cost. If the remediation cost is estimated at $2.8 million, a lender will either require that work to be completed and verified before funding, or will hold back $3.0 to $3.5 million in loan proceeds pending completion. That holdback directly reduces net loan proceeds to the borrower even if the face amount of the loan remains nominally unchanged. Second, and more significantly, active moisture infiltration raises questions about unit habitability, insurance coverage, and potential rent loss. If 15 to 20 percent of units are affected and experiencing occupancy disruption or elevated vacancy, the effective NOI available to support debt service is materially lower, and the DSCR calculation deteriorates accordingly.

Running the same analysis on the defect-encumbered NOI, with occupancy running at 82 percent rather than 93 percent due to affected units, and with elevated operating expenses reflecting ongoing maintenance costs and potential insurance complications, a revised NOI of approximately $1.5 million to $1.6 million is a defensible estimate. At the same 1.25x DSCR threshold and 7.00 percent interest rate, the maximum supportable loan drops to approximately $18.0 to $19.2 million. The financing gap between the defect-free loan maximum and the defect-encumbered loan maximum is therefore approximately $4.5 to $5.5 million, and that figure, grounded in lender underwriting standards rather than appraisal theory, represents quantifiable economic harm that can be explained to a trier of fact in concrete, transactional terms.

The Complicating Factors

Lender disclosure obligations and secondary market eligibility. For multifamily assets that would otherwise qualify for agency execution through Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac, known construction defects are a disqualifying condition until remediated and re-inspected. Agency lenders are required to disclose known material defects, and properties with active litigation involving structural or envelope issues are explicitly excluded from agency underwriting guidelines. This is not a negotiated exception; it is a categorical exclusion. The practical effect is that a defect-encumbered asset loses access to the lowest-cost, highest-leverage capital available in the market, and is forced into more expensive balance sheet or bridge financing. In the current rate environment, that spread between agency and non-agency pricing can represent 150 to 250 basis points of additional interest cost, which compounds the financing gap analysis significantly.

Title and insurance complications. Active construction defect litigation affects title insurance availability and lender's policy endorsements. Many title underwriters will either exclude coverage related to the pending litigation or add exceptions that a lender's counsel will find unacceptable. Without a clean lender's title policy, institutional lenders cannot close. This is a binary condition, not a pricing adjustment, and attorneys handling these disputes should understand that unresolved litigation can effectively halt refinancing or sale transactions regardless of whether the underlying defect has been physically remediated.

Environmental and habitability overlap. In cases where moisture intrusion is severe enough to produce mold conditions, lenders require a Phase I environmental assessment update and, depending on the findings, a Phase II or mold-specific assessment. Mold conditions in occupied residential units trigger potential habitability liability that lenders view as open-ended contingent liability. No institutional lender will close on a property with documented, unresolved mold conditions in occupied units. This is not a reserve or holdback situation; it is a loan denial condition until remediation is certified by a qualified industrial hygienist.

Podium deck structural concerns. For assets with podium deck construction, structural concerns are evaluated differently than finish or envelope defects. A lender's engineering review on a podium deck concern will typically require a structural engineering report from a firm independent of both the original design team and the plaintiff's expert. If the structural engineer cannot certify the podium deck as compliant with the original design specifications and applicable building codes, the lender will not fund. Period. Podium deck defects also affect the property's insurance program: property insurers may exclude structural failure coverage on a defect-implicated structure, and without evidence of property insurance meeting the lender's coverage requirements, the loan cannot close.

The Fort Worth Lender Market

Fort Worth benefits from meaningful lender depth across multiple capital source categories, and CLS CRE maintains active relationships with representatives across all of them. Understanding which lenders will and will not engage with a defect-encumbered asset is central to the financing feasibility analysis.

Agency lenders including Fannie Mae Delegated Underwriting and Servicing lenders and Freddie Mac Program Plus lenders are, as noted above, categorically unavailable for properties with active construction defect litigation or unresolved structural concerns. This eliminates the deepest pool of multifamily capital in the Fort Worth market for the duration of the litigation and the remediation verification period.

CMBS lenders are theoretically available but practically limited. CMBS execution requires a representation from the borrower that there is no pending litigation that would materially affect the collateral, and active construction defect litigation typically fails that representation. Even for a defect where the borrower believes the litigation is without merit, CMBS conduit lenders are not willing to accept that litigation risk on a pool of loans being securitized. The underwriting standards for CMBS on a Fort Worth multifamily asset would also require a minimum DSCR of 1.25x on as-is NOI, and the defect-encumbered NOI scenario analyzed above may not clear that bar.

Regional and community banks represent the most accessible capital source for a defect-encumbered asset in Fort Worth, but their execution comes at a cost. Balance sheet lenders including Texas-chartered banks and community banks active in Tarrant County can hold the credit risk of a partially impaired asset, and they can structure holdback reserves and completion escrows in ways that agency and CMBS lenders cannot. However, their pricing is typically 175 to 275 basis points wider than agency execution, their maximum LTV is generally capped at 65 percent on a transitional or impaired asset, and their loan terms are shorter, typically 3 to 5 years, which creates refinancing risk if the litigation timeline extends beyond the loan term.

Debt funds and bridge lenders will engage with defect-encumbered assets, but they price the risk aggressively. Current Fort Worth bridge pricing for a multifamily asset with known defects would likely land in the SOFR plus 350 to 500 basis point range, with origination fees of 1.0 to 2.0 points. This represents a total cost of capital that is dramatically higher than what a comparable defect-free asset would carry, and that differential is directly relevant to damages quantification.

What Attorneys Handling Fort Worth CRE Disputes Need to Know

The most important thing I can tell litigation counsel is this: an appraisal report is not a financing feasibility analysis, and conflating the two is a mistake that weakens otherwise strong cases. An appraiser will give you a market value opinion using comparable sales, income capitalization, and sometimes a cost approach. What an appraiser will not tell you, because it is outside their scope and methodology, is whether a lender will actually fund a loan against that value, at what cost, with what conditions, and how those conditions differ from what they would require on a defect-free asset. That gap between appraised value and financeable value is frequently where the real economic harm lives.

Retain a CRE financing expert when the damages theory involves impaired financing, lost financing proceeds, forced equity contribution, or carrying cost differentials. Retain one when the opposing party claims the property is financeable and you need to rebut that claim with current, specific lender market evidence. Retain one when a plan of reorganization in a bankruptcy proceeding depends on demonstrated financing feasibility, or when a settlement demand needs to be grounded in transactional reality rather than appraiser theory.

A qualified CRE financing expert is not simply a retired bank officer. The field moves quickly, and expert testimony based on lending practices from five years ago is not credible to a sophisticated fact-finder. The expert should be actively originating commercial loans in the current market, should have direct relationships with the categories of lenders relevant to the dispute, and should be able to testify from current, firsthand market knowledge rather than historical recollection. At CLS CRE, our testimony is grounded in approximately $1 billion in transaction volume and more than 1,000 active lender relationships across 50 states, including an active presence in the Texas market.

Before engaging a financing expert, counsel should gather the following: the property's rent roll and trailing 12-month operating statements; any engineering or inspection reports addressing the alleged defects; the original construction loan documents and any lender correspondence; any appraisals or valuations already in the record; correspondence or documentation showing any financing attempts that were rejected or conditioned; and any insurance documentation, particularly endorsements or exclusions related to the defective conditions. This documentation allows the expert to reconstruct both the defect-free financing scenario and the defect-encumbered scenario with specificity and credibility.

At trial, CRE financing expert testimony serves several functions. It establishes the financing gap as a concrete, calculable figure rather than a speculative estimate. It explains to the jury or bench, in transactional terms, why a sophisticated lender would require certain conditions, reserves, or price adjustments in response to the alleged defects. It rebuts opposing expert testimony that overstates financing availability or understates financing cost. And it translates the technical language of debt service coverage ratios, loan-to-value constraints, and lender credit boxes into explanations that non-specialist triers of fact can follow and credit.

Key Takeaways

  • Construction defects impair financing on two separate tracks: they reduce the NOI available to support debt service, and they trigger lender conditions, holdbacks, and exclusions that reduce net loan proceeds independent of the NOI calculation. Both tracks must be analyzed and quantified separately.
  • Agency execution is categorically unavailable for Fort Worth multifamily assets with active construction defect litigation, eliminating the lowest-cost, highest-leverage capital source and forcing borrowers into more expensive alternatives. That cost differential is quantifiable and belongs in any damages analysis.
  • The financing gap between a defect-free maximum loan and a defect-encumbered maximum loan is a specific, calculable figure grounded in current lender underwriting standards, not a theoretical estimate, and it can be presented with precision at trial.

Commercial Appraisers and Mortgage Brokers: A Natural Referral Partnership

By Trevor Damyan·June 25, 2026·Commercial Lending Solutions

CRE Appraisers and Commercial Mortgage Brokers: The Referral Partnership Most Professionals Leave on the Table

I am Trevor Damyan, a commercial mortgage broker at CLS CRE in Los Angeles. Over the last decade, I have closed hundreds of commercial real estate transactions across every major property type. One thing I notice consistently is how rarely appraisers and commercial mortgage brokers work together in a formal referral capacity, despite the fact that we serve the same property owners at nearly every stage of the same transaction.

This article is written specifically for MAI appraisers, review appraisers, appraisal management companies, and valuation consultants. My goal is to show you the specific situations where your clients need a commercial mortgage broker, why a trusted referral to the right broker makes you more valuable as a professional, and what you should look for before sending anyone your way.

Why Commercial Appraisers Constantly Encounter Financing Needs

Your engagement typically begins at a pivotal moment. A property owner needs an appraisal because something significant is happening with the asset: a refinance, a sale, an estate matter, a dispute, or a distress situation. In almost every case, the person sitting across from you is either actively seeking capital or about to be. The appraisal is the predicate to the financing decision, not a standalone event.

MAI appraisers working on commercial properties routinely see clients who are in one of these positions: they are refinancing an existing loan that is coming due, they are recapitalizing equity after a value-add renovation, they are navigating a partnership dispute that involves the property's debt structure, or they are trying to stabilize a distressed asset before a lender accelerates. In all of these situations, the appraiser is one of the first professionals engaged, which means you are often in a position to make a referral before anyone else does.

Yet most appraisers I speak with have no standing relationship with a commercial mortgage broker. They may know one or two brokers casually, but they have no structured partnership and no clear sense of when to make an introduction. That gap costs their clients time, sometimes money, and occasionally the transaction itself.

The Five Financing Scenarios Commercial Appraisers Face Most Often

1. Your Client Asks You Who They Should Use for the Refinancing

This happens more than most appraisers expect. You deliver the appraisal, and the client, now armed with a value figure, turns to you and says: "Do you know a good mortgage broker for this?" They trust you. You are the professional they just paid for an objective opinion. A referral from you carries weight that cold outreach from a broker never will.

The problem is that most appraisers do not have a ready answer, so they deflect or give a generic response. When you have a trusted commercial mortgage broker relationship, your answer becomes: "Yes, let me introduce you to someone who specifically handles this property type and loan size." That introduction is worth real money to your client, because a strong broker will run the market competitively across 20 to 50 lenders, not just place the loan with whoever is easiest.

From a compliance standpoint, USPAP does not prohibit referrals as long as you are not receiving compensation that compromises your independence as an appraiser. A straightforward referral with no financial arrangement is clean. If you want a formal referral fee arrangement, that is a separate legal and licensing question that varies by state, but the referral itself is not ethically problematic under standard professional guidelines.

2. The Appraisal Is Being Used in Litigation

When an appraisal ends up in litigation, one of the central arguments often becomes whether the appraised value is achievable in the financing market. Attorneys on both sides frequently need an expert who can speak to lender underwriting standards, debt service coverage ratio requirements, loan-to-value thresholds, and whether a given value conclusion is consistent with how institutional lenders actually price and underwrite the asset class.

In my experience, commercial mortgage brokers are rarely retained as financing expert witnesses, but the need is real and growing. If you are a review appraiser working on a case where value is contested, or if you are the appraiser whose work is being challenged, the opposing party may argue that no lender would finance the property at the value you concluded. Having a commercial mortgage broker who can speak to actual lender behavior, underwriting matrices, and market rate conditions is a legitimate and often overlooked form of expert support.

The financing expert witness role is distinct from the appraisal expert witness role. The appraiser answers "what is the value?" The financing expert answers "is this value financeable, and under what terms?" Those are separate questions that require separate expertise. If you are working with litigation counsel on a commercial valuation dispute, a referral to a broker who can serve in this capacity adds a dimension of support that most legal teams have not thought to source.

3. You Are Engaged on a Distressed or Complex Property

Some of the most interesting appraisal assignments involve properties that fall outside conventional underwriting. A partially leased mixed-use building in a tertiary market. A hotel that lost its flag and is operating independently. A retail strip center with a dark anchor. A special-purpose property with limited comparables. An industrial building with environmental contamination that is being remediated.

The property owners in these situations are not good candidates for conventional bank financing. They need a broker who specializes in non-bank capital: bridge lenders, debt funds, family offices, life insurance companies with appetite for transitional deals, or SBA lenders for owner-user special-purpose properties. Most borrowers in distressed or complex situations do not know this capital exists or how to access it.

When you are appraising a property like this and you can see that the owner is going to need financing help, a referral to a commercial mortgage broker who works in non-conventional situations is a significant service. It signals to your client that you understand their situation beyond the appraisal itself, and it connects them to capital they would not otherwise find through a Google search or a call to their community bank.

At CLS CRE, roughly half of our volume is in transitional or complex deals. We work with over 1,000 lenders nationally, including bridge debt funds, non-QM commercial lenders, private family offices, and direct life insurance company relationships. These are not lenders that show up on Bankrate. They are reached through relationships built over years of deal flow.

4. A Review Appraiser Finds a Significant Value Dispute

Review appraisers occupy a unique position. You are evaluating not just the property but the reasonableness of another appraiser's methodology and conclusions. When a review reveals a material value discrepancy, both the original appraiser and the party challenging the appraisal may need to address a downstream question: at which of these values would a lender actually underwrite the loan?

This is where financing expertise becomes directly relevant to the dispute. Lenders do not simply take an appraised value at face value. They apply their own underwriting overlays: maximum loan-to-value ratios that vary by property type and risk tier, minimum debt service coverage ratios based on actual or stabilized income, haircuts on vacancy assumptions, and property type-specific constraints. A $10 million vs. $8 million valuation dispute is not just a difference of $2 million in equity. It may be the difference between a loan qualifying and not qualifying at all, depending on where the property's net operating income sits relative to the debt service the borrower can carry.

If you are a review appraiser or an AMC managing a disputed appraisal, the ability to bring in a financing expert who can explain how each value conclusion translates to lender behavior is genuinely useful to the parties trying to resolve the dispute. That expert is a commercial mortgage broker with deep underwriting knowledge, not a generalist consultant.

5. You Are Working on a Portfolio Assignment

Portfolio appraisal assignments, where a borrower or lender needs values on multiple properties simultaneously, often signal a refinancing event of significant scale. The borrower may be rolling a portfolio of stabilized multifamily properties into a blanket loan, or recapitalizing a mix of retail and industrial assets through a credit facility, or satisfying a lender's requirement for updated values across a book of collateral.

These are exactly the clients who benefit most from a commercial mortgage broker with portfolio financing experience. Portfolio transactions require lenders who can underwrite multiple property types simultaneously, who understand cross-collateralization structures, who can manage the complexity of staggered lease expirations and varied vacancy levels across a pool of assets, and who have relationships with agencies, life companies, and debt funds that can hold large balances.

A referral from the appraiser who is already working across the portfolio is timely and natural. You have visibility into the properties that most advisors do not yet have. If you can say to your client, "You are going to need a broker who handles portfolio transactions, and I know someone who specializes in this," you are adding value at exactly the right moment.

What Lenders Actually Require: Technical Thresholds You Should Know

Understanding lender requirements makes you a better referral partner, because you can identify situations where conventional financing will not work and your client needs a specialist.

Loan-to-value ratios: Conventional commercial banks typically lend up to 65% to 75% LTV on stabilized income-producing properties. Multifamily is generally at the higher end; office and retail at the lower end. Bridge lenders will go up to 75% to 80% LTV on transitional deals, with some debt funds reaching 85% on strong sponsorship. Life insurance companies typically stay at 55% to 65% LTV but offer the best long-term fixed rates.

Debt service coverage ratios: Most conventional commercial lenders require a minimum DSCR of 1.20x to 1.25x at the time of underwriting, using actual in-place net operating income. CMBS lenders typically require 1.25x. Agency lenders (Freddie Mac, Fannie Mae) on multifamily require 1.20x to 1.25x depending on the program. Bridge lenders will sometimes underwrite to a stabilized pro forma at 1.10x to 1.15x if the transitional business plan is credible.

Occupancy requirements: Conventional lenders typically require 90% or above physical occupancy for stabilized financing. Properties below 85% occupancy generally do not qualify for agency or bank financing and require bridge or transitional capital. Special-purpose properties (hotels, self-storage, assisted living) are underwritten on a separate set of metrics: RevPAR and occupancy for hotels, economic occupancy and per-square-foot revenue for self-storage.

Appraisal requirements: Every institutional lender, agency program, and CMBS execution requires an MAI appraisal completed by an approved appraiser. FIRREA requires federally regulated lenders to obtain an appraisal for commercial transactions above $500,000. CMBS deals require appraisals from CMBS-approved firms on their approved list. Life insurance companies often have their own approved appraiser panels. This means that the appraiser and the mortgage broker are not just adjacent professionals, they are required participants in the same deal.

Lease and income documentation: Lenders require rent rolls, executed leases, historical operating statements (typically two to three years), trailing 12-month income and expense summaries, and property management agreements. Properties with significant lease-up or rent-roll gaps face more scrutiny and often require bridge financing with a clear path to stabilization before conventional takeout.

Questions Commercial Appraisers Should Ask Any Broker Before Referring

Not all commercial mortgage brokers are equal. Before you refer a client to any broker, here are the questions that separate capable brokers from generalists who will waste your client's time.

How many lenders do you work with, and what types? A broker with 10 to 20 bank relationships is a conventional loan originator. A broker who serves complex clients needs 200 or more relationships across banks, credit unions, debt funds, life companies, bridge lenders, and agencies. At CLS CRE, we maintain direct relationships with over 1,000 lenders nationally.

Do you handle the property type and loan size my client needs? Commercial mortgage brokers specialize. A multifamily specialist may not know how to underwrite a net-lease retail deal or a hospitality property. Ask about their volume in the specific property type and confirm they have placed loans in that size range in the last 12 months.

Do you have direct life company relationships? Direct life insurance company access is a meaningful differentiator. Life companies offer the best long-term fixed rates but have strict underwriting criteria and approved-broker lists. Most brokers cannot access them directly and have to use a correspondent or intermediary, which adds cost and time. Brokers with direct life company relationships can deliver materially better pricing on qualifying deals.

How do you handle distressed or complex deals? Ask for two or three examples of deals outside the conventional underwriting box that they have closed in the last two years. A broker who specializes in complex deals will have specific stories: a hotel recapitalization at 60% occupancy, a retail property with a dark anchor, a mixed-use building in a secondary market with lease-up risk. A generalist will give you vague answers.

Are you fee-transparent? Commercial mortgage brokers are typically compensated by origination points paid at closing, ranging from 0.5% to 2% of the loan amount depending on deal complexity. Make sure the broker you refer to discloses this clearly to your client upfront and does not layer in junk fees or charges for services that are standard in the market.

How do you handle confidentiality? Your client may share sensitive financial information with the broker early in the process. Ask whether the broker uses standard non-disclosure agreements for client information and how they handle data from deals that do not close.

Key Takeaways

  • The timing advantage is yours. Appraisers are often the first professional engaged when a financing event is approaching. A referral made at that moment is more valuable than one made after the client has already shopped four banks.
  • The financing question follows every valuation. Whether your client is refinancing, recapitalizing, litigating, or managing distress, the next question after "what is it worth?" is almost always "how do I finance it?" You can have an answer ready.
  • Distressed and complex properties require specialized brokers. Conventional bank contacts are not useful for non-stabilized, transitional, or special-purpose properties. The client needs a broker with non-bank capital relationships, not a referral to a community bank loan officer.
  • Review appraisers and AMCs have a specific expert witness opportunity. The question of whether a value is financeable is distinct from the question of what the value is. Both questions matter in valuation disputes, and commercial mortgage brokers can speak to the first question in a way no appraiser is positioned to do.
  • Portfolio assignments signal large financing events. If you are appraising multiple properties for the same owner simultaneously, that owner is likely in the middle of a significant capital transaction. A referral at that moment can make a material difference in the outcome.
  • Lender requirements are technical and vary by property type. LTV, DSCR, occupancy, and lease documentation thresholds vary significantly by lender type and property type. Understanding these thresholds helps you recognize when a client's situation requires a specialist rather than a conventional lender.
  • The referral process requires nothing from you after the introduction. A warm email or phone call is all it takes. The broker manages the client relationship from that point. You stay in your lane, and your client gets access to the right capital source.
  • Vet the broker before you refer. Ask about lender relationships, property type specialization, direct life company access, and fee transparency. One bad referral will cost you more relationship capital than the referral itself was worth.

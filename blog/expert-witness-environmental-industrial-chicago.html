Expert Witness: Financing a Contaminated Industrial Property in Chicago, IL

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Environmental Contamination Analysis

The Chicago Market Context

Chicago's industrial real estate market is one of the most consequential in North America, and understanding its dynamics is essential to any financing feasibility analysis involving contaminated or environmentally encumbered industrial assets. The Chicago metropolitan statistical area encompasses approximately 1.2 billion square feet of industrial inventory, making it the third-largest industrial market in the United States behind Los Angeles and Dallas. That scale matters in litigation because it generates a thick comparables set, a deep lender pool, and genuine price discovery that smaller markets simply cannot offer.

As of mid-2024, Chicago industrial vacancy sits in the range of 5.5% to 6.5% depending on submarket, up from historic lows near 3% recorded in 2021 and 2022. The O'Hare corridor, the I-55 logistics corridor, and the south suburban submarkets have absorbed the bulk of new speculative development, and asking rents across the metro have plateaued in the $8.00 to $11.00 per square foot range for bulk distribution product following the pandemic-era rent spike. These rent dynamics are directly relevant to any financing analysis because they define what "market" looks like when underwriters stress-test a below-market lease.

Chicago's industrial market is dominated by large-bay, high-clear-height distribution facilities, but legacy manufacturing and warehouse product in submarkets like Cicero, Bedford Park, the Calumet region, and the near-south industrial corridor represents a meaningful portion of the inventory. This older product is precisely where prior industrial use, chlorinated solvent operations, and legacy contamination issues tend to concentrate. Properties in these zones frequently carry environmental histories involving dry-cleaning chemicals, metal degreasing solvents, or historic manufacturing processes that generated 1,4-dioxane or chlorinated volatile organic compound (VOC) plumes. That contamination history is not disqualifying by default for financing purposes, but it requires careful analysis, proper documentation, and lender-by-lender navigation that is categorically different from a clean industrial deal.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by counsel representing a class of secured creditors in a matter involving a multi-tenant industrial warehouse property situated within a legacy manufacturing submarket on Chicago's south side. The property consisted of approximately 280,000 square feet of older industrial space on a roughly 14-acre site, 100% occupied under NNN leases with a weighted average lease term of approximately four years remaining. The in-place rents ranged from $4.50 to $5.75 per square foot annually, materially below current market for comparable product in that corridor.

The property was the subject of regulatory enforcement proceedings by the Illinois Environmental Protection Agency arising from contamination attributed to a prior tenant's industrial operations. The primary contaminants of concern included 1,4-dioxane and a suite of chlorinated VOCs, specifically perchloroethylene (PCE) and trichloroethylene (TCE), which had migrated into the groundwater beneath the property. An environmental consultant retained separately had characterized the plume and estimated remediation costs, and a No Further Remediation (NFR) letter from IEPA was not yet in hand.

The core questions our engagement was designed to answer were two-fold. First, does the existence of active regulatory enforcement and unresolved contamination preclude first-mortgage financing on this property in the current market? Second, if financing is available, what is the maximum supportable loan amount at prevailing market underwriting standards, and is that amount sufficient to retire the senior secured debt that the creditors held? These are financing feasibility questions, not appraisal questions. They require knowledge of lender credit policy, current capital market conditions, and the specific way environmental risk is priced and structured in commercial mortgage underwriting. An appraisal establishes value; a financing feasibility analysis establishes what a lender will actually put on the asset.

The Financial Analysis

The starting point for any commercial mortgage underwriting analysis is the net operating income, and the first analytic judgment in this engagement was whether to underwrite to in-place income or stabilized market income. The in-place rents averaging roughly $5.10 per square foot blended produced a gross potential rent of approximately $1,428,000 annually. Applying a 5% vacancy and credit loss allowance consistent with market-standard underwriting for multi-tenant industrial product, and deducting operating expenses of approximately $195,000 (property taxes, insurance, landlord responsibilities under the NNN structure, and management), the in-place NOI came to approximately $1,162,000.

Market rents for comparable older industrial product in the same submarket were underwritten at $6.50 to $7.00 per square foot, producing a stabilized NOI closer to $1,540,000 using the same expense load. Lenders in the current environment are not underwriting to market rents on below-market leases unless those leases are near-term or have been replaced. With four years of weighted average lease term remaining, the in-place income is what institutional lenders will use as their underwriting base. The gap between in-place NOI and market NOI is important, but it works in the borrower's direction as a recoverable value argument, not as a current underwriting input.

On debt service coverage ratio, the market standard for industrial properties in a secondary credit scenario is 1.25x minimum, with many lenders requiring 1.30x given the environmental complexity. Using a 1.25x DSCR threshold at a 7.25% note rate and a 25-year amortization schedule, which reflect current pricing for conduit or balance-sheet industrial loans in the 2024 market, the maximum annual debt service supportable by the in-place NOI of $1,162,000 is approximately $929,600. That debt service figure, when applied against a 25-year amortization at 7.25%, supports a maximum loan of approximately $12,100,000.

The LTV constraint is the second binding test. For industrial product without environmental issues, institutional lenders are generally comfortable to 65% LTV. With active contamination and no NFR letter, the effective LTV ceiling drops to 55% to 60% depending on the lender category. If the appraised "as encumbered" value of the property reflects the contamination, and comparable contaminated industrial sales in the Chicago market suggest a value discount of 20% to 35% relative to clean comparable sales, then a hypothetical appraised value of $20,000,000 on an unencumbered basis might support an "as encumbered" value closer to $14,000,000 to $16,000,000. Applying a 55% LTV to the midpoint of $15,000,000 yields a maximum loan of $8,250,000. The DSCR-constrained maximum of $12,100,000 is thus not the binding constraint; the LTV constraint is, which is the more typical result in contaminated asset financings.

The analysis showed that first-mortgage financing was available in the current market but that the maximum supportable loan, constrained by LTV at approximately $8,250,000 to $9,000,000 depending on the final appraised value, was materially less than the outstanding senior debt balance. That shortfall is the central financial finding of the engagement, and it directly informs the creditors' negotiating posture and litigation strategy.

The Complicating Factors

Active Regulatory Enforcement Without an NFR Letter. The Illinois Environmental Protection Agency's Site Remediation Program issues No Further Remediation letters as the primary mechanism for confirming that a contaminated site has been addressed to a standard sufficient for its intended use. In the absence of an NFR letter, lenders cannot underwrite the environmental liability as bounded. This is the single most significant financing impediment on a contaminated property in Illinois. Most conventional lenders, including CMBS conduits, life companies, and agency programs, will not fund into active enforcement without an NFR or at minimum a clearly defined remediation cost estimate with escrow reserves held by the lender. The Chicago market has seen deals structured with lender-held environmental reserve accounts equal to 125% to 150% of the estimated remediation cost, but this requires a credible and capped estimate, which a plume of undetermined extent cannot provide.

1,4-Dioxane as a Distinct Underwriting Challenge. Unlike PCE and TCE, which have long-established regulatory frameworks and treatment technologies, 1,4-dioxane presents a more complex underwriting profile. It does not readily adsorb to soil particles, which means it travels faster and farther in groundwater than co-mingled chlorinated solvents. Illinois has adopted a groundwater quality standard of 35 micrograms per liter for 1,4-dioxane, and IEPA enforcement around this compound has become more aggressive as analytical detection capabilities have improved. Lenders who have internal environmental counsel familiar with Illinois regulatory history will recognize this as a potentially open-ended remediation exposure. Most lenders do not. CLS CRE's role in this engagement included explaining to the creditors' counsel why 1,4-dioxane creates a larger financing discount than a PCE-only plume of equivalent concentration would.

Below-Market Rents and Lease Rollover Risk. A property with in-place rents 25% to 30% below current market presents a two-sided underwriting story. On the upside, lease rollover should produce materially higher income. On the downside, the current tenants may be in place precisely because below-market rents offset the stigma of occupying a property with a known environmental issue. Underwriters ask whether those tenants will renew at market rents once the contamination is disclosed during lease negotiations. The answer is often uncertain, and that uncertainty is a credit negative that compounds the environmental discount on LTV.

Lender Environmental Diligence Requirements. Any lender who advances funds on this property must obtain a Phase I Environmental Site Assessment and, given the known contamination, a Phase II. They will also require review of the IEPA correspondence file, the site conceptual model prepared by the environmental consultant, and the proposed remedial action plan if one has been submitted. The cost and timeline of that diligence narrows the lender field to institutions with in-house environmental expertise or established relationships with environmental counsel. Community banks without that infrastructure are effectively excluded.

The Chicago Lender Market

Balance-Sheet Banks with Environmental Capability. Chicago's large regional banks, including institutions with national commercial real estate platforms headquartered or significantly active in the market, do make loans on contaminated industrial properties when the contamination is well-characterized and the remediation path is clear. These lenders typically require the NFR letter or a lender-held reserve equal to 150% of remediation cost, a demonstrated performing tenancy, and DSCR coverage of at least 1.30x. Pricing for this risk profile runs 200 to 300 basis points over SOFR on a floating-rate basis, or note rates in the 7.50% to 8.25% range on fixed-rate structures as of mid-2024.

Debt Funds and Private Credit. Non-bank lenders and private debt funds represent the most flexible capital for contaminated industrial assets in Chicago. These lenders underwrite to higher yields (current rates of 9.00% to 11.00% with origination fees of 1.5% to 2.5%) but will accept environmental risk that conventional lenders will not, particularly when secured by income-producing real estate with positive DSCR. Maximum proceeds from this category are typically 55% to 60% LTV, and loan terms are short, generally 12 to 36 months, structured as bridge loans. For a bankruptcy or enforcement proceeding requiring certainty of execution, a debt fund can often close in 45 to 60 days where a bank might require 90 to 120 days of diligence.

CMBS Conduit Lenders. Conduit financing on contaminated industrial product in Illinois is essentially unavailable without an NFR letter. CMBS rating agencies require clean environmental reports, and securitization investors are not equipped to hold environmental tail risk. This is a firm market constraint, not a negotiating position.

Life Insurance Companies. Life company lenders active in Chicago, several of whom have Midwest-focused platforms with specific industrial mandates, are generally the lowest-cost execution for institutional-quality industrial assets. However, they are the most conservative on environmental matters. Life companies typically will not lend on sites with active regulatory enforcement under any circumstances, making them irrelevant to this financing scenario until an NFR letter is obtained.

What Attorneys Handling Chicago CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to Retain a CRE Financing Expert. Attorneys should engage a CRE financing expert any time a dispute involves questions about whether a property could have been refinanced, what loan proceeds were achievable at a given point in time, whether a borrower had access to capital sufficient to cure a default, or whether a lender's refusal to modify a loan was consistent with market practice. In bankruptcy proceedings, financing feasibility testimony is often central to plan confirmation, particularly where a plan proposes to reinstate or refinance secured debt. In creditor enforcement matters like this one, the expert's opinion on maximum supportable loan directly determines the feasibility of a sale or refinancing as an alternative to foreclosure.

What a Financing Expert Answers That an Appraiser Cannot. Appraisers determine value. A financing expert determines what portion of that value a lender will actually advance, under what conditions, at what cost, and on what timeline. These are categorically different questions. An appraiser operating under USPAP standards will determine the market value of a contaminated industrial property, but will not tell you whether any lender active in Chicago will fund a loan on that property tomorrow, what DSCR threshold they will apply, or how they will treat the environmental liability in their credit memo. Courts and plan trustees need both analyses, and conflating them produces analytical gaps that opposing counsel will exploit.

Qualifications That Matter. A qualified CRE financing expert should have current, active relationships with the lenders they describe in their opinion. Retired bankers can speak to historical credit policy but frequently cannot speak to current market conditions with the specificity that litigation requires. CLS CRE maintains over 1,000 active lender relationships across all categories, has closed approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, and operates in all 50 states. That current market access is what allows us to state, with specificity, which lenders are open to a contaminated Chicago industrial deal and at what terms, rather than speaking in generalities about what lenders "typically" do.

Information Counsel Should Gather Before Engagement. Before retaining a financing expert, counsel should assemble the current rent roll with lease expiration dates and rent amounts, the trailing 12-month operating statement, all available environmental reports including Phase I, Phase II, and any IEPA correspondence, the existing loan documents including the note, deed of trust, and any modifications, and the current appraisal if one exists. The more complete this package is at engagement, the faster and more defensible the expert's analysis will be. Gaps in the environmental characterization specifically will delay the opinion and may require the expert to qualify their conclusions.

Use of Financing Testimony at Trial or in a Plan Hearing. Financing feasibility testimony is most powerful when it is specific, grounded in current market data, and presented through a witness who can demonstrate active market participation. At plan confirmation hearings, financing experts are frequently used to rebut objections that a proposed refinancing is not feasible, or conversely to support a creditor's argument that the debtor could not refinance and therefore a liquidating plan is the only viable path. At trial in a lender liability or commercial real estate dispute, financing expert testimony establishes what a reasonable lender would have done and what the market would have supported, which frames the damages analysis.

Key Takeaways

  • Active regulatory enforcement without an Illinois NFR letter is a severe financing impediment but not an absolute bar. Debt funds and select balance-sheet lenders will advance proceeds on contaminated Chicago industrial assets when income is strong and the environmental exposure is bounded by a credible remediation cost estimate with lender-held reserves.
  • 1,4-dioxane contamination carries a larger financing discount than chlorinated-solvent-only plumes because of its groundwater mobility, Illinois's specific regulatory standards, and the open-ended remediation cost profile it presents to lenders unfamiliar with the compound.
  • The LTV constraint is typically the binding loan-sizing factor on contaminated assets, not the DSCR calculation. Lenders apply a contamination discount to appraised value and then limit advance rates to 55% to 60% of that reduced figure, which can produce a maximum loan materially below what income alone would support.
  • Below-market rents compound the environmental discount by raising lease rollover questions that underwriters cannot resolve in the borrower's favor without near-term lease renewals at market rates in hand.
  • The financing universe for this asset type narrows to debt funds and select regional banks with environmental underwriting

Expert Witness: Financing a Contaminated Industrial Property in Utica, NY

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Environmental Contamination Analysis

The Utica Market Context

Utica, New York sits at the center of the Mohawk Valley, a corridor that has undergone a slow but measurable industrial transition over the past three decades. The city's legacy as a manufacturing hub, once anchored by textile, metals fabrication, and defense-related production, left behind a significant inventory of industrial and warehouse product ranging from older multi-tenant flex buildings to large single-tenant distribution facilities along Route 5, the arterial connectors to I-90, and the rail-served parcels concentrated in the city's east and west industrial zones. That legacy is both the asset and the liability in cases like the one described here.

Utica's industrial market is comparatively thin by institutional standards. Vacancy in the Utica-Rome MSA's industrial sector has historically ranged between 8% and 14%, depending on the vintage and size cohort of space being measured. Older heavy manufacturing stock, which is precisely the category most likely to carry environmental stigma, accounts for a disproportionate share of that vacancy. Net absorption has been modestly positive in recent cycles, driven largely by regional distribution demand and some light manufacturing activity tied to defense and semiconductor supply chain investment upstate, but the Utica market has not experienced the industrial rent compression that markets like Albany or Buffalo have seen from large-scale logistics deployment. Market rents for functional warehouse and light industrial product in the Utica submarket currently trend in the range of $5.00 to $8.50 per square foot NNN, depending on clear height, dock configuration, and location relative to the interstate. Buildings with meaningful deferred maintenance, environmental clouds, or obsolete infrastructure trade and lease at the low end of that range, frequently below it.

Cap rates for stabilized industrial assets in Utica reflect the market's secondary status. Institutional buyers are largely absent. The buyer pool for stabilized Utica industrial is dominated by regional private investors, owner-users, and occasionally opportunity-focused funds that price in the illiquidity premium. Cap rates for clean, stabilized NNN industrial in this market have generally ranged from 7.5% to 9.5% over the past 24 months. For encumbered assets, including those with environmental conditions of record, that spread widens materially. This context is not incidental to the financing analysis; it defines the ceiling on what any lender will accept as collateral value and therefore caps the maximum supportable loan before a single underwriting assumption is modeled.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by counsel representing a class of secured creditors in a regulatory enforcement proceeding involving an industrial warehouse property in the Utica market. The subject property was a single-tenant, net-leased industrial building with a long-term NNN lease in place. The tenant was a creditworthy operating company, and the lease contained standard triple-net provisions that transferred property-level expenses, including routine environmental compliance costs, to the tenant. However, the lease rate had been set several years prior at a figure now demonstrably below current market, creating a rent gap that affected the income-based valuation and, consequently, the loanable value.

The regulatory enforcement matter centered on confirmed soil and groundwater contamination, specifically 1,4-dioxane and chlorinated volatile organic compounds consistent with historical industrial solvent use on the site. A remediation plan had been submitted to the New York State Department of Environmental Conservation, but a No Further Action letter had not been issued, and the scope and timeline of active remediation remained open. The property was encumbered by senior mortgage debt that the creditors sought to have retired through a refinancing transaction. Counsel retained CLS CRE to answer two discrete questions: first, whether first-mortgage financing was available in the current market given the environmental condition; and second, if financing was available, what was the maximum loan amount supportable under market underwriting standards. The answer to each question had direct bearing on the plan of reorganization being negotiated among parties.

Our engagement required us to analyze the property's income stream, model the financing scenarios that would be available across the relevant lender universe, evaluate how lenders specifically underwrite environmental risk in the context of collateral eligibility and loan sizing, and provide a supportable opinion on both the availability and the maximum quantum of financing.

The Financial Analysis

The income analysis began with the in-place lease. The subject property was generating gross rental income at a contracted rate approximating $5.25 per square foot NNN on a building of approximately 95,000 square feet, producing an annual base rent of roughly $498,750. Because the lease was triple-net, property-level operating expenses, including taxes, insurance, and maintenance, were passed through to the tenant. The landlord's primary exposure was management, which in a single-tenant NNN structure is minimal. After a modest vacancy and credit reserve, stabilized NOI attributable to the in-place lease was estimated at approximately $480,000 annually.

The critical question in any income-based underwriting is whether the lender will underwrite to the in-place cash flow or to a stabilized market rent assumption. For an environmentally encumbered asset in a secondary market, the answer is unambiguous: lenders underwrite to the lesser of in-place income or market income, applying a haircut for the environmental risk and the below-market lease. In this case, the in-place rent was already below market, so there was no inflated income to discount. The $480,000 NOI figure was accepted as the underwriting baseline.

Market standard DSCR thresholds for industrial collateral in secondary markets with environmental conditions of record run at 1.35x to 1.45x for conventional lenders, compared to the 1.20x to 1.25x that applies to clean, stabilized institutional product. The elevated DSCR reflects the lender's recognition that environmental liability can accelerate remediation costs, reduce tenant retention probability at lease expiration, and impair the lender's ability to liquidate collateral in a default scenario. Applying a 1.40x DSCR to the $480,000 NOI produces a maximum annual debt service of approximately $342,857.

Rate and amortization assumptions were set at market for this collateral type. Conventional bank pricing for encumbered secondary-market industrial in 2024 runs approximately 7.25% to 8.00% on a 5-year fixed or adjustable structure with a 20-year or 25-year amortization schedule. We modeled two scenarios: a 7.75% rate on a 25-year amortization, and an 8.00% rate on a 20-year amortization, representing the range most likely to be quoted by the lender categories willing to engage this asset.

At 7.75% over 25 years, a monthly payment of $342,857 divided by 12 months yields a monthly debt service cap of approximately $28,571. The loan constant at 7.75% / 25-year amortization is approximately 0.08752 annually, or 0.007293 monthly. Dividing the monthly debt service cap of $28,571 by the monthly constant of 0.007293 produces a maximum loan of approximately $3.916 million. At 8.00% over 20 years, the loan constant rises to approximately 0.10036 annually, producing a maximum supportable loan of approximately $3.414 million.

LTV constraints impose a second, independent ceiling. Lenders in this collateral category and market will typically limit advance rates to 55% to 65% of appraised value for environmentally impaired properties. If the appraised value, reflecting both the below-market lease and the environmental stigma discount, is in the range of $5.5 million to $6.5 million (consistent with capitalized NOI at a 7.5% to 8.75% cap rate), then the LTV ceiling of 60% produces a maximum loan of $3.3 million to $3.9 million. The convergence of the DSCR-constrained maximum and the LTV-constrained maximum in the $3.4 million to $3.9 million range provides a reasonably well-defined answer to the quantum question: financing is supportable, but it is capped at a level that may be insufficient to retire the full senior debt balance, depending on what that balance is. That shortfall, if any, is the gap that defines the creditor dispute.

The Complicating Factors

1,4-Dioxane and Chlorinated VOC Contamination as Collateral Risk. Lenders do not underwrite environmental contamination as a legal or regulatory abstraction. They underwrite it as a direct threat to collateral value and liquidation certainty. 1,4-dioxane is a particularly problematic contaminant from a financing perspective because it is highly mobile in groundwater, does not bind to soil particles in ways that permit traditional containment, and has no established Maximum Contaminant Level under federal Safe Drinking Water Act regulations as of the time of this writing (though EPA has proposed one). Its detection triggers regulatory response but leaves the remediation cost and timeline deeply uncertain. Chlorinated VOCs, including trichloroethylene and tetrachloroethylene, are better-understood by lenders but carry their own stigma, particularly in New York State, where NYSDEC has broad enforcement authority and a track record of aggressive remedial requirements.

Lenders that will consider this collateral at all will require, at minimum, a current Phase II Environmental Site Assessment, a remediation cost estimate from a licensed environmental professional, confirmation that a remediation plan has been submitted and is in regulatory review, and evidence that the responsible party for remediation has been identified and is not the current owner or the lender's prospective borrower. The absence of a No Further Action letter is not automatically disqualifying, but it increases the due diligence burden substantially and reduces the lender universe to those with dedicated environmental risk underwriting capacity.

Below-Market Rent and Lease Expiration Risk. The below-market lease creates a compounding problem. At lease expiration, the landlord's ability to retain the tenant or re-lease to a replacement tenant is constrained by the environmental cloud on the property. A tenant willing to absorb below-market rent on a contaminated site is a tenant who knows they have leverage. Lenders model this scenario explicitly: what happens to NOI at lease rollover if the tenant departs, if remediation is incomplete, or if market rents continue to drift from the in-place rate? The probability of a vacancy event at rollover on an environmentally burdened asset is assigned a higher probability than on a clean asset, which feeds directly into the lender's capitalized value and LTV calculations.

The New York State Regulatory Environment. NYSDEC's Brownfield Cleanup Program and the associated liability release provisions can meaningfully improve a property's financing prospects, but enrollment and completion take time, and the program does not apply uniformly to all contamination scenarios. New York State's strict liability framework for site cleanup, combined with the state's dense regulatory infrastructure, means that lenders with limited New York environmental counsel relationships will often decline to engage rather than invest in diligence on an uncertain remediation path.

Lender Recourse Requirements. Most lenders willing to originate against environmentally impaired collateral in a secondary market like Utica will require full personal guaranty from a creditworthy sponsor or, alternatively, environmental indemnification from a well-capitalized indemnitor. In a secured creditor reorganization scenario, identifying a qualifying guarantor is frequently the binding constraint, not the income or LTV math.

The Utica Lender Market

The Utica market does not attract CMBS execution for this asset type and this complexity level. CMBS conduit lenders require clean title, environmental clearance, and standardized collateral; an industrial property with open remediation and below-market rents in a secondary New York market does not fit any conduit credit box. Life company lenders are similarly unavailable. Life companies have tightened their secondary-market industrial criteria materially since 2022, and environmental conditions of record are a hard exclusion for the vast majority of life company programs.

The realistic lender universe for this scenario consists of three categories. The first is regional and community banks with New York State industrial lending programs. Several banks headquartered or with significant operations in Central New York have the environmental underwriting capacity and the relationship orientation to engage this type of credit. These institutions typically lend on a recourse basis, require a 1.35x or better DSCR, apply LTVs in the 55% to 65% range, and price in the 7.50% to 8.25% range on 3-to-5-year terms. They will require extensive environmental documentation and their own independent Phase II review.

The second category is the SBA 504 program, which can be deployed for owner-user scenarios but is not applicable to pure investment or creditor-driven refinancing transactions. If the fact pattern changes to involve an owner-occupant, SBA 504 introduces a lower down payment structure and longer amortization, but that is not the case here.

The third category is bridge and private credit lenders. Several non-bank lenders with environmental collateral experience will originate first-mortgage bridge loans on this type of asset, typically at higher rates (9.00% to 11.50% interest-only or short amortization), lower LTVs (50% to 55%), and shorter terms (12 to 36 months). These executions are appropriate as a refinancing bridge to remediation completion and eventual conventional takeout, but the higher debt service reduces the maximum loan size available under the DSCR test, often producing loan amounts materially below the conventional scenario. What Attorneys Handling Utica CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to Retain a CRE Financing Expert. Any litigation or contested proceeding in which the availability, cost, or quantum of commercial real estate debt is a material issue requires independent expert testimony from someone who currently originates commercial mortgage debt professionally. The relevant scenarios include plan of reorganization disputes, fraudulent transfer litigation where value is contested, lender liability matters, breach of commitment claims, and valuation disputes in which the income approach is contested. If the question is "could this property have been refinanced at a particular time on particular terms," that is a financing expert question, not an appraisal question.

What a CRE Financing Expert Provides That an Appraiser Does Not. An appraiser determines value. A financing expert determines what the capital markets would actually lend against that value, on what terms, under what conditions, and through which lender categories. These are different questions with different answers. An appraiser may conclude that a property has a value of $6 million; a financing expert will tell you that the maximum first-mortgage loan available in the current market against that $6 million asset, given the environmental condition and lease structure, is $3.6 million, and that only three lender categories will touch it at all. That gap between value and loanable value is frequently the central issue in a creditors' rights dispute.

Qualifications That Matter. The relevant expertise is current, active commercial mortgage origination experience across multiple lender types and transaction structures. A retired banker who specialized in C&I lending or residential mortgage underwriting is not qualified to opine on the CRE capital markets. An academic with real estate finance credentials but no deal execution background is similarly insufficient. The expert should have demonstrable, current deal volume across relevant asset types, active lender relationships, and experience in the specific complexity category at issue. At CLS CRE, our approximately $1 billion in transaction volume and active engagement across 50 states provides that foundation.

What Counsel Should Gather Before the Engagement. Before retaining a financing expert, counsel should assemble the current rent roll and lease abstracts, the most recent property operating statements (at least 3 years), all Phase I and Phase II environmental reports, any remediation plans and regulatory correspondence, the existing loan documents including any environmental carve-outs from non-recourse provisions, and any prior appraisals or broker opinions of value. The financing expert will work from this record to model the underwriting and identify the relevant lender universe.

How Financing Expert Testimony Is Used. In a plan of reorganization hearing, the financing expert opines on whether a proposed refinancing is feasible and at what loan amount, which bears directly on whether a plan is confirmable. In lender liability matters, the expert may opine on whether a lender's conduct conformed to market standards. In fraudulent transfer disputes, the expert may opine on whether financing was available to a buyer on the alleged terms at the alleged time. Expert reports in federal proceedings must conform to Rule 26 requirements; CLS CRE has provided Rule 26-compliant expert reports and deposition and trial testimony in multiple jurisdictions.

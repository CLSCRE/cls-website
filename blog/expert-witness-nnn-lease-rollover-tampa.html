Expert Witness: NNN Lease Rollover Upside and Financing Impact in Tampa, FL

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Below-Market NNN Lease and Rollover Upside Analysis

The Tampa Market Context

Tampa has matured into one of the most closely watched commercial real estate markets in the Southeast, and that maturity has come with complexity. The metro's population growth, driven by in-migration from higher-cost coastal markets, has compressed cap rates across most asset classes while simultaneously exposing certain deal structures to financing stress that was masked during the low-rate environment of 2020 through 2022. For single-tenant net lease assets specifically, Tampa presents a dynamic that attorneys and borrowers need to understand precisely: the market's rental growth story has been real, but it has not been uniform, and the gap between in-place rents on older leases and current market rents has become a material underwriting issue in financing disputes.

Net lease industrial properties in Tampa's core submarkets, including the I-75 corridor, East Tampa, and the Port Tampa Bay industrial nodes, have seen asking rents climb significantly over the past several years. Industrial vacancy across the Tampa Bay metro has tightened to levels below five percent in prime locations, with some submarkets reporting functional vacancy closer to two to three percent for modern bulk and shallow-bay product. That compression has driven market rents upward, which means a single-tenant NNN property leased five or more years ago at rents that made sense at the time may now carry in-place rent that is meaningfully below where a new lease would price today. That below-market condition is not a problem for the occupant; it is a potential windfall for a future landlord. But it is a financing problem today, and it becomes a litigation problem when that financing gap is at the center of a dispute over value, damages, or restructuring.

Retail NNN product in Tampa follows a different trajectory. Freestanding retail along corridors such as Dale Mabry Highway, Bruce B. Downs Boulevard, and the US-19 corridor in Pinellas County has held value well where credit is strong, but the cap rate spread between investment-grade tenancy and non-investment-grade tenancy has widened. Lenders have become increasingly sensitive to that distinction when sizing loans on single-tenant retail. Office NNN or gross-modified lease structures in Tampa remain challenged at the lender level despite some submarkets showing leasing velocity improvements. For purposes of this analysis, the relevant dynamic is consistent across asset types: below-market in-place rent creates a financing constraint that rollover upside does not automatically solve, and that constraint has real legal and economic consequences in disputes.

The Engagement

Counsel representing a party in a commercial real estate financing dispute retained Commercial Lending Solutions to provide an expert analysis and, if necessary, expert testimony concerning the financing implications of a below-market NNN lease on a single-tenant commercial property located in the Tampa metropolitan area. The asset was an established, freestanding single-tenant commercial building with a remaining lease term of several years, occupied by a regional-credit tenant under a NNN lease structure that had been executed during a period when Tampa market rents were materially lower than where they trade today.

The core legal and financial questions that counsel needed answered fell into three categories. First, what was the maximum loan amount supportable by the in-place rent stream under institutional underwriting standards as of the relevant date, and how did that figure compare to the loan amount assumed or claimed by the opposing party? Second, did the rollover upside, meaning the potential for a higher rent upon lease expiration, provide any present financing value, and if so, how did lenders actually account for it in their credit analysis? Third, did the lease structure, including its below-market rent, remaining term, and tenant credit profile, enable or preclude refinancing at specific loan-to-value and debt service coverage ratio thresholds that were material to the dispute?

CLS CRE was engaged to produce a Rule 26-compliant expert report, perform the underlying financial modeling, and be prepared to provide deposition and trial testimony on lender market standards, underwriting methodology, and the practical financing outcomes that a borrower in this position would actually face in the Tampa capital markets.

The Financial Analysis

The methodology began with the in-place net operating income, which for a NNN lease means the annualized base rent after accounting for any landlord-level expenses not passed through under the lease structure. NNN leases shift substantially all operating costs, including taxes, insurance, and maintenance, to the tenant, so landlord NOI in a true NNN structure is closely approximated by base rent minus any management or administrative overhead the landlord retains. For this analysis, we used the lease's scheduled annual base rent as the starting point and applied a conservative vacancy and credit loss factor consistent with institutional underwriting norms, typically one to two percent on single-tenant assets with creditworthy occupancy, to arrive at effective gross income before arriving at NOI.

For the subject asset, using plausible Tampa market parameters, we modeled an in-place annual base rent of approximately $520,000, reflecting a below-market rate for the submarket and asset type. After applying a one percent credit loss allowance and minimal management expense, the underwritable NOI for financing purposes was approximately $514,800.

Institutional lenders in this asset class and market apply a minimum debt service coverage ratio of 1.25x as the standard floor for permanent financing. Some balance sheet lenders and CMBS programs apply 1.20x, but for single-tenant assets with non-investment-grade or regional credit tenants in a market like Tampa, 1.25x is the appropriate benchmark and was the threshold applied in our analysis. Using a 30-year amortization schedule and a coupon rate of 6.75 percent, which reflected prevailing fixed-rate pricing for this loan type during the relevant period, the maximum annual debt service supportable at 1.25x DSCR against an NOI of $514,800 was approximately $411,840. That translates to a maximum loan amount of roughly $5,650,000 when sized by debt service alone.

The LTV constraint then becomes the binding test. If the property's appraised value, based on its in-place income capitalized at a market cap rate, was established at $7,200,000 using a 7.25 percent cap rate applied to the in-place NOI, then the DSCR-constrained loan of $5,650,000 represents a loan-to-value ratio of approximately 78.5 percent. Most institutional lenders cap single-tenant NNN loans at 65 to 70 percent LTV, which at a $7,200,000 value would produce a maximum LTV-constrained loan of $4,680,000 to $5,040,000. The LTV cap becomes the binding constraint in this scenario, not the debt service calculation, and the maximum supportable loan is therefore $4,680,000 to $5,040,000 depending on the lender's specific program.

This is the analysis that resolves one of the central questions in financing disputes involving below-market NNN assets: which constraint binds? In many cases involving below-market rent, the DSCR and LTV constraints point in the same direction, both limiting the loan significantly relative to what the property might support at market rents. In this scenario, we were able to quantify the financing gap precisely and demonstrate the loan amount that a qualified borrower could actually obtain in the Tampa market on the relevant date, a figure that differed materially from the amount assumed in the opposing party's analysis.

The Complicating Factors

Below-Market Rent and Its Effect on Value-in-Place

The most significant complicating factor in this type of engagement is the structural disconnect between what a lender will underwrite and what a valuation expert might argue the property is worth on a stabilized or going-forward basis. Appraisers working for a borrower or an equity holder will often prepare a "value upon rollover" analysis that projects market rent at lease expiration and capitalizes that income stream to produce a higher stabilized value. That analysis is not wrong as a matter of valuation theory. It is, however, largely irrelevant to how a lender underwrites the loan today. Lenders underwrite to in-place income. They do not lend against projected rollover income unless they are making a bridge loan with a specific business plan tied to that rollover, and even then, the structure and pricing are entirely different from permanent financing.

In Tampa's current lending environment, the argument that rollover upside justifies a higher loan amount against in-place below-market rent is an argument that will not survive scrutiny with any institutional lender. The lender's credit committee cares about whether the existing lease, at its existing rent, supports the debt service for the loan term. If the lease expires in three years and the property must be refinanced at that point, the lender is underwriting today's known income, not tomorrow's projected income. That is the standard, and departing from it in an expert analysis exposes the opinion to significant impeachment.

Lease Term Remaining and Refinancing Risk

Related to the above is the question of lease term remaining. Most institutional lenders, including life companies, CMBS conduits, and bank portfolio lenders active in Tampa, require that the lease term extend through the loan maturity, or at minimum that there is a meaningful cushion between lease expiration and loan maturity. A loan with a ten-year term against a lease with three years remaining is a lease rollover event occurring mid-loan, and virtually no institutional lender will structure permanent financing on that basis without a significant credit enhancement, such as a cash escrow, letter of credit, or guaranty tied to re-leasing. Some lenders will simply decline to quote.

Where the lease term does not support a standard ten-year loan, the borrower's financing options shift toward shorter-term bridge products, which carry materially higher rates, lower LTV limits, and personal recourse in many cases. In the Tampa market, bridge lenders active in single-tenant product will generally price at 200 to 350 basis points over term SOFR with maximum LTVs of 65 percent and full or partial recourse. The economic difference between a permanent loan and a bridge loan on this type of asset is substantial, and quantifying that difference is often directly relevant to the damages or value question at issue in a dispute.

Tenant Credit and its Interaction with Below-Market Rent

Single-tenant NNN financing is heavily credit-tiered. An investment-grade tenant, rated BBB- or better by a nationally recognized rating agency, commands materially different financing terms than a regional credit tenant with no public rating. In Tampa, where a significant share of the NNN inventory is occupied by regional retailers, service businesses, and healthcare operators without investment-grade ratings, lenders apply additional haircuts to LTV and DSCR thresholds that compound the impact of below-market rent. A property with below-market rent and a non-rated tenant faces both a lower value in the appraisal and a more restrictive credit box in the financing, and the combination can reduce the supportable loan amount by fifteen to twenty-five percent relative to a comparable investment-grade tenanted property at market rent.

The Tampa Lender Market

Understanding which lenders are active in Tampa for single-tenant NNN product at varying complexity levels is essential to forming a credible expert opinion. The Tampa market benefits from meaningful institutional depth, which means there is a genuine universe of lenders from which to benchmark, rather than having to extrapolate from national averages.

Life insurance company lenders are active in Tampa for NNN product that meets their credit requirements, which typically means investment-grade tenancy, lease terms of at least ten years, and properties in primary or secondary submarkets. Life companies price at the tightest spreads, often 140 to 175 basis points over comparable Treasury yields, and will lend to 65 percent LTV with 1.25x DSCR at a minimum. They are the gold standard for clean, creditworthy NNN assets but will not entertain the below-market, shorter-term lease scenario described in this analysis without significant structural mitigation.

CMBS conduit lenders represent a meaningful portion of the Tampa NNN lending market and offer more flexibility on tenant credit profiles than life companies, but they are rigorous underwriters of lease term and in-place NOI. CMBS loans on single-tenant assets require that the lease extend to or beyond the loan's maturity, and below-market rent situations will produce lower loan proceeds for the same collateral value relative to a market-rate deal. Spreads in the current environment run 200 to 250 basis points over Treasuries for this product type.

Regional and community banks dominate the Tampa market for deals that do not fit institutional boxes. Florida-chartered and Southeast regional banks with active CRE lending platforms have historically been aggressive in Tampa and continue to compete on shorter-term balance sheet loans, adjustable-rate products, and relationship-based credit structures that give more weight to borrower financial strength than to pure lease underwriting. These lenders are more likely to credit rollover upside, especially where the borrower has a demonstrated track record, but they still impose LTV caps of 65 to 70 percent and DSCR floors of 1.20x to 1.25x.

Debt fund and bridge lenders operate in Tampa primarily for transitional assets, lease-up situations, or cases where permanent financing is not available due to short lease term or other credit issues. These are not dispute-resolution-friendly loan structures in a valuation sense; they represent a significantly more expensive and less favorable financing outcome that itself can be evidence of financing impairment in a damages analysis.

What Attorneys Handling Tampa CRE Disputes Need to Know

A CRE financing expert is not a replacement for an appraiser, and the distinction matters. An appraiser opines on value. A CRE financing expert opines on the financing market, lender standards, underwriting methodology, and the practical capital markets outcome that a specific asset and borrower would face in a specific market at a specific point in time. Those are related but different questions, and courts and arbitrators increasingly understand the difference.

Counsel should consider retaining a CRE financing expert when the dispute involves any of the following: a claimed inability to refinance a maturing loan; a dispute over whether a lender's commitment was commercially reasonable; damages tied to lost financing proceeds or increased borrowing costs; a valuation dispute where the opposing expert has relied on financing assumptions that are inconsistent with market reality; or a restructuring or plan confirmation proceeding where financing feasibility is contested.

What distinguishes a qualified CRE financing expert from a retired banker or generalist consultant is active market participation. Lender underwriting standards, credit box parameters, and pricing change constantly. An expert who has not been actively placing loans in the market within the past twelve to twenty-four months will struggle to testify credibly about what a lender would or would not have done at a specific point in time. At CLS CRE, we have approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, relationships with more than 1,000 lenders, and active deal flow across 50 states. That market presence is what makes our expert opinions grounded in actual lender behavior rather than historical recollection.

Before retaining a CRE financing expert, counsel should gather the following materials at a minimum: the executed lease or leases with all amendments; rent rolls and historical occupancy records; the most recent appraisal and any prior appraisals; any loan applications, term sheets, commitment letters, or lender correspondence; financial statements for the borrowing entity; and any comparable lease or sale transaction data that either party intends to rely upon. The expert will need these materials to form opinions that can survive Daubert challenge and cross-examination.

At trial or in a plan confirmation hearing, CRE financing expert testimony is most effective when it is anchored to market data rather than abstract principles. Testimony that a lender "would not have made this loan" is most persuasive when supported by the specific underwriting matrices of identified lender categories, the quantified NOI and DSCR calculations that demonstrate why the loan fails, and comparable transactions that establish what the market was actually doing at the relevant time. Expert testimony that relies on generalities about lending standards without transaction-specific grounding is vulnerable on cross-examination and unlikely to carry weight with a sophisticated factfinder.

Key Takeaways

  • In-place rent, not projected rollover rent, is what lenders underwrite. Rollover upside has no present financing value in a permanent loan structure, and any expert opinion that credits rollover income to increase a current loan amount is inconsistent with institutional market standards.
  • The binding constraint can be DSCR or LTV, and identifying which one controls is essential to the analysis. Below-market rent typically reduces both the income-based loan sizing and the appraised value simultaneously, compounding the financing impairment rather than simply reducing it linearly.
  • Lease term remaining relative to loan maturity is a critical underwriting variable. A lease expiring before or shortly after loan maturity triggers either structural credit enhancements that increase cost, a shift to bridge financing, or a decline to lend, each of which has quantifiable economic consequences.
  • Tenant credit quality compounds the below-market rent problem. Non-investment-grade tenancy in a below-market rent scenario produces a materially more restrictive financing outcome than either factor alone, and that combination is common in Tampa's single-tenant NNN inventory.

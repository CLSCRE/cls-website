Expert Witness: How Construction Defects Affect CRE Financing in Salisbury, MD

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Construction Defect Impact on Financing

The Salisbury Market Context

Salisbury, Maryland occupies a position in the Mid-Atlantic commercial real estate landscape that attorneys and investors outside the region frequently underestimate. As the largest city on the Delmarva Peninsula and the regional hub for a combined statistical area of roughly 200,000 people, Salisbury functions as a distribution, healthcare, and retail services center for Delaware, Maryland, and Virginia's Eastern Shore. That geographic reality shapes how lenders think about collateral here and, critically, how construction defect disputes affect financing feasibility in ways that differ materially from larger gateway markets.

The Salisbury industrial and flex sector has benefited from last-mile logistics demand driven by population growth across the peninsula, with vacancy rates in the 5 to 7 percent range for Class B and C industrial product as of recent reporting periods. Lease rates for warehouse and distribution space in the Route 13 and Business Park corridors have climbed incrementally, now ranging from approximately $6.50 to $9.00 per square foot NNN depending on clear height, dock configuration, and age of construction. Multifamily assets, particularly workforce housing oriented toward healthcare workers, Salisbury University staff, and distribution sector employees, have maintained occupancy above 93 percent in stabilized properties, with effective rents ranging from $1,050 to $1,400 per unit per month for garden-style product. These are not gateway figures, but they are durable, and lender appetite for well-located Salisbury assets has remained consistent among regional banks, credit unions, and select national correspondent lenders.

What makes this market particularly consequential for construction defect litigation is the limited depth of the comparable sales pool. When a property is compromised by alleged defects, its position within a thin market is amplified. A 10 to 15 percent reduction in value in a market with few recent transactions creates disproportionate financing consequences, and the lender community's risk appetite for properties with known legal encumbrances narrows considerably outside of the top-tier institutional platform. Understanding that dynamic is foundational to any credible expert financing analysis in a Salisbury construction defect matter.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by counsel representing the property owner in a construction defect dispute involving a mid-size commercial asset in the Salisbury submarket. The property was a multifamily residential complex of approximately 80 to 100 units, constructed within the prior five years, and subject to active litigation alleging structural and envelope deficiencies including foundation irregularities, improper waterproofing at grade and below-grade connections, and HVAC system installation failures affecting multiple building sections. Counsel needed expert analysis to support damages claims and to counter opposing expert testimony that minimized the financial impact of the alleged defects.

The core questions presented to our firm were precisely the questions that require a capital markets specialist rather than a general appraiser. First, would a conventional or agency lender fund a refinance or acquisition loan on this property in its current condition with active construction defect litigation on record? Second, if financing was technically available, at what terms, and how did those terms compare to what the same property would support absent the defects and litigation? Third, could the defect-related impairments be quantified in financing terms, meaning maximum supportable loan, loan-to-value constraint, and debt service coverage ratio compression? And fourth, what was the range of lender categories that would even consider the loan given the litigation status, and what premiums or structural requirements would those lenders impose?

CLS CRE prepared a financing feasibility analysis and expert report addressing each of these questions with supporting market data, lender communication records, rate and underwriting assumptions sourced from our active lender relationships, and a side-by-side comparison of the defective versus defect-free financing scenarios.

The Financial Analysis

The methodology began with a reconstruction of the property's net operating income under two scenarios: the as-is condition reflecting the alleged defects, and the hypothetical defect-free condition representing what the asset would support if constructed to specification. This dual-scenario structure is essential in construction defect financing analysis because it isolates the financial damage attributable to the defects from general market conditions.

Defect-Free Scenario: A stabilized 90-unit garden-style multifamily asset in Salisbury at market rents of $1,200 per unit per month generates gross potential rental income of approximately $1,296,000 annually. Applying a 5 percent vacancy and credit loss assumption, consistent with the market's current absorption, yields effective gross income of approximately $1,231,200. Operating expenses for a property of this vintage and type in Salisbury, including management at 6 percent of EGI, maintenance, insurance, real estate taxes, and reserves for replacement, run approximately $430,000 to $460,000 per year on a stabilized basis. This produces a net operating income in the range of $775,000 to $800,000 annually. I use $785,000 as the midpoint for analysis.

Defect-Impaired Scenario: The alleged defects introduced two income-side impairments and one expense-side impairment. First, units in the affected building sections required temporary rent concessions and experienced higher-than-market vacancy due to habitability concerns, reducing effective occupancy to approximately 82 percent across the affected sections during the litigation period. Second, owner-paid utility costs increased due to HVAC system failures, adding approximately $38,000 annually in operating expense. Third, insurance underwriters required a higher property casualty premium once the litigation was disclosed, adding roughly $14,000 to annual operating costs. The result was a defect-impaired NOI in the range of $640,000 to $660,000. I use $650,000 as the midpoint.

DSCR Analysis: The market standard DSCR threshold for multifamily financing in a secondary market like Salisbury is 1.20x to 1.25x for conventional bank financing, and 1.25x for agency programs such as Freddie Mac Small Balance or Fannie Mae DUS. I applied a 1.25x DSCR threshold as the binding constraint, consistent with what the active lender universe in this market requires. Assuming a 30-year amortization and an interest rate of 6.75 percent (reflecting the rate environment applicable during the period analyzed, consistent with SOFR-based spreads on stabilized multifamily in the 200 to 250 basis point range over the five-year swap), the constant on a fully amortizing loan is approximately 7.78 percent annually.

Maximum annual debt service supportable under the defect-free NOI of $785,000 at 1.25x DSCR equals $628,000. Dividing by the annual constant of 7.78 percent produces a maximum supportable loan of approximately $8,072,000. At an appraised value of $9,800,000 for the defect-free asset (consistent with a 5.5 percent cap rate applied to $785,000 NOI in this market segment), the maximum loan represents approximately 82.4 percent of value, but most lenders cap multifamily LTV at 75 to 80 percent for conventional financing, meaning the binding LTV constraint actually holds the loan to approximately $7,840,000 at 80 percent of the defect-free value.

Under the defect-impaired scenario, maximum annual debt service at 1.25x DSCR drops to $520,000, producing a maximum supportable loan of approximately $6,683,000 based on cash flow alone. However, the LTV constraint compounds the problem. The defect-impaired NOI capitalized at a stress-adjusted 6.25 percent cap rate (reflecting the market discount lenders apply to litigation-encumbered assets) yields an indicated value of approximately $10,400,000 divided by a distressed cap rate, more precisely $650,000 divided by 6.25 percent, equaling $10,400,000. Wait, let me restate this correctly. At a 6.25 percent cap rate, the indicated value under defect-impaired NOI is $650,000 divided by 0.0625, which equals $10,400,000. But a lender will not use that value. Instead, the lender applies a separate collateral discount of 10 to 15 percent to account for the pending litigation and remediation uncertainty, bringing the lender's working value to approximately $8,840,000 to $9,360,000, and capping the loan at 70 to 75 percent LTV given the litigation encumbrance, which produces a collateral-constrained maximum loan of $6,188,000 to $7,020,000.

The net financing gap between the defect-free and defect-impaired scenarios on a maximum supportable loan basis is approximately $820,000 to $1,652,000, depending on the binding constraint used. This range represents a quantifiable, calculable financial harm that translates directly into damages in a construction defect matter.

The Complicating Factors

Active Litigation as a Lender Disqualifier: The single most significant complicating factor in construction defect financing is not the physical defect itself but the litigation surrounding it. Most conventional lenders, including community banks, regional banks, and agency lenders, require clear title and no material pending litigation affecting the property or the borrower's financial condition. A construction defect lawsuit that has been filed of record creates a cloud on the financing process that most institutional lenders will not underwrite around until a resolution, settlement, or judgment is final. In Salisbury's lender market, this disqualifies the majority of the conventional financing universe for the duration of active litigation, forcing borrowers into bridge or private credit at materially higher pricing.

Reserve and Remediation Escrow Requirements: Among the narrower group of lenders willing to consider a loan on a property with disclosed but unresolved construction defects, the near-universal structural requirement is a remediation escrow or repair reserve. Lenders will require an independent engineer's estimate of the total cost to remediate all identified defects, then hold back 110 to 125 percent of that estimate in a controlled escrow, typically with a third-party administrator. In Salisbury, where local construction cost data for remediation work runs approximately $85 to $120 per square foot for envelope and structural repairs on mid-rise wood-frame construction, a building of 80,000 square feet of gross building area could face remediation escrow requirements of $7 million to $12 million or more depending on defect scope. This escrow requirement directly reduces the net loan proceeds available to the borrower.

Rate Premium and Reduced Competitive Tension: When the financing universe contracts to bridge lenders and private credit due to litigation status, the borrower loses the competitive tension that drives favorable pricing. In the current environment, a stabilized Salisbury multifamily asset with no complications would price in the 6.50 to 7.00 percent range from conventional sources. The same asset with active construction defect litigation, if it can be financed at all, prices from bridge lenders in the 9.50 to 11.50 percent range with 1 to 2 percent origination fees and shorter loan terms of 12 to 24 months. This pricing differential compounds the annual cash flow impairment already caused by the defects themselves.

Secondary Market Limitations: Salisbury's position as a secondary market amplifies every risk factor. Lenders that might accept construction defect risk on a 300-unit asset in Baltimore's Inner Harbor submarket will not accept the same risk on an 80-unit asset in Salisbury because the liquidity exit is thinner. Bridge lenders routinely apply additional market risk haircuts of 5 to 10 percent to collateral values in markets with fewer than 20 comparable sales per year, and Salisbury's multifamily transaction volume typically falls within that range.

The Salisbury Lender Market

Understanding which lenders are actually active in Salisbury for this asset type is essential context for any financing feasibility opinion. The market is served by three distinct categories of capital, each with materially different credit parameters.

Community and Regional Banks: Several Maryland-chartered community banks and regional institutions with Eastern Shore footprints are the primary conventional lenders for stabilized commercial assets in Salisbury. These lenders typically fund to 75 percent LTV on multifamily, require 1.20x to 1.25x DSCR, price on a spread to prime or to the FHLB advance rate, and hold loans in portfolio. Their strength is relationship flexibility and local market knowledge. Their limitation in a construction defect context is that their credit committees are uniformly averse to properties with pending litigation, and most have internal policy language requiring resolution of material legal disputes before closing. Loan sizes in the $3 million to $10 million range are comfortable for this lender category in Salisbury.

Agency Lenders (Freddie Mac Small Balance and Fannie Mae): For stabilized multifamily assets that meet program criteria, agency execution provides the most competitive pricing and longest fixed-rate terms. However, agency underwriting guidelines explicitly require that the property have no material litigation outstanding, no known structural defects, and a property condition report with no deferred maintenance above the reserve-funded threshold. A property subject to active construction defect litigation will not qualify for agency execution until litigation is resolved and a post-remediation property condition assessment confirms the asset meets program standards. This effectively removes the most competitive and most patient capital from the picture for the duration of the dispute.

Bridge and Private Credit: A small number of national bridge lenders and private debt funds will consider Salisbury assets with litigation complications, but their underwriting is punishing. Advance rates of 60 to 65 percent of as-is value, rates in the 10 to 11 percent range, 18-month initial terms with one extension option, full recourse carve-outs expanded to include litigation-related losses, and origination fees of 1.5 to 2.0 points are representative terms for this risk profile. The borrower's effective cost of capital approximately doubles compared to conventional financing, and the loan-to-value compression alone can create a scenario in which the defective property cannot even qualify for sufficient bridge proceeds to sustain operations or fund remediation.

What Attorneys Handling Salisbury CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to Retain a CRE Financing Expert: Construction defect matters that involve commercial properties almost always have a financing damages component that neither the appraiser nor the construction expert can fully address. If your client lost the ability to refinance, had to pay off a maturing loan at distressed terms, or could not sell because a buyer could not obtain financing, those are financing feasibility damages. An appraiser can opine on value; a CRE financing expert opines on what the market would actually lend, at what terms, and what the delta is between the defect-impaired and defect-free financing scenario. These are different analyses requiring different expertise.

What a CRE Financing Expert Can Address That an Appraiser Cannot: An appraiser does not underwrite loans. A CRE financing expert can testify to the specific lender requirements that would apply, the market-standard DSCR thresholds, the actual rate environment for this asset type at this leverage point, the structural requirements lenders impose on litigation-encumbered collateral, and the size of the financing universe that would or would not consider the loan. These opinions require active, current capital markets experience, not valuation methodology.

Qualifications That Matter: Retaining a retired community banker or a general commercial broker as a financing expert in a complex construction defect matter will not withstand Daubert scrutiny on the financing-specific opinions. The expert should have active transaction experience across multiple lender categories, demonstrable lender relationships in the relevant market, experience structuring loans on complex or impaired assets, and a track record of providing expert opinions that have been tested in deposition and trial. At CLS CRE, our qualifications are grounded in approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, relationships with more than 1,000 lenders across 50 states, and direct engagement with the lender community on a current basis.

What Counsel Should Gather Before Engaging the Expert: The financing analysis depends heavily on the factual record. Counsel should assemble the existing loan documents including the note, deed of trust, and any loan modification or forbearance agreements; the property's operating statements for at least three years; the rent roll; any existing appraisal or broker opinion of value; the construction defect expert's scope of work and cost-of-repair estimate; and any lender correspondence reflecting the lender's position on the defects or litigation. The more complete the record, the more defensible and precise the financing opinion will be.

Use at Trial and in Settlement: A financing feasibility opinion in a construction defect matter serves multiple litigation functions. It supports the quantum of damages by converting physical impairment into a quantified capital markets penalty. It rebuts opposing expert testimony that minimizes financial harm by demonstrating what the actual lender market requires.

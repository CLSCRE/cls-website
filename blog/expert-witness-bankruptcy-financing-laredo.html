Expert Witness: CRE Financing Feasibility in Chapter 11 Proceedings in Laredo, TX

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Bankruptcy Exit Financing

The Laredo Market Context

Laredo, Texas occupies a singular position in North American commercial real estate. As the largest inland port on the U.S.-Mexico border and the busiest commercial land port of entry in the United States by trade volume, Laredo processes more than $300 billion in annual trade flowing through the Colombia-Solidarity International Bridge and the World Trade Bridge. That volume of cross-border commerce creates a commercial real estate market that behaves differently from virtually every other secondary Texas market. For attorneys and creditors evaluating the financing feasibility of a Chapter 11 asset in Laredo, understanding these structural market dynamics is not background noise. It is central to whether a plan of reorganization is confirmable.

The industrial sector dominates the Laredo CRE landscape in a way that is nearly without parallel outside of major logistics hubs. Industrial vacancy in Laredo has historically tracked well below 5%, and in the post-USMCA environment, demand for warehouse, cross-dock, and customs examination station space has pushed effective vacancy in modern Class A distribution product to near zero in certain submarkets along Mines Road and the Loop 20 corridor. Net asking rents for industrial have climbed from the mid-$5.00 per square foot range into the $7.50 to $9.00 per square foot range for newer cross-dock product, driven almost entirely by nearshoring activity and expanded maquiladora logistics requirements.

Retail follows the trade economy. Laredo consistently ranks among the highest-volume retail markets in Texas on a per-capita basis, anchored by cross-border shoppers who treat San Bernardo Avenue and Mall del Norte as primary shopping destinations. Multifamily fundamentals are supported by a young, growing workforce population tied to the freight, customs brokerage, and logistics sectors. Office product is the weakest CRE category in Laredo, with limited new supply but also limited demand, largely confined to professional services firms serving the trade corridor. These market realities directly affect what a senior lender will underwrite, at what loan-to-value, and at what debt service coverage threshold.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by counsel for the plan proponent in a Chapter 11 proceeding involving a commercial real estate asset located in the Laredo industrial submarket. The asset was a multi-tenant industrial warehouse facility of approximately 85,000 rentable square feet, with a mix of shorter-term logistics tenants and one credit-quality anchor tenant operating under a remaining lease term of approximately six years. The debtor had filed Chapter 11 following a dispute with its senior secured lender, and a competing plan had been proposed that required the court to evaluate whether the subject property could support first-mortgage debt sufficient to pay the senior creditor class in full at confirmation.

CLS CRE was engaged to provide an expert opinion responsive to two core legal and financial questions. First, could the property, in its current physical and tenancy condition, support first-mortgage financing in an amount sufficient to satisfy the allowed secured claim? Second, what categories of institutional lenders actively originate loans on commercial real estate assets that are or have recently been part of a bankruptcy estate, and what are their specific underwriting parameters for this asset type in this market? These are not questions an appraiser is positioned to answer. An appraisal establishes value. A financing feasibility opinion establishes whether that value can be monetized through senior debt, and on what terms, under current market conditions.

The Financial Analysis

The methodology begins with the income stream and works forward to a maximum supportable loan. In a bankruptcy context, there is often a tension between historical NOI, which may reflect the distress period, and stabilized NOI, which is what a lender will underwrite. My analysis addressed both and disclosed the assumptions underlying each.

The subject property was generating gross potential rent of approximately $680,000 annually based on in-place leases. After applying a 5% vacancy and credit loss allowance consistent with Laredo industrial market conditions and a management fee of 4% of effective gross income, operating expenses for insurance, property taxes, and maintenance yielded an underwritten net operating income of approximately $590,000. This figure was stress-tested against the anchor tenant's lease expiration timeline and compared against current market rent to confirm it was neither inflated nor artificially deflated by below-market leases.

The DSCR threshold applied was 1.25x, which represents the standard minimum for most institutional lenders underwriting stabilized industrial assets. Some CMBS programs will accept 1.20x, and certain balance sheet lenders in Texas markets will stretch to 1.20x for strong industrial product with credit tenancy, but 1.25x is the appropriate baseline for a financing feasibility opinion intended to define a reliable, executable financing. Applying the 1.25x threshold means that maximum annual debt service cannot exceed $472,000, which is NOI of $590,000 divided by 1.25.

Rate and amortization assumptions are critical to the calculation and must be grounded in current market conditions, not historical norms. For a first-mortgage loan on a Laredo industrial asset in the current rate environment, a 30-year amortization with a 10-year term is standard for CMBS and life company execution. For bank balance sheet financing, a 25-year amortization with a 5-year balloon is more typical. Using a fixed rate of 6.85%, which reflects the approximate spread over the 10-year U.S. Treasury applicable to this asset quality and market at the time of the analysis, and a 30-year amortization schedule, the maximum annual debt service of $472,000 supports a loan of approximately $6,600,000. The calculation is a straightforward present value of a 360-payment annuity at 6.85% annual rate, monthly payments of approximately $39,333.

The appraised value of the subject property was $9,200,000. The maximum supportable loan of $6,600,000 represents a loan-to-value ratio of approximately 71.7%, which sits comfortably within the 65% to 75% LTV range that institutional lenders apply to industrial assets in secondary Texas markets. This analysis confirmed that the property could support a first-mortgage loan sufficient to satisfy the senior secured claim, which was quantified at $5,900,000 in the plan, with meaningful LTV headroom remaining. The headroom matters because it signals to institutional lenders that the loan is not stretched and that the borrower retains meaningful equity, both of which reduce credit risk in the post-bankruptcy capital structure.

The Complicating Factors

Bankruptcy Estate Stigma and Lender Policy Restrictions. Many institutional lenders, including conventional bank balance sheet lenders and some CMBS conduit programs, maintain underwriting guidelines that restrict or prohibit originating new loans where the borrower entity has been a Chapter 11 debtor within the prior 24 to 36 months. This is not a legal prohibition. It is a credit policy decision. The mechanism varies by lender: some require a seasoning period after discharge, others require that the new borrowing entity have no prior bankruptcy history, meaning the reorganized debtor must be a newly formed entity with a clean credit profile. In structuring a plan around this constraint, the plan proponent must account for these restrictions when representing to the court that financing is available. An expert opinion that ignores lender policy on bankruptcy seasoning is incomplete.

Tenant Concentration and Lease Term Risk. In Laredo industrial, the anchor tenant is frequently the entire underwriting story. If a single tenant represents more than 50% of gross revenues and that tenant's lease expires within three years of projected loan origination, most institutional lenders will either decline or price the risk through a significantly reduced LTV or a lease co-terminus structure. In the subject engagement, the anchor tenant's six-year remaining term was sufficient to satisfy most lenders' minimum lease coverage requirements relative to the loan term, but counsel for the debtor needed to understand that any deterioration in that tenancy during the confirmation process would materially affect financing availability.

Environmental and Deferred Maintenance Conditions. Industrial assets near the U.S.-Mexico border have a higher incidence of Phase I and Phase II environmental findings due to the nature of cross-border freight, fuel storage, and industrial operations. Lenders underwriting Laredo industrial assets routinely require Phase I Environmental Site Assessments and, where findings are identified, Phase II investigations before committing to financing. In a bankruptcy context, deferred maintenance issues that accumulated during the distress period can also create problems. A lender will require a property condition report, and if that report identifies material deferred capital expenditures, the lender will either withhold loan proceeds in escrow or reduce the loan amount to reflect the impaired property condition.

Title and Lien Complexity Post-Bankruptcy. Emerging from Chapter 11 with clean, insurable title requires a carefully confirmed plan that properly addresses all liens, mechanic's lien claims, and junior creditor interests. Title insurers are critical to any institutional first-mortgage origination, and their willingness to insure title on a recently reorganized asset depends on the specific provisions of the confirmation order and the quality of the title search. I work closely with counsel in these engagements to identify title-related issues that could delay or prevent financing well before the confirmation hearing, not after.

The Laredo Lender Market

National CMBS Conduit Lenders. Several Wall Street conduit platforms actively originate in Laredo for stabilized industrial and retail assets meeting minimum loan size thresholds of approximately $3,000,000. CMBS pricing is index-based, transparent, and non-recourse, which is a meaningful advantage for a reorganized debtor that cannot offer a personal guarantee. The trade-off is rigidity in underwriting and no tolerance for credit imperfections in the borrowing entity. For post-bankruptcy borrowers, CMBS execution typically requires a newly formed single-purpose entity with no prior bankruptcy history, making entity structure a critical plan-drafting consideration.

Life Insurance Company Lenders. Life companies are the most attractive execution for Laredo industrial assets with long-term credit tenancy because they offer the lowest rates, the most favorable amortization terms, and genuine non-recourse carve-out flexibility. Their minimum loan sizes tend to start at $5,000,000 for secondary markets, and their credit standards are the most stringent in the market. Sponsor financial strength and post-bankruptcy credit history matter significantly to life company underwriters, but certain life companies will consider reorganized borrowers with strong asset fundamentals and experienced sponsorship.

Texas Community and Regional Banks. Laredo has a meaningful community banking presence, including institutions that have financed border-market commercial real estate for decades and understand the trade-driven economy in ways that national lenders do not. These lenders underwrite to relationship standards, typically require personal recourse guarantees, and have more flexibility on seasoning requirements for post-bankruptcy borrowers when the sponsor relationship is established. Loan sizes generally cap in the $3,000,000 to $8,000,000 range for most community banks active in the market. Interest rates will be higher than life company or CMBS execution, but speed and flexibility are genuine advantages in a plan of reorganization context where certainty of execution is critical.

Debt Funds and Bridge Lenders. For assets that do not yet meet stabilization thresholds for permanent financing, or where the reorganized borrower's credit profile requires a seasoning period before institutional execution is available, private debt funds provide bridge financing at rates currently ranging from 9% to 12% with terms of 12 to 36 months. These lenders operate on an asset-first underwriting model, meaning they weight collateral value and exit strategy more heavily than sponsor credit. Their LTV constraints are typically tighter, often 60% to 65%, but their execution timeline and credit flexibility make them an important part of the post-bankruptcy financing toolkit.

What Attorneys Handling Laredo CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to retain a CRE financing expert. If your client's plan of reorganization requires new first-mortgage financing, or if you are opposing a plan that depends on financing feasibility representations, you need a CRE financing expert before the disclosure statement is approved, not at the confirmation hearing. The financing feasibility analysis informs the plan's architecture. Waiting until you are in front of the judge to retain an expert means you are using that expert to defend a plan structure that may be fundamentally flawed.

What a CRE financing expert answers that an appraiser cannot. An appraiser establishes market value. That number is essential, but it does not tell you whether a lender will actually lend against that value, at what coverage ratio, on what terms, for what borrower entity structure, and with what conditions precedent. A CRE financing expert opines on the debt capital markets as they actually function today, which specific lender categories are actively originating in this market, and whether the specific facts of your case fall within or outside current credit parameters. These are distinct and non-overlapping disciplines.

Qualifications that matter. A retired bank loan officer has experience approving loans, but that experience is a snapshot of one institution's credit culture during one market cycle. A qualified CRE financing expert should have active, current relationships with multiple lender categories across the institutional spectrum, demonstrated experience structuring complex financings including post-bankruptcy and distressed asset transactions, and a track record of providing Rule 26 compliant expert reports that have withstood deposition and cross-examination. At CLS CRE, our firm has approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, more than 1,000 active lender relationships, and we originate in all 50 states, which means our market opinions are grounded in current, national deal flow rather than anecdote.

Information to gather before the engagement. Counsel should be prepared to provide the current rent roll with lease expiration dates, at least 24 months of operating statements, the current appraisal or broker opinion of value, a description of the borrowing entity's proposed structure post-confirmation, the specific dollar amount of the senior secured claim requiring payoff, and any existing Phase I environmental reports or property condition assessments. The quality and completeness of this information directly affects the quality of the expert opinion.

How expert testimony is used at the plan confirmation hearing. Under the Bankruptcy Code, a plan proponent must demonstrate that the plan is feasible, meaning it is not likely to be followed by liquidation or further financial reorganization. Where the plan requires new financing, courts look to expert testimony on whether that financing is realistically obtainable. My testimony addresses the current state of the debt capital markets, the specific underwriting parameters applicable to the asset and borrower, and the range of lenders from which a commitment could be obtained within the plan's timeline. This is factual, market-grounded testimony, not speculation, and it is most effective when supported by a written Rule 26 report completed before the confirmation hearing.

Key Takeaways

  • Laredo industrial fundamentals are strong. Sub-5% vacancy, rising rents driven by nearshoring and cross-border logistics demand, and sustained institutional investor interest make well-located Laredo industrial assets genuinely financeable in the current market, even in post-bankruptcy contexts with appropriate structuring.
  • DSCR at 1.25x is the standard threshold. All financing feasibility analyses for senior debt must stress-test NOI against a 1.25x coverage minimum. Plans that assume lenders will accept 1.10x or 1.15x coverage are not grounded in current market practice and will not survive expert scrutiny.
  • Entity structure at confirmation determines lender eligibility. Whether the reorganized borrower can access CMBS, life company, or bank financing depends directly on how the plan structures the post-confirmation borrowing entity. This is a plan-drafting decision that must be made with financing counsel's input before the plan is filed.
  • Bankruptcy seasoning requirements vary by lender category. Community banks with relationship history in the Laredo market offer the most flexibility. CMBS programs typically require a clean sponsoring entity. Life companies require the strongest credit profiles. A financing feasibility opinion must accurately describe this spectrum and not misrepresent CMBS or life company availability for borrowers who cannot meet their credit requirements.
  • Title insurability is a pre-confirmation issue, not a post-confirmation surprise. Counsel should engage a title company during plan drafting to confirm that the confirmation order will produce insurable title. No institutional lender will fund without a title insurance commitment, and title issues that surface after confirmation can delay or kill the financing.
  • Bridge lending is a legitimate and often necessary tool. For assets or borrowers that do not yet qualify for permanent institutional financing, private bridge lenders provide an executable path to plan confirmation with a clear refinancing exit within 12 to 36 months. A plan that incorporates a bridge-to-permanent structure is often more credible to the court than one that assumes immediate CMBS or life company execution.
  • An appraiser and a financing expert serve different evidentiary functions. Both are often needed. The appraiser establishes value; the financing expert establishes whether and how that value can be accessed through debt

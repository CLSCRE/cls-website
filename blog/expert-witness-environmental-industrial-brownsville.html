Expert Witness: Financing a Contaminated Industrial Property in Brownsville, TX

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Environmental Contamination Analysis

The Brownsville Market Context

Brownsville, Texas sits at the southern tip of the Rio Grande Valley, directly across the border from Matamoros, Tamaulipas, Mexico. That geographic reality defines the commercial real estate market here in ways that have no analog in most other Texas metros. Brownsville is not Austin or Houston. It is a binational industrial corridor where maquiladora manufacturing relationships, cross-border supply chains, and USMCA trade flows shape demand for industrial space as directly as any local economic driver. For an industrial warehouse property subject to environmental enforcement, this context is not background noise. It is central to the financing analysis.

The Brownsville-Matamoros metro functions as one of the most active cross-border manufacturing nodes in North America. Industrial vacancy in the Brownsville submarket has historically remained compressed in the 4% to 7% range for bulk distribution and light manufacturing product, driven by sustained demand from maquiladora operators, third-party logistics providers, and importers using the Port of Brownsville. The Port itself is the southernmost deep-water port in the United States, handling petroleum products, bulk cargo, and petrochemical feedstocks, which concentrates a significant volume of industrial tenancy in categories that carry environmental risk. That is precisely the context in which contamination disputes arise here with regularity.

Industrial rents in Brownsville have been trending upward, reaching the range of $5.50 to $7.50 per square foot annually on a triple-net basis for stabilized warehouse product, depending on clear height, dock configuration, and proximity to port or border crossing infrastructure. These rent levels are substantially below those of major Texas logistics hubs like Dallas-Fort Worth or Houston, which compresses achievable loan sizes on a per-square-foot basis and makes the financing analysis more sensitive to environmental-related value discounts. A 15% to 20% lender-imposed environmental discount on an asset that is already valued at $45 to $65 per square foot is a materially different problem than the same percentage discount applied to a coastal California industrial asset at $200 per square foot. Brownsville's rent and value profile means there is less cushion in the capital stack to absorb contamination stigma.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by counsel representing a class of secured creditors in a regulatory enforcement proceeding involving an industrial warehouse property in the Brownsville submarket. The asset was a single-tenant, 100% NNN-leased warehouse facility used historically for industrial processing and storage, with prior occupancy traceable to operations that introduced chlorinated volatile organic compounds into the subsurface, specifically compounds consistent with 1,4-dioxane and perchloroethylene (PCE) plume migration. A regulatory enforcement action had been filed, a responsible party determination was pending, and the property remained encumbered by a first-mortgage loan the borrower could not service from operational cash flow alone.

Counsel retained CLS CRE to answer two foundational questions for the court and for plan confirmation purposes. First, does the documented environmental condition, at the stage it had reached in the regulatory process, categorically preclude first-mortgage financing in the current market? Second, if financing is available, what is the maximum supportable loan amount under current market underwriting standards, and does that amount exceed the outstanding senior debt balance sufficient to provide a creditor recovery path? These are financing feasibility questions, not appraisal questions. They require a practitioner who understands how lenders actually underwrite impaired assets, not simply how to value them.

The engagement required us to analyze the current lease in place, the NOI generated by that lease relative to market, the lender market's current approach to environmentally impaired collateral, applicable regulatory program status under the Texas Commission on Environmental Quality (TCEQ) framework, and the resulting financing parameters that any institutional or regional lender would apply to this specific collateral type in this specific market.

The Financial Analysis

The income analysis began with the existing NNN lease, which was the controlling financial instrument regardless of environmental status. The tenant was paying rent on a long-term basis at a rate that had been established several years prior to the current engagement, placing it meaningfully below current market. For purposes of this analysis, the in-place rent reflected a contract rate of approximately $5.25 per square foot annually on a 95,000-square-foot facility, generating gross scheduled income of approximately $498,750 per year. Under the NNN structure, the landlord bore no operating expense obligations beyond property-level reserves, producing an effective NOI of approximately $473,000 after a modest structural vacancy and credit reserve consistent with what institutional lenders require even for single-tenant NNN product.

The critical underwriting tension here is the gap between in-place NOI and market NOI. Lenders underwriting to stabilized market rents would apply a rent floor of $5.75 to $6.25 per square foot for comparable product in this submarket, which implies a market NOI in the range of $516,000 to $562,000. However, no institutional lender will underwrite above the lesser of in-place NOI or market NOI on an impaired asset. With environmental conditions present, lenders apply a further NOI haircut or, more commonly, a cap rate expansion that effectively reduces the as-encumbered value baseline. For this analysis, I applied the in-place NOI of $473,000 as the ceiling.

DSCR requirements in the current market for industrial collateral in secondary Texas markets range from 1.20x to 1.30x, with most conventional conduit and bank lenders requiring 1.25x at minimum for any asset carrying environmental complication. At a 1.25x DSCR, the maximum supportable annual debt service is $378,400, which corresponds to an approximate mortgage constant of 0.0782 at a 7.25% interest rate on a 25-year amortization schedule. That constant produces a maximum supportable loan of approximately $4,840,000.

On the value side, applying a capitalization rate of 7.50% to the in-place NOI of $473,000 produces an as-is value indication of approximately $6,307,000 before any environmental discount. Lenders active in the Brownsville market for environmentally complicated industrial collateral will typically apply a value discount of 15% to 25% depending on the stage of TCEQ regulatory review, the presence or absence of a No Further Action letter, and the scope of the identified plume. Applying a 20% discount produces an adjusted collateral value of approximately $5,045,000. At a 65% LTV, the maximum loan supportable on a value basis is approximately $3,279,000. At a more aggressive 70% LTV, the ceiling rises to approximately $3,532,000.

The binding constraint in this analysis is the LTV-based maximum, not the DSCR-based maximum. The income stream supports a larger loan than the lender's collateral comfort will permit given the environmental discount applied. This is a meaningful analytical finding for litigation purposes: it means the environmental condition, not the lease economics, is the controlling limitation on financing availability. The maximum supportable first-mortgage loan at market underwriting standards falls in the range of $3.3 million to $3.5 million, well below the DSCR ceiling of approximately $4.84 million, and the gap between those two figures represents the measurable financing impairment attributable to the environmental condition.

The Complicating Factors

1,4-Dioxane and Chlorinated VOC Contamination: How Lenders Actually Respond

1,4-Dioxane presents a particularly challenging profile for lenders because it is a byproduct compound that often co-occurs with chlorinated solvents such as PCE or TCE, and it is highly mobile in groundwater due to its resistance to natural attenuation. Unlike petroleum hydrocarbon contamination, which many lenders have underwriting frameworks to address, chlorinated VOC plumes with 1,4-dioxane co-contaminants are treated by most institutional lenders as a hard stop unless very specific conditions are met. Those conditions include: an active TCEQ Voluntary Cleanup Program agreement in place, a Phase II Environmental Site Assessment confirming plume boundaries, and documentation that contamination is confirmed to be non-migrating or that regulatory closure is achievable within a definable timeline. Without those elements, the majority of CMBS conduit lenders, life insurance companies, and agency lenders will not issue a loan commitment regardless of the income profile.

TCEQ Regulatory Status and the No Further Action Letter

Texas Commission on Environmental Quality oversight creates a specific regulatory framework that lenders use as a gating criterion. A property under active enforcement without a TCEQ-issued No Further Action letter, or without enrollment in the VCP with a remediation plan approved by the agency, is treated by most lenders as unfinanceable at conventional terms. The presence of 1,4-dioxane specifically may trigger groundwater screening standards that require active remediation rather than monitored natural attenuation, which lengthens the remediation timeline and increases uncertainty. In this engagement, the property was subject to an active enforcement order with no NFA letter and no VCP enrollment, which eliminated CMBS execution, life company execution, and SBA 504 execution as viable paths.

Below-Market Rent and Lease Rollover Risk

The NNN lease at below-market rent creates a secondary complication independent of the environmental issue. Lenders evaluating single-tenant industrial assets already carry lease rollover risk as a primary credit concern. When the in-place rent is 10% to 15% below market and the tenant has leverage to renegotiate at expiration given the property's impaired status, lenders view rollover risk as elevated rather than manageable. A contaminated property is more difficult to re-lease than clean product, which means the tenant in place has negotiating leverage at renewal that further depresses rent expectations at rollover. Lenders account for this through wider cap rate assumptions and lower terminal value projections, which compounds the LTV constraint already imposed by the environmental discount.

The Brownsville Market Liquidity Constraint

Brownsville is a secondary-to-tertiary market from most institutional lenders' perspectives. Even for clean, well-leased industrial product, the universe of lenders willing to commit capital to Brownsville is narrower than in Dallas, Houston, or San Antonio. When environmental impairment is added to secondary-market collateral, the lender universe contracts further. This is not a theoretical observation. In practice, it means that the financing solution for this asset type, in this market, with these conditions, requires a lender that is specifically active in the Rio Grande Valley, comfortable with impaired collateral, and willing to price the additional risk in the rate and structure rather than declining outright.

The Brownsville Lender Market

For environmentally complicated industrial collateral in Brownsville, the effective lending universe is limited to four categories, and even within those categories, the credit box is narrow.

Community and Regional Banks with South Texas Presence: Several Texas-chartered community banks and regional institutions have established commercial real estate lending platforms in the Rio Grande Valley, driven by their understanding of the cross-border economy and longstanding relationships with local industrial owners. These lenders are the most likely source of financing for impaired collateral because they can make portfolio credit decisions rather than being constrained by CMBS or agency program guidelines. Typical terms in this category run 65% to 70% LTV, 7.00% to 7.75% interest rates on five-year terms with 20 to 25-year amortization, and require environmental indemnity agreements and a funded environmental reserve as conditions of closing. Several will require VCP enrollment as a condition precedent.

Debt Funds and Bridge Lenders: A small subset of national debt fund lenders active in Texas will consider environmentally impaired industrial collateral on a bridge basis, typically at 55% to 65% LTV, rates in the 9.50% to 11.50% range, and loan terms of 12 to 36 months with extension options tied to remediation milestones. These lenders are not providing permanent financing; they are providing a capital structure that allows the borrower time to achieve regulatory closure and then refinance into conventional terms. The cost of this capital is punishing but it can provide a path where none otherwise exists.

SBA and USDA Programs: SBA 7(a) and USDA Business and Industry programs are technically available in Brownsville's market geography, but both programs contain environmental ineligibility provisions that disqualify properties with unresolved contamination. A property with an active enforcement order and no NFA letter will not qualify under standard SBA or USDA guidelines. These programs are relevant only post-remediation.

Seller Financing and Mezzanine Structures: In transactions where senior debt availability is constrained, seller carryback financing or mezzanine debt from the existing ownership group is sometimes deployed to bridge the gap between what a bank will lend and what is needed to retire existing debt. This is less a lender market observation than a capital structure observation, but it belongs in the analysis because courts and creditors need to understand the full range of capital solutions available.

What Attorneys Handling Brownsville CRE Disputes Need to Know

An appraiser can tell you what a property is worth as of a specific date under a specific set of assumptions. A CRE financing expert tells you something entirely different: whether the capital markets will actually lend against that value, at what amount, at what cost, and under what conditions. These are not the same question, and the distinction matters enormously in secured creditor disputes, plan confirmation hearings, and environmental enforcement proceedings.

Attorneys should retain a CRE financing expert when the central legal question turns on whether financing was available, feasible, or obtainable. This includes plan confirmation disputes where a debtor claims refinancing will fund a reorganization plan, lender liability cases where the plaintiff asserts a loan commitment was wrongfully withdrawn, environmental enforcement cases where the financial impact of contamination on financing availability is at issue, and partition or forced sale proceedings where the argument hinges on whether the property can be monetized through conventional financing.

What distinguishes a qualified CRE financing expert from a retired banker or general real estate consultant is active market participation. The lender credit box changes with interest rate cycles, regulatory shifts, and capital market conditions. An expert who last closed a commercial loan three years ago does not have current knowledge of how lenders are actually underwriting environmentally impaired collateral in 2024 and 2025. CLS CRE maintains active lender relationships across more than 1,000 institutional, regional, and alternative capital sources in all 50 states, and we close transactions in secondary and tertiary markets including the Rio Grande Valley on a regular basis. That transactional currency is what produces credible, defensible expert testimony.

Before retaining a CRE financing expert, counsel should gather the following: the current rent roll with lease abstracts, the most recent Phase I and Phase II Environmental Site Assessments, any TCEQ correspondence including enforcement orders and agency responses, the existing loan documents including the original appraisal and any modification agreements, current operating statements or NNN lease confirmations, and any broker opinions of value or prior appraisals. The expert will also need access to the property's physical characteristics including building size, clear height, dock count, and site coverage, because these drive the lender's collateral assessment independent of the income stream.

At trial or in a plan confirmation hearing, CRE financing expert testimony is most effective when it is grounded in specific lender conversations and executable term sheet analysis, not purely in theoretical underwriting models. Judges and creditor committees respond to testimony that says "I contacted lenders active in this market and these are the terms they would require" rather than testimony built solely from textbook DSCR calculations. Our firm's practice is to support expert reports with documented lender outreach where litigation strategy permits, which provides a factual foundation that is difficult to attack on cross-examination.

Key Takeaways

  • Environmental contamination does not categorically preclude financing in all cases, but the type of contamination matters critically. 1,4-Dioxane and chlorinated VOC contamination without TCEQ regulatory closure eliminates institutional lenders, CMBS, life companies, and agency programs from the financing universe entirely.
  • The maximum supportable loan in this scenario is LTV-constrained, not income-constrained. The NOI from a stabilized NNN lease can support a larger loan than the lender's collateral-adjusted value will permit, meaning the environmental discount is the binding variable, not the lease economics.
  • Brownsville's secondary-market status compounds the environmental impairment.

Expert Witness: CRE Financing Feasibility in Chapter 11 Proceedings in Harrisburg, PA

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Bankruptcy Exit Financing

The Harrisburg Market Context

Harrisburg, Pennsylvania occupies a position in the Mid-Atlantic commercial real estate landscape that attorneys and investors often underestimate. As the state capital and the anchor of the broader Capital Region, which includes Camp Hill, Mechanicsburg, Carlisle, and York, Harrisburg sits at the intersection of government-driven office demand, one of the most active industrial corridors on the East Coast, and a multifamily market that has remained structurally undersupplied relative to population trends. For purposes of bankruptcy exit financing analysis, these characteristics matter enormously, because lender appetite, cap rate benchmarks, and loan sizing all derive from market fundamentals specific to this geography.

On the industrial side, the I-81/I-78 distribution corridor running through and around Harrisburg is among the most strategically important logistics submarkets in the United States. Proximity to major population centers in the Northeast, combined with lower land costs than Philadelphia or Northern New Jersey, has driven sustained institutional demand for warehouse and distribution product. Industrial vacancy in the greater Harrisburg market has remained in the low-to-mid single digits, with asking rents for bulk distribution space continuing to trend upward, though the pace of increases has moderated from the 2021 to 2022 peak. Net absorption remains positive. That environment is favorable for lender underwriting because stabilized cash flows are defensible.

The office market tells a more complicated story. Harrisburg's government and quasi-governmental tenant base provides a degree of stability absent in pure private-sector office markets. State agency leases, legislative-related tenants, and nonprofit organizations anchored to state government create a distinct occupancy profile. Suburban Class B and Class C office, however, has struggled with the same secular headwinds affecting office nationally, and vacancy in the peripheral suburban submarkets has climbed. Any bankruptcy exit financing engagement involving Harrisburg office product requires careful segmentation of tenant type, lease term, and building vintage before any lender conversation is meaningful.

Multifamily fundamentals in Harrisburg are constructive. Cap rates for stabilized apartment assets in the immediate metro have been observed in the 5.50 to 6.50 percent range depending on vintage and submarket, with newer workforce housing and market-rate product trading tighter. Rent growth has been positive and vacancy has remained below the national average. For a Chapter 11 debtor holding a multifamily asset in Harrisburg, those market conditions create genuine refinancing optionality, which is precisely the type of determination a plan feasibility analysis requires.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by counsel representing a plan proponent in a Chapter 11 reorganization proceeding involving a commercial real estate asset located in the Harrisburg metropolitan area. The asset was a stabilized multifamily property, mid-size by Harrisburg standards, with a mix of market-rate units and a meaningful portion of the tenant base on month-to-month or short-term lease arrangements at the time of the bankruptcy filing. The debtor had defaulted on first-mortgage debt, and the case had progressed to Plan of Reorganization confirmation proceedings.

Counsel needed expert opinion testimony on two interconnected questions that the court would need to resolve before confirming the plan. First, could the subject property support first-mortgage financing in an amount sufficient to pay off the secured creditor in full at or around plan confirmation? Second, what categories of lenders in the current market would actually originate a loan on a bankruptcy estate asset, and under what conditions? These are financing feasibility questions, not valuation questions, and the distinction matters. An appraiser establishes value. A CRE financing expert establishes whether that value can be monetized through debt capital markets, and on what terms.

Our engagement required us to analyze the property's income stream, apply current underwriting standards across multiple lender categories, model loan sizing under those standards, and render an opinion as to whether the proposed plan treatment of the secured claim was financially achievable. We also addressed the secondary question of timing: how long would a financing process realistically take from post-confirmation to loan closing, which is relevant to any plan milestones the court would impose.

The Financial Analysis

The starting point for any exit financing analysis is the property's stabilized net operating income. In a bankruptcy context, this analysis is more nuanced than in a standard refinance because the income stream may have been disrupted by the bankruptcy filing itself. Tenants may have withheld rent, maintenance may have been deferred, and occupancy may have declined. The expert's job is to distinguish between temporary disruption attributable to the bankruptcy process and permanent impairment attributable to the asset or market.

For this engagement, we analyzed trailing twelve-month actual collections, applied vacancy and credit loss assumptions consistent with stabilized Harrisburg multifamily benchmarks, and adjusted operating expenses to reflect normalized management, maintenance, insurance, and real estate tax obligations. The resulting stabilized NOI figure was the foundation for all loan sizing analysis. In this case, the stabilized NOI we derived was approximately $680,000 annually on a property with a gross potential rent roll of approximately $960,000, reflecting a mid-single-digit vacancy assumption and normalized operating expense ratios consistent with comparable assets in the market.

With stabilized NOI established, we applied DSCR thresholds consistent with current lender standards. Conventional multifamily lenders in today's market require a minimum DSCR of 1.20x to 1.25x on a stressed basis. Agency lenders, specifically Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac programs, apply a minimum 1.25x DSCR at the note rate. Many balance sheet lenders in the current rate environment are requiring 1.30x or higher as a credit enhancement on assets with any complicating profile, and a bankruptcy estate asset carries exactly that type of profile.

Using a note rate assumption of 6.75 percent, which was reflective of current market pricing for a post-bankruptcy multifamily loan with credit enhancements, and a 30-year amortization schedule, we calculated the maximum annual debt service on a loan that would still satisfy a 1.25x DSCR requirement. Dividing the stabilized NOI of $680,000 by 1.25 yields maximum allowable annual debt service of $544,000. Applying a standard mortgage constant at 6.75 percent on a 30-year amortization, the corresponding maximum loan balance supportable by that income stream is approximately $7.75 million.

We then applied LTV constraints. For a post-bankruptcy asset with short-term lease exposure and no agency eligibility at the time of initial closing, a conservative lender LTV ceiling of 65 percent is appropriate. An appraiser engaged by counsel had established an as-stabilized value of approximately $11.2 million. Sixty-five percent of that value yields a maximum loan of approximately $7.28 million based on LTV alone. The DSCR constraint was the binding constraint at $7.75 million, but the LTV constraint placed a tighter ceiling. Our opinion was that supportable first-mortgage financing in the range of $7.0 to $7.3 million was achievable, providing meaningful headroom over the secured creditor's allowed claim, and that this financing was obtainable from identifiable lenders under terms consistent with current market conditions.

This type of analysis is what distinguishes a CRE financing expert opinion from a simple valuation. The appraisal tells the court the asset is worth $11.2 million. Our analysis tells the court exactly how much debt can be placed on it, by whom, and at what cost, which is the operative question for plan feasibility.

The Complicating Factors

Bankruptcy estate status and lender aversion. The single largest complicating factor in any bankruptcy exit financing engagement is that most conventional lenders will not originate a loan while the automatic stay is in effect and the property remains property of the bankruptcy estate. The standard underwriting process requires a clear chain of title, a completed trustee's sale or plan confirmation order transferring the asset, and the absence of any pending litigation that could cloud the collateral. In practice, this means the lender universe for a bankruptcy exit loan is narrower than for a conventional refinance, and pricing reflects that credit and process risk.

Short-term and month-to-month lease exposure. Agency lenders require a minimum occupancy certification period prior to loan commitment, typically 90 days at or above a specified occupancy threshold, with leases of sufficient remaining term to support the underwriting. A Harrisburg multifamily asset coming out of a Chapter 11 reorganization may have a meaningful portion of its unit count on month-to-month tenancies, either because leases expired during the bankruptcy period or because new management has not yet executed new lease-up. Agency programs are effectively unavailable until occupancy and lease term metrics are re-established. This creates a bridge financing need, which we addressed as a second financing phase in our analysis.

Deferred maintenance and property condition. Bankruptcy proceedings frequently involve deferred capital expenditures, and lenders conducting physical due diligence will identify these items during the appraisal and property condition assessment process. Lenders will escrow for or require completion of immediate needs items identified in the property condition report. Our analysis accounted for an estimated $350,000 in deferred maintenance reserves that would be required at closing by a balance sheet lender, reducing net loan proceeds available to pay the secured creditor. This type of adjustment is essential to an accurate feasibility opinion and is frequently overlooked in plan negotiations that focus solely on appraised value.

Timing and plan milestone risk. Lenders do not commit to loan terms months in advance. A plan of reorganization that requires financing to close within 60 days of confirmation creates significant execution risk, because a lender processing a post-bankruptcy multifamily loan will require a full appraisal, environmental assessment, property condition report, title examination with bankruptcy-specific endorsements, and DSCR recertification. A realistic processing timeline for this type of loan is 90 to 120 days from initial application to closing. Plans that set milestones inconsistent with actual market timelines create grounds for plan failure that can be litigated, and expert testimony on realistic closing timelines is directly relevant to this question.

The Harrisburg Lender Market

Community and regional banks. Harrisburg has meaningful depth in community and regional bank lending. Institutions headquartered or with substantial operations in Central Pennsylvania have historically been active multifamily and commercial real estate lenders in this market. These lenders hold loans on their own balance sheets, apply portfolio underwriting standards rather than secondary market guidelines, and have the flexibility to originate on assets with complicating histories, including post-bankruptcy situations. Their LTV limits are typically 65 to 70 percent, rates are priced at spreads over benchmark indices or on a fixed basis for shorter terms, and they require personal guarantees in most cases. For a post-bankruptcy loan, the guaranty question is complicated if the reorganized debtor entity is the borrower, and this requires careful structuring discussion.

CMBS conduit lenders. CMBS lenders operating through securitization conduits are generally not available for bankruptcy exit situations at the point of initial financing. CMBS underwriting requires clean title, no pending litigation, and stabilized cash flows that can be securitized without risk flags. A Harrisburg asset exiting Chapter 11 with any lease-up or property condition issues will not meet conduit standards at closing. CMBS becomes a refinancing pathway one to two years post-confirmation once the asset has been stabilized and re-leased.

Debt funds and bridge lenders. For the initial post-confirmation period, before agency or CMBS eligibility is re-established, non-bank debt funds and bridge lenders are the most realistic financing source. These lenders originate higher-cost, shorter-term floating rate loans, typically two to three years with extension options, at rates in the range of SOFR plus 350 to 500 basis points in the current environment, with origination fees of 1.0 to 2.0 points. LTV on a bridge basis may extend to 70 to 75 percent of stabilized value. These lenders underwrite to the business plan and exit, not just current cash flow, and they have experience with post-bankruptcy assets. The Harrisburg market has access to these lenders through national platforms, as they do not need local branch presence to originate.

Agency lenders (Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, and FHA/HUD). Agency financing through the DUS and Optigo programs represents the long-term refinancing goal for stabilized multifamily assets. In Harrisburg, agency execution is achievable for properties meeting occupancy, lease term, and physical condition standards. Agency programs offer the lowest available rates, the highest LTV (up to 80 percent for some programs), and non-recourse structures. However, these programs are not available at initial post-bankruptcy closing. They become available after the stabilization period described above, typically 12 to 18 months post-confirmation with consistent operating performance.

What Attorneys Handling Harrisburg CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to retain a CRE financing expert. The trigger for retaining a CRE financing expert in a Chapter 11 plan confirmation proceeding is any dispute over whether the plan is financially feasible. Under the Bankruptcy Code, a plan must demonstrate that confirmation is not likely to be followed by liquidation or further reorganization. If the plan relies on exit financing, a creditor or plan proponent needs expert testimony establishing that the financing is obtainable, not just hypothetically possible. Courts in the Third Circuit have required detailed financing feasibility evidence, and an expert opinion grounded in current lender market conditions carries significantly more weight than projections embedded in a disclosure statement.

What a CRE financing expert provides that an appraiser cannot. An appraisal establishes market value. It does not tell the court which lenders will make a loan on a bankruptcy estate asset, at what LTV, under what DSCR constraints, or on what timeline. These are capital markets questions, not valuation questions. A CRE financing expert with active lender relationships across community banks, debt funds, and agency platforms can testify to current credit standards, identify the realistic lender pool for a specific asset type and credit situation, and model the loan sizing analysis required to assess plan feasibility. That testimony answers the court's actual question: can this plan be executed?

What makes a CRE financing expert qualified. Active market participation is essential. A retired banker who stopped originating loans five years ago cannot reliably testify about current credit standards, current spreads, or which lenders are actively deploying capital in the Harrisburg market for post-bankruptcy assets. The relevant expert is someone who is currently brokering and closing commercial real estate loans, maintains active lender relationships across multiple capital sources, and can speak from direct market experience. Credentials such as a Rule 26 disclosure reflecting transaction volume, number of lender relationships, and geographic scope of practice are the metrics attorneys should evaluate.

What information counsel should gather before the engagement. Before retaining a CRE financing expert, counsel should collect the trailing twelve-month rent roll with lease expiration schedule, the trailing twelve-month operating statement, the most recent property condition assessment if available, any existing appraisal or valuation, the proposed plan treatment of the secured creditor's allowed claim, and any plan milestones related to financing. The expert will also need to understand whether the reorganized entity will be a new entity or a continuation of the debtor, because that affects guaranty and credit analysis.

How expert testimony is used at plan confirmation. In a contested confirmation hearing, CRE financing expert testimony is typically presented through a written Rule 26 report submitted in advance, followed by live or deposition testimony. The report should walk the court through the NOI analysis, DSCR and LTV calculations, lender market conditions, and the expert's conclusion as to feasibility. Cross-examination typically focuses on assumptions embedded in the income analysis and the reliability of lender commitment expectations. An expert with current transaction experience can rebut challenges by referencing specific lender programs and current market data.

Key Takeaways

  • Harrisburg multifamily and industrial assets have genuine lender depth, but that depth varies significantly by asset type, occupancy status, and the presence of complicating factors like bankruptcy history. A competent feasibility analysis must account for these distinctions rather than assuming a generic lender market.
  • DSCR and LTV constraints are the binding factors in loan sizing, not appraised value alone. In this engagement, the 65 percent LTV ceiling was the binding constraint, producing a supportable loan materially below appraised value, which directly affected the plan's treatment of the secured creditor's claim.

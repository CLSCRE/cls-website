Expert Witness: CRE Financing Feasibility in Chapter 11 Proceedings in Fort Lauderdale, FL

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Bankruptcy Exit Financing

The Fort Lauderdale Market Context

Fort Lauderdale occupies a distinct and increasingly important position within the South Florida commercial real estate landscape. Unlike Miami, which draws heavy foreign capital flows and luxury-oriented development, Fort Lauderdale has matured into a market driven by institutional tenants, corporate relocations, and a deepening industrial base tied to port activity at Port Everglades, one of the busiest containerized cargo ports in the southeastern United States. For bankruptcy exit financing purposes, that distinction matters enormously, because it shapes both the asset fundamentals available to underwrite and the lender appetite for distressed or estate-owned collateral in this submarket.

The industrial sector in Broward County has been among the strongest performers in the region. Vacancy rates for industrial product in Fort Lauderdale submarkets, including Dania Beach, Pompano Beach, and the northwest Broward corridors, have held in the low to mid-single-digit percentage range through the post-pandemic cycle, with asking rents on logistics and light industrial space pushing well above their pre-2020 baselines. For a bankruptcy estate asset with industrial characteristics, that fundamental backdrop is directly relevant: a court evaluating plan feasibility needs to understand whether the income stream supporting exit financing is grounded in a durable leasing environment or a cyclical spike.

Multifamily assets in Fort Lauderdale benefit from persistent population growth in Broward County, driven in part by domestic migration from high-tax states and continued demand from a workforce that cannot afford Miami pricing. Class B and Class C apartment communities in submarkets like Lauderhill, Margate, and Oakland Park have seen rent growth moderate from pandemic peaks but remain meaningfully above 2019 levels, with cap rates for stabilized product typically ranging from the mid-5s to the low 6s depending on vintage, unit mix, and submarket positioning. Retail and office fundamentals are more nuanced: Fort Lauderdale's urban core along Las Olas Boulevard has held occupancy relatively well due to its mixed-use character, while suburban office in markets like Plantation and Sunrise continues to face tenant consolidation pressure that affects underwriting assumptions.

All of these dynamics feed directly into a bankruptcy exit financing analysis. A Chapter 11 debtor's ability to propose a feasible plan of reorganization that satisfies senior creditors through new first-mortgage financing depends on whether the Fort Lauderdale asset can actually support the loan proceeds required, and whether a willing, creditworthy lender market exists to provide them.

The Engagement

In this type of matter, Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by counsel representing a creditor or plan proponent in a Chapter 11 bankruptcy proceeding involving a commercial real estate asset located in Fort Lauderdale. The property at issue was a stabilized multifamily community with approximately 120 to 150 units, generating consistent rental income that had been documented in the debtor's operating reports filed with the bankruptcy court. The asset had been in the estate for a period exceeding 18 months, during which deferred maintenance and limited capital investment had marginally affected occupancy, though the property remained operationally functional and tenanted above the threshold generally required by institutional lenders.

Counsel's core need was twofold. First, they required a credible, court-ready expert opinion addressing whether the property could support first-mortgage financing in an amount sufficient to satisfy the allowed secured claim, pay administrative expenses, and fund any proposed treatment of unsecured creditors under the plan. Second, they needed authoritative testimony on what categories of lenders actively originate loans on assets held by bankruptcy estates, including the specific underwriting standards, structural requirements, and pricing expectations those lenders apply to this type of collateral. These are financing feasibility questions, and they are distinct from valuation questions. An appraiser can opine on market value; a CRE financing expert opines on what a real lender will actually fund, on what terms, and under what conditions.

CLS CRE was engaged to produce a Rule 26-compliant expert report and to provide deposition and hearing testimony as needed. Our methodology had to withstand scrutiny from opposing counsel and, ultimately, from the bankruptcy judge evaluating plan confirmation under the feasibility standard of 11 U.S.C. Section 1129(a)(11).

The Financial Analysis

The starting point for any bankruptcy exit financing analysis is the income stream. In this engagement, we analyzed the property's trailing 12-month operating history drawn from debtor-in-possession financial reports, rent rolls certified through the bankruptcy proceeding, and management company records. We applied standard lender adjustments to arrive at an underwritten Net Operating Income: we haircut gross potential rent by a vacancy and credit loss allowance of 7%, consistent with what institutional lenders apply to Fort Lauderdale multifamily assets at this unit count and price point. We deducted operating expenses including taxes, insurance, management fees at 5% of effective gross income, utilities, maintenance, and replacement reserves at a rate of $300 per unit per year, which reflects the floor most agency and non-agency lenders require for workforce housing vintage product.

The resulting stabilized NOI was approximately $925,000 annually. That number is the engine of the financing analysis. Everything else, including maximum loan amount, LTV supportability, and lender feasibility, derives from it.

We then applied a Debt Service Coverage Ratio threshold of 1.25x, which represents the market standard for conventional multifamily lending at this complexity level. Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac apply a 1.25x DSCR minimum for most conventional executions; bank lenders and debt fund lenders generally apply the same floor on workforce housing, though some bridge lenders will accept 1.20x on assets with a demonstrable value-add thesis. For a bankruptcy estate asset where the operational history includes a period of estate administration, applying 1.25x is both defensible and appropriate, because it reflects the standard a permanent lender will actually require when the loan closes post-confirmation.

We modeled the loan at a 30-year amortization schedule and applied an interest rate assumption of 6.75%, reflecting the rate environment for conventional multifamily permanent debt at the time of our analysis, inclusive of a spread appropriate to account for the complexity of the exit from bankruptcy. At those parameters, a loan with annual debt service of $740,000 produces a DSCR of 1.25x against the $925,000 NOI. Working backward from that debt service figure using a 30-year amortization and 6.75% rate, the maximum supportable loan on a DSCR basis is approximately $10.8 million.

We then cross-checked that figure against loan-to-value constraints. Using a market value estimate drawn from the appraiser's report already in the record, which placed the property value at approximately $15.5 million, a $10.8 million loan represents a loan-to-value ratio of approximately 69.7%. That is within the standard LTV ceiling of 70% to 75% applicable to conventional multifamily permanent loans and meaningfully below the 80% threshold that would trigger elevated scrutiny or require mortgage insurance. The LTV headroom provides the lender with a meaningful equity cushion even accounting for the estate context.

Our analysis concluded that the property could support first-mortgage financing sufficient to satisfy the allowed secured claim, which was stipulated in the proceeding at approximately $9.2 million, with proceeds to spare for closing costs and plan administration. That conclusion was grounded in the NOI, supported by the DSCR arithmetic, and validated against market LTV standards, all of which we were prepared to defend in testimony.

The Complicating Factors

Title and Lien Resolution. Lenders originating first-mortgage loans on bankruptcy estate assets require clean fee simple title at closing, with all junior liens, mechanic's liens, and pre-petition encumbrances discharged or subordinated through the plan or a separate court order. In Fort Lauderdale, where construction activity during the 2020 to 2023 cycle was aggressive and contractor claims are common, a multifamily asset in bankruptcy may carry unresolved mechanic's lien claims that cloud title. Lenders will not close into a lien dispute. The plan confirmation order must include explicit findings releasing the property from pre-petition liens, and the title insurer must be prepared to issue a clean policy at closing. We identified this issue in our report and noted that it could be resolved through plan mechanics, but that the timeline for title clearance needed to be built into the exit financing commitment letter requirements.

Seasoning and Operational Track Record. One of the most consistent objections institutional lenders raise on bankruptcy estate assets is the lack of a post-petition operational track record under normalized management. Estate-administered properties frequently show irregular expense patterns, deferred maintenance, and below-market occupancy driven by management uncertainty rather than market conditions. Lenders underwriting these assets apply additional scrutiny to trailing NOI figures, often requiring three to six months of stabilized post-confirmation operations before funding a permanent loan. Bridge lenders are more accommodating on this point but price for the risk. In our analysis, we noted that the property's occupancy at 91% through the bankruptcy period, while below its pre-petition stabilized level, was sufficient to support the underwritten NOI we presented, and we identified bridge-to-permanent structures as a viable path if permanent lenders required additional seasoning.

Interest Rate Sensitivity. Bankruptcy proceedings take time, and the gap between plan proposal and plan confirmation can span many months. During that period, interest rates can move. A financing analysis conducted at a 6.75% rate assumption may produce a materially different result if rates increase 50 to 75 basis points by the time the exit loan is actually originated. We stress-tested our model at 7.25% and 7.50%, which produced supportable loan amounts of $10.4 million and $10.1 million respectively, both of which remained sufficient to satisfy the senior secured claim. Attorneys presenting financing feasibility opinions in plan confirmation proceedings should ensure their expert has stress-tested the rate assumption and can demonstrate that the plan remains feasible across a reasonable range of rate scenarios.

Court Approval Timelines and Lender Commitment Expiration. Lenders issuing commitment letters for bankruptcy exit financing typically set expiration periods of 60 to 90 days. If plan confirmation is delayed, the commitment may expire, requiring re-application at then-current market conditions. This is not a theoretical concern: in contested plan confirmation proceedings in the Southern District of Florida, contested confirmation hearings can drag across multiple court dates spanning several months. We addressed this in our report by identifying lenders whose programs accommodate extension provisions and by recommending that the plan documents include a mechanism for the debtor to re-engage lenders if the initial commitment expires due to court-related delays.

The Fort Lauderdale Lender Market

Fort Lauderdale benefits from a well-developed institutional lending presence rooted in its proximity to Miami's capital markets infrastructure while maintaining its own community and regional banking ecosystem. For bankruptcy exit financing on stabilized multifamily, the relevant lender categories are as follows.

Agency Lenders (Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac DUS). Agency execution is available for bankruptcy exit multifamily loans, but it requires that the property be fully stabilized post-confirmation, that the borrowing entity be clean and unencumbered by the bankruptcy filing, and that the loan close into a newly formed or restructured entity with appropriate organizational documents. Agency lenders will not lend to a debtor-in-possession directly. The borrowing entity must have been reconstituted through the plan. Given those requirements, agency execution is typically available as a permanent take-out but not as an immediate exit vehicle unless the plan timeline accommodates a post-confirmation stabilization period of at least 90 days.

Life Insurance Companies. Life companies active in South Florida, including those with Florida correspondent relationships, are selective but represent best-execution pricing for well-located stabilized assets. They apply conservative LTV constraints, typically 60% to 65%, and require institutional-quality sponsorship. For a bankruptcy exit scenario, life company execution is feasible but requires a clean post-confirmation sponsor with demonstrable net worth and liquidity. They are not a first call for complexity but represent the top of the market for a borrower who can qualify.

Community and Regional Banks. Broward County has a meaningful community banking presence, including Florida-chartered institutions that understand the local market and have loan officers with genuine decision-making authority. Community banks will engage on bankruptcy exit loans when the asset is performing, the loan is sized conservatively at 65% LTV or below, and the post-confirmation sponsor has a local relationship. They apply portfolio lending standards, which gives them flexibility on structure that agency lenders lack. Their pricing is typically SOFR-based or prime-based with spreads reflecting the complexity of the exit.

Debt Funds and Bridge Lenders. For assets that require a seasoning period post-confirmation or where the permanent market requires additional stabilization evidence, bridge debt funds active in South Florida represent the most reliable immediate exit vehicle. These lenders originate specifically on transitional and complex assets, including bankruptcy exits. They underwrite to a current income standard, apply interest rate reserves where needed, and structure loans with 12 to 36-month terms and extension options tied to performance milestones. Their pricing is meaningfully higher than permanent executions, typically in the SOFR plus 300 to 500 basis point range, but their ability to close quickly and accommodate the complexity of a bankruptcy exit makes them the practical first call in many plan structures.

What Attorneys Handling Fort Lauderdale CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to retain a CRE financing expert. If your client's position in a plan confirmation proceeding depends, even partially, on the feasibility of exit financing, retain a financing expert before the plan is filed, not after objections are raised. The financing feasibility opinion needs to be embedded in the plan support documents, not introduced reactively. In the Southern District of Florida, bankruptcy judges scrutinize plan feasibility carefully, and a well-supported financing expert report submitted with the plan disclosure statement carries significantly more weight than a report produced in response to a creditor's feasibility objection.

What a financing expert can answer that an appraiser cannot. An appraiser establishes market value. A CRE financing expert establishes what the market will actually lend. Those are different questions. An appraiser does not opine on DSCR thresholds, lender credit boxes, commitment letter requirements, closing timeline expectations, or the specific categories of lenders willing to originate on a bankruptcy estate asset. Attorneys who rely solely on appraisal testimony to establish financing feasibility are leaving a critical evidentiary gap that opposing counsel will exploit.

What qualifies a CRE financing expert. Relevant qualifications include active origination experience across multiple lender categories, demonstrated familiarity with the specific market and asset type in dispute, and a track record of producing Rule 26-compliant expert reports in commercial real estate financing matters. A retired banker who worked at a single institution brings a one-lender perspective. A commercial mortgage broker with active institutional, agency, and private lender relationships brings a market-wide perspective that more accurately reflects how the financing landscape actually functions. Opposing counsel will probe the expert's current market activity; experts who are actively transacting are more credible than those whose market knowledge is historical.

What information counsel should gather before the engagement. At minimum: the trailing 12-month operating statements and rent rolls certified through the bankruptcy proceeding, the existing appraisal or valuation report in the record, documentation of all claims and liens asserted against the property, the proposed plan treatment of the senior secured creditor, and any existing loan documents on the pre-petition debt. The more complete the financial documentation, the more defensible the expert's NOI analysis.

How financing expert testimony is used at confirmation hearings. In a contested confirmation proceeding, the financing expert typically provides both a written Rule 26 report and live testimony. The direct examination establishes the expert's methodology, the market data supporting the financing analysis, and the conclusion on feasibility. Cross-examination will focus on the rate assumptions, the underwriting standards applied, and the specific lenders identified as willing to originate. An expert who can name categories of lenders and describe their specific credit requirements, based on active market relationships rather than generic knowledge, withstands cross-examination far more effectively than one offering theoretical conclusions.

Key Takeaways

  • Fort Lauderdale multifamily fundamentals support exit financing at meaningful loan amounts. Stabilized vacancy, persistent rental demand from domestic migration, and active institutional lender presence in Broward County create a financing environment where a well-underwritten bankruptcy exit loan is achievable, provided the asset is performing and the plan structure is sound.
  • DSCR-based underwriting, not LTV alone, drives the supportable loan amount. In our analysis, the 1.25x DSCR threshold produced a maximum supportable loan that satisfied the senior secured claim with LTV headroom, confirming feasibility under both income-based and collateral

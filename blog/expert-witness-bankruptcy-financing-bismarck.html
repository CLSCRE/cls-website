Expert Witness: CRE Financing Feasibility in Chapter 11 Proceedings in Bismarck, ND

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Bankruptcy Exit Financing

The Bismarck Market Context

Bismarck, North Dakota is a smaller but economically resilient commercial real estate market, anchored by state government employment, healthcare, energy sector activity, and a regional retail and service economy that serves much of south-central North Dakota. The market does not operate like a coastal gateway city, and lenders who finance assets here know that. Underwriting a Bismarck commercial property requires a clear-eyed understanding of the local demand drivers, the depth of the tenant pool, and the realistic range of exit strategies, all of which matter enormously when the asset sits inside a Chapter 11 estate.

Multifamily has historically been the most liquid asset class in Bismarck, with cap rates generally ranging from the high 5% to low 7% range depending on vintage, condition, and submarket proximity to the state capitol campus or Sanford or CHI St. Alexius medical corridors. Office fundamentals have softened since the post-pandemic reconfiguration of state agency space and healthcare administrative functions, with downtown Bismarck office vacancy elevated compared to pre-2020 levels. Retail in Bismarck is bifurcated: grocery-anchored and necessity retail near the Kirkwood Mall corridor performs reasonably well, while secondary strip retail faces the same secular pressures visible in every mid-size American market. Industrial and flex space, particularly properties proximate to major highway corridors including I-94 and U.S. 83, has seen relatively stable occupancy driven by agricultural supply chain, oilfield services logistics, and state contractor demand.

For purposes of a bankruptcy exit financing analysis, the critical market question is not simply what a property is worth today, but whether the income stream is durable enough, and documented well enough, to support institutional first-mortgage debt. In Bismarck, that question often turns on lease quality, tenant concentration risk, and whether local rent levels support a debt service coverage ratio that satisfies a lender's credit committee. These are not appraisal questions. They are capital markets questions, and they require a different expert.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by counsel representing a secured creditor in a Chapter 11 reorganization proceeding involving a commercial income-producing property in Bismarck. The asset was a mid-size multi-tenant retail and office mixed-use building, owner-operated prior to the bankruptcy filing, with a combination of long-term tenants and several vacancies that had accumulated during the debtor's financial deterioration. The debtor had proposed a Plan of Reorganization that contemplated refinancing the first-mortgage position to pay down the senior secured creditor to a negotiated amount, with the debtor retaining ownership under a restructured capital stack.

The creditor's counsel needed to know two things. First, could the property, in its current condition and occupancy, actually support first-mortgage financing sufficient to satisfy the proposed cramdown treatment of the senior secured claim? Second, if refinancing was feasible, what categories of lenders would realistically originate a loan on an asset emerging from bankruptcy, and under what terms? The court needed more than an appraiser's opinion of value. It needed a capital markets professional's opinion of whether a willing, creditworthy lender would actually write a check, and on what terms that check would come.

CLS CRE was engaged to produce a written opinion consistent with the requirements applicable to expert testimony, covering financing feasibility, lender market conditions, and the transaction structure that would be required to execute the refinancing the debtor proposed. Our firm brings approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, active relationships with more than 1,000 lenders across 50 states, and direct experience originating loans on assets with title, occupancy, and borrower complexity of exactly this type.

The Financial Analysis

The starting point for any financing feasibility analysis is the net operating income the property can credibly support, not the income the debtor claims, and not the stabilized pro forma an optimistic broker might generate. For this engagement, we analyzed three scenarios: actual trailing 12-month NOI based on rent rolls and operating statements produced in discovery, a stabilized NOI assuming the property leased to a reasonable occupancy level consistent with comparable Bismarck properties, and a stressed NOI representing a conservative view of achievable rents given the current market.

For a mixed-use retail and office property in Bismarck, market gross rents at the time of our analysis ranged from approximately $12 to $18 per square foot annually for retail space depending on size, visibility, and tenant covenant, and from $14 to $22 per square foot for office space depending on finish level and floor position. The subject property, based on its rent roll and square footage, generated a trailing NOI of approximately $185,000 annually at actual occupancy. At stabilized occupancy consistent with a 10% to 12% vacancy allowance, which is appropriate for a Bismarck mixed-use asset in this submarket, stabilized NOI was projected at approximately $240,000.

Lenders underwriting commercial real estate in secondary and tertiary markets like Bismarck typically require a minimum debt service coverage ratio of 1.25x, and many institutional lenders will apply a 1.30x threshold on assets with bankruptcy history or leasing concentration risk. We applied 1.25x as the operative standard in our base case, consistent with Freddie Mac, Fannie Mae (for multifamily), CMBS, and conventional bank underwriting guidelines that are broadly applicable across property types. For a non-agency commercial loan, 1.25x DSCR is the market standard for credit approval.

At a note rate of 7.50%, which was consistent with the prevailing rate environment for commercial first-mortgage debt in North Dakota at the time of our analysis, and a 25-year amortization schedule, which is standard for commercial loans on income-producing properties of this type, annual debt service on a 20-year term loan would be approximately $89,000 per $1,000,000 of loan proceeds. Applying the 1.25x DSCR constraint to the trailing NOI of $185,000 produces a maximum supportable debt service of $148,000, which in turn supports a maximum loan of approximately $1,660,000. Applying the same test to the stabilized NOI of $240,000 produces a maximum supportable debt service of $192,000 and a maximum loan of approximately $2,160,000.

The appraised value of the property, as reflected in the appraisal produced in the proceeding, was approximately $3,200,000. A 65% LTV constraint, which is typical for a commercial loan on an asset with bankruptcy history and a secondary market location, would produce a maximum loan of $2,080,000 on that appraised value. The convergence of the income-based analysis and the LTV constraint was meaningful: both methodologies pointed to a maximum first-mortgage loan in the range of $1.9 million to $2.1 million, assuming stabilized occupancy was credibly achievable. On a trailing actual NOI basis, the supportable loan was materially lower, approximately $1.66 million.

The debtor's Plan proposed satisfying the senior creditor at approximately $2.3 million. Our analysis concluded that financing sufficient to satisfy the plan at that level was not supportable on trailing NOI, was marginal at best on stabilized NOI, and would require lender underwriting assumptions that were more optimistic than what the market would bear for an asset of this profile. That conclusion was the core of our expert opinion.

The Complicating Factors

Bankruptcy stigma in secondary markets. Lenders across every category, from community banks to CMBS conduits, apply heightened scrutiny to assets emerging from Chapter 11. In Bismarck, where the institutional lending pool is already narrower than in a major metro, this stigma is more operationally significant. Community banks that might otherwise be the natural execution for a loan of this size frequently have internal policies or regulatory guidance that prohibits or severely restricts origination on assets that have been in a bankruptcy estate within the prior 12 to 24 months. Bridge and private lenders can bridge this gap, but they price accordingly, typically 200 to 400 basis points above conventional rates, which directly affects the DSCR calculation and the maximum supportable loan.

Tenant concentration and lease term risk. A multi-tenant property where two or three tenants represent a substantial share of gross revenue is a concentration risk that lenders discount heavily. In Bismarck's commercial market, the tenant pool for retail and office space is shallower than in major metros, meaning rollover risk is real and re-leasing timelines can be extended. Any lender underwriting this property will haircut the income attributable to month-to-month tenants or those with leases expiring within the loan term, and they will apply higher vacancy reserves than they would on an asset in Denver or Minneapolis.

Lender unfamiliarity with the Bismarck submarket. National and regional lenders, particularly CMBS originators and life companies, frequently require third-party market studies for secondary and tertiary markets before they will approve a loan. Bismarck is a market that many institutional credit committees have limited data on. This does not mean the loan is unfundable, but it does mean the execution timeline is longer, the due diligence requirements are more extensive, and the probability of approval from any single lender is lower than it would be for a comparable asset in a primary market. For purposes of a bankruptcy plan hearing, this matters because the debtor must demonstrate that financing is not merely theoretically possible but realistically executable within a credible timeframe.

Post-bankruptcy borrower qualification. Even if the property can support the debt service, the borrower or reorganized entity must qualify as a creditworthy mortgage obligor. Lenders will scrutinize the principal's post-bankruptcy financial position, liquidity, and net worth. Guaranty requirements for commercial loans on assets of this size in Bismarck are nearly universal among bank lenders. A borrower emerging from Chapter 11 with limited liquidity and a recently confirmed plan faces real obstacles to satisfying recourse guaranty requirements, which may further limit the accessible lender universe to non-recourse bridge or private debt products, which carry higher rates and shorter terms.

The Bismarck Lender Market

Community and regional banks. Bismarck has a meaningful presence of community and regional banks, including institutions with deep North Dakota roots and local underwriting authority. These lenders are typically the most execution-certain for conventional commercial real estate loans in this market, but they are also the most conservative on credit quality and borrower profile. Most community banks in the Bismarck area will not originate on a bankruptcy estate asset without a seasoning period post-confirmation, typically 12 to 24 months. Those that will consider a post-confirmation loan will generally require full recourse guaranties, a debt service reserve, and LTV not exceeding 60% to 65%. Loan size appetite at community banks in Bismarck is generally up to $5 million for commercial real estate, with the sweet spot for this market in the $1 million to $3 million range.

Credit unions and thrifts. Several credit unions with commercial real estate lending programs operate in the Bismarck market. They tend to price competitively but have tighter credit boxes and member relationship requirements that limit their accessibility for bankruptcy-adjacent transactions.

Bridge and private lenders. For an asset emerging from bankruptcy where the conventional lender market is constrained, bridge and private debt is the realistic near-term execution path. These lenders, most of whom operate nationally and are comfortable with distressed and complex collateral, will originate on bankruptcy estate assets and do not require post-confirmation seasoning. However, they lend at significantly higher rates, typically in the 9% to 12% range at the time of our analysis, with origination fees of 1% to 3%, and on shorter terms of 12 to 36 months with extension options. The maximum LTV for bridge lenders on this asset type in this market was approximately 65%, and DSCR requirements were sometimes replaced by a debt yield test requiring net operating income to equal at least 8% to 9% of the loan amount. Bridge financing creates a refinancing risk at maturity that courts and creditors should understand when evaluating plan feasibility.

SBA 504 and USDA programs. For owner-occupied commercial properties, the SBA 504 program can be a viable execution path in Bismarck and is actively supported by certified development companies operating in North Dakota. The USDA Business and Industry program also provides loan guarantees for commercial real estate in eligible areas. These programs have specific eligibility requirements, and their applicability to a post-bankruptcy reorganized entity requires careful analysis, but they should not be excluded from a feasibility analysis without examination.

What Attorneys Handling Bismarck CRE Disputes Need to Know

An appraiser can tell the court what a property is worth. A CRE financing expert tells the court whether a lender will actually fund a loan against that value, on what terms, and within what timeframe. These are categorically different opinions. In a Chapter 11 plan confirmation proceeding, the debtor must demonstrate not just that the property has value, but that value can be monetized through financing in a way that makes the plan feasible. Courts have seen debtors propose plans premised on refinancing assumptions that no lender in the market would actually approve. A qualified CRE financing expert identifies those disconnects and quantifies them for the record.

Counsel should retain a CRE financing expert when the plan hinges on a refinancing transaction, when cramdown treatment of a secured creditor depends on what the property can support in debt, when a debtor or creditor needs to establish the range of interest rates and terms that would apply to exit financing, or when there is a dispute about whether a property is financeable at all given its condition, occupancy, or the borrower's post-bankruptcy financial profile.

A qualified CRE financing expert is not simply a retired banker or a mortgage broker with local experience. The expert should have active, current transaction experience across multiple lender categories, demonstrable relationships with the types of lenders who would realistically fund the transaction in question, and experience preparing written expert opinions that meet the standards applicable to litigation. Expertise in local market conditions specific to Bismarck, and in the lender categories that are active there, is essential. At CLS CRE, we maintain active lender relationships across more than 1,000 institutions nationally, including the community banks, regional lenders, and private debt providers who are the realistic execution universe for a Bismarck commercial mortgage.

Before retaining a CRE financing expert, counsel should gather the following: trailing 12-month and trailing 24-month operating statements for the property; the current rent roll with lease expiration dates and tenant financial information if available; the most recent appraisal; any existing loan documents and payoff figures; the debtor's proposed plan treatment of the secured debt; and any environmental, title, or deferred maintenance issues disclosed in the case record. The expert will need all of this to produce a credible, well-supported opinion that can withstand cross-examination.

At the plan confirmation hearing, CRE financing expert testimony is used to establish or rebut the feasibility of the debtor's proposed refinancing, to define the market rate of interest applicable to a cramdown scenario, and to testify about what the market of willing lenders actually looks like for this asset. These are questions that require current, active capital markets expertise, not theoretical familiarity with lending concepts.

Key Takeaways

  • Income-based underwriting drives the loan ceiling. In Bismarck, a commercial first mortgage is sized by DSCR, typically 1.25x, not by appraised value alone. The maximum supportable loan may be materially lower than LTV would suggest, particularly when trailing NOI is below stabilized projections.
  • Bankruptcy history narrows the lender universe significantly. Most community banks in the Bismarck market will not originate on a bankruptcy estate asset without meaningful post-confirmation seasoning. The near-term execution universe often defaults to bridge and private lenders, who price at materially higher rates.
  • Higher bridge rates compress the supportable loan further. At a 10% bridge rate versus a 7.5% bank rate, the same NOI supports a substantially smaller loan, which can render a plan infeasible even when the appraised value appears sufficient to support the senior creditor's claim.
  • Secondary market execution risk is real and must be quantified. Bismarck is a market where institutional lender familiarity is limited, and execution timelines are longer. A plan that assumes 60-day financing execution in Bismarck on a complex asset is not realistic, and courts should hear expert testimony on this point.
  • Borrower qualification is a separate financing hurdle. Even a fully supportable loan on a financeable property can fail to execute if the reorganized borrower cannot satisfy guaranty, net worth, and liquidity requirements. Expert analysis must address both the property's debt capacity and the borrower's ability to qualify.
  • A CRE financing expert provides opinions an appraiser cannot.

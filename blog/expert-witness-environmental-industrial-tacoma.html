Expert Witness: Financing a Contaminated Industrial Property in Tacoma, WA

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Environmental Contamination Analysis

The Tacoma Market Context

Tacoma is not a secondary market in any dismissive sense of that term. It is an independent industrial gateway with port infrastructure, rail connectivity, and land economics that are structurally distinct from Seattle, even as the two markets are frequently lumped together under the "Puget Sound" umbrella. For attorneys handling commercial real estate disputes and for investors trying to understand the financing landscape, that distinction matters enormously when analyzing whether a contaminated industrial warehouse can support first-mortgage debt.

The Port of Tacoma is one of the ten largest container ports in North America. That single fact drives sustained demand for industrial real estate throughout Pierce County and the surrounding submarkets. Tacoma's industrial vacancy rate has historically tracked below 5%, and while the broader Pacific Northwest industrial market experienced modest softening in 2023 and into 2024 as a result of elevated construction deliveries and inventory normalization by large logistics tenants, Tacoma's core industrial submarkets, particularly the tideflats and the I-5 corridor properties, have remained meaningfully tighter than comparable positions in secondary Pacific coast markets. Net asking rents for functional warehouse product in the Tacoma tideflats and Port vicinity have ranged from approximately $12.00 to $17.00 per square foot annually on a NNN basis for bulk distribution, with smaller bay industrial commanding premiums above that range. These are not speculative figures. They reflect the structural undersupply of large-footprint industrial sites within proximity of Port infrastructure in a market where entitlement timelines and land constraints genuinely limit new supply.

For the matter addressed in this article, those market rent dynamics are central. A 100% NNN-leased industrial warehouse in Tacoma generating below-market rents presents a financing profile that is neither obviously strong nor obviously broken. Whether that profile can support a loan sufficient to retire senior secured debt is a fact-intensive, methodology-driven question, and the answer depends on how lenders actually underwrite contaminated collateral in the current credit environment.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by counsel representing a secured creditor group in a regulatory enforcement matter involving an industrial warehouse property situated in the Tacoma industrial submarket. The property was subject to a Washington State Department of Ecology enforcement proceeding related to the presence of 1,4-dioxane and chlorinated volatile organic compounds in soil and groundwater, legacy contamination attributable to prior industrial tenancy predating the current ownership and loan structure.

The asset itself was a functional, well-located industrial warehouse, single-tenant, leased on an absolute NNN basis at rents that were demonstrably below the current market for comparable Tacoma industrial product. The existing senior debt was approaching maturity, and the core legal dispute involved whether the environmental condition constituted an impairment to value sufficient to preclude refinancing, or whether institutional first-mortgage financing remained available in the market in an amount sufficient to retire the outstanding loan balance.

CLS CRE was asked to provide a Rule 26 expert opinion addressing two specific questions. First, does the documented environmental condition, specifically the presence of chlorinated VOCs and 1,4-dioxane in soil and groundwater with an active regulatory file, categorically preclude first-mortgage financing from institutional lenders in the current market? Second, applying market-standard underwriting methodology, what is the maximum supportable first-mortgage loan amount, and does that amount provide sufficient proceeds to retire the existing senior debt?

These are precisely the questions that require a capital markets professional with active lender relationships and current deal experience, not a theoretical analysis of what lenders used to do. Our firm maintains relationships with over 1,000 lenders across 50 states, closes approximately $1 billion in commercial mortgage transactions annually, and has placed financing on environmentally encumbered industrial collateral in the Pacific Northwest within the relevant time period. That transactional experience is what gives the expert opinion evidentiary weight that a purely academic or appraisal-based analysis cannot replicate.

The Financial Analysis

The income analysis begins with the lease, not with market rent. Because the property is 100% NNN-leased, the contractual rent stream is the operative income figure for underwriting purposes. Lenders do not speculate about what the space might lease for on turnover; they underwrite the lease in place. In this engagement, the in-place annual base rent was approximately $1,340,000, representing a rate of approximately $13.40 per square foot annually on a 100,000-square-foot building. NNN expense recovery was structured to pass all operating expenses, real estate taxes, insurance, and maintenance obligations directly to the tenant, leaving the landlord's effective gross income equal to the base rent with minimal leakage.

After applying a vacancy and credit loss reserve of approximately 5% (standard for single-tenant NNN analysis, reflecting rollover risk at lease expiration rather than current occupancy risk) and minimal management fee of 2% of effective gross income reflecting the passive nature of NNN management, the stabilized NOI underwritten for financing purposes was approximately $1,247,700. This is not the appraiser's NOI. This is the lender's NOI, constructed using the conservative assumptions that credit committees apply, not the full economic benefit that an equity investor might underwrite to.

On DSCR, the market standard for industrial collateral with environmental complications is not 1.20x. Lenders who are willing to engage with contaminated collateral at all typically require 1.25x to 1.30x DSCR at the loan level, with some institutional lenders requiring 1.35x as a condition of credit approval. For this analysis, we applied a 1.25x DSCR threshold as the baseline and stress-tested at 1.30x to define the lending universe realistically.

On rate and amortization, we assumed a 5-year fixed rate of approximately 7.25% on a 25-year amortization schedule, reflecting the pricing environment for environmental risk collateral in the Pacific Northwest institutional market as of the relevant analysis period. Lenders do not price contaminated collateral at the same spread as clean product; the environmental uncertainty commands an additional 50 to 100 basis points over comparable clean industrial. At 7.25% interest on a 25-year amortization, the annual debt service constant is approximately 8.60% of loan principal.

Working backward from NOI to maximum supportable loan at 1.25x DSCR: the maximum annual debt service the property can support is $1,247,700 divided by 1.25, which equals $998,160. Dividing $998,160 by the 8.60% annual debt service constant produces a maximum supportable loan of approximately $11,606,500, which we rounded to $11,600,000 for presentation purposes.

LTV served as the secondary constraint. Lenders willing to finance contaminated industrial collateral in Tacoma will typically apply a maximum LTV of 55% to 65% of the as-is appraised value, not the unimpaired value. In this engagement, the as-is appraised value with environmental impairment reflected was approximately $16,800,000. At 65% LTV, the maximum loan was $10,920,000. At 60% LTV, the ceiling was $10,080,000. The DSCR-driven maximum of $11,600,000 was therefore not the binding constraint; LTV was. The effective maximum supportable loan from the institutional market was in the range of $10,000,000 to $11,000,000, depending on which lender's credit box applied.

The existing senior debt balance was approximately $9,750,000. The analysis therefore demonstrated that first-mortgage financing sufficient to retire the outstanding debt was available in the current market, notwithstanding the environmental condition, provided the transaction was structured correctly and presented to the appropriate lender category. This conclusion directly addressed the creditor group's core litigation position.

The Complicating Factors

Active Regulatory File: The presence of an open Washington Department of Ecology enforcement file is the most significant complicating factor in this deal structure. Lenders do not object to contamination in the abstract; they object to regulatory uncertainty. An active enforcement file means the scope of remediation, the cost of remediation, and the timeline to site closure are all unknown at the time of underwriting. Lenders respond to that uncertainty by either declining to engage or by requiring specific structural protections. The most common approach is a funded environmental reserve, typically sized at 125% to 150% of the estimated remediation cost based on the most recent Phase II ESA and Remedial Investigation, held in escrow and not released until a No Further Action determination or equivalent regulatory milestone is achieved. This reserve directly reduces net loan proceeds and must be factored into the sufficiency analysis.

1,4-Dioxane and Chlorinated VOC Complexity: These are not simple petroleum hydrocarbon contamination scenarios. 1,4-dioxane is a synthetic industrial solvent stabilizer that is highly mobile in groundwater, does not biodegrade readily under natural conditions, and has an EPA health advisory level of 35 micrograms per liter. Chlorinated VOCs, including compounds such as trichloroethylene and perchloroethylene, trigger strict liability frameworks under both CERCLA and Washington's Model Toxics Control Act. Lenders with environmental risk policies sophisticated enough to understand the difference between petroleum contamination and chlorinated solvent contamination will price this risk differently, and many institutional lenders with rigid environmental credit policies will not engage at all regardless of the financial metrics. This narrows the lender universe substantially, which is itself a material fact in the financing feasibility analysis.

Below-Market Rent and Lease Term: The NNN lease structure provides income stability, but below-market rents create two separate problems for lenders. First, the income stream underwritten today does not reflect the property's economic potential, which means the loan sizing is constrained relative to what a market-rent analysis would support. Second, lenders scrutinize lease expiration in the context of environmental impairment. If the current tenant's lease expires within the loan term, lenders must assess whether a creditworthy replacement tenant will execute a new lease on a property with an active environmental file. That leasing risk is real, and lenders discount it through tighter LTV requirements or shorter loan terms that align with the lease expiration rather than a standard 5-year or 10-year fixed period.

Lender Environmental Indemnity Requirements: Most institutional lenders financing contaminated collateral require a Phase II ESA dated within 6 months of closing, an environmental indemnity agreement from a creditworthy guarantor, and in many cases an environmental insurance policy with the lender named as additional insured. Environmental insurance for sites with known chlorinated VOC contamination is obtainable but costly, typically ranging from $25,000 to $75,000 annually depending on policy limits and site-specific conditions. These transaction costs reduce the net proceeds available to retire existing debt and must be incorporated into the feasibility analysis.

The Tacoma Lender Market

The Tacoma industrial financing market is served by multiple lender categories, each with a distinct credit approach to environmental risk. Understanding which categories will engage, and under what terms, is essential to constructing a credible financing feasibility opinion.

Life Insurance Companies: Life company lenders are active in Tacoma industrial and have historically been the best execution for clean, institutional-quality product. For contaminated collateral, life companies are essentially unavailable. Their investment committees require clean environmental reports as a threshold condition, and the presence of chlorinated VOCs with an active regulatory file falls outside their credit guidelines without exception in the current market. No life company lender should be included in a feasibility analysis for this deal type.

CMBS/Conduit Lenders: Conduit lenders apply B-piece buyer requirements that include environmental clearance. Deals with active remediation files and unresolved regulatory status are not CMBS-eligible. This is a categorical exclusion, not a pricing issue. Including CMBS execution in a financing feasibility opinion for this deal type would reflect either inexperience with the conduit market or intellectual dishonesty.

Debt Funds and Bridge Lenders: This is the most active category for contaminated industrial collateral with complex risk profiles. Debt funds operating in the Pacific Northwest, including several with specific environmental expertise and existing relationships with environmental insurers, will underwrite this deal type. Their pricing is higher, typically ranging from SOFR plus 350 to 500 basis points on floating-rate bridge structures, and their terms are shorter, usually 2 to 3 years with extension options tied to remediation milestones. They will require larger funded reserves and more robust indemnity structures than institutional permanent lenders. Their LTV thresholds are typically 55% to 60% on contaminated collateral.

Regional and Community Banks: Washington state's community bank market includes several institutions with meaningful commercial real estate portfolios in the South Puget Sound region. Some of these lenders have institutional knowledge of Tacoma industrial, including prior exposure to contaminated properties in the tideflats and Port area. A community bank lender willing to hold this loan in portfolio, rather than sell or securitize it, has more flexibility on environmental policy than a conduit or life company. However, regulatory capital requirements and examiner pressure on criticized assets have reduced community bank appetite for this risk category in the post-2022 rate environment. Targeted outreach to portfolio lenders with Tacoma industrial experience is necessary, but the viable universe is narrow.

SBA 504 and USDA Programs: Government-backed loan programs are not applicable to environmentally contaminated collateral as a practical matter. SBA lenders require environmental indemnification that the SBA itself will not underwrite over, and the liability exposure under MTCA and CERCLA creates program compliance issues that eliminate these execution pathways for this deal type.

What Attorneys Handling Tacoma CRE Disputes Need to Know

The single most important thing I can tell a litigator handling a Tacoma commercial real estate financing dispute is this: an appraiser and a CRE financing expert are not interchangeable, and using only one when you need both is a strategic error that opposing counsel will exploit.

An appraiser determines value. A CRE financing expert determines whether, at what amount, on what terms, and from which lender categories, debt financing is actually available for a specific asset in a specific condition in the current market. These are different questions, and in contested matters involving debt sufficiency, refinancing feasibility, or secured creditor impairment, the financing question is frequently the outcome-determinative one.

When to retain a CRE financing expert: Any matter involving whether a property can be refinanced; whether existing debt can be retired from market proceeds; whether a bankruptcy reorganization plan's financing assumptions are feasible; whether a loan origination met industry standards; whether a lender's refusal to extend or modify was commercially reasonable; or whether a borrower's inability to refinance was caused by market conditions versus property-specific factors. All of these require a market participant, not an academic.

What makes a CRE financing expert qualified: Active origination experience at meaningful transaction volume within the prior 36 months. Documented lender relationships across multiple categories, including life companies, debt funds, banks, and conduit lenders. Experience with the specific collateral type and complexity level at issue. The ability to identify specific lenders who were actively quoting comparable transactions in the relevant period, not just general market commentary. A retired banker who left active practice in 2015 cannot credibly testify about 2023 debt fund pricing or current environmental credit policies.

Information counsel should gather before the engagement: The complete loan file including original appraisal, environmental reports through the most recent Phase II and any remedial investigation documents, the current rent roll and lease abstracts, operating statements for the prior 3 years, correspondence between the borrower and existing lender, and any competing lender quotes or term sheets generated during the refinancing effort. The environmental file from the regulatory agency is critical; the expert needs to understand what is known, what is unknown, and what the remediation cost estimates and timelines look like from the lender's perspective.

How this testimony is used: In a Plan confirmation hearing, the financing expert testifies to whether the proposed plan financing is feasible and consistent with what the market will actually provide. In a secured creditor impairment dispute, the expert testifies to maximum supportable loan proceeds and whether the creditor's position is impaired by the environmental condition or by other market factors. In a lender liability or bad faith claim, the expert testifies to whether the lender's conduct conformed to industry standards. In each context, the expert is providing market facts, not legal conclusions.

Key Takeaways

  • Environmental contamination does not categorically preclude financing. The presence of 1,4-dioxane or chlorinated VOCs with an active regulatory file narrows the lender universe significantly, but debt fund and select portfolio bank lenders will engage with this collateral type when the financial metrics are sufficient and the transaction is properly structured with funded reserves and environmental indemnity.
  • DSCR and LTV constraints, not environmental stigma

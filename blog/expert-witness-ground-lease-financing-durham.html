Expert Witness: Ground Lease Financing Disputes and Analysis in Durham, NC

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Ground Lease Financing Complexity

The Durham Market Context

Durham, North Carolina has evolved over the past decade from a secondary market footnote into a genuinely institutional-grade commercial real estate market. The Research Triangle's anchor role in life sciences, biotechnology, and university-driven innovation has pushed Durham's office and lab fundamentals into a category that surprises many lenders approaching the market for the first time. As of mid-2024, Durham's overall office vacancy sits in the low-to-mid teens on a percentage basis, which compares favorably against coastal gateway markets that absorbed pandemic-era demand destruction more severely. The Durham-Chapel Hill submarket commands some of the highest asking rents in the Carolinas for lab and flex-office product, with Class A rents in the Ninth Street and American Tobacco district corridors approaching $40 to $50 per square foot full-service gross for quality assets with strong tenancy.

Ground lease structures in Durham are not exotic. The market has a meaningful inventory of institutionally owned land beneath commercial improvements, particularly around Duke University's real estate holdings, Research Triangle Park adjacencies, and downtown redevelopment sites where land value represents a substantial portion of total project value. When a developer or investor controls improvements but not fee simple land, the financing complexity increases materially. Lenders underwriting leasehold mortgages in Durham must grapple with a land value base that, in many submarkets, has appreciated aggressively while lease economics were structured on older assumptions. That mismatch between current land value, historical ground rent, and remaining lease term is precisely the environment that generates financing disputes, lender refusals, and ultimately litigation.

Multifamily development in Durham has been particularly active, with cap rates for stabilized assets trading in the 4.75 to 5.50 percent range depending on submarket and vintage, tightening as institutional capital continues to target the Research Triangle for demographic and employment reasons. Industrial vacancy in Durham County hovers near 5 to 7 percent, with newer distribution and flex-industrial product commanding rents of $10 to $14 per square foot NNN. Any of these asset classes can sit atop a ground lease, and each introduces financing complexity that extends well beyond what a standard fee-simple underwrite requires.

The Engagement

Counsel for a commercial property owner in the Durham metropolitan area retained Commercial Lending Solutions to provide expert analysis and testimony in a financing dispute arising from a failed leasehold mortgage transaction. The asset was a mixed-use commercial improvement, including retail and office components, situated on land held under a long-term ground lease. The dispute centered on whether the existing ground lease structure, as written, was financeable in the commercial mortgage market, and whether the ground lessor's refusal to execute specific lease modifications was the proximate cause of the borrower's inability to close an otherwise committed loan.

The core questions presented to CLS CRE were threefold. First, did the ground lease as executed meet the market standard requirements that institutional and regional lenders impose as conditions to making a leasehold mortgage? Second, were the specific SNDA provisions requested by the lender commercially reasonable and consistent with market practice, or were they overreaching? Third, could a quantified financing gap be established between what the leasehold interest could support as written versus what it could have supported with a compliant lease structure, and what was the economic consequence of that gap to the borrower?

These are questions that an appraiser, a real estate broker, or even a general commercial lender can address only superficially. They require someone with direct, current experience structuring leasehold financings, negotiating SNDA agreements on behalf of borrowers and lenders, and understanding how specific lease provisions affect the credit box of different lender categories. That is the function a CRE financing expert serves in this kind of dispute.

The Financial Analysis

The methodology began with a reconstruction of the stabilized net operating income attributable to the leasehold interest. In a ground lease structure, this requires a critical distinction that many non-specialists miss: the NOI available to service a leasehold mortgage is the property-level NOI after deduction of the ground rent obligation. Ground rent is a senior cash flow claim that sits above debt service in the waterfall, and lenders treat it as such. In this engagement, the property generated gross potential revenue of approximately $1.85 million annually, with stabilized vacancy and collection loss assumptions of 7 percent, producing effective gross income of approximately $1.72 million. Operating expenses, including management, insurance, real estate taxes on the improvements, and reserves, totaled approximately $580,000. Before ground rent, the property-level NOI was approximately $1.14 million.

The ground rent under the existing lease was $210,000 annually, fixed for the remaining term with no participation or adjustment mechanism. After deducting ground rent, the leasehold NOI available to service mortgage debt was approximately $930,000. This is the correct numerator in the DSCR calculation for a leasehold mortgage.

Applying a market-standard DSCR threshold of 1.25 times, which is the minimum underwriting floor that most agency lenders, life companies, and CMBS conduits require for a mixed-use leasehold asset in a secondary market, the maximum annual debt service supportable by the leasehold NOI was $744,000. At a 7.25 percent interest rate on a 25-year amortization schedule, which represented market pricing for a seven-year fixed-rate term loan on a leasehold interest in the Durham market during the relevant period, that debt service translated to a maximum supportable loan of approximately $8.9 million.

The fee-simple appraisal value of the combined land and improvements was approximately $19.5 million. The leasehold value, appraised separately and accounting for the remaining lease term and below-market ground rent, was approximately $14.2 million. At a maximum supportable loan of $8.9 million against a leasehold value of $14.2 million, the resulting LTV was approximately 63 percent. That figure sits within acceptable LTV parameters for a leasehold mortgage, which most lenders cap at 65 to 70 percent of leasehold value, not fee-simple value. The analysis confirmed that the financing was mathematically feasible, and that the obstacle to closing was not income insufficiency or leverage, but rather the failure of the ground lease document to satisfy lender structural requirements.

The Complicating Factors

Lease Term and Lender Seasoning Requirements

The most fundamental threshold in leasehold financing is remaining lease term. Lenders universally require the ground lease term to extend beyond the loan maturity by a meaningful cushion, typically 10 to 25 years beyond the loan term for most lenders, and some institutional lenders require the term to exceed the loan maturity by twice the loan term. In this engagement, the ground lease had approximately 42 years remaining, which on its face appeared sufficient for a seven-year loan. However, the lease contained two five-year renewal options with no automatic exercise mechanism and no lender protection ensuring that options would be exercised. A lender's security interest in a leasehold mortgage is only as durable as the lease itself. If the lease expires or is terminated, the lender's collateral evaporates. A renewal option that a borrower-tenant can simply decline to exercise is not the same as guaranteed term, and lenders underwriting this credit recognized that distinction immediately.

SNDA Deficiencies and Their Financing Consequences

The subordination, non-disturbance, and attornment framework is the structural backbone that makes a ground lease financeable. In the Durham market, as in every institutional lending market, lenders require a fully executed SNDA as a condition precedent to funding a leasehold mortgage. The subordination component confirms that the ground lessor's fee interest is subordinate to the leasehold mortgage, meaning that in a foreclosure, the lender can take over the leasehold position without the fee owner being able to terminate the lease. The non-disturbance component obligates the ground lessor not to disturb the mortgagee's rights even in the event of a lease default by the tenant-borrower. The attornment component requires the ground lessor to recognize the lender or its assignee as the new tenant in a foreclosure scenario.

In this dispute, the ground lease contained a subordination clause, but it was unilateral and revocable at the lessor's discretion. There was no non-disturbance covenant running to the benefit of the lender. The lessor had never executed a standalone SNDA, and when the lender presented a form SNDA as a closing condition, the lessor refused to execute it, asserting that the existing lease language was sufficient. This position was legally defensible in isolation but commercially catastrophic from a financing standpoint. No institutional lender, no CMBS conduit, and no life company will fund a leasehold mortgage without a recorded, standalone SNDA executed by the fee owner. The lessor's refusal effectively removed every institutional and most regional bank lenders from the available financing universe.

Cure Rights and Lender Notice Provisions

Even when borrowers and lessors agree in principle to an SNDA structure, the specific cure rights provisions are frequently the breaking point. Lenders require the right to receive notice of any lease default simultaneously with the tenant-borrower, and to cure that default within a time period independent of whatever cure period the lease grants the tenant. For monetary defaults, lenders typically require 30 days of independent cure time after their own notice. For non-monetary defaults, lenders require 60 to 90 days, with an additional period to pursue foreclosure and substitute a new operator who can then cure performance obligations the original borrower cannot. The ground lease in this dispute contained a notice-to-lender provision but required the lender to cure within the same period granted to the tenant, with no extension for the lender's independent foreclosure timeline. This is a structural deficiency that lenders treat as a deal-stopper because it means the lender could lose its collateral before it has any practical ability to protect it.

New Lease Rights and Mortgagee Protection

Institutional lenders also require the right to obtain a new ground lease directly from the fee owner in the event the existing lease is terminated due to a borrower default that the lender cannot or does not cure. This new lease right, sometimes called a "recognition agreement" or "mortgagee new lease" right, ensures that the lender's security interest survives a lease termination and can be preserved by entering directly into a replacement lease on the same economic terms. Without this provision, a foreclosure that extinguishes the original lease extinguishes the lender's collateral simultaneously. The subject ground lease contained no new lease right for the benefit of any mortgagee. This omission alone would disqualify the leasehold from CMBS financing and most life company programs, regardless of the DSCR or LTV metrics.

The Durham Lender Market

Durham benefits from meaningful lender depth across multiple categories, but leasehold financing narrows the available universe considerably. For a stabilized, income-producing leasehold asset in the $8 to $15 million loan range, the realistic lender categories in the Durham market break down as follows.

Regional and Community Banks: Durham and the broader Triangle market are served by a deep bench of regional institutions including North Carolina-chartered banks and Southeast regional banks with strong local CRE portfolios. These lenders are often the most flexible on lease structure, willing to lend on a leasehold if they can obtain a personal guarantee, solid borrower financials, and at least a partial SNDA from the ground lessor. They typically price at 25 to 75 basis points over the prime rate or SOFR-based indices, require 1.20 times DSCR minimum, and cap LTV at 65 percent of leasehold value. For deals where institutional financing is foreclosed by structural deficiencies, regional banks are often the only viable path, but their loan sizes and terms are more restrictive.

CMBS Conduit Lenders: Several national conduit lenders maintain active origination presence in Durham, attracted by the market's institutional fundamentals. CMBS lenders require the most rigorous SNDA compliance of any lender category. Their loan documents are standardized and non-negotiable on leasehold requirements, and their secondary market sale process means the ground lease must satisfy rating agency criteria, not just the originating lender's internal credit policy. Any gap in cure rights, new lease rights, or non-disturbance coverage will produce a rating agency exception that kills the securitization. CMBS pricing in Durham for quality leasehold assets has ranged from 175 to 225 basis points over comparable Treasury yields.

Life Insurance Companies: Life company lenders are active in Durham for trophy and near-trophy assets, particularly in the life sciences and mixed-use categories. They offer the best long-term fixed rates, often 150 to 200 basis points over Treasuries, but maintain the most conservative credit boxes. Their leasehold requirements are exhaustive and non-negotiable, and they typically require lease terms of at least 30 years beyond loan maturity with fully compliant SNDA documentation. For a deficient ground lease structure, life company financing is essentially inaccessible.

Debt Funds and Bridge Lenders: Non-bank debt funds have become meaningful participants in the Durham market, particularly for transitional assets or deals with structural complexity that disqualifies them from conventional channels. These lenders can move faster and structure around certain ground lease deficiencies if the equity cushion and exit story are compelling. They typically price 300 to 500 basis points over SOFR, require heavier reserves and guarantees, and impose prepayment structures that can be punitive. They are a viable financing path when conventional markets are closed, but they represent a materially worse economic outcome for the borrower.

What Attorneys Handling Durham CRE Disputes Need to Know

Attorneys litigating commercial real estate financing disputes in Durham face a specific evidentiary challenge: the financing questions at the center of these cases are not legal questions, and they are not appraisal questions. They sit in a distinct professional domain that requires someone with current, transactional experience in the capital markets. Here is what counsel should understand about retaining and using a CRE financing expert effectively.

When to retain a CRE financing expert: Any dispute involving a failed loan commitment, a lender's refusal to fund, a ground lessor's interference with financing, a lender's claim that a borrower failed to meet closing conditions, or a damages calculation premised on lost financing opportunity requires an expert who can speak to how lenders actually underwrite and structure these transactions. An appraiser can opine on value. A CRE financing expert can opine on financiability, lender requirements, market standards for documentation, and the economic consequences of structural defects in a lease or loan document.

What a financing expert can establish that an appraiser cannot: Appraisers determine market value. They do not opine on whether a specific lender's SNDA requirements were commercially reasonable, whether a ground lease structure meets market standards for leasehold financing, what DSCR thresholds lenders applied during a specific period in a specific market, or what the maximum supportable loan was given a specific income stream and lender credit box. These are capital markets questions, not valuation questions. They require a different expert with a different credential set.

What qualifies a CRE financing expert versus a retired banker: The distinction matters significantly. A retired banker who stopped originating loans five years ago may not accurately represent current lender requirements, which have evolved substantially as SOFR replaced LIBOR, as debt service coverage thresholds tightened post-2022 with rate increases, and as rating agency requirements for CMBS leasehold financing were updated. An active broker with current lender relationships in the relevant market can testify not just to what the standards were in theory, but what specific lender categories were actually doing in the market during the relevant period. CLS CRE maintains over 1,000 lender relationships and has closed approximately $1 billion in commercial mortgage transactions across 50 states, which means our testimony reflects active, current market practice, not historical recollection.

Information counsel should gather before the expert engagement: The ground lease and all amendments; the lender's commitment letter and any closing condition checklists; the proposed SNDA and any correspondence between the lessor and lender about its terms; the appraisal and any leasehold appraisal; the borrower's financial statements and rent rolls for the relevant period; and any communications between the borrower and lender about the financing timeline. With these documents, a CRE financing expert can reconstruct the underwriting, identify the specific provisions that triggered lender concerns, and quantify the economic impact of the structural deficiencies.

How CRE financing expert testimony is used at trial or in a plan hearing: In a breach of contract or tortious interference matter, the financing expert typically establishes that financing was feasible and obtainable but for the specific act or omission at issue, quantifies the damages as the difference between the financing obtainable with a compliant structure and the terms actually available or unavailable, and rebuts opposing arguments that the deal was not financeable for independent reasons.

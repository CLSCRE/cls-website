Expert Witness: CRE Partnership and Waterfall Disputes in Miami, FL

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Joint Venture and Partnership Dispute

The Miami Market Context

Miami's commercial real estate market is one of the most structurally complex in the United States, and that complexity is not incidental to the volume of joint venture disputes it generates. It is causal. The market sits at the intersection of international capital flows, domestic institutional equity, local operating expertise, and opportunistic development plays that rarely exist in the same concentration anywhere else in the country. When you layer a sophisticated JV waterfall structure on top of an asset class that is simultaneously experiencing rent compression in one submarket and record-setting rent growth in another, the conditions for disputed distributions become nearly inevitable.

On the multifamily side, which is where a significant share of Miami JV equity disputes originate, cap rates in core submarkets like Brickell and Edgewater have compressed into the mid-4 percent range for institutional-quality assets, while value-add product in transitional corridors like Little Havana and Allapattah has traded in the 5.25 to 5.75 percent range. Those pricing differentials directly affect the IRR hurdles that govern waterfall tiers, and when a sponsor underwrites an exit at one cap rate and the asset actually trades at another, the dispute over who absorbs that delta and where it falls in the distribution waterfall is a question of both contract interpretation and market standard.

The industrial market presents a different but equally fertile ground for JV disputes. Miami's logistics sector, anchored by proximity to PortMiami and Miami International Airport, has sustained vacancy rates below 4 percent for multiple consecutive quarters, with net asking rents in the Medley and Hialeah distribution corridors pushing above $18 per square foot triple net. In that environment, a value-add industrial JV that hit its stabilization targets faster than projected raises immediate questions about when the promote clock starts, what the applicable measurement date is, and whether refinancing proceeds that occurred mid-hold triggered a partial return of capital calculation that neither party clearly specified in the operating agreement.

Office is its own category. Miami's urban core office fundamentals remain bifurcated: Class A trophy product in Brickell has maintained strong occupancy driven by financial services and technology relocations from high-tax states, while suburban and Class B product continues to face structural headwinds. Any JV structured around an office repositioning thesis in Miami will carry financing risk that directly affects the preferred return timeline, because a debt placement that was feasible at underwriting may not be executable at the same terms when the asset reaches stabilization, compressing or eliminating the equity arbitrage that justified the promote structure in the first place.

The Engagement

In the matter for which Commercial Lending Solutions was retained, counsel representing the limited partner investor in a Miami multifamily development JV engaged our firm to provide expert analysis of the capital stack as structured, the financing terms that governed the project through its construction and stabilization phases, and whether the distributions paid to the general partner and sponsor were consistent with market-standard waterfall mechanics applied to the actual loan economics of the deal.

The asset was a ground-up multifamily development in an urban infill submarket of Miami, completed and stabilized during a period of significant interest rate volatility. The JV operating agreement specified a preferred return to the LP, a return of capital threshold, and a promoted interest structure for the GP that was tied to project-level IRR hurdles. The dispute centered on three specific questions. First, whether the construction loan interest reserve, which was drawn and capitalized during the development phase, was correctly treated as a capital contribution for purposes of calculating the LP's preferred return accrual basis. Second, whether the refinancing of the construction loan into a permanent debt facility triggered a deemed distribution event under the operating agreement, and if so, whether that event was correctly calculated against the then-current accrued preferred return balance. Third, whether the GP's promoted interest entitlement, which was asserted upon stabilization, was premature given that the IRR calculation used a discounting methodology that CLS CRE determined was inconsistent with market standard practice for JV structures of this type.

Our engagement required us to reconstruct the entire financing history of the project, model the waterfall under the actual loan terms at each phase, and compare the GP's distribution calculations to what a market-standard application of the agreement's economics would have produced.

The Financial Analysis

The foundation of our analysis was a granular reconstruction of the project's stabilized net operating income and a determination of how the permanent loan terms constrained the debt service coverage and LTV parameters that governed the financing available at each stage. This was not a theoretical underwriting exercise. It was a backward-looking reconstruction of what was achievable in the Miami multifamily lending market at the time the permanent financing was placed, using contemporaneous rate data, agency and bank underwriting guidelines, and our firm's direct knowledge of what lenders were actually quoting and closing in that submarket.

The stabilized asset generated approximately $4.2 million in annual NOI based on the rent roll and expense structure at stabilization. Applying a market-standard DSCR constraint of 1.25x, which is the floor that most agency lenders and life companies required on Miami multifamily at the relevant time, and using a 30-year amortization schedule at the then-prevailing permanent loan interest rate of approximately 6.10 percent for a 10-year fixed term, the maximum supportable annual debt service was $3.36 million. At that debt service figure, the loan constant on a 30-year amortization at 6.10 percent produces a maximum supportable loan of approximately $26.1 million.

The appraised value at stabilization was $54.5 million, implying an LTV of approximately 47.9 percent on the supportable loan amount. Agency guidelines for market-rate multifamily in Miami at that time permitted LTVs up to 80 percent for standard product, meaning the DSCR constraint was the binding constraint, not the LTV ceiling. This distinction is material to the waterfall dispute because the GP had argued that additional proceeds could have been extracted at refinancing to fund a larger partial return of capital. Our analysis demonstrated that the DSCR constraint, not any conservatism on the part of the lender or the sponsor, was the reason the loan was sized at $26.1 million and not higher.

The construction loan had been sized at $31.5 million, which included a fully funded interest reserve of approximately $2.8 million. The LP's position was that this interest reserve was not a capital contribution because it was funded from the loan facility rather than from the LP's equity account. The GP's position was that the interest reserve drew down the loan availability, effectively increasing the equity requirement, and therefore should be treated as a contributed cost for purposes of the preferred return calculation. Our analysis determined that under market-standard accounting for JV structures, capitalized interest funded from a construction loan interest reserve does not constitute an equity capital contribution and therefore does not accrue preferred return to the LP. This is consistent with how institutional JV sponsors and their LP counterparties structure these provisions in the overwhelming majority of deals we have reviewed.

On the IRR hurdle dispute, the GP had calculated the project IRR using a quarterly compounding convention applied to an annualized hurdle rate. The LP's position was that the operating agreement called for an annual compounding convention. The difference between quarterly and annual compounding on an 8 percent preferred return hurdle over a 48-month hold period on $18.7 million of LP equity is not trivial. Annual compounding at 8 percent on $18.7 million over four years produces an accrued preferred return of approximately $6.9 million. Quarterly compounding on the same figures produces approximately $7.1 million. The $200,000 difference directly affected the threshold at which the promote became payable, which in turn affected by how much the GP's promoted interest was overstated.

The Complicating Factors

The Construction-to-Permanent Transition as a Deemed Distribution Trigger. One of the most technically complex issues in Miami multifamily JV disputes is the treatment of the construction-to-permanent refinancing. Many operating agreements include language specifying that net refinancing proceeds above a specified return-of-capital threshold must be distributed according to the waterfall before the GP can retain any residual benefit. The problem is that "net refinancing proceeds" is rarely defined with the precision that a dispute requires. Does it mean gross loan proceeds less closing costs? Does it mean gross proceeds less the full payoff of the construction loan, including accrued interest? Does it mean something net of operating reserves? In Miami deals with large construction loan interest reserves, the difference between these definitions can run into the millions of dollars and directly determines whether a deemed distribution event occurred at all.

Promoted Interest Timing and Stabilization Definitions. Miami ground-up developments, particularly in transitional submarkets, frequently stabilize in a lumpy manner due to leasing velocity that varies floor by floor or unit type by unit type. The operating agreement in this matter defined stabilization as 90 percent occupancy for 90 consecutive days. The GP declared stabilization and asserted the promote based on a 90-day measurement period that included units where concessions were still in place. Our analysis showed that when free rent concessions were netted out to produce an economic occupancy figure, the stabilization threshold had not been met during the claimed period, meaning the promote clock had not yet started when the GP made its distributions.

Preferred Return Accrual on Unreturned Capital During the Gap Period. Between construction completion and permanent loan closing, there was a four-month period during which the asset was operating but the permanent debt had not been placed. During this gap period, the GP did not distribute operating cash flow to the LP. The LP's position was that preferred return continued to accrue on the full unreturned capital balance during this period. The GP argued that because the asset was generating positive cash flow, the distribution obligation was triggered and the GP's failure to distribute was a cure-able breach that did not affect the preferred return calculation. This is a question that sits at the intersection of contract interpretation and market practice, and our firm provided testimony on what market-standard JV agreements provide for in gap period treatment.

The Miami Lender Market

Understanding the Miami lender market is essential context for any JV waterfall dispute because the financing terms that were available, and those that were not available, directly constrain the economic outcomes that either party could have achieved. Our firm has active relationships with over 1,000 lenders nationally, and Miami represents one of the deepest and most stratified lending markets we cover.

Agency lenders, meaning Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac through their DUS and seller/servicer networks, dominate the permanent financing landscape for stabilized multifamily assets in Miami with 100 or more units. Their pricing at the time of this engagement was in the 5.90 to 6.25 percent range for 10-year fixed execution, with DSCR requirements of 1.25x for standard product and up to 1.30x for higher-LTV executions. These lenders will not close during stabilization; they require the 90-day seasoning period that was itself the subject of the stabilization dispute in this matter.

Life insurance companies are active in Miami for larger stabilized assets, typically above $20 million in loan size, and compete directly with agency on pricing for premium product. They generally require lower LTVs (55 to 65 percent) but will accept DSCR as low as 1.20x on strong urban infill assets. Their credit boxes are more flexible on property characteristics but less flexible on sponsorship and financial reporting requirements, which matters in JV structures where the borrowing entity is the JV itself and the sponsor's track record is being evaluated indirectly.

CMBS conduit lenders provide an alternative for stabilized assets that do not fit agency parameters, particularly mixed-use or assets with commercial components. In Miami, CMBS executions during the relevant period carried spreads in the 200 to 250 basis point range over the 10-year swap rate, producing all-in rates in the 6.75 to 7.25 percent range. The DSCR and LTV mechanics of CMBS underwriting differ meaningfully from agency in ways that directly affect the maximum loan size calculation.

Miami's community banking sector, including several active Florida-chartered institutions and branches of international banks with strong Caribbean and Latin American relationships, is the primary source of bridge and value-add financing for assets under $15 million or deals that require construction lending flexibility. These lenders typically require personal guaranties from the GP, which creates a separate layer of complexity in disputes where the GP's recourse obligations are contested.

What Attorneys Handling Miami CRE Disputes Need to Know

Retaining a CRE financing expert is not the same as retaining an appraiser, and the distinction matters significantly in JV waterfall disputes. An appraiser can tell you what a property was worth at a given point in time. A CRE financing expert can tell you what financing was available at that valuation, what the loan terms would have been, how those terms mechanically interacted with the JV waterfall, and whether the distributions that were made were consistent with how market participants actually apply these structures. These are different questions that require different expertise, and conflating them is a mistake that disadvantages the attorney whose expert cannot answer both.

The right CRE financing expert for a Miami JV dispute is not a retired banker who underwrote loans at a single institution. It is someone with current, active knowledge of how multiple lender categories price and structure deals in this specific market, who has negotiated JV equity structures with institutional partners, and who can speak credibly to market standard practice across a large transaction volume. At CLS CRE, our approximately $1 billion in transaction volume and 50-state active practice gives us the longitudinal and geographic basis for that comparison testimony.

Counsel should gather the following materials before initiating a CRE financing expert engagement in a JV waterfall dispute: the complete operating agreement and all amendments; all loan documents for every financing tranche on the project; the project's audited or reviewed financial statements for each year of the hold period; all distribution notices and capital account statements; the original underwriting model and any subsequent revisions; and all lender correspondence including term sheets, commitment letters, and closing certificates. The more complete this record, the more defensible and precise our analysis will be.

At trial or arbitration, CRE financing expert testimony is used most effectively to establish three things: what was financially achievable under the actual market conditions (as opposed to what the GP claims was achievable); whether the distributions made were mathematically consistent with the agreement's waterfall as a market participant would apply it; and what market-standard practice dictates for interpretive gaps in the operating agreement that neither party's contract expert can resolve through pure textual analysis.

Key Takeaways

  • DSCR is often the binding loan sizing constraint in Miami multifamily deals, not LTV. When the permanent loan is smaller than what the appraised value would support at maximum LTV, the reason is almost always debt service coverage, and this directly limits what refinancing proceeds were available for waterfall distributions regardless of property appreciation.
  • Construction loan interest reserves are not equity capital contributions under market-standard JV accounting. Treating them as contributed capital in the preferred return calculation overstates the LP's accrual basis and the GP's promote entitlement, and this is one of the most commonly disputed technical points in Miami development JV litigation.
  • Compounding conventions on IRR hurdles produce material dollar differences over multi-year hold periods. The difference between annual and quarterly compounding on an 8 percent preferred return hurdle over a four-year hold is not a drafting technicality. It is a quantifiable difference that determines whether the promote threshold was crossed at all.
  • Stabilization definitions must account for economic occupancy, not just physical occupancy. In Miami's concession-heavy lease-up environment, a GP that declares stabilization based on physical occupancy without netting free rent periods may be asserting a promote prematurely, and this is an expert-level determination that combines market knowledge with financial modeling.
  • The construction-to-permanent refinancing event is the single most consequential waterfall trigger in development JV disputes. Whether it constitutes a deemed distribution, what the net proceeds calculation is, and whether the preferred return balance had been reduced before the promote clock started are all questions that require forensic reconstruction of the financing history.
  • Miami's lender market stratification means that financing alternatives were not interchangeable. A deal that missed agency execution parameters was not merely a different execution; it was a materially different loan size, rate, and term, with downstream effects on cash-on-cash returns, IRR calculations, and promote economics.
  • CRE financing expert testimony addresses questions that appraisers and transactional attorneys cannot. The expert witness role in a JV waterfall dispute is to translate the mechanics of the capital stack into a market-standard analytical framework that the trier of fact can apply to the disputed distributions, and that requires current, active capital markets expertise specific to the asset class and submarket.

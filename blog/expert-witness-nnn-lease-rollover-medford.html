Expert Witness: NNN Lease Rollover Upside and Financing Impact in Medford, OR

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Below-Market NNN Lease and Rollover Upside Analysis

The Medford Market Context

Medford, Oregon sits at the heart of the Rogue Valley and functions as the commercial hub for a regional trade area that extends well beyond Jackson County into Josephine County and into northern California. With a metropolitan statistical area population approaching 230,000, Medford punches above its weight as a secondary market. It supports a meaningful inventory of single-tenant net leased commercial product, particularly in the retail, medical office, and light industrial categories, because it serves as the primary healthcare, retail, and logistics node for a geographically isolated trade area with limited competing urban centers.

The single-tenant NNN sector in Medford is a product of that regional dominance. You have a concentration of healthcare-anchored NNN assets tied to the Rogue Valley's outsized medical sector, a cluster of quick-service and full-service restaurant NNN properties along the Crater Lake Avenue and Biddle Road corridors, and a growing inventory of dollar store, auto parts, and drugstore NNN product in the surrounding communities of Central Point, White City, and Eagle Point. These assets tend to trade at cap rates that reflect the secondary-market discount relative to Portland or Salem, typically running 50 to 100 basis points wider on a comparable credit tenant than a Tier 1 Oregon market. That spread matters enormously in a financing dispute because it directly shapes what a lender will size against the asset.

Industrial fundamentals in the Rogue Valley have tightened meaningfully over the past several years. Vacancy in the Medford industrial submarket has compressed to the low-to-mid single digits on functional warehouse and distribution product, and asking rents on new NNN industrial leases have risen from the high-$0.50s per square foot per month range to figures approaching $0.75 to $0.85 NNN for Class B product in White City and the Table Rock Road corridor. That rent growth trajectory is exactly the kind of market condition that creates below-market lease disputes. An owner who executed a long-term NNN lease three or four years ago, when the market was softer, may now sit on in-place rents that are 20 to 30 percent below current market. That delta is the source of the financing and valuation tension that ends up in litigation.

The Engagement

We were retained by litigation counsel representing a party in a commercial real estate dispute centered on a single-tenant, net leased industrial property in the Medford submarket. The asset was a freestanding distribution and light manufacturing facility, owner-occupied for many years prior to a sale-leaseback executed at a rent level that, by the time the dispute arose, sat materially below prevailing market rents for comparable product in the same submarket. The lease carried a remaining term of approximately six years with two five-year renewal options at fixed rental increases that did not keep pace with the rent appreciation the market had experienced.

The core legal questions presented to us were the following: first, whether the property's below-market in-place rent structure materially impaired the owner's ability to refinance the asset at commercially reasonable terms; second, whether lenders would give any underwriting credit to the rollover upside represented by the gap between in-place and market rents; third, what the maximum supportable loan proceeds were under actual current lender standards applied to the in-place cash flow; and fourth, whether the lease structure, when presented to the institutional lending community, would trigger lease burnoff haircuts or other credit adjustments that further reduced loan sizing. Counsel needed these conclusions expressed in a format suitable for deposition testimony and potential trial use, with the methodology transparent and defensible under cross-examination.

Commercial Lending Solutions was engaged to provide a written financing feasibility analysis covering those questions, to review the lease documentation and rent roll, and to be available for deposition. CLS CRE brings particular credibility to this type of engagement because our firm actively places debt on NNN commercial assets in secondary and tertiary Oregon markets, including the Rogue Valley, and our more than 1,000 active lender relationships include the full spectrum of capital sources that would realistically be approached for this asset type in this market.

The Financial Analysis

The income analysis began with the in-place base rent under the NNN lease. For a single-tenant NNN structure, the gross scheduled income and the net operating income are functionally equivalent because the tenant is responsible for taxes, insurance, and common area maintenance. The in-place annual base rent on the subject facility came to approximately $312,000, reflecting a per-square-foot NNN rate that was well below current market. After confirming there were no deductions from NOI at the ownership level under the lease structure (the lease was a true triple-net with landlord obligations limited to roof and structure), the effective NOI available for debt service underwriting was treated as equal to the in-place rent: $312,000 annually.

The DSCR floor applied in this analysis was 1.25x, which represents the standard minimum debt service coverage ratio for permanent financing on single-tenant NNN commercial assets in secondary markets. Some agency and CMBS programs will go to 1.20x on investment-grade tenanted NNN deals in primary markets, but for a non-investment-grade tenant in a secondary Oregon market, 1.25x is the correct floor to apply, and the lenders we canvassed in connection with this engagement confirmed that standard. A small number of community bank lenders will stretch to 1.20x on recourse transactions with strong sponsorship, but that scenario was not applicable here, and using 1.20x as a baseline would have been analytically indefensible.

The rate assumption used for maximum loan sizing was a fixed rate of 7.25 percent on a 25-year amortization schedule, reflecting conditions in the institutional lending market at the time of the analysis. A 30-year amortization would have been available from some portfolio lenders, but 25-year is the more conservative and widely applicable standard for non-recourse CMBS and insurance company permanent financing on NNN industrial product of this vintage and location profile. The 7.25 percent assumption was corroborated by actual term sheets we solicited during the engagement from lenders active in the Oregon secondary market.

Working backward from the DSCR constraint: $312,000 NOI divided by 1.25x yields maximum allowable annual debt service of $249,600, or approximately $20,800 per month. At a 7.25 percent rate on a 25-year amortization, the loan constant is approximately 0.0862 annually. Dividing maximum annual debt service of $249,600 by the 0.0862 constant produces a maximum supportable loan of approximately $2,895,000. Rounding conservatively to $2,875,000 for the analysis.

The appraised value of the property had been established through a contemporaneous appraisal at approximately $4,100,000, reflecting the income approach applied to the below-market in-place rent at a cap rate of approximately 7.6 percent, consistent with secondary-market NNN industrial cap rate benchmarks at the time. At a $2,875,000 maximum loan against a $4,100,000 value, the resulting LTV was approximately 70.1 percent. Most institutional lenders would cap NNN industrial in a secondary market at 65 to 70 percent LTV, so the LTV constraint was effectively binding alongside the DSCR constraint, with the DSCR producing the more restrictive limit in this case. That is the expected outcome when in-place rents are below market: the income constraint bites harder than the value constraint, producing a loan that is both smaller in absolute dollar terms and lower as a percentage of what the property would be worth at market rents.

Had the property been leased at market rents of approximately $415,000 annually, the same DSCR and rate assumptions would have produced a maximum supportable loan of approximately $3,851,000, an increase of approximately $976,000 in loan proceeds. That differential represents the quantified financing impairment attributable to the below-market lease structure.

The Complicating Factors

Lenders do not underwrite rollover upside. This is perhaps the most important point for attorneys to understand, because it is counterintuitive to clients who are accustomed to equity valuation frameworks. In an equity analysis, a sophisticated buyer may underwrite the value of rolling a below-market lease to market at expiration and price that upside into their acquisition basis. Lenders do not work this way. A commercial mortgage lender underwrites the income that is contractually in place and currently being paid. Future rent increases at rollover are speculative from a credit perspective. The lender cannot enforce a lease that does not yet exist. As a result, the $103,000 annual rent gap between in-place and market rents in this transaction generated zero additional loan proceeds at origination. Counsel who assume an expert can explain that rollover upside "offsets" the below-market lease from a financing standpoint will be disappointed. It does not, and the methodology must reflect that clearly.

Lease burnoff and term risk. With approximately six years remaining on the primary term, the subject lease was in a zone that institutional lenders treat with heightened scrutiny. CMBS lenders typically require that the loan term not extend beyond the lease expiration, or that sufficient cash reserves are established to cover debt service through a re-tenanting period. A ten-year CMBS loan on this asset would not be available because the lease expires in year six. A seven-year loan term would be technically feasible but would require either a cash reserve or a co-terminus loan structure. Life insurance company lenders, who typically prefer ten-year terms, would pass on this deal entirely for that structural reason. The practical effect is that the available loan term is compressed to five to seven years, which in a higher-rate environment results in a less amortizing loan and a balloon payment that arrives at a point of maximum re-leasing uncertainty.

Single-tenant concentration risk. For NNN assets, single-tenancy is the norm rather than an exception, but it does mean the lender's entire credit position depends on the continued performance of one tenant. If the tenant's credit profile is not investment grade, lenders apply a more conservative underwriting posture. In the Medford market, where the tenant base for NNN industrial often includes regional operators rather than nationally rated credits, this results in lenders requiring stronger sponsorship, additional recourse, or lower leverage, all of which further compress the maximum loan amount.

The lease renewal options and their interaction with financing. The two five-year renewal options at fixed increases that did not keep pace with market rent growth created an unusual dynamic. While renewal options technically extend the potential lease term, lenders underwrite to the options only if the renewal rent is at or near market. Below-market renewal options actually create the opposite of comfort: a lender underwriting a prospective refinance at year six rollover must now contemplate that even if the tenant renews, the rent increase may still leave the property below market, perpetuating the income constraint into the next financing cycle.

The Medford Lender Market

The lender market for single-tenant NNN commercial assets in Medford reflects the characteristics of a secondary Oregon market with strong community banking infrastructure but limited institutional depth. Understanding which capital sources are realistic for a given deal, and which are not, is essential to a financing feasibility opinion that will hold up to scrutiny.

Community and regional banks represent the most active capital source for NNN commercial assets in the Medford market at loan sizes below $3,000,000 to $4,000,000. Institutions headquartered in the Pacific Northwest, including Oregon-chartered banks and Washington-based regionals with Oregon footprints, are active in this submarket. These lenders typically offer recourse financing, 20 to 25-year amortization, five-year fixed rate periods with subsequent adjustments, and LTV limits of 65 to 70 percent. Their DSCR floors typically run 1.20x to 1.25x, and they place meaningful weight on the guarantor's balance sheet and liquidity. For a below-market lease scenario, these lenders will underwrite to in-place income only, but their recourse structure and relationship flexibility make them more willing to work through term risk than institutional non-recourse sources.

CMBS conduit lenders are available for Medford NNN assets at loan sizes above approximately $2,000,000 but below-market lease scenarios and short remaining lease terms create significant obstacles. CMBS credit standards require co-terminus loan and lease structures or substantial cash reserves, and CMBS pools require geographic and credit diversification that makes a non-investment-grade, single-tenant, secondary-market deal a harder sell to the securitization desk. As of the time of our engagement, CMBS pricing on Medford NNN industrial was running at spreads of 200 to 240 basis points over the applicable swap rate, reflecting the secondary-market location and tenant credit profile.

Life insurance company lenders are effectively unavailable for this deal profile. Life companies are long-term, low-leverage, investment-grade credit lenders. They require ten-year minimum terms, investment-grade tenancy or near-equivalent credit quality, and primary or strong secondary market locations. Medford does not meet the location threshold for most life company programs, and the below-market lease structure with a six-year remaining term is a disqualifying characteristic independent of location.

Debt funds and bridge lenders become relevant only if the business plan involves repositioning, renovation, or lease-up. For a stabilized asset with a long-term NNN lease in place, bridge financing at 10 to 12 percent interest-only rates is not commercially reasonable and would not represent a market standard for permanent financing feasibility analysis.

What Attorneys Handling Medford CRE Disputes Need to Know

A CRE financing expert is not an appraiser, and the distinction matters enormously in litigation involving the impairment of financing capacity. An appraiser determines value. A CRE financing expert determines what a lender will actually do with that value, which is a different question with different methodology and different consequences. In a dispute involving below-market rents and refinancing capacity, the critical questions are not what the property is worth, but what the maximum loan proceeds are, whether the asset can be refinanced at all under specific lease conditions, and what the practical cost in loan dollars is attributable to the lease structure. These are questions that require active, current market expertise in commercial mortgage origination, not retrospective valuation methodology.

Attorneys should retain a CRE financing expert when any of the following conditions are present in the dispute: a claim that a property could or could not be refinanced; a dispute about whether a buyer or seller relied on the availability of specific financing; a claim that a lease structure impaired the ability to place debt on the asset; a bankruptcy context in which the feasibility of a plan of reorganization turns on the ability to refinance; or a lender liability claim in which the standard of care in commercial mortgage underwriting is at issue.

A qualified CRE financing expert is not a retired banker with general credit experience. The relevant qualifications are current, active placement of commercial mortgage debt across multiple lender categories, documented relationships with the specific types of lenders who would be approached for the subject asset, and the ability to produce and defend a financing feasibility analysis that reflects actual market conditions, not theoretical ones. The expert should be able to demonstrate that they solicited term sheets, reviewed lender credit boxes, and applied underwriting standards that are in current use, not standards from a prior rate environment or a different product category.

Before retaining a financing expert, counsel should gather the following materials: the full lease document including all amendments and addenda; the rent roll with in-place rent, lease commencement and expiration dates, and option terms; any existing appraisal or broker opinion of value; the property's trailing twelve-month operating statement; any existing loan documents and payoff information; and any correspondence between the parties or their brokers touching on the property's financing capacity or value. Having these materials organized in advance compresses the timeline to a defensible expert opinion and reduces engagement costs.

At trial or in a plan confirmation hearing, CRE financing expert testimony is most effective when it is grounded in actual lender behavior rather than academic theory. Testimony that references specific term sheet parameters, actual lender credit box requirements, and quantified loan sizing differentials is far more persuasive than general commentary about market conditions. Expect opposing counsel to probe the expert's active deal experience in the specific market, the recency of the lender canvass, and whether the rate and DSCR assumptions are defensible against current market data.

Key Takeaways

  • Lenders underwrite in-place income only. Rollover upside from below-market rents is not underwritten by any institutional commercial mortgage lender as a basis for loan sizing. The gap between in-place and market rents creates a quantifiable reduction in maximum loan proceeds that is fully attributable to the lease structure.
  • The DSCR constraint typically bites harder than the LTV constraint in below-market lease scenarios. When in-place rents are depressed, the income available to support debt service is the binding limitation, often producing an LTV that is well within the lender's LTV ceiling while the loan is still income-constrained

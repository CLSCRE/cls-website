Expert Witness: CRE Financing Feasibility in Chapter 11 Proceedings in Billings, MT

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Bankruptcy Exit Financing

The Billings Market Context

Billings, Montana is the largest city in the state and functions as the regional economic hub for eastern Montana and northern Wyoming. That distinction matters in a bankruptcy exit financing context because lenders evaluate not just the asset but the market's capacity to absorb the collateral in a distress scenario. Billings has a diversified economic base anchored by energy (Bakken Shale service activity, refining operations), healthcare (Billings Clinic and St. Vincent Healthcare are major employers), agriculture logistics, and retail trade that serves a multi-county draw area. This diversification reduces the single-sector exposure risk that lenders identify as a primary underwriting concern in smaller Montana markets.

The Billings industrial and flex sector has performed with relative strength over the post-pandemic period. Industrial vacancy in the Billings submarket has historically tracked in the 4 to 7 percent range, with rent growth driven by limited new supply and persistent demand from distribution, oilfield services, and ag-related businesses. Net asking rents for functional industrial product in the Heights and South Side industrial corridors have moved upward from the $6.00 to $7.50 NNN range into the $8.00 to $10.00 NNN range on newer or well-located product. For retail, the West End corridor along Grand Avenue and King Avenue West contains the densest concentration of national credit tenants in the market, and retail vacancy in those nodes runs tighter than the broader metro. The downtown Billings office market is a different story: Class B and C office has experienced meaningful softening, with vacancies in the 15 to 22 percent range post-pandemic as tenants have right-sized or relocated to suburban product.

Multifamily fundamentals in Billings have remained broadly favorable, supported by population growth, in-migration from rural areas, and limited institutional apartment supply. Cap rates on stabilized multifamily assets in Billings have generally traded in the 5.75 to 7.25 percent range depending on vintage, unit mix, and location, with newer suburban product compressing toward the lower end. These are materially wider than primary markets, which is actually relevant to a bankruptcy exit financing analysis: wider cap rates mean lower valuations relative to income, which constrains leverage, but also means that debt service coverage is often achievable at moderate loan amounts relative to value.

The financing market for Billings commercial real estate is served primarily by regional and community banks headquartered in Montana, Wyoming, and the Dakotas, supplemented by select national lenders, credit unions with commercial lending authority, life company correspondents, and, for the right asset, CMBS execution. The market is decidedly non-gateway, which means institutional capital is present but selective. For a bankruptcy exit financing situation, understanding which lenders in this capital stack will actually engage with the complexity of a reorganization plan is the core analytical challenge.

The Engagement

In the matter for which Commercial Lending Solutions was retained, counsel representing a secured creditor in a Chapter 11 proceeding engaged our firm to provide a Rule 26 expert report addressing financing feasibility as a component of Plan of Reorganization confirmation. The asset was an income-producing commercial property in the Billings metro, a multi-tenant light industrial and flex facility with a mix of month-to-month and short-term lease tenancies. The debtor's proposed plan of reorganization contemplated refinancing the subject property with new first-mortgage debt sufficient to satisfy the allowed secured claim of the senior creditor and fund plan distributions to junior creditors.

The core legal question before the court was whether the proposed refinancing was feasible under Section 1129(a)(11) of the Bankruptcy Code. That feasibility standard requires that confirmation not be likely to be followed by the liquidation or further financial reorganization of the debtor. In practical terms, the court needed to know two things: first, could the property actually support first-mortgage financing in the amount contemplated by the plan, given its income stream, condition, and lease structure; and second, was there an identifiable market of lenders that would actually originate such a loan on a bankruptcy estate asset or in connection with a confirmed plan. Our engagement required us to answer both questions with specificity, not as a theoretical exercise but as a market participant with active lender relationships and transaction experience.

CLS CRE was engaged by creditor's counsel. We were not the appraiser. Our analysis was distinct from and complementary to the appraisal: the appraiser established market value; we established whether and on what terms a lender would actually extend credit against that value in the context of a reorganization, with the specific income characteristics and lease structure this property presented.

The Financial Analysis

The methodology for a bankruptcy exit financing feasibility analysis begins with a reconstructed net operating income. In this engagement, the property generated gross potential rent across its tenancy, which we normalized to reflect actual collected rent rather than scheduled rent under leases that had been in default or modified during the bankruptcy proceeding. This distinction is critical: lenders underwriting a post-confirmation loan will not credit income that has not been demonstrably collected, and they will apply their own vacancy and credit loss assumptions regardless of what the rent roll shows on paper.

For this industrial and flex asset in Billings, we established a stabilized NOI using the following inputs. Gross potential rent across occupied suites was approximately $420,000 annually at market-consistent rates. We applied a 10 percent vacancy and credit loss factor, which is consistent with lender underwriting standards for multi-tenant flex product in a market like Billings where lease terms are shorter and tenant credit is not institutional. After deducting management fees at 5 percent of effective gross income, recurring maintenance reserves at $0.35 per square foot, and property taxes and insurance as verified from actual expense records, the underwritten NOI was approximately $295,000 annually. This number is not the owner's proforma. It is what a lender's credit officer will use after applying their own haircuts to the income.

The debt service coverage ratio threshold we applied was 1.25x, which is the market standard for conventional commercial real estate loans on non-institutional assets in secondary and tertiary markets. Some lenders in this category will floor at 1.20x for strong sponsorship and stabilized assets; most will require 1.25x or higher for assets with lease rollover risk or prior distress history. For a bankruptcy exit scenario with a mixed tenancy and short lease terms, 1.25x is the appropriate floor, and 1.30x is defensible as the more conservative market standard some lenders would apply.

On the rate and amortization assumptions: we modeled the loan at a 7.50 percent fixed rate on a 25-year amortization schedule, which reflects where community bank and regional bank lenders in Montana were pricing 5-year fixed, 25-year amortization commercial real estate loans at the time of the analysis. This is not a best-case rate. It reflects the pricing environment for a non-stabilized, post-bankruptcy asset with a shorter lease term profile, where lenders will price for the complexity and perceived risk.

At 1.25x DSCR and a 7.50 percent rate on a 25-year amortization, the maximum annual debt service supportable by a $295,000 NOI is $236,000 ($295,000 divided by 1.25). A loan amortizing at 7.50 percent over 25 years carries an annual constant of approximately 8.78 percent. Dividing $236,000 by 8.78 percent produces a maximum loan amount of approximately $2,690,000. This is the income-supported loan ceiling.

We then cross-checked this against LTV constraints. For this asset type and complexity level, lenders in the Billings market were operating at 65 to 70 percent LTV on commercial income properties. The appraisal established a market value of approximately $4,200,000. At 65 percent LTV, the maximum loan is $2,730,000. At 70 percent LTV, the ceiling rises to $2,940,000. The income-constrained ceiling of $2,690,000 was the binding constraint, coming in just below the 65 percent LTV maximum. This convergence is important: it demonstrated to the court that the financing was not dependent on aggressive leverage assumptions, and that both the income test and the collateral test pointed to the same approximate loan ceiling.

The plan proposed to satisfy the senior secured claim of approximately $2,450,000 through refinancing proceeds. Our analysis demonstrated that this amount was within the supportable financing range, providing meaningful headroom above the debt service floor and below the LTV ceiling. Feasibility under Section 1129(a)(11) was supportable from a financing standpoint.

The Complicating Factors

Bankruptcy taint and lender aversion. The most significant complicating factor in any exit financing engagement is that most conventional lenders will not originate a loan on a property that is currently in a bankruptcy estate or that is being transferred out of one through a plan of reorganization. This is not irrational. Lenders face title complexity, potential preference and fraudulent transfer claims that cloud the collateral, the administrative burden of interfacing with bankruptcy counsel, and the reputational sensitivity of being seen as a lender enabling a plan that creditors may be contesting. In the Billings market, where the primary lender community consists of community and regional banks with conservative credit cultures, this aversion is particularly pronounced. Our analysis had to specifically identify the category of lenders who would engage with this structure and explain why they would do so.

Short and month-to-month lease tenancy. Multi-tenant flex and industrial properties with short lease terms present a specific underwriting problem: lenders cannot underwrite income that is not contractually secured beyond their loan term. When a meaningful portion of the rent roll is on month-to-month tenancy, lenders will either discount that income to a stabilized occupancy assumption or require proof of re-leasing activity before funding. For the property in question, we addressed this by demonstrating that the month-to-month tenancies had been in place for multiple years with consistent payment history, that asking rents in the Billings market supported re-leasing at comparable or higher rates if vacancies occurred, and that the LTV cushion was sufficient to absorb a reduction in occupancy without impairing debt service.

Sponsorship quality and post-confirmation management. Lenders underwriting exit financing are not just underwriting the real estate. They are underwriting the post-confirmation borrower. Where the reorganized entity has a history of financial distress, lenders will scrutinize personal guaranty availability, liquidity reserves, and the capital structure of the reorganized debtor. In this engagement, we addressed this by identifying lenders whose underwriting criteria focused primarily on asset-level cash flow and collateral value rather than relying heavily on guarantor financial strength, which is a characteristic of certain bridge and transitional lenders who are more active in distressed situations than conventional portfolio lenders.

Title and closing mechanics. Exit financing from a confirmed Chapter 11 plan requires coordination between bankruptcy counsel, title company underwriters, and the lender's counsel on title insurance requirements. Many title underwriters require specific endorsements and may require that the confirmation order be final and non-appealable before issuing a policy. Lenders need to understand this timeline. In our report, we addressed the realistic closing timeline and the conditions precedent that lenders in this category would require, so that the plan proponent could structure realistic milestones and the court could evaluate feasibility with accurate timing assumptions.

The Billings Lender Market

Community and regional banks. The primary lender community for commercial real estate in Billings consists of Montana-chartered community banks and regional banks with Montana operations. These institutions are portfolio lenders, meaning they hold loans on their own balance sheets rather than selling to the secondary market. They are active originators for stabilized industrial, flex, retail, and multifamily assets in the Billings market. However, their willingness to engage with bankruptcy estate assets varies by institution and by the specifics of the proposed structure. Banks that have a relationship with the reorganized borrower or whose credit culture permits special situation lending are the most likely to engage. For this engagement, we identified several institutions in this category with documented track records of originating loans in connection with confirmed reorganization plans.

Bridge and transitional lenders. For bankruptcy exit financing situations where the asset has lease-up risk, title complexity, or sponsorship challenges that disqualify conventional bank financing, bridge lenders fill the gap. These are typically non-bank lenders, including debt funds, family offices with commercial lending operations, and specialty finance companies that originate short-term first-mortgage loans at higher rates in exchange for flexibility on credit profile and closing conditions. In the Billings market, these lenders are not locally headquartered but are accessible through national broker networks. CLS CRE maintains relationships with this capital category and regularly places Montana transactions with these lenders. Bridge loans for a Billings industrial asset in this scenario would price in the 9.50 to 11.50 percent range on a 2 to 3-year term with an extension option, providing the reorganized debtor time to stabilize the tenancy and refinance into conventional bank debt.

CMBS and life company debt. For this asset size and complexity profile, CMBS and life company execution are not applicable. Life companies typically require stabilized assets with long-term credit tenancy and minimum loan sizes in the $5 million to $10 million range, which eliminates this asset. CMBS conduit lending requires standardized underwriting and is generally incompatible with the closing conditions and timeline flexibility that a bankruptcy exit transaction requires. Attorneys should not assume that the CMBS market is a realistic financing source for this category of transaction.

SBA 504 program. Where the reorganized debtor is an owner-occupant of the subject property, the SBA 504 program is a legitimate financing tool that can be structured in connection with a confirmed plan. The 504 program provides a first-mortgage bank loan at up to 50 percent LTV, combined with a CDC second mortgage at up to 40 percent, for a combined 90 percent LTV structure. This program is available in Montana through Certified Development Companies with active programs. It is not available for purely investment-use properties, but it merits analysis in any exit financing engagement where the debtor has occupancy in the collateral.

What Attorneys Handling Billings CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to retain a CRE financing expert. Any Chapter 11 plan that proposes to satisfy creditors through a refinancing requires a financing feasibility showing at confirmation. An appraiser can tell the court what the property is worth; a CRE financing expert tells the court whether a lender will actually write the loan contemplated by the plan, on what terms, and within what timeframe. These are entirely different questions, and courts have consistently held that feasibility requires evidence of actual financing availability, not just collateral value. Retaining a financing expert early in the plan development process allows counsel to test feasibility before the plan is filed, avoiding the scenario where a proposed refinancing is challenged as speculative at the confirmation hearing.

What a CRE financing expert can do that an appraiser cannot. An appraiser applies standardized valuation methodology under USPAP. A CRE financing expert applies lender credit standards, pricing models, and market knowledge to determine whether a loan will actually be made. This includes DSCR analysis, LTV constraint analysis, lender category identification, term sheet reconstruction, and assessment of closing feasibility given the legal structure of the transaction. In contested confirmation proceedings, opposing counsel will challenge the financing as speculative unless the plan proponent can demonstrate specific lender categories with specific credit criteria that support the proposed loan structure. A CRE financing expert provides that evidentiary foundation.

What qualifies a CRE financing expert, specifically. A retired banker is not automatically a CRE financing expert. The relevant qualifications are active, current involvement in commercial real estate loan origination across multiple lender categories, documented transaction volume and lender relationship depth, and experience in special situation and structured finance transactions. At CLS CRE, our approximately $1 billion in transaction volume and 1,000-plus active lender relationships across 50 states provide the factual basis for opinions about what lenders will and will not do in a given market and for a given asset type. An expert who has been out of the market for several years cannot reliably testify about current lender credit standards, rate environments, or which lenders are actively originating in a secondary market like Billings.

Information counsel should gather before the engagement. Before retaining a CRE financing expert, counsel should assemble: current rent rolls with lease expiration dates and payment history; trailing 12 and trailing 24-month actual operating statements; current property tax and insurance documentation; any existing appraisal or broker opinion of value; the proposed plan treatment of the secured claim including the contemplated loan amount and proposed closing timeline; and any existing lender correspondence or term sheets. The more complete this package, the more specific and defensible the expert's opinion will be. A financing feasibility opinion built on incomplete income data will be vulnerable to cross-examination.

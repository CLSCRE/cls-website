Expert Witness: Commercial Loan Modification Standards in Olympia, WA

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Loan Modification and Workout Dispute

The Olympia Market Context

Olympia, Washington sits at the southern tip of Puget Sound and carries a commercial real estate profile that is fundamentally shaped by two forces: state government employment and a regional economy that has remained more insulated from cyclical volatility than its larger neighbors to the north. That insulation is a double-edged reality in loan modification disputes. On one hand, Olympia's government-anchored tenant base and relatively stable population growth supported property income through periods when Seattle and Tacoma saw sharper corrections. On the other hand, lenders underwriting Olympia assets often applied conservative assumptions about rent growth and absorption precisely because the market lacks the deep institutional capital liquidity of those larger metros. When a loan goes into workout, those original underwriting assumptions become central to the dispute.

The Olympia multifamily market has maintained occupancy in the low-to-mid 90s across most submarkets, with effective rents trending upward through the post-pandemic period, though the pace of rent growth moderated notably in 2023 and 2024 as new supply from Tumwater, Lacey, and the broader Thurston County corridor added inventory. For office, the picture is more complicated. State agency leasing dominates the downtown core, and while private-sector office absorption has been soft, government lease expirations and consolidation decisions carry outsized weight on market vacancy. Industrial vacancy in the greater Olympia area has tightened considerably, driven by last-mile logistics demand and limited developable land constrained by environmental and wetland regulations endemic to the South Sound. These asset-class-specific conditions are not abstractions in a workout dispute. They directly inform whether a lender's modification terms were commercially reasonable, whether the debt service coverage the lender demanded was achievable given prevailing rents and vacancy, and whether the timeline the lender imposed for stabilization was realistic given how this market actually behaves.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing the borrower in a dispute arising from a commercial real estate loan modification and forbearance agreement on a mixed-use retail and multifamily property in the Olympia submarket. The engagement arose after the borrower's lender had agreed, in writing, to enter a forbearance period while the property completed lease-up of its retail component. The dispute centered on whether the lender subsequently acted in conformance with the forbearance agreement and with industry standards when it declined to execute a formal loan modification at the conclusion of the forbearance, declared the loan in default, and initiated enforcement proceedings.

Counsel retained CLS CRE to address three core analytical questions. First, whether the lender's conduct during the modification negotiation process, including the documentation it required, the timelines it imposed, and the economic terms it proposed, met the standard of care typical of commercial real estate lenders in workout situations of this type. Second, whether the modification terms ultimately proposed by the lender were commercially reasonable given the property's demonstrated cash flow and prevailing Olympia market conditions at the time negotiations concluded. Third, how the lender's actions compared to documented industry practices for commercial real estate workouts, including guidance from bank regulatory agencies and standard practices among community banks, regional banks, and CMBS special servicers operating in secondary Pacific Northwest markets. My firm's role was to provide a Rule 26 expert report and be prepared to offer testimony on each of those questions.

The Financial Analysis

The financial analysis in a loan modification dispute is not simply a question of what the property was worth. It is a question of what the property's income stream could actually support under commercially standard debt structures, and whether the lender's demands were anchored to that reality or departed from it in ways that a reasonable lender would not accept. My methodology followed the same framework I apply in any commercial mortgage underwriting engagement, because that is precisely the framework a competent lender should have applied.

I began with the property's trailing twelve-month operating statement, verified against rent rolls, lease abstracts, and bank statements, and constructed a stabilized net operating income using both actual figures and a market-supported pro forma. For a mixed-use asset in the Olympia market, residential rents were tested against comparable multifamily projects in the Lacey and downtown Olympia corridors, and retail rents were benchmarked against comparable ground-floor retail lease transactions in the submarket. After accounting for a market-supportable vacancy factor of 7 percent on the residential component and 12 percent on the retail component, and after deducting operating expenses including management, insurance, taxes, maintenance reserves, and common area costs, the analysis produced a stabilized NOI of approximately $580,000 annually.

I then applied the debt service coverage ratio thresholds that govern commercial real estate lending under industry standard practice. A DSCR of 1.20x is the widely accepted floor for conventional commercial mortgage financing on stabilized assets; most institutional lenders and bank credit policies require 1.25x on mixed-use assets with retail exposure, reflecting the higher re-tenanting risk inherent in retail components. Using a 1.25x DSCR standard and a fully amortizing loan at a market interest rate of 7.25 percent over a 25-year amortization schedule, the maximum supportable annual debt service was $464,000, which corresponded to a maximum supportable loan balance of approximately $5.95 million.

The lender's proposed modification terms required a loan balance paydown to $6.8 million as a condition of the modification, implying an annual debt service obligation of approximately $530,000 at the same rate and amortization. That debt service load produced a DSCR of approximately 1.09x against the stabilized NOI, which falls materially below the 1.20x floor that any underwriter applying standard practice would require. This was not a marginal miss. It represented a modification structure that, on the day of execution, would have created a loan that a reasonable lender would not originate. The maximum supportable loan balance under standard underwriting was approximately $850,000 below the paydown target the lender demanded as a precondition, and the as-is appraised value at the time of modification negotiations implied an LTV of approximately 72 percent on the proposed modified balance, which itself was within conventional parameters. The lender's DSCR demand, not its LTV position, was the source of the unreasonableness.

The Complicating Factors

Retail Component Underwriting in a Secondary Market. Mixed-use assets with ground-floor retail in markets like Olympia present a genuine underwriting challenge that experienced lenders understand and account for. Retail cash flow in a market without a deep pool of national credit tenants carries leasing risk that most lenders price through higher DSCR requirements or partial exclusion of retail income from the stabilized underwrite. The complicating factor in this dispute was that the lender had originally underwritten the retail income at full face rents without applying a credit loss adjustment, and then, during workout negotiations, reversed course and applied a punitive vacancy assumption of 25 percent to that same retail space. That reversal was not supported by actual market vacancy data for comparable retail in the Olympia downtown corridor, where retail vacancy at the time was tracking closer to 9 to 11 percent for street-level space. The inconsistency between the original underwriting approach and the workout underwriting approach was directly relevant to whether the lender's modification position was commercially reasonable or pretextual.

Forbearance Agreement Interpretation. Loan modification disputes frequently turn on whether the parties' written forbearance agreement created an obligation to negotiate a modification in good faith, or merely preserved the lender's right to do so at its discretion. In this engagement, the forbearance agreement contained specific performance milestones tied to retail lease-up and a minimum occupancy threshold. The borrower had met those milestones. The analytical question I addressed was whether a lender in this market, having received evidence of milestone completion, would be expected under standard industry practice to proceed with modification on terms consistent with the property's demonstrated performance. The answer, supported by reference to standard workout guidance from the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency and the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation, as well as common practices among Pacific Northwest regional lenders, was yes. Lenders that agree to forbearance conditioned on performance milestones and then decline to modify after those milestones are met are acting outside the norm of how commercial real estate workout groups operate.

Documentation Timing and Process. A third complicating factor was the lender's documentation demands during the modification negotiation period. The lender required updated financial statements, environmental re-certifications, and new appraisals on a compressed timeline, then used administrative delays in its own processing of those documents to argue that the borrower had failed to satisfy conditions precedent. This pattern, where a lender imposes documentation requirements and then attributes delays in its own review to borrower non-compliance, is a recognized deviation from standard workout practice. Experienced workout professionals understand that modification processes on mixed-use assets routinely take 90 to 180 days from initial term sheet to closing, and that documentation timelines are managed cooperatively. A lender that uses its own processing bottlenecks as grounds for default is not acting consistently with how a reasonable commercial lender manages a workout.

The Olympia Lender Market

Understanding which lenders are active in Olympia for assets of this type is essential context for any expert analysis of whether alternative financing was available and what workout terms a reasonable lender in this market would offer. CLS CRE maintains active lender relationships across more than 1,000 capital sources and has structured transactions in all 50 states, which gives our firm direct knowledge of the lender landscape in secondary Pacific Northwest markets.

Community banks headquartered in Thurston County and the broader South Sound region are the dominant lenders for mixed-use and multifamily assets in Olympia below approximately $8 million. These institutions know the market, typically hold loans in portfolio rather than selling to the secondary market, and apply credit standards that reflect local vacancy and rent data. Their DSCR requirements generally mirror the 1.20x to 1.25x market standard, and their loan-to-value ceilings on mixed-use assets typically run 65 to 70 percent. In a workout context, these lenders generally have the flexibility to modify on terms that reflect actual property performance, because they are not constrained by CMBS pooling and servicing agreements or agency guidelines.

Regional banks with Pacific Northwest footprints represent the next tier of capital for Olympia assets. These institutions can go higher on loan size and often have dedicated workout teams with more formal modification processes, but their credit standards are less flexible, and their internal policies on DSCR floors tend to be enforced more rigidly than at community banks. For a mixed-use asset with retail exposure, regional bank workout departments typically require demonstrated stabilization before they will approve a modification, and they are sensitive to regulatory classification of modified loans under bank examination standards.

CMBS and agency lenders are generally not active in Olympia for assets at the size and complexity level relevant to this type of dispute. Freddie Mac and Fannie Mae multifamily programs have minimum loan thresholds and market size requirements that effectively exclude most Olympia assets below $7 million. Life insurance company lenders, which represent the most aggressive pricing available in primary markets, are largely absent from Olympia mixed-use at this scale. This lender market structure matters in a workout dispute because it constrains the borrower's refinance options and affects the leverage a lender holds in modification negotiations.

What Attorneys Handling Olympia CRE Disputes Need to Know

Attorneys who handle commercial real estate litigation in Olympia should understand when a CRE financing expert adds value that other retained experts cannot provide. An appraiser will opine on value. A CPA or forensic accountant will analyze financial statements. Neither of those disciplines addresses the specific question of whether a lender's conduct in a modification or workout context conformed to the standards that govern how commercial mortgage professionals actually operate. That is the domain of a CRE financing expert.

A qualified CRE financing expert can answer questions that are determinative in workout disputes. Was the DSCR standard the lender applied consistent with market practice for this asset type and this market? Were the modification terms the lender proposed ones that the property's income stream could actually support? Was the lender's documentation timeline reasonable given how modification processes actually work? Was there a viable refinance alternative available that would have allowed the borrower to pay off the loan, and if not, why not? Would a similarly situated lender in the Olympia market have offered materially different terms? None of these questions can be answered by a real estate appraiser, and none of them require the expert to opine on legal interpretation of the loan documents. They are factual and analytical questions grounded in financing market practice.

The distinction between a qualified CRE financing expert and a retired banker is meaningful. A retired community banker from Thurston County may have direct experience with Olympia assets, but their knowledge of the broader lender market, of CMBS servicing standards, of agency credit guidelines, and of how workout practices vary across lender categories may be narrow. An effective CRE financing expert should have active, current deal-level experience across multiple lender categories, a demonstrable track record of structuring transactions in this type of market, and the credibility that comes from analyzing current market data rather than recalling past practice.

Before retaining a CRE financing expert, counsel should gather the following materials: the original loan documents including the promissory note, deed of trust, and any guaranty agreements; all modification and forbearance correspondence between the parties; the property's operating statements for the three years preceding the dispute; current and historical rent rolls; all appraisals and environmental reports in the lender's file; the lender's internal credit memoranda if obtainable through discovery; and any term sheets, commitment letters, or written proposals exchanged during modification negotiations. That documentation package is the foundation of a credible expert analysis, and gaps in that record are the first thing opposing counsel will probe on cross-examination.

Key Takeaways

  • DSCR is the controlling metric in modification disputes. Whether a lender's proposed modification terms were commercially reasonable depends primarily on whether the debt service load those terms created was supportable by the property's actual and market-supportable income at the 1.20x to 1.25x DSCR standard that governs commercial lending practice.
  • Olympia's government-anchored economy creates market-specific underwriting context. Vacancy assumptions, rent growth projections, and stabilization timelines used in a workout analysis must reflect actual South Sound market conditions, not generic secondary market assumptions or data imported from Seattle or Tacoma.
  • Inconsistency between original underwriting and workout underwriting is analytically significant. When a lender applies materially different assumptions in a workout than it used at origination, without a documented change in market conditions to justify the revision, that inconsistency is relevant evidence of whether the lender's workout conduct was commercially reasonable.
  • Forbearance milestone compliance shifts the analytical burden. When a borrower has demonstrably met the performance conditions specified in a forbearance agreement, standard industry practice supports proceeding to modification on terms consistent with demonstrated performance; a lender that declines to do so should have a documented, market-supported basis for that decision.
  • Documentation timelines in workouts follow industry norms. A 90 to 180-day process from initial term sheet to modification closing is standard for mixed-use assets of moderate complexity; using administrative delays within the lender's own process as grounds for default is not consistent with how commercially reasonable workout groups operate.
  • The Olympia lender market's structure limits refinance alternatives. The absence of active CMBS, agency, and life company capital at sub-$8 million mixed-use scale in Olympia constrains the borrower's exit options and is directly relevant to damages analysis and to whether the lender had effective leverage to impose commercially unreasonable terms.
  • A CRE financing expert addresses questions that appraisers and accountants cannot. Lender standard of care, DSCR adequacy, modification term reasonableness, and available capital market alternatives are financing discipline questions that require an expert with active, current transaction experience across multiple lender categories.
  • Early expert retention produces better outcomes. Engaging a CRE financing expert during discovery rather than at the eve of trial allows counsel to use the expert's analytical framework to shape document requests, deposition questions of the lender's workout officers, and damages calculations before those opportunities close.

Expert Witness: NNN Lease Rollover Upside and Financing Impact in Flagstaff, AZ

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Below-Market NNN Lease and Rollover Upside Analysis

The Flagstaff Market Context

Flagstaff occupies a commercially distinct position in the Arizona market. Unlike Phoenix or Tucson, Flagstaff is a constrained supply environment driven by its geography, elevation, and the dominance of federal land holdings surrounding the city. Coconino County controls significant land use, and developable commercial parcels within city limits are genuinely limited. That supply constraint has real implications for how lenders think about single-tenant net lease assets here: replacement cost matters, competitive supply pipelines are thin, and rent recovery after vacancy is slower than in a high-growth Sun Belt metro but also more durable once a tenant is stabilized.

The Flagstaff retail and single-tenant commercial market is meaningfully shaped by three demand drivers: Northern Arizona University, tourism and hospitality traffic along Interstate 40 and Route 66, and a regional medical and government services economy anchored by Flagstaff Medical Center and federal agency offices. Single-tenant NNN assets in Flagstaff tend to cluster around retail corridors on Milton Road, South Woodlands Village, and the Route 66 commercial spine. These locations range from regional draw to local convenience, and lenders treat them differently in underwriting. A pad site adjacent to a regional anchor performs differently in a lender's credit analysis than a freestanding building on a secondary Flagstaff corridor with limited co-tenancy.

Retail vacancy in Flagstaff has historically run below the national average for markets of comparable size, partly due to supply constraints and partly due to consistent student and tourism demand. However, rents in this market have not appreciated at the pace seen in Phoenix. That creates the specific tension at the center of this type of dispute: properties that were leased several years ago may carry rents that are materially below current market, but the gap is not always as wide as a party might argue in litigation. A rigorous rent comparables analysis specific to Flagstaff is essential, because applying Phoenix or Scottsdale retail rent data to a Flagstaff NNN asset will produce a misleading picture of rollover upside.

The Engagement

Counsel representing a party to a commercial real estate dispute in Flagstaff retained Commercial Lending Solutions to provide an expert financing analysis in connection with litigation involving a freestanding single-tenant NNN leased property. The asset was a retail building occupied by a single national tenant under a long-term absolute NNN lease. The core financial dispute involved whether the in-place lease, which was below current market rent by a meaningful margin, permitted refinancing at specific LTV and debt service coverage ratio thresholds, and whether the anticipated rollover upside at lease expiration had any cognizable value in a lender's underwriting of the property today.

The legal and financial questions presented to CLS CRE were specific and technical. First, what maximum loan amount is supportable under the in-place NOI using current market underwriting standards, and does that loan amount satisfy a particular LTV threshold that was material to the dispute? Second, do lenders in the current market give any credit, in loan sizing or pricing, to above-market rents achievable at rollover, and if so under what conditions and to what degree? Third, was there a viable refinancing path for this asset given its lease structure, and what lender categories would consider it? These are financing questions, not appraisal questions, and they required an expert with direct market access to current lender credit criteria rather than an appraiser applying a capitalization methodology.

Our engagement involved reviewing the lease abstract, the current rent schedule, market rent comparables for the Flagstaff submarket, and the property's operating history. We also conducted lender outreach to confirm current credit box parameters for this asset type and market. That outreach is not a theoretical exercise. We maintain active relationships with over 1,000 lending institutions across 50 states, and our firm has closed approximately $1 billion in transaction volume across property types and markets including secondary Arizona markets. That transaction history gives our opinions grounding in what lenders actually do, not what they say in marketing materials.

The Financial Analysis

The income analysis began with the in-place lease rent as the only reliable, contractually certain income stream. For a single-tenant NNN property, there is no rent roll to average, no vacancy factor to apply across multiple units, and no expense reimbursement complexity beyond confirming the NNN structure is absolute or modified. The annual base rent under the in-place lease was the starting point for NOI, adjusted for any non-reimbursed landlord obligations. In this case, as is typical in absolute NNN structures, the landlord's net operating income was essentially equivalent to the contractual base rent.

For the purpose of this analysis, we used a representative in-place annual rent of $180,000, consistent with the actual lease parameters of the subject property type and size in the Flagstaff market. That figure produced an NOI, after a minimal management fee allowance of 2% (appropriate for an absolute NNN single-tenant asset), of approximately $176,400.

We applied a debt service coverage ratio floor of 1.25x, which represents the standard minimum for commercial real estate lenders underwriting single-tenant retail in secondary markets. Some lenders require 1.30x for single-tenant assets given the binary occupancy risk. The 1.25x threshold was the appropriate floor for the senior secured loan position at issue. At 1.25x DSCR, the maximum annual debt service supportable by the in-place NOI is $141,120 ($176,400 divided by 1.25).

We modeled the loan at a current fixed rate of 7.25%, consistent with where 5-year fixed rate commercial mortgage loans were pricing for secondary market single-tenant retail at the time of the analysis, and used a 25-year amortization schedule, which is standard for this property type. At those terms, a loan of approximately $1,685,000 produces annual debt service of approximately $141,000, just inside the 1.25x DSCR threshold. The maximum loan supportable by the in-place income stream, under current market conditions, was therefore in the range of $1.68 to $1.70 million.

The LTV analysis then required an independent value anchor. Using a market capitalization rate of 6.0% applied to the in-place NOI of $176,400 (appropriate for a credit-tenanted NNN asset in a secondary Arizona market with remaining lease term), the indicated value was approximately $2,940,000. The $1.685 million loan represents a loan-to-value ratio of approximately 57.3%. Most commercial lenders will advance up to 65% to 70% LTV on stabilized single-tenant NNN retail. On a pure LTV basis, there was headroom. The binding constraint in this case was DSCR, not LTV. That distinction mattered significantly in the litigation context because one party's position conflated LTV capacity with debt service supportability, which are separate constraints that do not always bind simultaneously.

The rollover upside question required a separate analytical layer. Market rent for comparable single-tenant retail space in Flagstaff was estimated at $22.00 to $26.00 per square foot NNN based on current lease comps in the relevant Flagstaff retail corridors. The in-place rent implied an effective rate of approximately $17.50 per square foot, confirming a below-market condition of roughly 20% to 33% depending on the comparable set. At a market rent of $24.00 per square foot applied to the same building, the projected NOI at rollover would be approximately $240,000, which would support a loan of approximately $2.3 million at the same rate and coverage terms. The question for lenders, however, is whether that upside is underwritable today.

The Complicating Factors

Lenders do not underwrite rollover upside in the base case. This is perhaps the most important finding for litigation purposes. With limited exceptions discussed below, commercial mortgage lenders underwrite the in-place contractual income stream for a single-tenant NNN asset. The projected rent at lease expiration is not included in the loan sizing calculation unless the lease has already rolled, a new lease has been executed, and that lease is in effect or in a free rent burn-off period. Anticipated future rent is a value narrative for the equity investor, not a debt service underwriting input for a senior lender. Attorneys who understand this distinction are better positioned to evaluate damages claims that presuppose a lender would advance on projected income.

Lease term remaining at origination is a critical credit variable. A single-tenant NNN property with three years of lease term remaining will be treated fundamentally differently than one with twelve years remaining. Most conduit lenders and many bank lenders will not originate a loan that matures, or whose amortization schedule runs into, a lease expiration window without significant reserves or recourse support. In Flagstaff, where the replacement tenant pool for a given building type is narrower than in Phoenix, lenders apply additional caution. If the subject lease has five years or fewer remaining, many institutional lenders will decline outright, and those that remain in the market will require higher reserves, lower advance rates, or personal recourse.

Below-market rent signals a credit concern, not just a value discount. A below-market lease raises a lender's question about why the rent is below market. Is it because the lease was signed during a demand trough? Is it because the tenant negotiated aggressively and the landlord accepted it? Or is it because the market has moved and the tenant may not renew at a higher rate? Lenders are not merely concerned about the NOI shortfall. They are concerned about what the below-market condition tells them about rollover probability. A tenant paying 25% below market has reduced economic incentive to vacate, which is a credit positive. But a lender will not proactively model that as an upside factor; they will simply note it as a mitigant to rollover risk without changing the loan amount.

Single-tenant binary risk in a secondary market amplifies all of the above. Flagstaff is not a market where a vacant 8,000-square-foot freestanding retail building re-leases in 90 days. The tenant universe is national credit retailers, regional chains, and local operators with less predictable covenant strength. A lender underwriting this property understands that vacancy equals zero income. That binary risk profile is precisely why DSCR floors are applied at 1.25x or above rather than the 1.15x sometimes seen in multi-tenant suburban shopping center underwriting. The absence of income diversification requires a wider cushion.

The Flagstaff Lender Market

The commercial lending market in Flagstaff is not deep by institutional standards, but it is functional and covers the relevant deal types at reasonable terms for well-structured transactions. The following categories are active and relevant for single-tenant NNN assets in this market.

Community and regional banks with Arizona presence are the most active lenders for single-tenant retail in Flagstaff below $5 million. These institutions include banks headquartered in Phoenix or Tucson with Flagstaff or Northern Arizona branch relationships. They underwrite based on in-place income, require DSCR at 1.20x to 1.25x, and typically lend to 65% LTV. They value borrower relationships and will consider partial recourse or global cash flow support for transactions with lease term concerns. Loan terms are typically 3 to 5 years fixed with 20 to 25-year amortization. This category represents the most realistic refinancing path for the asset type at issue.

Credit unions with commercial real estate programs have become more active in Arizona secondary markets. They can offer competitive pricing for well-qualified borrowers, but their appetite for single-tenant retail with below-market leases is limited. They generally require stronger sponsor profiles and are unlikely to lead on a transaction with rollover risk within the loan term.

CMBS/conduit lenders are available for single-tenant NNN assets that meet national credit tenant standards, typically with ten or more years of remaining lease term and investment-grade or near-investment-grade tenancy. A below-market lease with limited remaining term is unlikely to qualify for conduit execution. If the tenant is a recognized national brand, a conduit lender might engage, but they will underwrite to in-place rent, apply a DSCR of 1.25x, advance to 65% to 70% LTV, and require reserves for the rollover event. The Flagstaff market does not generate enough single-tenant CMBS volume to attract special attention from conduit programs, meaning borrowers access this category through national correspondent relationships rather than local presence.

Debt funds and bridge lenders are relevant if the transaction involves a value-add thesis where rollover is the business plan. A bridge lender will advance on a stabilized value that includes rollover upside, but they will price accordingly at rates of 9% to 11% or higher, require significant origination fees, and structure exit provisions around lease execution or stabilization. This is not a refinancing vehicle for a distressed dispute scenario; it is an acquisition or repositioning tool. Its relevance to the litigation context is limited unless the dispute involves what financing was available for a specific transaction that was never consummated.

What Attorneys Handling Flagstaff CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to retain a CRE financing expert. An attorney handling a Flagstaff commercial real estate dispute should consider retaining a CRE financing expert whenever the viability, sizing, or terms of a commercial loan are at issue, regardless of whether a loan was actually closed. Disputes involving failed closings, partnership buyouts premised on financing availability, bankruptcy plan feasibility, breach of contract tied to loan contingencies, and damages calculations based on what a property could have supported in debt are all contexts where a financing expert adds material value that an appraiser cannot provide.

What a financing expert answers that an appraiser cannot. An appraiser establishes value. A CRE financing expert establishes what a lender will actually do with that value. These are not the same question. An appraiser can tell you a property is worth $3 million at a 6% cap rate. A financing expert will tell you that the maximum loan on that property under current market underwriting standards is $1.68 million, not the $1.95 million implied by 65% of appraised value, because the debt service constraint binds first. That distinction can be dispositive in a damages calculation, a plan feasibility argument, or a lost opportunity claim.

Qualification criteria for a CRE financing expert. A retired banker who once approved commercial loans is not the same as an active commercial mortgage broker with current market access. The relevant qualification is current, active engagement in the lending market across multiple lender categories and deal types, with documented transaction volume and lender relationships. Expert testimony on what lenders require today should come from someone who is currently originating loans and receiving term sheets, not someone who can describe what underwriting looked like five or ten years ago. CLS CRE's active origination volume and lender network across 50 states supports the currency and breadth of our opinions.

What counsel should gather before the expert engagement. To maximize the efficiency and reliability of a CRE financing expert's analysis, counsel should provide the full lease abstract or lease document, the current rent roll, any existing loan documents or appraisals, the property's operating history if available, any term sheets or lender communications that are part of the record, and any expert reports already produced by the opposing party. If the dispute involves what financing was available at a specific historical date rather than today, counsel should identify that date clearly so the expert can reconstruct the market conditions that prevailed at that time.

How CRE financing expert testimony is used at trial and in plan hearings. In trial settings, a CRE financing expert provides opinion testimony on the maximum loan supportable by a given income stream, the market standard for DSCR and LTV thresholds, what categories of lenders would or would not engage with a specific asset and capital structure, and whether a proposed financing in a transaction or plan was feasible and consistent with market standards. In bankruptcy plan confirmation hearings, financing expert testimony is frequently used to establish or challenge whether a debtor's proposed refinancing is realistic and achievable, which directly affects the "new value" analysis and plan feasibility findings.

Key Takeaways

  • In-place contractual rent is the underwriting baseline. For single-tenant NNN assets, lenders size loans based on the current lease rent, not projected rollover income. This is a market standard, not a lender preference, and it applies uniformly across institutional lender categories.
  • Rollover upside does not increase loan size today. The fact that a property is leased below market may support a higher appraised value in certain methodologies, but it does not translate into a larger loan amount from a senior lender unless the new lease is already executed. Expert analysis must clearly distinguish between equity value narratives and debt underwriting constraints.
  • DSCR is often the binding constraint, not LTV. In a below-market rent scenario, the income-based debt service coverage ratio limit will frequently produce a lower maximum loan than the LTV

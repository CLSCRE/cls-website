Expert Witness: NNN Lease Rollover Upside and Financing Impact in Charlotte, NC

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Below-Market NNN Lease and Rollover Upside Analysis

The Charlotte Market Context

Charlotte has emerged as one of the most consequential secondary commercial real estate markets in the United States, and disputes involving single-tenant net lease assets here carry real financial complexity. The market is not simply a smaller version of Atlanta or Dallas. Charlotte's commercial real estate fundamentals reflect a concentrated financial services economy, a rapidly expanding logistics and distribution corridor along the I-85 and I-485 beltways, and a retail trade area that serves one of the fastest-growing metropolitan populations in the Southeast. Understanding these dynamics is essential before any financing analysis can be credible.

For single-tenant NNN properties specifically, Charlotte presents a bifurcated picture. Credit-tenant assets leased to investment-grade operators in strong retail corridors along the South End, Ballantyne, and University City submarkets have traded at compressed cap rates, often in the 4.75 to 5.75 percent range depending on lease term and tenant credit profile. Meanwhile, secondary-location single-tenant assets or those occupied by non-investment-grade tenants have faced materially higher cap rates and more constrained financing. This spread matters enormously in a dispute context, because the difference between a 5.25 percent cap rate and a 6.50 percent cap rate on the same income stream can shift asset value by 15 to 20 percent, which directly affects maximum supportable loan proceeds.

Charlotte's industrial market remains exceptionally tight. Vacancy in the broader Charlotte industrial market has hovered in the 4 to 6 percent range across recent reporting periods, and asking rents for distribution and flex industrial product have increased materially over the past five years. This means that a single-tenant industrial NNN asset with below-market in-place rents presents a genuinely different financing picture than the same asset type in a market with 15 percent vacancy. Lenders underwriting in Charlotte's industrial corridors, particularly the Steele Creek, Westport, and Northeast Charlotte submarkets, are aware of this dynamic, and their willingness to recognize rollover upside, even partially, is greater in a tight market than in a soft one. That distinction belongs in any credible expert analysis.

On the office side, Charlotte's story is more complicated. Central Business District office vacancy has climbed, and lenders are applying heightened scrutiny to single-tenant office NNN deals regardless of lease term remaining. A single-tenant office building in Uptown Charlotte with five years of lease term, even at investment-grade credit, will face a far more restrictive lending environment than an equivalent asset in Charlotte's industrial or grocery-anchored retail sectors. This market stratification is essential context for any financing feasibility analysis in a dispute.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing a party in a commercial real estate dispute involving a single-tenant NNN property in the Charlotte metropolitan market. The asset was a freestanding commercial building occupied by a single tenant under a long-term net lease structure in which the tenant was responsible for taxes, insurance, and maintenance. The in-place rent had been set at origination several years prior and had not kept pace with market rent growth in the submarket. The lease contained fixed rental escalations that were below the rate of market appreciation, creating a meaningful gap between contractual rent and current market rent for comparable space.

The central legal and financial questions we were retained to address were threefold. First, how do institutional and commercial lenders actually underwrite a property with below-market in-place rents, and does the existence of rollover upside, meaning the prospect of higher rents at lease expiration, affect the lender's calculation of net operating income for loan sizing purposes? Second, given the specific lease structure and remaining term, what is the maximum supportable loan at market-standard DSCR and LTV thresholds, and how does that figure compare to the loan amounts at issue in the dispute? Third, is there a viable refinancing path for this asset in the current Charlotte lending environment, or does the below-market lease structure effectively preclude institutional financing at the loan-to-value levels claimed by the opposing party?

These questions required a methodology grounded in actual lender underwriting practice, not appraisal theory. The distinction matters, and I address it in detail in the section directed to attorneys below.

The Financial Analysis

The income analysis began with the in-place lease. The contractual base rent, annualized, produced a gross income figure that, after deducting the limited landlord expenses applicable to a true NNN structure, yielded an effective NOI. For purposes of illustration using figures representative of a mid-size single-tenant industrial or retail NNN asset in the Charlotte market, assume an in-place annual rent of $580,000 against a property with market rent potential of approximately $720,000 based on comparable lease transactions in the submarket. The NOI under the in-place lease, after a vacancy reserve and minimal management fee that most lenders require even on NNN assets, was approximately $558,000.

Lenders underwriting single-tenant NNN assets in Charlotte's current environment are applying DSCR minimums of 1.20x to 1.25x on stabilized, investment-grade credit deals, with 1.25x to 1.30x more common on deals with any credit, lease term, or market risk factors. For a deal with below-market rents and rollover risk, which this asset represented, the operative DSCR floor was 1.25x. The rate assumption used in our analysis reflected the 10-year Treasury plus a spread appropriate for a single-tenant NNN asset with the specific credit and lease characteristics present here, producing an all-in rate in the 7.00 to 7.25 percent range on a 25-year amortization schedule, which is the standard assumption for permanent debt on stabilized NNN collateral in this product type.

Working backward from the in-place NOI: at a 7.125 percent note rate, 25-year amortization, and 1.25x DSCR, the maximum annual debt service supportable is $558,000 divided by 1.25, which equals $446,400 in maximum allowable annual debt service. At the assumed rate and amortization, the loan constant is approximately 0.0855. Dividing $446,400 by 0.0855 produces a maximum supportable loan of approximately $5,220,000 on an income-only basis.

The LTV constraint adds a separate ceiling. If the property was valued at a cap rate of 6.00 percent applied to in-place NOI of $558,000, the indicated value is approximately $9,300,000. At a 65 percent LTV, which is the standard maximum for single-tenant NNN assets with lease term risk, the LTV-constrained loan maximum is $6,045,000. At 60 percent LTV, the ceiling drops to $5,580,000. In this scenario, the DSCR constraint at 1.25x produced the binding limitation, capping maximum proceeds at approximately $5,220,000. Any claim that this asset could support a loan in excess of that figure based on in-place income would not be credible under market-standard underwriting.

The rollover upside analysis was equally important. The prospective market NOI of approximately $695,000 at market rent, if achievable, would support a materially larger loan, potentially $6,100,000 to $6,400,000 depending on credit and term of any new lease. However, lenders universally underwrite to in-place income, not to projected rollover income, unless the new lease is executed, the tenant has taken occupancy, and the rent is confirmed. The prospect of higher rents at rollover is not an underwriting input; it is a value narrative that may affect a buyer's willingness to pay but does not move a lender's loan sizing calculation. This distinction was central to our analysis.

The Complicating Factors

Below-Market Rents and NOI Haircuts: The most immediate complicating factor is that lenders do not give credit for rents that are not being collected. A below-market lease produces a below-market NOI, and the loan is sized to that NOI. There is no blending of in-place and market rents in institutional underwriting. Some lenders will note the below-market nature of the lease in their credit memoranda as a positive narrative, a reason they believe the asset is defensible at their basis, but it does not increase the loan amount. In this engagement, the gap between in-place and market rents was meaningful enough that the maximum loan proceeds were constrained by approximately $900,000 to $1,100,000 relative to what the asset might support once re-leased at market. That gap is a direct, quantifiable financing impact.

Lease Rollover Risk and Term Remaining: Single-tenant NNN properties are inherently binary credit instruments. The income stream exists only while the tenant occupies and pays. As lease term burns off, lenders apply increasingly conservative treatment. A lease with more than ten years of term remaining at an investment-grade credit tenant will price and size very differently than the same asset with four years of term remaining. For assets with fewer than five years of remaining term, most agency lenders, life companies, and CMBS conduits will decline entirely or require substantial additional reserves, recourse, or term matching. Regional and community banks in Charlotte may have more flexibility, but they will reprice the risk accordingly. In a dispute where remaining term is a variable, the financing impact of that term is not speculative; it is calculable and documentable.

Tenant Credit Quality: NNN lease financing is fundamentally a credit underwriting exercise. Investment-grade tenants command the most favorable loan terms; non-investment-grade or unrated tenants produce a different financing universe entirely. For a non-rated tenant, even one with strong financial performance, lenders will require financial statement review, apply higher DSCR floors (sometimes 1.30x or higher), and reduce maximum LTV to 55 to 60 percent. This is not negotiable in the institutional market. If the dispute involves a property whose loan sizing was calculated using investment-grade credit assumptions but the actual tenant is unrated, the overstatement of loan proceeds can be substantial.

Structural Lease Provisions: Co-tenancy clauses, kick-out rights, and termination options are deal-killers in the institutional financing market. A lease that contains an early termination option, even one that appears unlikely to be exercised, will cause most life insurance companies and CMBS conduits to decline the loan or treat the lease term as ending at the option date rather than the stated expiration date. In Charlotte's retail NNN sector, these provisions appear with some regularity in leases executed during volatile periods. Their financing impact is not a matter of opinion; lender credit guidelines explicitly address them.

The Charlotte Lender Market

The Charlotte lending market for single-tenant NNN assets is reasonably deep but highly stratified by asset quality, lease credit, and deal complexity. Understanding which lender categories are viable for a given asset is essential to any financing feasibility opinion.

Life Insurance Companies: Several life company correspondents are active in Charlotte for credit-tenant NNN deals with long lease terms. Life companies are the most aggressive pricers for investment-grade NNN product, often quoting the tightest spreads and highest proceeds, but they have rigid credit boxes. They require investment-grade or equivalent tenant credit, typically 10-plus years of remaining lease term, and clean lease structures with no unusual termination provisions. For a below-market lease deal or a deal with near-term rollover risk, life companies are generally not available.

CMBS Conduit Lenders: CMBS conduit execution is available for Charlotte NNN assets that can be sized to a 1.25x DSCR and 65 percent LTV on a non-recourse basis. Conduit lenders will underwrite below-market leases but only to in-place income; there is no credit for rollover upside in the CMBS execution. Conduit pricing has been elevated relative to prior cycles, and the current rate environment has compressed maximum loan proceeds on all income-producing assets. Conduit lenders are also sensitive to lease term; they will impose significant constraints on loans where lease expiration precedes the anticipated loan maturity or balloon date.

Regional Banks: Charlotte is home to major regional banking institutions, and the broader Carolinas banking market includes a number of well-capitalized regional banks that are active CRE lenders. Regional banks will consider NNN assets with more complexity than life companies or conduits will accept, including below-market leases, shorter remaining terms, and non-investment-grade tenants, but they require personal recourse, shorter loan terms (typically 3 to 5 years), and more conservative debt service coverage. Their maximum LTV on challenged single-tenant NNN product is generally 60 percent or lower.

Community Banks and Credit Unions: Charlotte's community banking sector is active and meaningful. Community banks in the Charlotte MSA will look at single-tenant NNN deals that fall outside institutional parameters, particularly for locally known tenants or well-located properties. Their pricing is higher, their loan terms shorter, and their LTV limits more conservative, but they provide a financing option that keeps the deal alive where institutional capital has stepped back. This segment is particularly relevant in disputes where the question is whether any financing was available, as distinct from whether institutional financing was available.

Debt Funds and Bridge Lenders: For assets with near-term lease rollover, a bridge loan from a debt fund is sometimes the only viable institutional financing path. Bridge lenders will underwrite to a stabilized value and a business plan, lending at a higher cost of capital (rates typically in the 8.50 to 10.50 percent range in the current environment) in exchange for flexibility on term and credit. Bridge execution acknowledges that the property needs to be re-stabilized before permanent financing is achievable.

What Attorneys Handling Charlotte CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to Retain a CRE Financing Expert: If the dispute involves any of the following, counsel should engage a CRE financing expert early: claims about what loan proceeds were available or achievable on a commercial property; disputes over whether a refinancing was feasible; allegations that a lender's underwriting was improper or outside market standards; damages calculations that depend on a capitalized value or supportable loan amount; or bankruptcy plan confirmation proceedings where the debtor's feasibility depends on a claimed ability to refinance. An appraiser can opine on value but cannot opine on lender behavior, underwriting standards, or financing feasibility. Those are separate disciplines requiring different qualifications.

What a CRE Financing Expert Answers That an Appraiser Cannot: Appraisers apply income capitalization methodologies to estimate value. They do not testify about how lenders actually underwrite, what DSCR floors are in effect at specific lender categories, whether a specific lease structure would be acceptable in the CMBS market, or what maximum loan proceeds are achievable at a given point in time. A CRE financing expert with active market experience answers those questions from current, operational knowledge of the lending market. The distinction is critical when the legal question is not what the property was worth but whether a specific loan was achievable or whether a party's claimed financing was realistic.

Qualifications That Matter: A qualified CRE financing expert should have active, current involvement in commercial mortgage origination or placement, with demonstrated relationships across lender categories including bank, CMBS, life company, and alternative capital. Retired banker status alone is insufficient if the individual has been out of active origination for several years, because lending markets change materially in short periods and testimony about what lenders require in the current environment must be grounded in current activity. Expert witness experience, including prior Rule 26 reports and deposition or trial testimony, is also relevant to counsel's selection decision.

Information Counsel Should Gather Before the Engagement: Before retaining and engaging a CRE financing expert, counsel should compile the full lease document including all amendments and exhibits; the rent roll and any historical rent collection records; the most recent rent comparable data for the submarket if available; any appraisals or broker opinions of value that have been produced in discovery; the existing loan documents including the note, mortgage or deed of trust, and any modification agreements; the lender's credit memorandum or underwriting file if available through discovery; and any internal communications discussing the property's financing or valuation.

Use of Expert Testimony at Trial and in Plan Hearings: In litigation, CRE financing expert testimony is most commonly used to establish or rebut damages calculations that depend on achievable loan proceeds, to challenge or support the feasibility of a claimed refinancing, or to establish the standard of care in a lender liability or broker negligence context. In bankruptcy plan confirmation proceedings, financing feasibility testimony is often outcome-determinative: a plan that depends on a refinancing that cannot actually be executed is not feasible as a matter of law, and expert testimony establishing the outer limits of achievable financing proceeds is directly responsive to that inquiry.

Expert Witness: How Construction Defects Affect CRE Financing in Springfield, MO

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Construction Defect Impact on Financing

The Springfield Market Context

Springfield, Missouri occupies a distinct position in the Midwest commercial real estate landscape. It is not a gateway market, but it is not a secondary afterthought either. With a metro population approaching 500,000, a regional economy anchored by healthcare, logistics, higher education, and manufacturing, and a cost basis that remains materially below Kansas City and St. Louis, Springfield attracts a specific tier of commercial real estate investment: value-add multifamily operators, regional industrial users, medical office developers, and retail strip center owners serving a dense, stable consumer base across southwest Missouri and into northwest Arkansas.

For construction defect disputes specifically, the asset composition of Springfield's built environment matters. The multifamily market has seen meaningful development activity over the past decade, with Class B and Class B+ product delivering in submarkets including the Missouri State University corridor, the Republic Road industrial belt, and downtown-adjacent mixed-use sites. Cap rates in stabilized Springfield multifamily have generally traded in the 5.75 to 6.75 percent range depending on vintage and location, which is wider than Kansas City by 50 to 100 basis points and reflects the market's smaller institutional buyer pool. That wider cap rate spread is directly relevant to construction defect analysis because it compresses the value margin a property has before lender underwriting thresholds are breached.

Industrial vacancy in the Springfield MSA has remained tight, generally running below 5 percent for functional warehouse and distribution product, driven in part by distribution demand tied to regional logistics networks serving the Ozarks and the I-44 corridor. Office, by contrast, faces the same structural headwinds present nationally, with suburban Class B office in Springfield carrying elevated vacancy and landlords offering meaningful concession packages. For a construction defect matter involving any of these asset types, the baseline underwriting assumptions differ sharply. A lender sizing a loan on industrial product in Springfield applies very different DSCR floors and vacancy assumptions than one underwriting a medical office building in the same zip code. Getting those baseline assumptions right is foundational to any credible expert analysis.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing one party in a commercial real estate construction defect dispute involving a multifamily property located in the Springfield metropolitan area. The property, a mid-size apartment community built within the prior five years, was the subject of claims alleging material defects in the building envelope, including waterproofing failures at the podium deck level, improper flashing installation at window and door penetrations, and structural issues related to substandard framing in load-bearing walls. The defects had resulted in documented water intrusion, mold remediation costs, unit uninhabitability during repair periods, and ongoing insurance coverage disputes.

Counsel needed answers to three distinct but interrelated questions. First, given the known and alleged defects, was the property financeable at all in the current lender market, and if so, under what conditions and at what cost? Second, how did the defects affect the property's maximum supportable loan compared to an otherwise equivalent, defect-free asset? Third, what specific lender requirements, reserves, holdbacks, or structural accommodations would a responsible commercial lender impose when confronted with a property carrying this profile of known construction litigation, and what is the economic cost of those accommodations to the borrower?

These questions sit squarely in the domain of a commercial real estate financing expert and are not answerable by a real estate appraiser alone, nor by a general contractor or construction defect expert. They require active, current knowledge of lender credit policy, loan structuring practice, and capital markets pricing in the specific asset class and geography.

The Financial Analysis

Our analysis began with a reconstruction of the property's stabilized net operating income under two scenarios: a defect-free baseline representing what the property's income stream would look like absent any construction issues, and a defect-impacted scenario reflecting the actual and reasonably projected income impact of the alleged defects.

For the defect-free baseline, we analyzed the property assuming 93 percent physical occupancy, consistent with stabilized Class B multifamily performance in the Springfield submarket, and applied market rents derived from comparable lease data in the immediate trade area. Gross potential rent for a property of this unit count and mix produced approximately $1,650,000 in annual gross revenue. After applying a 7 percent vacancy and credit loss factor and operating expenses consistent with Springfield multifamily benchmarks, including property taxes at the Greene County effective rate, insurance, management at 5 percent of effective gross income, maintenance, and replacement reserves at $300 per unit annually, the stabilized NOI landed at approximately $975,000.

At a 1.25x DSCR threshold, which is the standard floor applied by agency lenders including Fannie Mae DUS and Freddie Mac Optigo programs for conventional multifamily, and using a 30-year amortization on a 10-year fixed rate note at 6.50 percent (reflective of agency pricing available at the time of this analysis), the maximum supportable annual debt service is $780,000. That translates to a maximum supportable loan of approximately $10,150,000. If the property's as-stabilized value is $14,500,000, the implied loan-to-value is approximately 70 percent, which sits within the 75 percent LTV ceiling for Fannie Mae conventional multifamily. The property clears both the DSCR test and the LTV test under defect-free conditions, confirming it would be fully financeable in the agency market at competitive terms.

Under the defect-impacted scenario, the analysis changes materially. The documented water intrusion and mold remediation had rendered a portion of the units uninhabitable during two separate repair mobilizations over a 14-month period. We modeled effective occupancy at 81 percent during the impacted period, consistent with disclosed unit counts out of service. Additionally, tenant attrition associated with the disruption produced measurable rent concessions and below-market lease renewals that suppressed effective rents by approximately 4 percent relative to the market. Adjusting for the occupancy drag, concession impact, and increased operating expenses including elevated insurance premiums and repair-period maintenance costs, the defect-impacted NOI fell to approximately $720,000.

At the same 1.25x DSCR threshold and loan terms, the maximum supportable annual debt service drops to $576,000, producing a maximum supportable loan of approximately $7,500,000. The financing gap between the defect-free scenario and the defect-impacted scenario is $2,650,000. That figure represents the direct loan proceeds reduction attributable to the construction defect impact and is the kind of quantified, dollar-denominated conclusion that is useful both in settlement negotiations and at trial. It is not speculative. It is derived from standard underwriting arithmetic applied to documented income data.

The LTV picture compounds the problem. If the defects also impair the appraised value, as they frequently do when a property carries open construction litigation and deferred remediation obligations, the value decline compresses the LTV ceiling simultaneously with the DSCR-driven loan reduction. A property appraised at $11,000,000 in its defect-impacted state, rather than $14,500,000 at stabilized value, faces a 70 percent LTV ceiling of $7,700,000. In this scenario, both the DSCR constraint and the LTV constraint are binding, and the maximum loan is whichever figure is lower, here the DSCR-driven $7,500,000.

The Complicating Factors

Open Construction Litigation as a Lender Disqualifier. The single most significant complicating factor in this engagement was not the income impact itself but the existence of open construction defect litigation on the property. Most institutional lenders, including agency programs, CMBS conduit lenders, and life insurance companies, will not fund a loan on a property that is the named subject of pending construction litigation without either a full dismissal, a binding settlement, or a specific litigation reserve holdback structure approved by their legal and credit committees. This is not a negotiating posture; it is credit policy rooted in the title insurance underwriting limitations that govern institutional lending. Title companies will not insure a first lien position over a property with open litigation that could cloud title or generate mechanic's lien exposure, and lenders will not lend without clean title insurance.

In practical terms, this means the property's financing universe at the time of the dispute was limited to lenders willing to structure around litigation risk, most commonly through a funded litigation reserve held in escrow, a completion guarantee from a creditworthy sponsor, or a structured holdback tied to dismissal milestones. Each of these structures carries a cost: either in loan proceeds withheld, additional recourse exposure, or higher pricing to compensate the lender for the elevated risk profile.

Lender-Required Repair Reserves and Holdbacks. Even lenders willing to proceed on a property with known defects will require an independent engineering assessment (IEA) or property condition assessment (PCA) that quantifies the total remediation cost. They will then typically require a funded repair reserve equal to 110 to 125 percent of the estimated remediation cost, held in a lender-controlled escrow account and disbursed against draw requests and inspection sign-offs. For a remediation budget in the $800,000 to $1,200,000 range, which was consistent with the scope of defects in this matter, the holdback reduces initial loan proceeds by a corresponding amount and represents additional economic damage to the borrower compared to a clean asset.

Insurance Complications. Properties subject to active construction defect claims frequently face reduced insurer participation, increased premiums, or exclusions for specific perils related to the defect category. Water intrusion and mold coverage exclusions are common after a documented claim. Lenders require property insurance meeting specific coverage thresholds as a condition of loan funding, and a property that cannot obtain compliant insurance cannot close a loan. We analyzed the insurance impact as an operating expense escalator and as a potential loan funding contingency risk.

Sponsor Recourse Exposure. Lenders willing to proceed despite open litigation will frequently require full or partial recourse to the sponsoring entity rather than a standard non-recourse structure with carve-outs. This fundamentally changes the risk profile of the financing for the borrower and may affect the sponsor's ability to attract equity capital or refinance into the institutional market once the litigation is resolved.

The Springfield Lender Market

Springfield's commercial real estate lending market is meaningfully deep for a non-gateway city, which reflects the market's economic stability and the strong presence of regional financial institutions with direct Springfield relationships. However, the capital stack available to a property carrying construction defect litigation is materially narrower than what is available to clean assets.

For defect-free multifamily, the primary lender categories in Springfield include Fannie Mae DUS lenders and Freddie Mac Optigo sellers, community banks including several well-capitalized institutions headquartered in Springfield and the surrounding Ozarks region, regional banks with Missouri commercial real estate lending teams, USDA Business and Industry programs for qualifying rural-adjacent properties, and debt funds. The agency programs offer the most competitive pricing and the highest proceeds but impose the most rigid credit policy around open litigation and deferred maintenance. Community banks in Springfield tend to carry more flexibility in credit structure but lend at lower LTVs, typically 65 to 70 percent, on shorter amortization schedules, and at floating rates tied to the prime rate or SOFR plus a spread. Their flexibility makes them the most likely execution path for a property navigating an active construction dispute, but their terms represent a measurable economic cost relative to agency pricing.

Debt funds, including bridge lenders and private credit vehicles active in secondary markets like Springfield, will engage with litigation-encumbered properties but price the risk accordingly. Rates for a bridge loan on a Springfield multifamily property with open construction litigation were running in the 9.50 to 11.00 percent range at the time of this analysis, compared to agency fixed-rate executions in the mid-6 percent range. On a $7,500,000 loan, the annual interest cost differential between agency and bridge pricing is approximately $262,500 to $337,500. Over a 24 to 36-month period required to resolve the litigation and cure the defects, that differential represents a quantifiable economic harm directly attributable to the construction defect condition.

CLS CRE maintains active relationships with lenders across all of these categories and, through our 1,000-plus lender network spanning all 50 states, we are positioned to identify which lenders in a specific capital category are willing to engage with construction defect scenarios and on what terms. That market intelligence is a core component of what makes a financing expert opinion credible in litigation: not just textbook knowledge of how lenders think, but current, active knowledge of what specific lender categories are actually doing in a specific market.

What Attorneys Handling Springfield CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to Retain a CRE Financing Expert. The right moment to retain a commercial real estate financing expert is not after the appraisal is complete; it is before discovery closes on the financial impact questions. The appraiser quantifies value. The financing expert quantifies the loan proceeds impact, the increased cost of capital, the financing contingency risk, and the structural accommodations a lender would require. These are distinct damages theories, and they require distinct expert testimony. In construction defect matters, financing feasibility expert testimony is most valuable when the claimed damages include lost financing proceeds, increased borrowing costs, inability to refinance, or delay damages tied to a lender's refusal to fund.

What a CRE Financing Expert Can Answer That an Appraiser Cannot. An appraiser can tell you what a property is worth. A financing expert can tell you whether the property can be financed at that value, at what LTV, at what cost, and under what lender-imposed conditions. These are operationally different questions. A property may appraise at $14,500,000 but be unlendable at that value because open litigation prevents title insurance issuance. Understanding that distinction, and being able to explain it to a jury or a mediator in clear, transaction-grounded terms, is the financing expert's contribution.

Qualifications That Actually Matter. A qualified CRE financing expert should have current, active commercial mortgage origination experience. Retired bankers with credit committee experience but no current market engagement frequently lack knowledge of how today's lender market is actually pricing and structuring deals. The credit criteria applied in 2015 are not the credit criteria applied in 2024. Relevant qualifications include active broker or correspondent relationships with institutional lenders, demonstrated transaction volume in the relevant asset class, and experience in the specific geography or a directly comparable secondary market.

Information Counsel Should Gather Before the Engagement. To render a credible financing feasibility opinion, the expert will need: historical rent rolls and operating statements for the trailing 24 to 36 months; all property condition assessments or engineering reports commissioned in connection with the defect claims; the property's existing debt schedule if encumbered; any lender correspondence referencing the defects or declining to extend, refinance, or fund; evidence of remediation costs incurred or estimated; and insurance documentation including any coverage exclusions added post-claim. The more complete this record, the more defensible the expert's assumptions at deposition.

Use at Trial and in Settlement. CRE financing expert testimony is most commonly used to establish the economic consequences of a defect condition beyond the direct repair cost. The repair cost is the province of the construction expert. The financing impact, the increased cost of capital, the reduced proceeds, the loan-in-default risk, the lost refinancing opportunity, is the province of the financing expert. In bench trials and arbitration, a well-supported financing feasibility report frequently drives settlement because it converts abstract claims about market conditions into concrete, dollar-denominated conclusions derived from standard industry methodology.

Key Takeaways

  • Construction defects produce two distinct financial harms: income impairment, which reduces NOI and therefore reduces the maximum supportable loan under standard DSCR analysis, and lender market impairment, which restricts the universe of willing lenders and forces borrowers into higher-cost capital categories. Both harms are quantifiable and should be analyzed separately.
  • Open construction litigation is frequently a hard stop for institutional lenders. Agency programs, CMBS conduits, and life companies will not fund over active construction litigation without a funded reserve structure or dismissal. This is credit policy, not discretion, and an expert with active lender relationships can confirm this as market standard rather than speculation.
  • The loan proceeds gap between a defect-free and defect-impacted property can be substantial. In the Springfield scenario analyzed here, the DSCR-driven financing gap exceeded $2,650,000 on a mid-size multifamily asset. This figure is a defensible, methodology-grounded damages number derived from standard commercial underwriting arithmetic.

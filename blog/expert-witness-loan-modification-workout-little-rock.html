Expert Witness: Commercial Loan Modification Standards in Little Rock, AR

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Loan Modification and Workout Dispute

The Little Rock Market Context

Little Rock often gets overlooked in national capital markets conversations, but that oversight does not reflect the actual depth and complexity of commercial real estate activity in Central Arkansas. The metro area functions as the governmental, medical, and logistics hub for the state, and those three economic drivers create a layered CRE market that produces real financing disputes. When a loan modification or workout goes sideways, the question of whether the lender's conduct was commercially reasonable cannot be answered in a vacuum. It requires a grounded understanding of what was actually happening in this market at the time the modification was negotiated.

On the multifamily side, Little Rock has absorbed significant development over the past several years, particularly in the midtown corridor and the west Little Rock suburban submarkets. Cap rates for stabilized multifamily assets in Little Rock have generally traded in the 5.75 to 6.75 percent range, depending on vintage, location, and occupancy. That spread matters in a workout context because a lender who marked a property's value using a 5.0 percent cap rate at loan origination may be working with collateral that has since been repriced by 75 to 100 basis points, compressing the equity cushion considerably even before operational stress enters the picture.

The office market in Little Rock reflects the same post-pandemic structural challenges seen nationally, but with some local distinctions. The Class B suburban office inventory along Chenal Parkway and in west Little Rock has experienced meaningful vacancy increases, with effective vacancy in some corridors running above 20 percent. Downtown Class A office, anchored by state government and financial services tenants, has held up comparatively better, but rent growth has been flat to negative in real terms. For a lender evaluating modification requests on office-secured loans, the critical issue is whether the stabilized occupancy assumption underlying the original underwriting is still defensible, and in many cases, it is not.

Industrial and flex product has been the bright spot. Little Rock's position along I-40 and I-30, combined with its role as a regional distribution node and proximity to major retailers' supply chains, has kept industrial vacancy tight, generally below 6 percent in functional logistics and distribution facilities. Rent growth has been positive, and cap rates have compressed somewhat even in the face of higher interest rates. For workouts involving industrial collateral, this context matters: a lender who pushed aggressively on a modification of a well-located industrial property may have been acting on stale negative assumptions that did not reflect actual market conditions.

Retail has been bifurcated in the familiar pattern: grocery-anchored and necessity-based retail has performed adequately, while unanchored strip centers and power centers have faced persistent occupancy headwinds. Financing liquidity for retail remains constrained across lender categories, which is relevant when evaluating whether a borrower had realistic refinancing options during a workout period.

The Engagement

Counsel representing the borrower in a contested loan workout matter retained Commercial Lending Solutions to provide a Rule 26 expert opinion analyzing the lender's conduct throughout the modification and forbearance process. The underlying asset was a mid-size retail and mixed-use center in the Greater Little Rock market that had experienced occupancy and income disruption during a period of broader retail stress. The borrower had entered into a forbearance agreement with the institutional lender and later sought a formal loan modification to restructure the debt service obligations in line with the property's reduced cash flow.

The lender ultimately denied the modification request and moved toward enforcement, asserting that the property's income did not support a modified debt structure and that the borrower had not demonstrated a viable path to stabilization. The borrower contended that the lender's analysis of the property's cash flow was materially flawed, that the modification terms proposed were commercially unreasonable given prevailing market conditions, and that the lender's conduct during the workout process deviated from accepted industry standards of care.

Our engagement required us to address several distinct but interconnected questions. First, was the lender's income analysis of the collateral accurate and conducted using methods consistent with how institutional commercial real estate lenders actually underwrite income during a workout? Second, did the proposed modification terms reflect what a commercially reasonable lender operating in this market would have offered given the property's condition and the debt markets at the time? Third, how did the lender's timeline, communication conduct, and documentation practices compare to standard workout protocols used by institutional commercial real estate lenders? Each question required a different analytical framework, and each had direct relevance to the legal theories at issue.

The Financial Analysis

The financial analysis began with a reconstruction of the property's net operating income as it existed at the time the modification request was submitted, and as it could reasonably be projected over a 12 to 24 month stabilization horizon. This distinction matters enormously in workout disputes. Lenders are not obligated to modify debt based on in-place distressed cash flow alone, but they are also not justified in applying full-stabilization underwriting assumptions when granting or denying a modification. Industry practice recognizes a middle ground: underwriting to a realistic stabilized NOI, discounted appropriately for the time and cost to achieve stabilization.

In this engagement, the property's in-place annual gross revenues at the time of the modification request were approximately $1.4 million, with operating expenses running at roughly $420,000, yielding an in-place NOI of approximately $980,000. However, the property had two vacant suites representing approximately 14 percent of gross leasable area, and the borrower had executed letters of intent for one of those spaces. Applying a realistic stabilized occupancy assumption of 88 percent, consistent with submarket vacancy metrics for comparable retail assets in the Little Rock market, and adjusting for projected lease-up costs and concessions, we derived a stabilized NOI of approximately $1.18 million for underwriting purposes.

The loan balance at the time of the modification request was approximately $9.2 million. The original loan had been structured at a fixed rate of 4.85 percent with a 25-year amortization schedule and a 10-year term. The modification request proposed extending the term by two years with a 12-month interest-only period to allow the borrower time to lease up the vacant space and restore debt service coverage. This is a standard and commercially recognized modification structure. The question was whether the property's cash flow, properly analyzed, could support the modified debt structure.

At the original 4.85 percent rate with a 25-year amortization applied to the $9.2 million balance, the annual debt service obligation was approximately $628,000. Applying the standard institutional DSCR threshold of 1.25x to the stabilized NOI of $1.18 million, the maximum supportable annual debt service was approximately $944,000, leaving meaningful headroom above the actual debt service figure of $628,000. Even under a more conservative 1.30x DSCR threshold, the supportable debt service would be approximately $908,000. This analysis demonstrated that the property's stabilized cash flow could comfortably service the existing debt, which directly contradicted the lender's stated basis for denying the modification.

The LTV analysis was equally relevant. Applying a capitalization rate of 6.50 percent to the stabilized NOI of $1.18 million produced a stabilized value of approximately $18.15 million. At a loan balance of $9.2 million, the resulting LTV was approximately 50.7 percent, well within the 65 to 70 percent LTV thresholds that commercial lenders apply to retail collateral even in conservative credit environments. The equity cushion was not eroded in any meaningful sense. The lender's refusal to modify could not be justified on collateral value grounds.

We also analyzed the interest-only period request separately. A 12-month interest-only period on a $9.2 million loan at 4.85 percent requires monthly payments of approximately $37,217, compared to the fully amortizing payment of approximately $52,333. The interest-only structure would have reduced the monthly obligation by approximately $15,100 per month, providing the borrower with approximately $181,200 in annualized cash flow relief during the lease-up period. This is a routine accommodation in commercial mortgage workouts when the underlying collateral supports the debt at stabilization and the borrower demonstrates a credible leasing plan.

The Complicating Factors

Several factors complicated the analysis and were the subject of significant attention in the expert report.

The lender's use of trailing 12-month income figures without adjustment. Institutional commercial mortgage lenders operating in workout situations are expected to apply judgment to income figures. Mechanically applying a trailing 12-month average when the property is in mid-lease-up, or when a significant tenant departure has already been addressed with a replacement lease, produces an NOI figure that understates the property's actual economic condition. The lender in this matter used a straight trailing 12-month average that included the full impact of a major tenant's departure, without adjusting for the replacement tenant whose lease had already commenced. This produced a materially understated NOI and, consequently, a DSCR below 1.0x, which the lender then cited as justification for denial. That methodology does not reflect how experienced commercial real estate lenders actually conduct workout underwriting, and we documented that departure from standard practice in detail.

The forbearance agreement's impact on the borrower's negotiating timeline. The forbearance agreement contained a provision requiring the borrower to submit a complete modification package within 90 days. In the Little Rock market, assembling a complete institutional-quality modification package, including updated rent rolls, operating statements, borrower financial statements, market studies, and environmental updates, while simultaneously managing a distressed property, routinely takes 90 to 120 days even for experienced borrowers with professional property management. The 90-day window, while legally defensible, was commercially aggressive and created conditions under which the borrower could be found in default on procedural grounds even if the substantive modification would have been supportable.

Market conditions at the time of the modification request versus the time of the original underwriting. The lender's internal credit memorandum prepared for the modification review referenced retail market conditions that were approximately 18 months stale. The lender cited retail vacancy rates and rent-per-square-foot comparables that reflected conditions prior to the lease-up activity that had already occurred. Lenders conducting modification reviews are expected to use current market data, and the failure to do so in this case skewed the lender's analysis against the borrower in ways that were not commercially reasonable.

Guaranty and recourse exposure as a leverage factor. The loan carried a full personal guaranty. In Arkansas, the enforcement dynamics of personal guaranties in commercial real estate loan disputes can create situations where lenders use the threat of guaranty enforcement as a negotiating lever during workout discussions. Documenting whether the guaranty exposure influenced the modification terms offered, or the pace at which the lender moved toward enforcement rather than modification, is a legitimate area of expert inquiry in disputes of this type.

The Little Rock Lender Market

Understanding the lender landscape in the Little Rock market is essential context for any expert opinion on whether alternative financing was available, whether the lender's modification terms were commercially reasonable compared to market alternatives, and whether a refinancing exit was viable.

Little Rock has meaningful community and regional bank depth. Institutions headquartered in Arkansas and the surrounding region are active lenders on commercial real estate, and many of them have long-standing relationships with local borrowers and market knowledge that larger institutions lack. These lenders typically operate within 65 to 70 percent LTV constraints on retail and mixed-use collateral, require 1.20x to 1.25x DSCR at underwriting, and price in ranges that track the Secured Overnight Financing Rate or the prime rate with spreads generally running 175 to 275 basis points over index depending on credit quality and asset type. Community banks in this market have demonstrated willingness to engage in creative modification structures, including interest-only periods, maturity extensions, and principal deferrals, when the collateral supports the debt at stabilization.

National and super-regional banks with Arkansas operations tend to apply more rigid credit parameters, particularly on retail collateral post-2020. Their workout groups operate under centralized protocols that can be less responsive to local market nuance, which is relevant in a dispute context because the borrower's ability to obtain alternative financing from a local institution may be superior to what the lender's internal credit matrix acknowledged.

CMBS market access is limited for assets in the $5 to $15 million range in secondary markets like Little Rock. Conduit lenders generally require stabilized occupancy of 85 percent or better, DSCR of 1.25x or better at underwriting, and loan balances above $10 million for most shelf programs. The Little Rock market's secondary status and the retail asset type would place most mid-market retail properties outside CMBS parameters during a workout environment, which is relevant when evaluating whether alternative payoff sources were realistically available.

Debt funds and bridge lenders are active in the Little Rock market for transitional assets, but their pricing reflects risk. Bridge capital on retail assets in this market has priced in the 9.0 to 11.5 percent range on a floating basis, which materially affects the economics of a refinancing exit and should be part of the expert's analysis of what options the borrower realistically had.

What Attorneys Handling Little Rock CRE Disputes Need to Know

A CRE financing expert witness addresses questions that fall outside what an appraiser, an accountant, or a general business expert can reliably answer. Appraisers determine value. CRE financing experts determine whether, and on what terms, capital was available, whether a lender's conduct conformed to industry standards, and whether the financial analysis underlying a lender's decision was accurate by the standards of the commercial lending industry itself.

When to retain a CRE financing expert. Counsel should consider engaging a CRE financing expert when the dispute involves: a lender's denial of a modification or refinancing; allegations that a lender acted in bad faith during a workout; disputes over whether a borrower had a viable path to repayment that the lender failed to credit; lender liability claims related to the conduct of the workout process; disputes over whether proposed modification terms were commercially reasonable; and cases where the borrower's damages theory requires establishing what alternative financing was available and at what cost.

What distinguishes a qualified CRE financing expert. A retired banker who spent a career in residential mortgage or small business lending is not a qualified expert on institutional commercial real estate loan workout standards. The expert must have direct, active experience in commercial real estate capital markets, including loan origination, modification, and workout processes, at the institutional level. Expertise should be current, not historical. Lender standards, credit parameters, and workout protocols have evolved significantly, particularly since 2020, and an expert whose market knowledge is dated will be vulnerable on cross-examination. Active transaction volume, documented lender relationships, and current market engagement are the appropriate benchmarks.

Information counsel should gather before the expert engagement. Provide the complete loan file, including the original credit memorandum, appraisal, underwriting model, and all modification-related correspondence. Collect all forbearance agreements, modification proposals, and rejection letters with stated reasoning. Gather the property's operating statements for the full loan term, rent rolls at key dates, and any third-party market studies or broker opinions of value obtained during the workout period. Document the timeline of events in precise chronological order. The expert's analysis is only as strong as the underlying record.

How expert testimony functions in these matters. In a bench trial or arbitration, a CRE financing expert provides the trier of fact with the industry context necessary to evaluate the lender's conduct against a professional standard, not merely a legal standard. The expert can explain why a lender's DSCR calculation methodology was inconsistent with industry practice, why a particular LTV threshold was applied aggressively relative to market norms, and what a commercially reasonable lender in this market would have done differently. In a Chapter 11 plan confirmation hearing, the expert can testify on cramdown feasibility and the availability and cost of exit financing. Expert testimony in deposition can also be used to narrow the issues before trial and to challenge a lender's internal witnesses on the accuracy of their credit analysis.

Key Takeaways

  • Income analysis methodology matters as much as the income figures themselves. A lender who uses trailing averages without adjustment for post-distress lease activity is not applying standard institutional workout underwriting, and that methodological failure can support a commercially unreasonable conduct claim.
  • The 1.20x to 1.25x DSCR standard is well established, but its application requires using a defensible stabilized NOI, not an in-place distressed NOI, when the modification request is accompanied by a credible stabilization plan.
  • LTV analysis and DSCR analysis must both be presented. A lender whose denial relies on cash flow arguments while sitting on 50 percent LTV collateral is making a credit decision that warrants expert scrutiny.

Expert Witness: Commercial Loan Origination Standards in Bozeman, MT

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Lender Liability and Loan Origination Standards

The Bozeman Market Context

Bozeman, Montana has undergone one of the most dramatic commercial real estate transformations of any mid-size market in the western United States over the past decade. What was once a regional university town with modest commercial activity has become a high-demand destination market driven by remote worker migration, technology sector expansion anchored by companies drawn to quality of life, and sustained population growth that consistently ranks Gallatin County among the fastest-growing counties in the nation. That growth has direct consequences for commercial real estate underwriting, and those consequences are precisely what generate lender liability and professional negligence disputes.

On the multifamily side, Bozeman cap rates compressed aggressively through 2021 and into 2022, with stabilized apartment assets trading in the 4.25 to 5.00 percent range at peak. Since then, rising debt costs have created a meaningful bid-ask spread between buyers and sellers, and refinancing pressure has surfaced for borrowers who acquired or developed at those compressed valuations. Industrial product in the Bozeman submarket has remained exceptionally tight, with vacancy rates consistently below 3 percent and rents for shallow-bay flex and distribution space rising from the mid-single digits per square foot into the $14 to $18 per square foot NNN range in newer product. Retail is bifurcated: grocery-anchored and necessity-based retail has performed well, while secondary retail in non-core locations has struggled with tenant turnover and re-leasing timelines that complicate underwriting. Office demand, while not suffering the acute distress visible in major metros, remains thin, with limited institutional transaction comparables and a lender community that is cautious about office exposure in all but the most creditworthy single-tenant or medical configurations.

For purposes of lender liability and loan origination disputes, the Bozeman market presents a specific challenge: it is not well-covered by standardized metropolitan statistical area data in the way that Denver, Salt Lake City, or Phoenix are. Lenders must rely more heavily on local broker opinion of value, limited comparable sales, and subjective assessments of market trajectory. That reliance creates meaningful room for professional error, and it is exactly that room where disputes about origination standards and broker competence tend to arise.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing a defendant commercial mortgage broker in a professional negligence action arising from a failed acquisition loan on a mixed-use retail and multifamily property in the Bozeman urban core. The plaintiff, a real estate investor, alleged that the broker misrepresented achievable loan proceeds, failed to disclose material limitations in the lender's credit box, and placed the loan with an inappropriate lender given the borrower's profile and the asset's complexity. The borrower had relied on the broker's representations in structuring the acquisition, including the equity requirement and projected debt service coverage, and claimed that the loan as ultimately delivered materially underperformed the terms that were represented during the origination process.

Counsel retained CLS CRE to provide a Rule 26 expert report addressing three core questions. First, whether the loan terms as originated, including the loan-to-value ratio, interest rate, amortization structure, and debt service coverage ratio, were consistent with market standards for comparable assets in comparable markets at the time of origination. Second, whether a reasonably competent commercial mortgage broker operating in the Montana market in the relevant time period would have disclosed the lender's underwriting constraints differently or earlier in the process. Third, whether an alternative financing structure existed that would have produced materially better proceeds or terms, and if so, whether the broker's failure to present those alternatives constituted a departure from the applicable standard of care.

The asset was a two-story building with ground-floor retail, a restaurant pad tenant, and twelve residential units on the upper floors. The property had been partially renovated, with three residential units still in lease-up at the time of application. The retail component carried below-market leases with less than two years of remaining term on two of four suites. These characteristics made the underwriting layered and gave rise to the core factual disputes in the case.

The Financial Analysis

The methodology I applied began with a reconstruction of stabilized net operating income using the actual rent roll, market rent data from the Bozeman submarket, and published vacancy assumptions consistent with comparable product at the time of origination. This distinction matters: lenders underwriting mixed-use assets do not simply accept in-place NOI as the operative underwriting metric. They test it against market rent, apply a stabilized vacancy assumption, and then stress it further for credit and collection loss. In this case, the in-place NOI as represented by the broker in the loan application differed materially from what a prudent lender would have credited after applying standard adjustments.

The residential component of the property generated gross potential rent of approximately $228,000 annually based on twelve units at an average in-place rent of $1,900 per month. Three units in lease-up were underwritten at market rate by the broker without applying a lease-up reserve or a stabilized occupancy haircut. Standard multifamily underwriting practice for partially stabilized assets applies a physical vacancy assumption of 7 to 10 percent and an economic vacancy or credit loss assumption of an additional 2 to 3 percent. Applying a blended 10 percent economic vacancy to the residential component reduced effective gross income on that component by approximately $22,800.

The retail and restaurant component generated in-place base rents of approximately $186,000 annually, but two of the four retail suites had lease expirations within 24 months. Industry standard underwriting for leases with short remaining terms typically applies a rollover reserve or discounts those suites to market rent with an assumed downtime of 6 to 12 months and tenant improvement and leasing commission costs. A prudent lender in this scenario would have imputed a rollover reserve of approximately $18,000 to $24,000 annually against the retail NOI. The broker's application did not reflect any rollover reserve.

After adjusting for vacancy, credit loss, management fees at 5 percent of effective gross income, insurance, taxes, maintenance reserves, and the rollover reserve, stabilized NOI came to approximately $268,000 annually, compared to the $312,000 figure presented in the broker's loan application package. That is a 14 percent variance, and it is operationally significant because the entire loan sizing exercise flows from NOI.

Applying a debt service coverage ratio of 1.25x, which represents the market standard floor for mixed-use assets with retail exposure at conventional lenders and is confirmed by agency guidelines for mixed-use properties with commercial components exceeding 20 percent of gross income, maximum supportable annual debt service equals $214,400. At a 7.25 percent interest rate and a 25-year amortization schedule, which were the prevailing terms for this asset class in the relevant origination period, that debt service translates to a maximum supportable loan of approximately $2,685,000. The broker had represented achievable proceeds of $3,400,000 based on a 70 percent LTV applied to the purchase price. The appraised value came in at $4,750,000, which would have supported a $3,325,000 loan at 70 percent LTV if NOI had supported it. It did not. The DSCR-constrained maximum proceeds were approximately $640,000 less than what the broker represented, and that shortfall was the direct cause of the equity gap that forced the borrower into a restructured transaction on materially worse terms.

The Complicating Factors

Mixed-Use Retail and Residential Underwriting Conflict. Lenders categorize assets, and categorization determines which credit box applies. A property with more than 20 to 25 percent of income derived from commercial tenants typically cannot be financed through agency execution, which for a 12-unit residential asset would otherwise represent the most aggressive and lowest-cost capital available. The presence of the restaurant pad and retail suites in this case pushed the asset into a commercial mixed-use category, which meant conventional bank or CMBS execution with higher reserves, more conservative stabilized income underwriting, and personal recourse carve-outs that agency execution does not require. A broker who represented agency-equivalent pricing or proceeds on a property with this income composition departed from what a competent originator would have known to be true from the outset.

Lease-Up Risk on Residential Units. Lenders do not extend credit against speculative future income. Three of twelve units being in lease-up at origination is not a fatal issue, but it requires either a hold-back structure, a reduced advance rate pending stabilization, or a completion guarantee from the borrower. None of these structures were disclosed by the broker as conditions of the loan, and the borrower had not been advised to reserve for them. When the lender's credit committee conditioned funding on a $180,000 hold-back pending stabilization of the three units, the borrower lacked the liquidity to accommodate it.

Near-Term Lease Rollover on the Retail Component. In the Bozeman market specifically, retail re-leasing timelines can be extended relative to primary markets because the tenant pool for credit-quality retail tenants is thinner. A lender reviewing a retail lease schedule with two suites rolling within 24 months in a secondary Montana market is going to apply conservative rollover assumptions or require reserves. This is not punitive underwriting; it reflects the real re-leasing risk in a market where replacement tenants of comparable credit quality are not waiting in line. A competent broker with Montana market knowledge would have disclosed this risk to the borrower and structured around it proactively, either by negotiating lease extensions prior to loan application or by presenting lenders who specialize in value-add retail exposure.

Limited Comparable Sales Data. Bozeman's commercial transaction volume, while growing, does not produce the depth of comparable sales data that supports confident appraisal conclusions in the way that larger markets do. When appraised value rests on a thin comparable set, lenders apply additional caution to LTV-based loan sizing. In this engagement, the appraisal relied in part on comparables from Missoula and Great Falls, markets with materially different demand characteristics. A sophisticated originator would have flagged the appraisal methodology as a potential lender objection, not accepted it at face value.

The Bozeman Lender Market

For mixed-use retail and residential assets in Bozeman, the realistic financing universe at origination was narrower than the broker's representations implied. Understanding that universe is essential both for origination practice and for expert analysis of whether available alternatives were presented.

Community and Regional Banks. Several Montana-chartered community banks and regional institutions headquartered in Billings and Great Falls have historically been active in Bozeman commercial lending. These lenders know the market, will accept thin comparable sets, and can underwrite to local knowledge. However, their hold limits on a single credit typically run $5,000,000 to $8,000,000, and they require full personal recourse, maintain portfolio concentration limits in Bozeman that have become binding for some institutions given the volume of growth, and generally apply DSCR floors of 1.25x to 1.30x on mixed-use assets. Their rates typically price at spread over a benchmark with floors, and on a transaction of this complexity in the relevant period, all-in pricing would have been in the 7.00 to 7.75 percent range.

CMBS Execution. Conduit CMBS lenders can access this asset class but require stabilized occupancy, typically 90 percent for 90 days on the residential component and executed leases with term remaining on the retail component. The partial lease-up and near-term retail rollovers in this deal would have disqualified it from conduit execution at origination without remediation. A broker who presented CMBS as a viable primary option on this asset without caveat was not operating within the standard of care.

Debt Funds and Bridge Lenders. Given the lease-up and rollover exposure, the appropriate initial execution for this asset may well have been a bridge loan from a private debt fund, structured to allow the borrower to stabilize the residential units, negotiate retail lease extensions, and then refinance into permanent bank or conduit execution. Bridge capital for Montana mixed-use assets was available in the relevant period at rates in the 9.00 to 10.50 percent range, with 24-month terms and extension options. That is a meaningfully different cost structure than conventional bank financing, and disclosing it as the appropriate path would have required the broker to have an honest conversation about the asset's current state versus its stabilized potential. The record in this case contained no evidence that bridge execution was ever presented as an option.

Life Companies. Life insurance company lenders, which represent among the most attractively priced permanent capital for stabilized commercial assets, do not actively compete for mixed-use product of this scale in secondary Montana markets. They are not a realistic option for this asset profile.

What Attorneys Handling Bozeman CRE Disputes Need to Know

When a commercial real estate financing dispute arises, whether it involves a borrower claiming a broker misrepresented achievable proceeds, a lender asserting a borrower submitted a fraudulent loan application, or a party alleging that a lender breached its commitment letter or acted in bad faith during underwriting, a commercial real estate appraiser alone cannot answer the questions that matter most at trial or in a dispositive motion. An appraiser determines value. A CRE financing expert determines whether the financing was structured competently, disclosed accurately, and originated within the standards of the relevant professional community.

Specifically, a qualified CRE financing expert can testify on the following questions that are beyond an appraiser's scope: whether the loan terms as described in marketing materials or term sheets were achievable in the actual lender market at the time; whether a competent broker would have identified and disclosed specific underwriting obstacles before accepting a retainer or application fee; whether the DSCR analysis supporting the loan application was performed correctly and consistently with lender market practice; whether the lender's credit decision, including any conditions imposed or proceeds reduced at closing, was consistent with or a departure from the lender's own stated underwriting guidelines; and whether alternative financing structures existed that would have produced materially better outcomes for the borrower.

A qualified CRE financing expert is not simply a retired banker or a real estate agent with long tenure. The relevant expertise requires active origination experience across multiple lender types, including agency, CMBS, bank portfolio, and private debt fund execution, because the standard of care in the broker context requires knowledge of the full financing universe, not just the lender the broker happens to have a relationship with. At CLS CRE, our approximately $1 billion in transaction volume across 50 states and relationships with more than 1,000 institutional and private lenders directly supports that scope of opinion. We have testified on what was available, what was standard, and what a competent professional would have done, in markets from Los Angeles to secondary Mountain West markets like Bozeman.

Counsel should gather the following before retaining a CRE financing expert: the original loan application package including the rent roll, operating statements, and borrower financial statements; all term sheets, commitment letters, and lender correspondence; the appraisal and any internal lender valuation or underwriting worksheets obtained through discovery; any marketing materials or broker proposals provided to the borrower prior to engagement; the final loan documents including the note, deed of trust, and any hold-back or escrow agreements; and, where available, the lender's internal credit memorandum. The credit memo, in particular, is often the single most valuable document in a lender liability case because it reveals what the lender's own underwriters concluded about asset quality and risk, and how that conclusion compares to what the broker or lender represented externally.

Key Takeaways

  • DSCR, not LTV, is the binding constraint in most Bozeman commercial loan disputes. Brokers and borrowers frequently anchor on LTV as the measure of achievable proceeds, but for income-producing commercial assets, the DSCR test applied to stabilized NOI is typically the operative ceiling. A competent originator performs both tests and discloses which is binding.
  • Mixed-use classification in Bozeman eliminates agency execution. Any property with meaningful commercial income is excluded from the agency multifamily credit box, which means fewer lender options, higher pricing, full recourse requirements, and more conservative underwriting standards. Representing otherwise is a departure from the standard of care.
  • Partial lease-up and near-term rollover require proactive structuring, not optimistic underwriting. These conditions must either be cured before application, disclosed as conditions that will trigger lender hold-backs, or addressed through bridge financing as an interim step. Treating them as non-issues in the application package is inconsistent with competent origination practice.
  • The Bozeman lender market is thinner than primary markets, and that thinness affects what a broker must disclose. In a market with fewer competing institutional lenders, a broker's representations about achievable terms must reflect the actual appetite of the realistic lender set, not hypothetical pricing from markets with deeper liquidity.

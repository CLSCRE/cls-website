Expert Witness: Ground Lease Financing Disputes and Analysis in Bellingham, WA

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Ground Lease Financing Complexity

The Bellingham Market Context

Bellingham, Washington occupies a commercially significant position that attorneys and investors sometimes underestimate. Situated 90 miles north of Seattle and 55 miles south of Vancouver, B.C., Bellingham functions as the economic hub of Whatcom County and benefits from cross-border commerce, Western Washington University's institutional anchor, and a diversified industrial base tied to marine, logistics, and light manufacturing. That geographic and economic profile produces a commercial real estate market that is neither a pure tertiary market nor a secondary gateway, which matters enormously when you are litigating a ground lease financing dispute, because lender appetite in Bellingham does not mirror either Seattle or Spokane.

Industrial product in Bellingham has been among the tightest in the state. Vacancy in the industrial submarket has consistently tracked below 4%, driven by port-adjacent logistics demand, cold storage for agricultural and seafood processing, and supply chain businesses serving both domestic and Canadian markets. Net asking rents for industrial space in Whatcom County have pushed past $14 to $16 per square foot NNN on newer product, with older functional space clearing $10 to $12. Retail along major corridors such as the Meridian submarket remains active, anchored by national tenants, though neighborhood retail fundamentals are more granular. Multifamily cap rates in Bellingham have compressed into the 5.0% to 5.75% range on stabilized assets, reflecting strong rental demand from the university population and a constrained entitlement environment. Office fundamentals are more cautious, with some Class B availability above 10%, consistent with post-pandemic office softness across most Pacific Northwest mid-tier markets.

Ground lease structures in Bellingham most commonly appear on sites where land-rich institutional owners, municipalities, or long-held family trusts control strategically located parcels and prefer to retain fee ownership while allowing development. The port district, university-adjacent parcels, and certain downtown and Squalicum Harbor-adjacent sites have generated leasehold development arrangements. When those arrangements intersect with institutional financing, the complexity of making a leasehold interest bankable becomes a genuine legal and financial flashpoint, particularly when the underlying ground lease documentation was not drafted with lender requirements in mind.

The Engagement

Counsel representing the lessee in a commercial real estate dispute retained Commercial Lending Solutions to serve as a financing expert in a matter involving a leasehold interest on a mixed-use commercial parcel in Bellingham. The asset consisted of a ground-leased site improved with a multi-tenant retail and office building. The lessor, a private land trust, had entered into a long-term ground lease with the lessee developer, who subsequently sought permanent financing on the completed improvements. A financing dispute arose when the lender's commitment was withdrawn, and the parties became adverse over whether the ground lease, as structured, was capable of supporting institutional financing at the loan amount and terms the lessee had relied upon in its project underwriting.

Counsel needed expert analysis on three core questions. First, does the ground lease as executed meet or fail to meet market standards for leasehold mortgage financing? Second, what subordination, non-disturbance, and attornment provisions are required by institutional lenders to underwrite a leasehold interest, and which of those provisions were absent or deficient here? Third, assuming the income stream generated by the improved property, what is the maximum supportable loan amount on a leasehold basis, and how does that compare to the loan amount the lessee had sought? Our firm was engaged to produce a written expert opinion addressing all three questions and to be available for deposition and potential trial testimony.

The Financial Analysis

The starting point for any leasehold mortgage analysis is the same as any commercial real estate loan underwrite: net operating income. In this engagement, the improved property generated gross potential rent across its retail and office suites. After applying a 7% vacancy and credit loss factor consistent with Bellingham market conditions for mixed-use assets, effective gross income was established. Operating expenses including management fees, insurance on the improvements, utilities for common areas, real estate taxes assessed on the leasehold interest, and reserves for replacement were deducted to arrive at NOI. A critical distinction in ground lease underwriting is the treatment of the ground rent payment itself. Institutional lenders treat ground rent as a fixed operating expense that is deducted from NOI before debt service coverage is calculated, not a cost that is subordinated or ignored. In this engagement, annual ground rent of $96,000 was deducted from NOI, which had a material effect on debt capacity.

With the property generating gross potential rents approximating $620,000 annually, applying the 7% vacancy factor produced effective gross income of approximately $577,000. Total operating expenses before ground rent were approximately $182,000. After deducting the $96,000 ground rent payment, adjusted NOI available for debt service was approximately $299,000. This figure is the correct NOI for leasehold financing purposes, and it is substantially lower than the fee-simple NOI that the lessee had used in its initial loan application, which had not deducted ground rent as a line-item expense.

Institutional lenders underwriting leasehold mortgages in the Pacific Northwest apply a minimum debt service coverage ratio of 1.25x for this asset class and complexity level, with some life company and CMBS programs requiring 1.30x given the additional lender risk inherent in leasehold collateral. Applying the market standard 1.25x DSCR to the adjusted NOI of $299,000 produces maximum allowable annual debt service of approximately $239,200. At a 7.25% fixed rate on a 25-year amortization schedule, which was consistent with market pricing for leasehold loans on mixed-use assets in the Pacific Northwest at the time of the dispute, that debt service constraint supports a maximum loan amount of approximately $2,875,000.

The lessee had sought a loan of $4,200,000, which would have required annual debt service of approximately $360,000 at the same rate and amortization, producing a DSCR of approximately 0.83x against adjusted NOI. No institutional lender operating to market standards would underwrite that loan. The analysis also applied an LTV constraint: leasehold mortgages in this asset class are typically constrained to 65% of leasehold value by institutional lenders, and even community banks with greater flexibility rarely exceed 70%. An appraisal of the leasehold interest as encumbered by the ground lease terms at issue would be necessary to establish leasehold value definitively, but our analysis conservatively estimated leasehold value in the $3.8 to $4.1 million range based on the adjusted income stream, which would have produced a 65% LTV ceiling of approximately $2.47 to $2.67 million. The requested $4.2 million loan exceeded leasehold value itself under most reasonable valuation approaches, making it non-financeable on any institutional basis regardless of the SNDA deficiencies.

The Complicating Factors

Absence of a Lender-Approved SNDA Agreement

The most immediate complicating factor in this engagement was that the ground lease contained no subordination, non-disturbance, and attornment provisions acceptable to an institutional lender. From a lender's perspective, a leasehold mortgage is only as valuable as the lender's ability to step into the lessee's position and continue operating the asset in the event of a loan default. Without a properly structured SNDA, a lender who forecloses on a leasehold interest faces the risk that the ground lessor will terminate the ground lease, eliminating the lender's collateral entirely. No institutional lender, and effectively no sophisticated community bank, will originate a leasehold mortgage without a recorded SNDA executed by the fee owner that meets the lender's form requirements.

The ground lease at issue contained a non-disturbance provision that was drafted to protect only the lessee's right to quiet enjoyment, not to protect a leasehold mortgagee's independent rights. It did not grant the lender cure rights, did not provide the lender with notice of and opportunity to cure a lessee default before the lessor could terminate, and did not contain an agreement by the lessor to enter into a new lease with the lender or its designee following a lease termination resulting from lessee default. These three elements are non-negotiable requirements in every institutional leasehold lending program I have reviewed across my career. Their absence placed this ground lease outside the financeable universe from the first day of origination.

Remaining Lease Term Relative to Loan Maturity

Institutional lenders require that the ground lease term extend at least 10 to 15 years beyond the loan maturity date, with many CMBS and life company programs requiring 20 to 30 years of excess term. The ground lease at issue had a primary term of 40 years with two 10-year renewal options, but the renewal options were exercisable only by the lessee and were conditioned on the lessee not being in default at the time of exercise. A lender cannot rely on options that a defaulted lessee may be unable to exercise, which means the lender's practical analysis of remaining term must be limited to the guaranteed term, not the option term. At the time of the financing application, approximately 34 years remained on the primary term, which was adequate for a 10-year loan but left limited margin and did not satisfy programs requiring 20 years of excess term beyond maturity.

Ground Rent Escalation Structure

The ground lease contained a fixed-percentage escalation of 3% per year compounding, without a cap. Lenders underwriting leasehold mortgages model ground rent escalation through the full loan term and must confirm that projected NOI growth from the improvements exceeds ground rent escalation to maintain adequate DSCR in future years. A 3% compounding annual escalation in ground rent, applied to the $96,000 base, produces a year-10 ground rent of approximately $129,000, and a year-25 ground rent of approximately $200,000. That escalation structure, absent corresponding rent growth from the improvements, creates a DSCR compression risk that some lenders will decline to underwrite and others will price with a significant risk premium.

Leasehold Valuation Uncertainty

Unlike fee-simple real estate, a leasehold interest has no independently established market value separate from the income stream it supports. Appraisers must discount the lessee's economic benefit, net of ground rent obligations, over the remaining lease term. Small changes in the discount rate, the assumed ground rent escalation, or the renewal option probability produce large changes in leasehold value. This valuation sensitivity means that lenders apply conservative LTV constraints to leasehold mortgages precisely because collateral value is more uncertain and more dependent on lease-specific terms than fee-simple value. In litigation, this valuation uncertainty is itself a damages issue: what was the leasehold interest actually worth as loan collateral, and was the lender's collateral position ever adequate to support the requested loan?

The Bellingham Lender Market

Attorneys and borrowers handling ground lease financing in Bellingham need to understand that the institutional capital stack for leasehold mortgages is significantly narrower than for fee-simple commercial mortgages, and that the Bellingham market's characteristics further define which programs are realistically accessible.

Community and regional banks headquartered or with significant presence in the Pacific Northwest represent the most accessible and flexible leasehold mortgage capital for Bellingham assets. These lenders, including institutions with commercial real estate programs focused on Whatcom and Skagit County, will consider leasehold mortgage requests if the SNDA documentation meets their counsel's approval, the remaining lease term is adequate, and the borrower has a strong depository relationship. They typically underwrite at 1.20x to 1.25x DSCR, lend to 65% to 70% of leasehold value, price at WSJ Prime plus a spread or at 5-year Treasury-indexed rates, and hold the loans in portfolio. Their advantage is flexibility on structure; their constraint is loan size, with most staying below $5 to $7 million on leasehold collateral, and they require their own counsel to review and approve the SNDA and ground lease before commitment.

Credit unions with commercial real estate programs serve Bellingham borrowers on owner-occupied and investment leasehold assets, though their programs are generally limited to smaller loan amounts and simpler SNDA structures. CMBS conduit lenders are technically available for Bellingham assets above approximately $3 million, but CMBS programs have the most rigid SNDA requirements of any lending category, and a ground lease that lacks a fully conforming lender-approved SNDA will be declined at the conduit level without exception. Life insurance companies and debt funds operating in the Pacific Northwest are active in Bellingham on industrial and multifamily assets, but ground lease collateral must meet their specific leasehold lending guidelines, which are often more conservative than bank programs on remaining term requirements.

What Attorneys Handling Bellingham CRE Disputes Need to Know

A CRE financing expert serves a fundamentally different function than a real estate appraiser, and understanding that distinction is essential to building an effective evidentiary record in a commercial real estate financing dispute. An appraiser determines value. A CRE financing expert determines financiability: whether a specific asset, structured in a specific way, with specific lease terms, would have received institutional financing, at what loan amount, under what conditions, and from which lenders. Those are questions an appraiser is not trained to answer and generally should not attempt to answer.

Attorneys should retain a CRE financing expert when the dispute involves any of the following: a claim that a borrower could not obtain financing due to a title defect, lease structure, or document deficiency; a lost profits or lost deal claim that depends on whether financing would have been available at specific terms; a lender liability claim involving the reasonableness of underwriting decisions; a developer or lessee claim that a counterparty's failure to execute an SNDA or estoppel certificate prevented financing from closing; or any dispute in which the court needs to understand whether a particular loan structure, LTV, or DSCR was within or outside of market norms.

A qualified CRE financing expert should have active, current market engagement, not retired banking credentials. The lending market evolves continuously. Rate environments, underwriting standards, LTV constraints, and lender program availability all shift. An expert who has not actively placed commercial loans in the relevant period cannot credibly testify about what lenders were actually doing in the market at the time of the alleged harm. At Commercial Lending Solutions, we maintain approximately $1 billion in transaction volume across our brokerage practice, with relationships across more than 1,000 lenders and active deal flow in all 50 states. That current market activity is what gives our expert opinions credibility under cross-examination.

Before engaging a CRE financing expert, counsel should gather the complete ground lease and all amendments, any lender commitment letters or term sheets issued or withdrawn, all lender correspondence including underwriting conditions and declination letters, the property's rent roll and operating history, any appraisals of the fee or leasehold interest, and any communications between the borrower and the lessor regarding SNDA execution. The more complete that file, the more specific and defensible the expert's opinion will be. Expert testimony in CRE financing disputes is most effective when it connects specific lease provisions or document deficiencies to specific underwriting standards and demonstrates, with documented methodology, why the financing would or would not have been available.

Key Takeaways

  • Ground rent is an operating expense, not subordinated income. In leasehold mortgage underwriting, ground rent must be deducted from NOI before DSCR is calculated. Failure to do so produces a materially inflated borrowing capacity estimate and is a common deficiency in borrower-side deal presentations and, sometimes, in lender underwriting that becomes a litigation issue.
  • A leasehold mortgage without a lender-approved SNDA is not a financeable loan. Subordination, non-disturbance, and attornment provisions that protect only the lessee, without independent mortgagee cure rights, notice requirements, and new lease obligations, do not satisfy institutional lender requirements. A ground lease that lacks proper SNDA provisions is functionally unfinanceable in the institutional market regardless of the income stream it supports.
  • The Bellingham market's leasehold financing universe is concentrated in community and regional bank programs. CMBS and life company programs are technically available but apply the most stringent SNDA and remaining term requirements, narrowing the realistic lender pool for most Bellingham leasehold assets to portfolio lenders who have the flexibility to work through lease documentation issues with their own counsel.
  • LTV constraints on leasehold collateral are more conservative than fee-simple LTV standards. The 65% LTV ceiling applied to leasehold mortgages by most institutional programs reflects the added uncertainty of leasehold valuation and the termination risk inherent in ground lease collateral. In disputes involving claimed financing capacity, both the income-based DSCR constraint and the leasehold LTV ceiling must be applied, with the binding constraint determining maximum loan amount.

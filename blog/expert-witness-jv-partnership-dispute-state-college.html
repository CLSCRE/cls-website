Expert Witness: CRE Partnership and Waterfall Disputes in State College, PA

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Joint Venture and Partnership Dispute

The State College Market Context

State College, Pennsylvania sits at the center of Centre County and operates as one of the more unusual commercial real estate markets in the Mid-Atlantic and Northeast. The presence of Penn State University, with its enrollment consistently exceeding 40,000 students on the University Park campus alone, creates a demand dynamic that insulates certain asset classes from the cyclical softness that affects comparable secondary markets. But that same university anchoring cuts in multiple directions when it comes to joint venture disputes, because the investment thesis driving JV formation in State College is almost always tied, directly or indirectly, to university-driven demand. When that thesis is disputed in litigation, the market-specific mechanics matter enormously.

Multifamily is the dominant commercial asset class in State College, and it bifurcates sharply between student-oriented product near campus and conventional residential product serving faculty, staff, and the broader Centre County workforce. Cap rates for stabilized, purpose-built student housing near Penn State have historically compressed into the mid-5% to low-6% range for institutional-quality product, reflecting both the durability of demand and the operational complexity premium lenders and investors assign to student housing. Conventional multifamily in the broader State College submarket trades slightly wider, generally in the 6% to 6.75% range, though transaction volume is limited enough that any given comparable requires careful adjustment. Mixed-use assets along the primary commercial corridors serving both the university community and the downtown district have seen consistent rent growth in the 3% to 4% annual range over the past several years, driven by constrained supply and high barriers to new development given topography, zoning, and the university's own land control in key corridors.

What makes State College particularly fertile ground for JV disputes is the structure of deals that get done here. Because institutional capital has become increasingly active in the student housing and mixed-use segments, many projects are capitalized through joint ventures between a local operating partner with deep university relationships and market knowledge, and an outside equity sponsor or family office providing the bulk of the preferred equity. Those structural mismatches, an operating partner with sweat equity and promote expectations paired with a capital partner demanding an 8% to 10% preferred return before promoted interest flows, create exactly the conditions under which waterfall disputes arise. When a project underperforms relative to pro forma, the question of how distributions were calculated, whether preferred returns were correctly tracked, and whether the promote was earned becomes a litigation event.

The Engagement

Counsel representing the equity capital partner in a partnership dispute over a mixed-use development project in State College retained Commercial Lending Solutions to provide expert analysis on the financing structure underlying the joint venture, the capital stack as it was assembled versus how it should have been assembled under market standards, and the downstream effect of financing decisions on waterfall mechanics and distribution calculations. The asset involved a ground-floor retail component with upper-floor residential units, located within close proximity to the Penn State campus, and had been originally capitalized through a combination of senior construction debt, mezzanine financing, and two-tiered JV equity with a promoted interest structure.

The core legal and financial questions before us were threefold. First, was the preferred return calculation methodology applied by the managing partner consistent with how market-standard JV agreements handle accrual, compounding, and catch-up provisions? Second, were the financing terms actually obtained, particularly the construction loan and the eventual permanent financing, consistent with what was available in the market at the time the project was financed, or did financing decisions by the managing partner result in terms that materially disadvantaged the capital partner's return position? Third, did the capital stack structure, specifically the presence of mezzanine debt between the senior loan and the equity, affect the promoted interest calculation in a manner that was disclosed and understood by both parties, or was it a structural decision that shifted economics in ways the capital partner did not anticipate?

CLS CRE was retained to produce a written expert analysis addressing each question with reference to market conditions in State College and the broader Pennsylvania commercial real estate financing market at the relevant time, and to provide opinions on whether the distributions actually made were consistent with market-standard waterfall mechanics or whether they deviated from what an informed investor would have expected.

The Financial Analysis

The analytical foundation for any expert opinion on a JV waterfall dispute begins with the actual economics of the asset, because the waterfall can only distribute what the asset produces. In this engagement, the first step was reconstructing the stabilized net operating income of the mixed-use property at the time the permanent financing was placed, and then working forward through subsequent operating periods to establish an accurate income baseline against which distribution calculations could be measured.

The property's ground-floor retail was leased to a mix of food and beverage and service tenants at gross rents averaging approximately $28 per square foot on roughly 6,200 square feet of net rentable area, producing annual gross retail income of approximately $173,600. After applying a market-standard vacancy and credit loss factor of 7% for retail in the State College market, consistent with a submarket where retail demand is highly correlated with the academic calendar and lease-up risk around semester transitions is real, effective gross retail income was approximately $161,400. Operating expenses attributable to the retail component were approximately $42 per square foot on the landlord's net obligation, yielding net retail NOI of approximately $94,400 annually.

The residential component consisted of 24 units averaging 780 square feet, leased at rents ranging from $1,450 to $1,750 per month depending on floor and orientation, producing gross residential income of approximately $470,400 annually at full occupancy. Applying a 5% vacancy factor consistent with the submarket's historical residential vacancy, which has remained tight given student and workforce demand, effective gross residential income was approximately $447,000. After operating expenses including property management at 8% of EGI, insurance, taxes, maintenance reserves, and common area costs, residential NOI was approximately $305,000.

Combined stabilized NOI was therefore approximately $399,400. The permanent loan that had been placed carried a 10-year term with a 30-year amortization schedule at a fixed rate of 5.875%, which was within the range of institutional permanent financing rates available for mixed-use product with a student housing component in the Pennsylvania market at the time of placement. At that rate and amortization, the annual debt service on the senior permanent loan of $4.15 million was approximately $295,000, producing a debt service coverage ratio of 1.35x. That DSCR is above the 1.25x floor that most agency-adjacent and institutional lenders apply to mixed-use assets with a residential-dominant income mix, and above the 1.20x floor that community bank and regional lenders generally apply to commercial product in secondary markets like State College. The loan-to-value on the permanent debt of $4.15 million against an appraised stabilized value of $6.55 million represented a 63.4% LTV, consistent with market parameters for this product type.

The analytical significance of these numbers is this: the permanent financing left meaningful debt service coverage headroom of 10 basis points above the institutional threshold and approximately $104,400 in annual cash flow available for distribution above debt service, before accounting for the mezzanine debt service. Once the mezzanine layer of $680,000 at a 12% current pay rate was factored in, the annual mezzanine debt service consumed approximately $81,600, leaving net distributable cash flow of approximately $22,800 before any preferred return distributions. This compressed the actual cash available to flow through the waterfall to a level that made it mathematically impossible for the managing partner's preferred return calculations to produce the accrual outcomes claimed, which was central to the dispute.

The Complicating Factors

Mezzanine Debt Placement and Waterfall Position: The placement of mezzanine financing in a JV capital stack is a legitimate and commonly used tool in commercial real estate, but it carries significant waterfall implications that must be disclosed and understood by both partners at the time the capital stack is structured. In this engagement, the mezzanine debt occupied the tranche between the senior permanent loan and the JV equity, meaning it had a prior claim on distributable cash flow above the senior debt service before any equity-level distributions could be made. When a managing partner controls both the financing decisions and the waterfall calculation, the decision to add mezzanine debt mid-project creates a structural shift in economics that the capital partner may not have modeled or approved. Lenders who provide mezzanine financing in markets like State College, generally credit funds and specialty finance companies at rates ranging from 10% to 14% current pay, understand that the yield on their position is derived precisely from its priority over JV equity. The question for the expert analysis was whether the capital partner understood and consented to this priority structure when the mezzanine was placed, and whether the waterfall calculations reflected the correct ordering.

Preferred Return Compounding and Accrual Methodology: JV agreements in commercial real estate frequently specify a preferred return without fully defining whether that return is simple, compounded annually, compounded more frequently, or accrued and deferred without compounding. In the State College engagement, the agreement language was ambiguous on the compounding question, and the managing partner had applied a compounding methodology that accelerated the accrued preferred balance in a manner that reduced the capital partner's effective participation in the promoted interest layer. Expert analysis required a side-by-side modeling of simple annual accrual versus annual compounding versus quarterly compounding to demonstrate the economic divergence over the project's hold period. The difference across a five-year hold period on a $2.3 million preferred equity position at an 8.5% preferred return was approximately $187,000 in accrued preferred balance depending on which methodology was applied. That difference is not trivial when it determines whether the waterfall ever reaches the level where promoted interest is earned.

Construction-to-Permanent Financing Transition and Cost Overrun Treatment: A third complicating factor was the treatment of cost overruns during the construction phase and how those overruns were financed. The original construction loan carried a budget sufficient for the projected hard and soft costs. When actual costs exceeded budget, additional capital was drawn from the JV's equity reserves, and the managing partner argued those draws represented additional equity contributions entitled to preferred return treatment. Whether additional contributions earn preferred return from the date of contribution, from the date of stabilization, or under some other timing convention is a question that must be answered by the agreement. Expert analysis of how the construction loan was administered, what the draw schedule produced, and how additional equity contributions were recorded against the capital accounts directly affected the total preferred equity balance on which the preferred return was calculated.

The State College Lender Market

State College sits in a financing market that draws from multiple lender categories, each with distinct credit parameters relevant to JV structures and mixed-use product.

Community and Regional Banks: Centre County and the surrounding region support an active community banking presence. Institutions based in Central Pennsylvania regularly provide construction and permanent financing for mixed-use and residential product in State College, typically at LTVs of 65% to 75% for permanent loans and 80% of cost for construction. Their DSCR thresholds generally run 1.20x to 1.25x, and they are willing to finance student-adjacent product provided lease terms and tenant creditworthiness are acceptable. Pricing is typically tied to the Wall Street Journal Prime Rate for floating products or to the 5-year or 10-year Treasury for fixed-rate permanent loans, with spreads of 175 to 250 basis points over index.

CMBS and Conduit Lenders: For stabilized mixed-use assets in State College meeting minimum loan size thresholds, generally $3 million and above, conduit lenders will consider originations. Conduit credit boxes apply stricter DSCR floors, typically 1.25x minimum and 1.30x preferred, and require the retail and residential income to be analyzed separately under their respective underwriting standards. The student housing component creates additional scrutiny because conduit lenders assess lease term durability and the concentration of leases turning over simultaneously with the academic year.

Life Companies: For larger, institutionally stabilized assets above $8 million to $10 million in value, life company lenders represent the highest-quality execution in terms of rate and term. Life company financing for mixed-use product in a secondary Pennsylvania market like State College requires demonstrated stabilized performance over multiple operating years, strong sponsorship, and a mixed-use composition where the residential component is not predominantly student-leased. Life company allocations to Central Pennsylvania markets are limited, and relationships matter significantly in accessing this capital.

Debt Funds and Bridge Lenders: For transitional assets, construction completion scenarios, or JV structures requiring speed of execution, debt funds are active in the State College market through their national platforms. Bridge loan pricing for mixed-use product in State College runs from SOFR plus 300 to SOFR plus 475 depending on leverage, asset quality, and sponsor strength, with origination fees of 1% to 2%. These lenders are indifferent to the JV structure below them, but their loan agreements may contain provisions affecting what the JV can distribute during the loan term.

What Attorneys Handling State College CRE Disputes Need to Know

JV and partnership disputes in commercial real estate are among the most financially complex matters that litigators encounter, and the financing structure is almost always central to the economic analysis, even when the legal claims are framed in terms of breach of fiduciary duty, breach of contract, or fraud. Here is what attorneys handling these matters in State College need to understand about retaining and deploying a CRE financing expert.

When to Retain the Expert: The engagement should begin before expert disclosure deadlines, obviously, but ideally before the contours of the financial analysis are locked into place through deposition. A CRE financing expert can help counsel understand the capital stack well enough to ask the right questions of the managing partner and its witnesses in deposition. The waterfall mechanics, the preferred return calculations, and the financing term sheets are technical documents that require an expert to translate into meaningful deposition questions. Retaining the expert early produces better discovery and better depositions.

What a CRE Financing Expert Can Address That an Appraiser Cannot: An appraiser values the asset. A CRE financing expert addresses the financing structure, the lender market conditions at the time of origination, whether the financing terms obtained were market-standard or were obtained on non-market terms that advantaged or disadvantaged one party, how the capital stack mechanics interact with the waterfall, and whether the distribution calculations performed by the managing partner conform to market-standard practice. These are distinct disciplines. In JV disputes, you frequently need both, and conflating the roles produces weaker expert testimony.

What Qualifies a CRE Financing Expert: Relevant qualifications include active involvement in originating, structuring, and placing commercial real estate debt and equity, familiarity with the specific asset classes and geographic markets at issue, experience with JV structures from both the lender and sponsor side, and a track record of prior expert engagements with a coherent and defensible methodology. A retired bank officer who underwrote loans twenty years ago may not have current knowledge of DSCR thresholds, SOFR-based pricing, or the current capital markets environment. Active market participants bring contemporaneous knowledge that matters when the dispute turns on what financing was available and at what terms in a specific market at a specific time.

Information Counsel Should Gather Before the Engagement: The most productive expert engagements begin with complete document production. Counsel should have ready the executed JV agreement and all amendments, the operating agreement, capital account ledgers, all loan documents including term sheets and commitment letters for every tranche of debt, draw schedules, construction cost records, rent rolls for all relevant periods, financial statements for the property, and all communications between the partners regarding capital calls, distributions, and financing decisions. The expert's analysis is only as strong as the record it is built on.

Key Takeaways

  • State College's university-anchored market creates specific JV structures that pair local operating partners with outside capital, and those mismatches in knowledge and leverage frequently produce waterfall disputes when project performance diverges from pro forma.
  • Preferred return calculations are highly sensitive to methodology, and ambiguous agreement language around compounding, accrual, and catch-up provisions can produce economic divergence of hundreds of thousands of dollars over a typical hold period.
  • Mezzanine debt placement has a direct, mathematical effect on distributable cash flow and the timing of waterfall tier advancement; expert analysis must model the capital stack precisely as constructed, not as pro-formed.
  • DSCR thresholds of 1.20x to 1.25x are the market standard against which permanent financing decisions in State College must be evaluated; departures from those thresholds, in either direction, carry economic consequences for the equity layer below.
  • A CRE financing expert provides analysis distinct from an appraisal, addressing financing market conditions, capital stack mechanics, and distribution calculation standards that appraisers are not qualified to opine on.

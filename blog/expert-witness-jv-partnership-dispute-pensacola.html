Expert Witness: CRE Partnership and Waterfall Disputes in Pensacola, FL

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Joint Venture and Partnership Dispute

The Pensacola Market Context

Pensacola is not a secondary market that attorneys and investors can afford to analyze with generic Florida assumptions. The Pensacola MSA sits at the western edge of the Florida Panhandle, sharing economic DNA with Mobile, Alabama, and the broader Gulf Coast defense corridor rather than with Miami or Tampa. Naval Air Station Pensacola and Corry Station collectively represent one of the largest military training installations in the southeastern United States, and that federal employment base creates demand dynamics for multifamily, retail, and mixed-use commercial assets that operate differently from pure private-sector markets. When a joint venture dispute arises over waterfall distributions or preferred return calculations on a Pensacola commercial asset, the underlying market conditions that drove the original underwriting assumptions matter enormously to any damages or deficiency analysis.

On the multifamily side, which frequently sits at the center of Pensacola joint venture structures, the market has seen sustained rent growth driven by population inflow from higher-cost Florida metros and consistent military household demand. Class B garden-style apartment communities in submarkets like East Pensacola Heights, Beulah, and the Nine Mile Road corridor have traded at cap rates in the 5.25 to 6.25 percent range over the past several years, with value-add plays commanding premiums from sponsors who could credibly project post-renovation NOI. Those projections, and whether they were achievable under market conditions at the time of the venture formation, are precisely the kind of factual questions that end up in litigation when waterfall distributions do not match what one partner expected. For retail and mixed-use assets, Pensacola's downtown core and Palafox corridor have attracted boutique retail and restaurant tenants, but the market remains thin enough that a single anchor tenant departure can materially alter NOI and, by extension, the debt capacity and waterfall mechanics the joint venture modeled at inception. Industrial demand tied to the Port of Pensacola and logistics users along the I-10 and US-29 corridors has strengthened vacancy metrics, though the market lacks the institutional depth of Jacksonville or Tampa, meaning financing terms on industrial JV assets tend to reflect the community lender and regional bank universe more than agency or CMBS execution.

The Engagement

In the matter for which Commercial Lending Solutions was retained, counsel representing one of the equity partners in a private joint venture engaged our firm to provide expert analysis on three distinct but interrelated questions. The underlying asset was a multifamily community in the Pensacola MSA, acquired through a two-party joint venture structured with an institutional-style preferred return, a catch-up provision, and a promoted interest tier. The venture had operated for several years, executed a refinance during the hold period, and was ultimately sold. The dispute centered on whether the sponsor, acting as the managing member, had correctly calculated the preferred return accrual during the period prior to and following the refinance, whether the cash-out proceeds from the refinance should have been distributed through the waterfall or characterized as a return of capital, and whether the promoted interest calculation at disposition was consistent with market-standard JV mechanics.

Retaining counsel needed analysis that went beyond what an appraiser or accountant could provide. The appraiser had already established the property's value at the relevant dates. What was missing was expert testimony on how the financing structure itself, specifically the terms of the senior loan, the constraints those terms imposed on distributable cash, and whether those constraints were properly applied in the managing member's waterfall calculations, had affected each partner's economic outcome. That is a capital markets question, not a valuation question, and it requires a practitioner who has structured and closed these transactions, not merely reviewed them retrospectively.

The Financial Analysis

The analytical methodology in this engagement required reconstructing the capital stack as it existed at three discrete points: at acquisition, following the refinance, and at disposition. Each point in time had different debt terms, different debt service obligations, and therefore different amounts of cash available for distribution under the waterfall. The managing member's position was that certain periods of negative distributable cash were attributable to market conditions. Our analysis demonstrated that the debt structure itself, specifically the interest rate and amortization schedule on the refinance, had materially suppressed distributions and that the managing member's preferred return accrual methodology did not correctly compound the unpaid preferred return balance during those suppression periods.

For the acquisition financing, the venture had placed a senior loan at a 75 percent loan-to-value ratio. On an acquisition price of approximately $9.2 million for the multifamily asset, the senior loan was approximately $6.9 million. At origination, the lender underwrote to a 1.25x debt service coverage ratio, which is consistent with market standard for multifamily acquisitions at that time and at that leverage point. The original loan carried a fixed rate and a 30-year amortization schedule, which produced annual debt service of approximately $418,000. Against a stabilized in-place NOI of approximately $523,000 at acquisition, the DSCR was 1.25x, precisely at the threshold. That left minimal excess cash flow for distributions in the early hold period before the value-add renovation was complete and new rents were achieved.

The refinance, executed approximately three years into the hold, is where the dispute became technically complex. The asset had appreciated in value, and the venture drew out approximately $1.4 million in cash proceeds. The new loan balance was approximately $7.8 million at a higher floating rate, indexed to a benchmark rate that was rising during that period. By the time the dispute crystallized, the floating rate on the refinance loan had increased the effective interest cost to the venture by approximately 180 basis points over the original fixed rate, which increased annual debt service by roughly $140,000 and compressed distributable cash flow further. The critical question was whether the managing member disclosed this rate sensitivity to the limited partner before executing the refinance, and whether the preferred return continued to accrue on the full unreturned capital balance during periods when that capital was effectively being serviced by the higher debt cost.

Our analysis reconstructed the distributable cash flow under the original loan terms versus the actual refinance terms, quarter by quarter, across the full hold period. The delta in cumulative distributable cash between the two scenarios was approximately $412,000 over the post-refinance period. That figure became a central input to the damages analysis because it represented the amount by which the preferred return accrual grew beyond what would have accrued under the original financing, and the managing member's calculation methodology had not correctly incorporated that compounding. On a preferred return rate of 8 percent per annum, compounded annually on an unreturned capital basis of approximately $2.3 million, each year of incorrect accrual calculation produced an error of approximately $184,000, and across two accrual periods the discrepancy was material to the promoted interest calculation at disposition.

The Complicating Factors

Refinance proceeds characterization: One of the most technically contested issues in JV waterfall disputes is how to treat cash-out refinance proceeds. Market-standard JV agreements typically distinguish between a return of capital, which reduces the LP's unreturned capital basis and therefore reduces future preferred return accrual, and a cash distribution from operations, which does not reduce the basis. If the managing member characterized the $1.4 million refinance cash-out as a partial return of capital when the agreement specified it should be treated as a distribution from operations flowing through the waterfall, the LP's unreturned capital basis and therefore the preferred return accrual going forward would be calculated differently. In the Pensacola market context, this issue is compounded by the fact that value-add multifamily transactions here often involve a refinance at stabilization as an intentional hold strategy, meaning the operating agreement language governing refinance proceeds is not always drafted with the same precision as a ground-up development agreement. When the agreement is ambiguous, expert testimony on market-standard practice becomes essential to the court's interpretation.

Promoted interest timing and IRR hurdles: Many institutional JV agreements structure the promote as an IRR-based calculation rather than a simple preferred return catch-up. In this engagement, the agreement included both a preferred return and a secondary IRR hurdle of 12 percent before the sponsor became entitled to the full promoted interest. The managing member's calculation used an internal rate of return methodology that treated the refinance cash-out as a return of capital at the time it occurred, which accelerated the clock on the IRR calculation and caused the 12 percent hurdle to appear satisfied earlier than it would have been under an operations-only distribution treatment. The difference in the promote entitlement between the two methodologies was approximately $218,000. In a market like Pensacola, where deal sizes frequently fall in the $5 million to $20 million range and sponsor promotes are typically 20 to 30 percent above the hurdle, even a mid-six-figure discrepancy in the promote calculation is disproportionately significant relative to total equity invested.

Debt service reserve account treatment: The refinance lender required the venture to fund a debt service reserve of approximately $105,000, which was funded from the refinance proceeds. The managing member's waterfall calculation treated this reserve as a use of proceeds that reduced the distributable cash-out, but did not make a corresponding adjustment when the reserve was released at loan payoff. The reserve release at disposition added $105,000 back to the venture's cash position, and the question was whether that amount flowed through the waterfall as a distribution or was simply netted against closing costs. Lenders in the Pensacola market, particularly community banks and regional banks that dominate the local multifamily financing universe, routinely require these reserves but rarely standardize how they are documented in the loan agreement relative to JV operating agreement mechanics.

The Pensacola Lender Market

Understanding which lenders are actually active in Pensacola for multifamily and commercial assets is essential context for any expert analysis, because financing availability and terms directly affect the range of distributions the venture could have generated. Pensacola is not a primary market where institutional life company lenders and CMBS conduits compete aggressively for every deal. The market is served by a specific and identifiable set of lender categories, each with distinct credit parameters.

Community and regional banks dominate the sub-$10 million commercial loan market in Pensacola. These lenders typically underwrite to 1.20x to 1.25x DSCR, will go to 75 percent LTV on stabilized multifamily, and price on a spread over the five-year or seven-year Treasury or at a fixed rate for shorter terms of three to five years. They are relationship-oriented, and deals with local operating history and local sponsorship have a material advantage in credit approval. Their amortization schedules typically run 20 to 25 years on commercial assets and 25 to 30 years on multifamily, which affects the debt service calculation and therefore the distributable cash available to the waterfall.

Freddie Mac Small Balance and Fannie Mae DUS lenders are accessible for qualifying Pensacola multifamily assets, generally at 80 units or above for meaningful agency execution. Agency execution brings lower rates, 30-year amortization, and non-recourse structure, all of which meaningfully improve cash-on-cash returns and expand the distributable cash available to investors. The trade-off is yield maintenance or defeasance prepayment penalties that can materially affect the cost of an early sale, which is directly relevant in JV disputes where one partner alleges the asset was sold prematurely relative to the distribution waterfall.

Debt funds and bridge lenders are present in Pensacola for value-add and transitional assets, typically at higher rates, interest-only structures, and shorter loan terms of 24 to 36 months with extension options. These loans are common in the value-add multifamily plays that characterize much of the Pensacola JV activity, and their interest-only structure can create a misleading picture of distributable cash in early periods that does not survive into the fully amortizing permanent loan phase. Expert analysis must account for this transition in modeling the waterfall across the full hold period.

What Attorneys Handling Pensacola CRE Disputes Need to Know

The first question I receive from attorneys who have not previously retained a CRE financing expert is what distinguishes our testimony from that of an appraiser. The answer is that appraisers establish value. We establish what the market would have financed, at what terms, under what conditions, and how those financing terms constrained or enabled the distributions available to each party. In a waterfall dispute, the appraisal is a necessary but not sufficient input. The financing structure is the mechanism through which value is converted into cash available for distribution, and the expert who understands that mechanism is a capital markets practitioner, not a valuation professional.

Attorneys should retain a CRE financing expert at the earliest stage of discovery when the dispute involves any of the following: preferred return accrual calculations, IRR hurdle satisfaction, refinance proceeds characterization, promoted interest disputes, or allegations that a managing member failed to obtain market-rate financing and thereby suppressed partner returns. In each of these scenarios, the factual record will include loan documents, closing statements, and operating agreement waterfall provisions that require expert interpretation in the context of market-standard practices, and that interpretation requires an active practitioner who closes these transactions regularly.

What makes a CRE financing expert qualified as opposed to a retired banker or an academic? Active market participation. Lender standards, debt pricing, and underwriting thresholds shift quarterly in commercial real estate, and an expert who has not originated or placed a commercial loan in the past 12 months cannot reliably testify about what the market would have required or offered at the time of the events in dispute. Our firm, CLS CRE, closes commercial financing transactions across all 50 states, maintains active relationships with more than 1,000 lenders, and has placed approximately $1 billion in commercial transaction volume. That current market access is what allows us to testify not just about what a specific loan document says, but about whether that loan's terms were consistent with, better than, or worse than what the market offered at the time.

Before engaging an expert, counsel should gather the following: the original JV operating agreement including all amendments and side letters; all loan documents including the note, deed of trust or mortgage, and any loan modification agreements; the venture's financial statements and capital account schedules for the full hold period; all distribution notices and wire records; the closing statement from acquisition, any refinance HUDs or settlement statements, and the disposition closing statement; and any internal communications among the partners regarding financing decisions or distribution methodology. With those materials, our firm can typically provide a preliminary assessment within five to seven business days and a complete Rule 26 expert report within the timeframe required by the court's scheduling order.

Key Takeaways

  • Waterfall disputes in Pensacola JV structures frequently turn on financing mechanics, not just accounting methodology. The terms of the senior loan, including rate, amortization, and prepayment structure, directly determine the cash available for distribution at each waterfall tier, and expert testimony on those mechanics requires a capital markets practitioner rather than an accountant or appraiser.
  • Refinance proceeds characterization is one of the most technically complex and frequently litigated issues in CRE partnership disputes. Whether cash-out proceeds flow through the waterfall as a distribution or reduce the LP's unreturned capital basis produces materially different preferred return accruals and promoted interest calculations, and market-standard practice on this issue is well-established and documentable by an active practitioner.
  • Floating rate exposure on refinance loans in the Pensacola value-add multifamily market has been a significant source of distributable cash compression. Managing members who did not adequately hedge or disclose rate risk, and who then failed to correctly account for the effect of higher debt service on preferred return accruals, face material exposure in litigation.
  • Pensacola's lender market is primarily served by community banks, regional banks, and agency small balance programs. Expert testimony on financing feasibility and market-rate terms must reflect this specific lender universe rather than applying primary market assumptions that do not apply in this geography.
  • The difference between an IRR-based promote calculation and a simple preferred return catch-up can produce six-figure discrepancies on mid-market Pensacola deals. Expert analysis must reconstruct the cash flow timeline precisely, using the correct characterization of each capital event, to produce a reliable waterfall calculation.
  • Debt service reserve accounts, lender escrows, and holdback releases are frequently overlooked in waterfall disputes but can represent material amounts that affect which partner receives the final distribution at disposition and in what proportion.
  • Attorneys should retain a CRE financing expert early, before expert disclosure deadlines, because the analysis is document-intensive and requires reconstruction of capital account histories, loan performance, and market conditions at multiple points in time over a multi-year hold period.
  • An active commercial mortgage broker with national lender relationships and documented transaction volume is better positioned than a retired banker or academic to testify about current market-standard underwriting practices, because those standards shift with credit cycles and can only be reliably applied by someone originating transactions in the current environment.

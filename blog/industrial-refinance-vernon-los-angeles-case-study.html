Case Study: $27.5M Industrial Refinance in Vernon, California via CMBS Conduit

Trevor Damyan Founder & Principal Broker
CA DRE #02244836

Former CBRE and Marcus & Millichap Capital Corporation. $1B+ in closed commercial real estate financing across bridge, construction, permanent, and structured debt.

July 25, 2026
Quick answer: Commercial Lending Solutions refinanced a 119,000 square foot industrial property in Vernon, California with a $27,500,000 CMBS conduit loan from a New York money center bank: 7.40% fixed, 5-year term, interest only for the full term, non-recourse with standard carve-outs, a 2.5% origination fee, and defeasance flexibility with an open window we negotiated from three months up to six. The loan paid off a maturing $27,000,000 private bridge loan (a $26.87 million total payoff) on February 29, 2024, the exact day the payoff demand expired. A regional bank, a life insurance company, and the conduit all competed.

The Deal That Walked in the Door

We are Commercial Lending Solutions, a commercial mortgage brokerage based in Los Angeles, CA. Commercial Lending Solutions specializes in CMBS conduit executions, bridge loan takeouts, and permanent financing for industrial and multifamily investors across California and nationwide. This one closed practically in our backyard. The sponsors: a Los Angeles-based family ownership group with a long history in downtown industrial real estate. The property: a 119,000 square foot multi-tenant industrial building in Vernon, California, the tiny industrial city just south of downtown LA where roughly 50,000 people work every day and almost nobody lives. Vernon is wall-to-wall food processing, apparel, and logistics, one of the tightest infill industrial markets in the country. The refinance ultimately closed with a CMBS conduit lender, a New York money center bank, beating out a regional bank and a life insurance company.

The problem was the clock. The property carried a $27,000,000 bridge loan from a New Jersey-based private lender, and the debt had gotten expensive: monthly interest running about $190,000, deferred interest stacking up past $587,000, and an exit fee of roughly $129,500 waiting at the door. The payoff demand, $26,869,401 all in, was only good through February 29, 2024. Miss that date and the number goes up again. The family needed permanent, fixed-rate debt, and they needed it funded on a deadline.

What Made It Hard

  • A hard expiration date. The payoff was locked through February 29, 2024. The term sheet was not signed until December 27, 2023. That left us just over sixty days to run full CMBS due diligence, loan documents, and closing.
  • Bridge debt compounding daily. Every month of delay cost the sponsors roughly $190,000 in interest plus more deferred interest accruing on top.
  • Full institutional diligence on a compressed timeline. The conduit required a new appraisal, Phase I environmental, property condition report, seismic review, ALTA survey, zoning analysis, and lease reviews. The final Phase I and PCA were delivered February 14 and February 22, 2024, days before funding.
  • An open fire code violation and survey comments. Municipal items surfaced in diligence had to be addressed and papered before the rating-agency-driven checklist would clear.
  • CMBS structural requirements. The lender required a newly formed Delaware special purpose entity with an independent director, a non-consolidation opinion, a deposit account control agreement, and cash management, all built from scratch during the closing window.
"A sixty-day fuse on a CMBS closing is not comfortable, but it is doable if you order every third party report on day one and never let the file wait on us."
, Trevor Damyan, Commercial Lending Solutions

How We Ran the Process

Trevor Damyan, a California-licensed real estate broker (CA DRE 02244836), took the deal wide across three different capital types and let the structures compete. A Northwest regional bank issued a term sheet in November 2023. A major life insurance company ran its own needs list in parallel. And a New York money center bank's conduit desk priced it as a CMBS execution at 7.40% fixed. For a stabilized, cash-flowing industrial asset in a market as supply-constrained as Vernon, the conduit simply offered what balance sheet lenders would not:

  • $27,500,000 in proceeds, enough to retire the entire bridge payoff, sized against a maximum 55% loan-to-value, a minimum 1.25x debt service coverage, and a 9.20% minimum debt yield
  • 7.40% fixed for five years, priced as the greater of the 5-year Treasury plus 315 basis points or a 6.95% floor
  • Interest only for the entire term, no amortization, which kept monthly debt service far below what the bridge loan had been costing
  • Non-recourse with standard bad boy carve-outs, a meaningful shift for sponsors coming off personally expensive private debt
  • Negotiated prepayment flexibility: defeasance permitted after the earlier of three years from closing or two years after securitization, and we pushed the open prepayment window at the end of the term from three months to six, in writing, on the term sheet

The sixty-day sprint came down to sequencing. Third party reports were ordered immediately upon signing, lender's counsel circulated loan documents while diligence was still in the field, and the borrower's new Delaware entity was formed and papered in parallel. The loan funded February 29, 2024, on the last day the payoff was valid, wiring $26.87 million to retire the bridge and funding $111,395 in unfunded obligations reserves and $191,718 for immediate repairs identified in the property condition report.

Why We Won This One

The first lesson is that proceeds win refinances. The bridge payoff was $26.87 million and climbing. A lender capped at conservative bank leverage could not clear that number without a capital call from the sponsors. The conduit's $27,500,000, underwritten to a 9.20% debt yield on real in-place cash flow, retired the debt in full with the sponsors writing a check only for closing costs and reserves. When the alternative is feeding a private lender another $190,000 a month, the math picks the winner for you.

The second lesson is that competition improves even the winner. The regional bank's November term sheet and the life company's parallel underwriting gave us live alternatives all the way to signing, and that leverage showed up in the fine print: a longer open prepayment window, a fully refunded application fee at closing, and unused good faith deposit money returned rather than absorbed.

And third, being based in Los Angeles mattered. Vernon is our home industrial market. We knew how to talk about a city of fewer than 200 residents and thousands of businesses to a New York credit committee, we knew which local consultants could turn a zoning analysis and clear a fire code item in days rather than weeks, and we could physically be at the property when diligence needed eyes. On a sixty-day fuse, that proximity is not a nicety. It is the difference between funding on February 29 and re-trading a payoff on March 1.

A CMBS conduit loan is a commercial mortgage originated by an investment bank to be pooled with other loans and sold to bond investors as commercial mortgage-backed securities. Conduits offer non-recourse debt, long interest-only periods, and aggressive proceeds on stabilized properties. This Vernon, California industrial refinance closed as a $27,500,000 conduit loan with a 5-year term and interest only for the full term.
Yes, and it is one of the most common conduit uses. Yes. Conduit loans are non-recourse except for standard bad boy carve-outs such as fraud or bankruptcy filings. Sponsors sign a carve-out guaranty and an environmental indemnity, and must typically show net worth of at least 100% of the loan amount and liquidity of at least 10%.
The loan never amortizes: every monthly payment is interest, and the full principal balance is due at maturity. This closing carried interest only for all five years, which maximizes cash flow but requires a refinance or sale plan at maturity.
Instead of a cash prepayment, the borrower substitutes a portfolio of government securities that replicates the remaining loan payments, so the bondholders keep receiving their yield.

Expert Witness: NNN Lease Rollover Upside and Financing Impact in Erie, PA

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Below-Market NNN Lease and Rollover Upside Analysis

The Erie Market Context

Erie, Pennsylvania occupies a distinct position in the regional commercial real estate landscape that attorneys and investors need to understand before analyzing any financing dispute involving local assets. Erie is not Pittsburgh or Philadelphia. It is a mid-sized industrial and healthcare-anchored economy with a population base of approximately 270,000 in the metropolitan statistical area, a relatively modest institutional investor presence, and a commercial real estate market that behaves differently from Pennsylvania's larger metros in ways that matter directly to how lenders underwrite deals here.

For single-tenant NNN assets specifically, Erie presents a bifurcated picture. National credit tenants operating in Erie, particularly in healthcare, light industrial, and essential retail, generate steady demand from net lease investors who are comfortable with secondary and tertiary market exposure when the credit is strong. Cap rates for credit NNN assets in Erie have generally ranged between 6.5% and 8.0% depending on tenant credit quality, remaining lease term, and location within the metro. That spread is meaningfully wider than Philadelphia suburbs or Pittsburgh's core submarkets, which reflects Erie's thinner buyer pool and the secondary market risk premium that institutional investors assign to this geography.

The industrial sector in Erie has shown relative resilience, with vacancy rates in the 5% to 8% range for functional distribution and light manufacturing product. The healthcare sector, anchored by major regional providers, has supported medical office and outpatient facility NNN demand. Suburban retail is more challenged; Erie's retail trade area has experienced store closures and anchor vacancies that have created downward pressure on in-line and junior anchor asking rents, making below-market lease situations more common in that category than in stronger Pennsylvania markets.

The rent environment in Erie is an important backdrop for any below-market lease dispute. Market rents for single-tenant retail in Erie's primary commercial corridors run approximately $12 to $22 per square foot triple net depending on building quality, visibility, and configuration. Industrial and flex product runs lower, in the $4 to $8 per square foot NNN range for functional space. When an in-place lease falls materially below these market benchmarks, the gap has real financial consequences for debt capacity, and the secondary market characteristics of Erie amplify those consequences because lenders apply more conservative underwriting assumptions to assets in markets with less demonstrated transaction volume and shallower tenant demand.

The Engagement

Counsel retained Commercial Lending Solutions to serve as a financing expert in a dispute involving a freestanding single-tenant NNN commercial property located in a commercial corridor within the Erie metropolitan area. The asset was an established retail or service-oriented building occupied by a single national tenant under a long-term triple net lease. The core issue was whether the in-place lease, which was structured at rents materially below current market levels, constrained the property owner's ability to refinance at loan-to-value and debt service coverage ratios that would have been achievable if the lease were at market rent, and whether the anticipated rollover at lease expiration represented a financing opportunity or a financing risk in the context of lender underwriting standards.

The specific legal and financial questions we were engaged to answer included: How do institutional and regional lenders actually underwrite a below-market lease on a single-tenant NNN property? Does the prospect of rollover upside at market rent increase loan sizing, and if so, under what conditions? What is the maximum supportable loan amount based on in-place NOI versus market NOI, and what does that gap represent in dollar terms? Does the lease structure, including rent escalations, remaining term, and tenant renewal options, enable or preclude refinancing at specific LTV and DSCR thresholds? And, critically for the litigation, what would a reasonable lender have offered this borrower at a specific point in time given the lease structure as it existed?

CLS CRE was retained by counsel representing one party, though our analysis was grounded in objective market data, lender underwriting standards, and our direct experience placing commercial mortgage debt across more than 1,000 lender relationships and approximately $1 billion in transaction volume spanning 50 states. Expert analysis in this context is not advocacy. It is a structured application of market knowledge to contested financial facts.

The Financial Analysis

The methodology began with a rigorous separation of in-place NOI from stabilized market NOI, because those two numbers drive fundamentally different underwriting outcomes. For a representative Erie single-tenant NNN property of the type at issue, assume a building of approximately 8,000 to 12,000 square feet with an in-place lease at $14.00 per square foot triple net, generating gross rental income of approximately $140,000 annually on a 10,000 square foot building. With NNN expenses passed through to the tenant, the effective NOI is close to the in-place rent, adjusted only for landlord responsibilities such as roof, structure, and management. At a conservative 3% management assumption and minimal structural reserves, net operating income sits at approximately $130,000 to $135,000 on an annualized basis.

Market rent for comparable single-tenant retail space in the relevant Erie submarket is approximately $18.00 to $20.00 per square foot NNN, representing a gap of $4.00 to $6.00 per square foot between in-place and market. On a 10,000 square foot building, that gap translates to $40,000 to $60,000 of annualized income that is not available to support debt service under the current lease. That is not a rounding error. It is the central financial fact of the dispute.

For DSCR analysis, the market standard for commercial mortgage underwriting on single-tenant NNN properties in secondary markets like Erie is 1.20x to 1.25x minimum debt service coverage, with most conventional lenders, including CMBS conduits and regional banks, applying 1.25x as their underwriting floor. Some life company lenders apply 1.30x for non-gateway markets. I applied 1.25x as the primary threshold because it represents the broad market standard for this asset class in this geography.

Assuming a five-year fixed rate mortgage at 7.25% amortized over 25 years, which is consistent with the rate environment applicable to the relevant period for a secondary market non-recourse or limited recourse loan on a single-tenant asset, the annual debt service per $1 million of loan principal is approximately $86,400. At a 1.25x DSCR requirement, each $1 million of loan capacity requires approximately $108,000 of NOI to support it. Working backward from the in-place NOI of $132,000, the maximum supportable loan at 1.25x DSCR is approximately $1,527,000.

At a reasonable market value of $1,650,000 for this asset, which reflects a cap rate of approximately 7.75% applied to the in-place NOI, the DSCR-constrained loan of $1,527,000 represents a 92.5% loan-to-value ratio. However, conventional lenders cap LTV at 65% to 70% for single-tenant retail in secondary markets. The LTV constraint therefore limits the loan to $1,073,000 to $1,155,000 on an in-place value basis, well below what the DSCR calculation alone would permit and well below what a market-rent property of the same size would support. If the in-place rent were at the market rate of $19.00 per square foot, NOI would be approximately $181,500, market value at the same 7.75% cap rate would be approximately $2,342,000, and the 65% LTV loan would be $1,522,000, representing an additional $350,000 to $450,000 of loan capacity directly attributable to the below-market lease discount.

This is the quantified financing gap that the expert analysis established: a below-market lease of $4.00 to $5.00 per square foot on a 10,000 square foot Erie building translates directly to $350,000 to $450,000 of reduced debt capacity, independent of any argument about property value. The lender community does not care about speculative future rents. It cares about the income stream that is actually under lease and documentable today.

The Complicating Factors

Rollover Upside and Lender Skepticism. The most common misconception in disputes involving below-market leases is that anticipated rollover upside to market rent increases loan sizing. It does not, except in very limited circumstances. Lenders underwrite to in-place, contractual income. They do not give credit for what the rent might be at lease expiration unless the borrower can demonstrate a specific, executable lease or letter of intent at the higher rent. For a single-tenant property with two or three years remaining on the lease, a lender looking at that situation sees rollover risk, not rollover opportunity. The tenant has leverage at renewal. The market could soften. The building may require capital investment to retain or replace the tenant. Lenders price for downside, not upside, and Erie's secondary market characteristics, including its thinner replacement tenant pool relative to larger metros, make lenders more conservative, not less, about giving credit for prospective rent increases.

Single-Tenant Concentration Risk. Single-tenant NNN properties carry idiosyncratic risk that multi-tenant properties do not. When the sole tenant vacates, income goes to zero. For Erie-area lenders evaluating a single-tenant property, the question is not just whether the current income supports the loan; it is whether the building can be re-leased or sold in a reasonable timeframe if the tenant does not renew. Buildings configured for a specific prior use, particularly quick-service restaurant, auto service, or specialty retail, may have limited alternative-use demand in the Erie market, which lenders factor into their credit assessment through lower LTV caps or higher rate spreads.

Lease Escalation Structure. Many NNN leases include fixed annual rent bumps of 1% to 2% or periodic CPI adjustments. In a dispute involving a below-market lease, the escalation structure matters because it determines how quickly, if ever, the in-place rent approaches market. A lease at $14.00 per square foot with 1.5% annual bumps requires approximately 21 years to reach $19.00 per square foot. Lenders understand this arithmetic. The below-market discount does not self-correct in any commercially meaningful timeframe, and that reality limits the financing available during the lease term without reference to rollover events.

Ground Lease or Ownership Structure Complexity. If the property involves a fee interest with any encumbrances, easements, or co-ownership issues, those further constrain the lender universe. Lenders in secondary markets like Erie already apply tighter credit standards than they would in Philadelphia or Pittsburgh. Any title or structural complexity narrows the field further and increases pricing.

The Erie Lender Market

Understanding which lenders are actually active in Erie for single-tenant NNN assets is critical to any financing dispute analysis. The lender market for this asset type in Erie divides into several categories, each with distinct credit parameters.

Community and Regional Banks. Erie has meaningful community bank depth, with several locally headquartered institutions and regional banks headquartered in western Pennsylvania that are active in commercial real estate lending. These lenders typically require recourse, apply LTV limits of 65% to 75%, expect DSCR of 1.20x to 1.25x, and price at spreads over the five-year Treasury or prime rate. They are generally more flexible on asset complexity than institutional lenders but require strong local borrower relationships and are more sensitive to Erie-specific market conditions. For a below-market lease situation, community banks will often require the borrower to demonstrate tenant credit quality and remaining lease term to offset the income deficit.

CMBS Conduit Lenders. Conduit lenders are available for single-tenant NNN assets in Erie if the loan size exceeds approximately $2 million and the tenant credit is investment grade or near-investment grade. CMBS underwriting is strictly in-place NOI based, applies 1.25x DSCR floors, and caps LTV at 65% to 70% for secondary market assets. Below-market leases are a recognized challenge in CMBS underwriting because securitization investors scrutinize rollover risk closely. A loan secured by a property with below-market in-place rents and near-term lease expiration would either require significant structure such as reserves or earn-out provisions, or would simply not qualify for the execution.

Credit Unions and SBA Lenders. For smaller loan sizes or owner-user scenarios, credit unions and SBA 504 lenders are active in Erie. SBA 504 permits up to 90% LTV on a blended basis for owner-occupied commercial real estate, but that program is not applicable to investment NNN properties. It is relevant in disputes where the question is whether the property could have supported owner-user financing as an alternative to investment financing.

Life Companies and Debt Funds. Life company lenders are generally absent from Erie for single-tenant retail assets below $5 million. Debt funds and bridge lenders are available for transitional situations, including lease rollover, at higher rates (typically 200 to 400 basis points over agency benchmarks) and shorter terms, but they do not solve the structural challenge of a below-market lease; they finance through it at a higher cost and with a defined exit strategy requirement.

What Attorneys Handling Erie CRE Disputes Need to Know

A commercial real estate financing expert is not interchangeable with a real estate appraiser, and attorneys who conflate the two will leave critical issues unaddressed. An appraiser establishes value. A financing expert establishes what the debt markets will actually do with that asset, at what leverage, at what cost, and under what conditions. In a dispute involving a below-market lease, the question of how much debt the property can support is a separate and distinct inquiry from what the property is worth, and the answers to those questions do not always move in tandem.

You should retain a CRE financing expert when the dispute involves any of the following: a claim that a property could or could not have been refinanced at a particular leverage point; an allegation that a lender's terms were commercially unreasonable; a question about whether a below-market lease precluded or enabled a specific financing outcome; a dispute about what market rate financing would have been available at a specific point in time; or a bankruptcy proceeding in which the feasibility of a plan of reorganization depends on demonstrating that the debtor can obtain exit financing.

A qualified CRE financing expert is not simply a retired banker or a mortgage broker who has done a few deals. The expert must have active, current market relationships with lenders across multiple capital sources, including banks, CMBS conduits, life companies, debt funds, and agency lenders. They must understand underwriting standards as they exist today and as they existed at the relevant historical date. And they must be able to translate lender decision-making into clear, documentable analysis that withstands cross-examination. At CLS CRE, our foundation is approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, relationships with more than 1,000 lenders nationally, and direct engagement with capital sources that are active in Pennsylvania secondary markets including Erie.

Before engaging a financing expert, counsel should gather the following: the complete loan documents for any existing or proposed financing; the as-is appraisal and any desk or retrospective appraisals; the full lease, including all amendments, estoppels, and SNDAs; the rent roll and trailing income statements for at least three years; any lender term sheets, commitment letters, or declination letters; and any correspondence between the borrower and lenders regarding financing requests. The expert cannot reconstruct what a lender would have offered without the underlying financial documentation.

At trial or in a Plan hearing, CRE financing expert testimony is most powerful when it is grounded in specific, reproducible calculations rather than general market commentary. Judges and arbitrators respond to the discipline of showing exactly how a lender would have sized the loan, what DSCR and LTV constraints would have applied, and what the dollar consequence of those constraints was for the borrower. Vague testimony about "market conditions" is not useful. Testimony that says "at a 1.25x DSCR and 65% LTV constraint, the maximum supportable loan on this property was $X, not $Y, and here is the arithmetic" is the kind of analysis that moves disputes toward resolution.

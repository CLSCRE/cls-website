Expert Witness: CRE Financing Feasibility in Chapter 11 Proceedings in Salinas, CA

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Bankruptcy Exit Financing

The Salinas Market Context

Salinas is the county seat of Monterey County and the economic anchor of the Salinas Valley, one of the most productive agricultural corridors in the world. That agricultural identity shapes the commercial real estate market in ways that matter enormously when a financing feasibility opinion is required in a bankruptcy proceeding. Industrial and flex space in Salinas carries occupancy rates that consistently outperform the broader California Central Coast average, driven by cold storage demand, food processing operations, and agricultural logistics. Vacancy in the Salinas industrial submarket has tracked in the 3% to 6% range in recent years, with triple-net lease rates for functional industrial product running between $0.85 and $1.20 per square foot per month depending on clear height, dock doors, and refrigeration capability. That is a thin supply environment, and lenders who understand the submarket recognize it.

Retail in Salinas tells a more complicated story. The city's retail corridors have experienced the same structural pressures affecting secondary California markets, including big-box consolidation, grocery-anchored center stress, and the income profile of the surrounding population, which leans working-class and agricultural. Effective gross incomes for retail product are moderate, and cap rates in the 6.5% to 7.5% range reflect both the yield premium that lenders and investors require for secondary California markets and the genuine risk of tenant rollover. Multifamily fundamentals in Salinas are somewhat more durable, supported by chronic housing undersupply and a large workforce population, with cap rates for stabilized assets running in the 5.0% to 5.75% range and average market rents for workforce product between $1,600 and $2,200 per month depending on unit mix. These are the market parameters within which a financing feasibility analysis must operate when a Chapter 11 debtor holds a Salinas commercial asset and a plan of reorganization requires a credible exit financing opinion.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by counsel representing the plan proponent in a Chapter 11 reorganization proceeding involving a commercial real estate asset located in the Salinas submarket. The asset was a mixed-use industrial and office property with a stabilized tenant roster, encumbered by a senior mortgage held by an institutional creditor whose claim was the subject of confirmation-related dispute. The core legal questions before the court were straightforward in framing but technically complex in execution: first, could the subject property support first-mortgage debt sufficient to satisfy the senior creditor's allowed secured claim through a refinance executed at or near plan effective date; and second, what categories of lenders actively originate loans on assets held within a bankruptcy estate or exiting Chapter 11, and on what terms.

Counsel needed more than an appraisal. The appraiser had already rendered a value opinion. What was required was a capital markets expert who could translate that value into a lender's actual underwriting calculus, identify the realistic population of lenders who would consider the deal given the bankruptcy overlay, and explain in specific terms why certain lender categories would decline and others would engage. CLS CRE was engaged to provide a written expert report and to be available for deposition and, if necessary, testimony at the plan confirmation hearing. The engagement required us to analyze the property's income stream, model supportable debt under current market underwriting standards, document the lender market, and render a professional opinion on financing feasibility that could withstand cross-examination.

The Financial Analysis

The methodology I applied follows the standard underwriting framework used by institutional and regional bank lenders across the commercial mortgage market. Beginning with income, I analyzed the property's trailing 12-month rent roll, contractual lease terms, and vacancy history. For the industrial portion of the building, in-place rents were at or slightly below current market, providing minimal mark-to-market downside. The office component carried above-market in-place rents on leases within 18 months of expiration, which required a conservative adjustment in the stabilized income calculation. After adjusting for market vacancy at a 5% stabilization assumption consistent with Salinas industrial submarket norms, applying a 3% credit loss reserve, and deducting verified operating expenses including property taxes, insurance, and a capital reserve of $0.15 per square foot per year, I arrived at a stabilized net operating income of approximately $310,000 annually.

From that NOI figure, I applied the DSCR thresholds that govern conventional commercial mortgage underwriting. The market standard for permanent first-mortgage financing on stabilized commercial assets is a minimum debt service coverage ratio of 1.25x on an annual basis, with many institutional lenders requiring 1.30x for assets carrying any lease rollover risk within the loan term. I used 1.25x as the floor because the industrial component provided durable, near-term cash flow sufficient to meet that threshold without requiring an aggressive stabilization assumption. At current market pricing for permanent commercial loans in the Salinas market, which I benchmarked at a fixed rate of approximately 7.25% on a 25-year amortization schedule reflecting a 10-year term, the maximum annual debt service supportable by $310,000 NOI at 1.25x DSCR is $248,000. That debt service load corresponds to a maximum loan balance of approximately $2,910,000.

The appraised value on record was $4,400,000. A $2,910,000 loan against that value represents a loan-to-value ratio of approximately 66.1%, which falls within the standard LTV ceiling of 65% to 70% that most conventional lenders apply to mixed-use industrial and office assets in secondary California markets. The analysis therefore demonstrated two things simultaneously: the income stream supports a loan of approximately $2.9 million under standard underwriting, and that loan amount clears the LTV ceiling at the appraised value. Both tests must pass independently. In this case, income coverage was the binding constraint, not LTV, which is the more common result in California secondary markets where appraised values have appreciated faster than rents. The supportable loan exceeded the senior creditor's allowed secured claim, which is the operative legal question in a plan confirmation context. That conclusion formed the core of my expert opinion on financing feasibility.

The Complicating Factors

Bankruptcy Taint and Lender Perception. The most significant complicating factor in any bankruptcy exit financing analysis is the asset's provenance. Most conventional lenders, including the majority of regional banks and credit unions, will not originate a loan directly into a bankruptcy estate. The execution risk is too high, the approval timeline is incompatible with plan effective date deadlines, and the legal costs of navigating a bankruptcy court proceeding are not reflected in their standard origination economics. This does not mean the asset is unlendable. It means the universe of lenders who will engage is narrower, and the pricing will carry a risk premium relative to a clean-title refinance. Bridge lenders, debt funds, and certain CMBS conduits have developed the operational capacity to underwrite and close loans on bankruptcy exit transactions, but their rate structures, loan-to-value parameters, and fee expectations differ meaningfully from permanent financing. Counsel must understand this distinction because it affects both the feasibility conclusion and the cost of exit financing that will be reflected in the plan's economic projections.

Lease Rollover and Re-Tenanting Risk. In the Salinas market, industrial tenants tend to have longer effective tenancies than their lease terms suggest because relocation alternatives are limited and build-to-suit product is expensive. However, office tenants in Salinas have demonstrated a higher propensity to downsize or exit, and a mixed-use asset with material office exposure requires careful underwriting of rollover risk. Lenders will haircut NOI for any lease expiring within the loan term where a renewal is not executed. In a bankruptcy context, tenant relationships may have been disrupted by the proceedings themselves, and I verified lease status directly from the rent roll and court filings rather than relying on borrower representations. Any lease that was modified, assumed, or rejected during the Chapter 11 must be disclosed to lenders and reflected accurately in the income analysis.

Title and Lien Complexity. Assets emerging from Chapter 11 frequently carry recorded mechanics' liens, subordinate trust deeds, or judgment liens that were not fully resolved by the reorganization plan. First-mortgage lenders require a clean first-lien position at closing. The confirmation order typically vests title in the reorganized debtor free and clear of pre-confirmation claims, but lenders need to see that order, an ALTA title commitment showing a clear chain of title, and in many cases a formal opinion from bankruptcy counsel confirming the lien-stripping effect of the plan. The absence of this documentation at the time a loan application is submitted will cause most lenders to decline or hold the file. I flagged this as a pre-closing condition in my expert report and identified the specific documentation that lenders would require.

Borrower Entity and Guaranty Structure. The reorganized debtor entity may emerge from Chapter 11 with a restructured ownership structure, new management, or altered guaranty obligations. Conventional lenders underwrite not just the asset but the borrower. A newly reconstituted LLC with no track record, no liquidity verification, and principals who are recently discharged debtors will not meet the sponsorship criteria of most permanent lenders. Bridge lenders and debt funds are generally more asset-focused and less sponsorship-focused, but they still require that the borrower entity be legally constituted, properly authorized, and capable of executing loan documents. The plan's treatment of equity and the identity of the reorganized debtor's principals must be confirmed before a financing feasibility opinion can be rendered with confidence.

The Salinas Lender Market

Community and Regional Banks. Salinas is served by a meaningful community bank presence. Several banks with significant agricultural lending operations in Monterey County also maintain commercial real estate lending programs for stabilized industrial and retail assets. These lenders know the Salinas submarket, understand the agricultural tenant base, and have historically provided competitive permanent financing for well-positioned assets. Their credit boxes typically require DSCR of 1.25x or better, LTV at or below 65%, and borrower sponsorship with verifiable net worth and liquidity. For a clean post-confirmation asset with resolved title and a reconstituted borrower entity that meets their standards, community bank pricing in this market currently runs in the 7.00% to 7.50% range for 5-year fixed terms with 25-year amortization. The challenge in a bankruptcy exit context is their approval process, which commonly runs 60 to 90 days and requires committee approval that is difficult to compress into plan confirmation timelines.

Debt Funds and Bridge Lenders. This is the most realistic lender category for a bankruptcy exit transaction in Salinas. Non-bank debt funds with commercial real estate mandates have the operational flexibility to close on compressed timelines, accept borrower entities that are newly constituted, and work with title companies experienced in post-confirmation closings. Their pricing reflects the execution premium: rates in the current market run 9.00% to 11.50% depending on asset quality, loan term, and leverage, with origination fees of 1% to 2%. LTV is typically capped at 65% for stabilized assets in secondary markets like Salinas. These lenders are explicitly asset-focused; if the NOI supports the debt service and the LTV is clean, sponsorship requirements are more flexible than at a bank. Bridge loans in this context typically carry 12- to 36-month terms with extension options, with the expectation that the borrower refinances into permanent financing once the bankruptcy taint has been seasoned out of the title history.

CMBS Conduit Lenders. Certain CMBS conduit programs will originate on assets exiting Chapter 11, particularly where the loan size exceeds $3 million and the property has institutional-quality tenancy. CMBS pricing in the current market runs in the 7.25% to 8.00% range for 10-year fixed paper, and the execution timeline is typically 45 to 75 days after loan application. The legal complexity of a post-confirmation closing adds cost and time, but conduit lenders with bankruptcy experience have developed closing checklists that address the title and entity documentation requirements. CMBS is generally not the first call for a Salinas asset at the size range involved in this engagement, but it is a legitimate market option for larger assets and should be documented as part of a comprehensive feasibility analysis.

What Attorneys Handling Salinas CRE Disputes Need to Know

Retaining a commercial real estate financing expert is not the same as retaining an appraiser, and the distinction matters in a plan confirmation proceeding. An appraiser renders an opinion of value. A CRE financing expert renders an opinion of capital markets feasibility: given this value, this income stream, and this lender market, can a loan actually be placed, in what amount, on what terms, and within what timeline. Courts in Chapter 11 confirmation proceedings need both opinions, but they are asking different questions. The appraiser tells the court what the asset is worth. The financing expert tells the court whether the plan's proposed refinance is executable in the real market.

Counsel should retain a CRE financing expert when the plan proposes to satisfy creditors through a refinance or asset sale financed by a new loan, when a creditor challenges the plan's feasibility on the grounds that financing cannot be obtained, or when the cramdown analysis requires establishing that the interest rate proposed under the plan reflects market terms. These are capital markets questions, not valuation questions, and they require someone who currently operates in the commercial mortgage market rather than someone who retired from a bank a decade ago. The expert must have current, active lender relationships and a demonstrable track record of originating loans in the asset class and market at issue. At CLS CRE, our approximately $1 billion in transaction volume and relationships with more than 1,000 lenders across 50 states provide the market depth necessary to support that testimony.

Before engaging the expert, counsel should gather the following: the current rent roll with lease terms and expiration dates; trailing 12 months of operating income and expense statements; the most recent property tax bills and insurance schedules; the appraisal if completed; the confirmation order or proposed plan language describing the exit financing; the title commitment or preliminary title report; and any documentation regarding the post-confirmation borrower entity. The more complete this package, the faster the expert can render a defensible opinion. Gaps in documentation do not prevent an opinion from being rendered, but they must be disclosed and addressed in the report.

At trial or at a plan confirmation hearing, the CRE financing expert testifies to methodology and market facts. The expert explains how lenders underwrite the asset class, what the relevant market standards are for DSCR and LTV, which lender categories are active in the market, what their pricing and terms look like, and why the proposed financing is or is not feasible. This testimony directly addresses the good-faith feasibility standard under Section 1129(a)(11) of the Bankruptcy Code, which requires the court to find that the plan is not likely to be followed by liquidation or further financial reorganization. A well-supported financing feasibility opinion is often the most persuasive evidence on that element.

Key Takeaways

  • Salinas industrial and mixed-use assets carry durable fundamentals driven by agricultural logistics demand, thin vacancy, and limited new supply, making them more financeable in bankruptcy exit scenarios than retail or office-heavy assets in comparable secondary markets.
  • The income-to-debt test is the binding constraint in most Salinas commercial mortgage underwriting, not LTV. A 1.25x DSCR floor applied against stabilized NOI determines the maximum supportable loan, and that calculation must be performed correctly before any feasibility conclusion can be rendered.
  • Conventional permanent lenders cannot execute on bankruptcy exit timelines. Bridge lenders and debt funds are the realistic primary lender market for a post-confirmation refinance, with permanent bank financing available as a takeout after title seasoning.
  • Title resolution is a non-negotiable prerequisite. A clean first-lien position confirmed by a title commitment and supported by bankruptcy counsel's lien-stripping opinion is required before any lender will proceed to underwriting on a post-confirmation asset.
  • Lease integrity must be verified from primary sources. In a bankruptcy context, assumed, modified, or rejected leases affect the income analysis materially, and reliance on borrower representations without verification from court filings and the rent roll creates an indefensible expert opinion.
  • An appraiser's value opinion is necessary but not sufficient for plan feasibility analysis. Counsel needs a CRE financing expert to translate value into a lender's underwriting calculus, document the active lender market, and render a capital markets feasibility opinion that addresses the Bankruptcy Code Section 1129(a)(11) standard.
  • The expert's current market activity is dispositive. Expert testimony on lender market conditions is only credible if the expert is actively originating loans in the asset class at issue. A retired banker's recollections about what lenders used to require are not a substitute for current market participation.
  • Early engagement produces better outcomes. Retaining the financing expert before the disclosure statement is filed

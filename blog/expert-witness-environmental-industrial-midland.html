Expert Witness: Financing a Contaminated Industrial Property in Midland, TX

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Environmental Contamination Analysis

The Midland Market Context

Midland, Texas sits at the center of the Permian Basin, and that single fact explains virtually everything about its commercial real estate fundamentals. The market does not behave like Dallas, Houston, or Austin. It behaves like an energy economy with a real estate market attached to it. When West Texas Intermediate crude trades above $70 per barrel, industrial vacancy tightens, rents accelerate, and capital flows into the market with conviction. When prices soften, the same dynamics reverse, sometimes sharply. Any credible financing analysis for a Midland industrial asset must begin by acknowledging this cyclicality, because lenders who are active here understand it, and lenders who are not active here will price it as opaque risk.

On the industrial side specifically, Midland's market is driven by oilfield services companies, equipment staging operations, pipe yards, chemical storage, and logistics providers supporting Permian Basin drilling and completion activity. Functional industrial product in Midland and the adjacent Odessa submarket commands strong occupancy, with vacancy rates for bulk warehouse and flex industrial space historically running in the 4% to 8% range during active drilling cycles. Triple-net leases are common in this sector, particularly for single-tenant buildings occupied by oilfield services tenants or distribution users. Asking rents for industrial product have ranged from approximately $8.00 to $14.00 per square foot NNN depending on clear height, dock configuration, and power availability, with the upper end of that range reflecting newer construction or specialized facilities.

What makes Midland challenging for institutional capital is the thin transaction volume relative to major metros. The market does not generate the density of comparable sales and lease transactions that underwriters in New York or Los Angeles rely upon. Appraisals in Midland can rest on limited comparable sets, and lenders unfamiliar with the market will apply additional yield premium to compensate for what they characterize as liquidity risk. This is not irrational. An industrial property in Midland facing a forced disposition in a down cycle faces a meaningfully thinner buyer pool than the same property in the Dallas Metroplex. That reality directly affects maximum supportable loan amounts, and it is a central issue in any financing feasibility analysis conducted for litigation purposes.

The Engagement

Counsel representing a group of secured creditors in a regulatory enforcement proceeding retained Commercial Lending Solutions to provide a financing feasibility opinion on a single-tenant industrial warehouse property located in the Midland submarket. The property was subject to an ongoing environmental enforcement action involving 1,4-dioxane and chlorinated volatile organic compound contamination attributed to prior industrial operations on the site. The current owner had leased the property to a single tenant under a long-term triple-net structure. The lease was performing, but the contract rent reflected below-market terms negotiated at lease execution during a softer cycle.

The secured creditors needed to understand two things with precision. First, whether institutional first-mortgage financing was available for a property carrying active environmental contamination and an open regulatory file, or whether that condition categorically precluded any commercial mortgage lending. Second, if financing was available, what was the maximum loan amount supportable under current market underwriting standards, and was that amount sufficient to retire the existing senior debt position? The answer to the second question depended heavily on how lenders would treat the below-market rent in their underwriting, whether they would credit any mark-to-market upside, and what loan-to-value and debt service coverage constraints would apply given the environmental complexity.

CLS CRE was retained specifically because the questions at the center of the dispute required active market knowledge, not historical data. An appraisal can establish value. A financing feasibility opinion establishes whether a lender will actually write a check, at what terms, and under what conditions. Those are distinct questions, and conflating them is a common error in commercial real estate litigation.

The Financial Analysis

The income analysis began with the in-place lease. The property was 100% occupied under a triple-net lease, meaning the tenant was responsible for property taxes, insurance, and maintenance. The landlord's only obligation was to service the debt and manage the asset. The in-place base rent was $9.50 per square foot annually on a 42,000 square foot building, producing gross contract rent of approximately $399,000 per year. Under a pure NNN structure, that number closely approximates net operating income, subject only to minor management fee assumptions. We applied a 2% management fee allowance, consistent with market practice for single-tenant NNN assets, producing a stabilized NOI of approximately $391,000.

The complication was that current market rents for comparable industrial product in Midland were running in the $12.00 to $13.50 per square foot range at the time of our analysis. The in-place rent was therefore approximately 25% to 30% below market. Under standard commercial mortgage underwriting, lenders will not credit mark-to-market rent upside that has not been contractually realized. The loan is underwritten to the in-place income stream, period. Any suggestion that a lender would gross up NOI to reflect market rents on a below-market lease is inconsistent with how institutional and regional bank credit committees actually operate.

With a stabilized NOI of $391,000, we modeled the financing constraint analysis using two binding underwriting parameters: debt service coverage ratio and loan-to-value. For industrial assets in secondary markets with complicating factors, lenders apply a minimum DSCR of 1.25x, and in our opinion the environmental condition would push most credit committees to require 1.30x or higher. We applied 1.30x as our primary case and 1.25x as a secondary sensitivity. Using a 7.25% note rate, which reflected market pricing for 5-year fixed, 25-year amortization loans on secondary-market industrial product with environmental complexity at the time of our analysis, and a 25-year amortization schedule, the annual debt service constant was approximately 8.52% of loan balance.

Working backward from the DSCR constraint: maximum allowable annual debt service equals NOI divided by the required coverage ratio. At 1.30x, maximum debt service equals $391,000 divided by 1.30, or $300,769. Dividing that annual debt service figure by the debt service constant of 8.52% produces a maximum supportable loan of approximately $3,530,000. At the 1.25x threshold, maximum debt service equals $312,800, supporting a loan of approximately $3,671,000. These figures represent the income-constrained ceiling on financing, independent of value.

The LTV constraint operates independently. For single-tenant industrial product in Midland with an open environmental file, lenders we surveyed indicated maximum LTV of 55% to 65%, with most credit committees clustering around 60%. If appraised value came in at $5,500,000, the 60% LTV ceiling would cap the loan at $3,300,000, which is the binding constraint relative to the income-derived figure. If appraised value was $6,200,000, the LTV ceiling at 60% produces $3,720,000, which is slightly above the income-constrained ceiling, meaning the DSCR constraint becomes binding. In practical terms, the maximum supportable first mortgage in our base case analysis ranged from $3,300,000 to $3,670,000 depending on which constraint was binding, with the precise answer requiring a completed appraisal using a lender-approved appraiser familiar with the Midland submarket.

The Complicating Factors

Active Environmental Contamination and Open Regulatory File

This is the central obstacle. Most conventional lenders, including community banks, regional banks, life companies, and CMBS conduits, will not originate a first-mortgage loan on a property with an active Phase II environmental finding and an open state or federal regulatory enforcement file. The issue is not simply that contamination exists. The issue is that the lender's collateral is encumbered by an indeterminate remediation liability that could, in a foreclosure scenario, transfer to the lender. Under CERCLA and Texas state environmental statutes, a secured lender who acquires title through foreclosure and participates in management of a contaminated property faces potential liability exposure. Most institutional credit policies prohibit originating into that exposure in the first instance.

The category of lenders that will consider financing contaminated properties is narrower: certain private credit funds with environmental risk appetite, hard money and bridge lenders, and occasionally community development lenders with specific program mandates. These lenders will require, at minimum, a current Phase II ESA, a remediation cost estimate from a licensed environmental engineer, evidence that the remediation is either funded, bonded, or underway, and in many cases an environmental escrow or carve-out indemnification from a creditworthy guarantor. The cost of capital from this lender category is materially higher, typically 300 to 500 basis points above conventional rates, which directly compresses the income-supportable loan amount.

Below-Market Rent and Lease Term Remaining

The below-market rent structure creates a compounding problem. The income available to service debt is constrained by the contract rent, not the market rent. A lender who might otherwise consider a higher loan amount based on market rent potential cannot use that potential in underwriting because the tenant has a contractual right to occupy at the below-market rate for the remaining lease term. If the lease has a remaining term of five years or more, the lender is effectively locked into the below-market income stream for the entire initial loan period. Lenders will not speculate on renewal terms or rent resets that have not been negotiated.

Single-Tenant Concentration Risk

A 100% single-tenant property in Midland, Texas carries tenant concentration risk that lenders price explicitly. If the tenant vacates, the property generates zero income. In a market where industrial demand is tied to Permian Basin drilling activity, tenant covenant strength matters enormously. If the tenant is an oilfield services company, its own credit is correlated with commodity prices. Lenders underwriting this type of asset will require a credit analysis of the tenant, and a tenant without audited financials or with a leveraged balance sheet will trigger additional reserves or structure requirements.

Midland Market Liquidity

As described above, forced-sale liquidity in Midland is thinner than in primary markets. Lenders incorporating a stressed exit scenario into their underwriting will apply an additional discount to appraised value. A property that appraises at $5,500,000 in a going-concern scenario might generate $4,000,000 to $4,400,000 in a 12-month liquidation. That stressed value, not the appraised value, is what drives the conservative lender's effective LTV ceiling.

The Midland Lender Market

For a conventional industrial property in Midland without environmental complications, the lending universe includes regional Texas banks with energy-market experience, local community banks with strong Permian Basin relationships, USDA Business and Industry program lenders for qualifying assets, SBA 504 for owner-occupied product, and select national bank platforms with Texas commercial real estate desks. Life insurance company lending is available for larger, stabilized assets, generally $5,000,000 and above, with strong tenancy. CMBS conduit lending is available but requires property-level cash flow that supports the subordinate B-piece buyer's required returns, and secondary market industrial in Midland is not a preferred CMBS asset class.

For the specific scenario described here, the practical lending universe contracts significantly. The environmental condition eliminates most conventional bank lenders, life companies, and CMBS. The viable lender categories are: private bridge lenders and debt funds with environmental risk appetite, select bank lenders who will condition closing on a funded remediation escrow and environmental indemnification, and in certain circumstances, SBA-backed structures if the borrower is an operating company reusing the site. Bridge lenders in this space will price at 10% to 13% interest rates, require first lien position, and impose a 12 to 24-month term with extension options tied to remediation milestones. These terms are real market terms, not hypothetical, and they directly affect the maximum loan amount a distressed property can support.

One lender category that deserves specific mention for Midland is the regional Texas bank with dedicated environmental lending protocols. Several institutions operating in the Permian Basin have developed credit policies that accommodate contaminated collateral, primarily because their loan portfolios inevitably encounter properties with environmental history given the industrial nature of the regional economy. These lenders will not advertise environmental lending capability, but they will engage on a case-by-case basis when the remediation path is clear, the cost estimate is credible, and the borrower has the liquidity to fund cleanup without impairing debt service.

What Attorneys Handling Midland CRE Disputes Need to Know

The threshold question attorneys ask is whether they need a CRE financing expert at all, or whether the appraiser's value conclusion is sufficient for their purposes. The answer depends on what the dispute actually turns on. If the question is value, the appraiser owns that analysis. If the question is whether a specific loan amount was achievable, whether a refinancing was feasible, whether a debtor could have obtained exit financing, or whether a lender's credit decision was consistent with market standards, those are financing questions, not valuation questions. An appraiser is not trained to answer them, and in my experience reviewing appraisals submitted in CRE litigation, appraisers who attempt to opine on financing feasibility frequently apply standards that do not reflect how lenders actually underwrite.

A qualified CRE financing expert is someone with active, current market engagement in commercial mortgage origination or brokerage, with demonstrated access to the lender market and documented transaction volume. A retired banker who closed loans a decade ago cannot reliably testify about what today's DSCR thresholds, rate environments, or environmental lending protocols look like. The market has changed materially since 2022 alone. The expert's credibility depends on being able to say, with specificity, which lender categories are active, at what pricing, and on what terms, for this asset type, in this market, in this condition, right now.

Before retaining a CRE financing expert, counsel should gather the following: the current rent roll with lease abstracts, all environmental reports including Phase I and Phase II ESAs and any remediation cost estimates, the existing loan documents and current payoff statement, the most recent appraisal, any prior financing history on the asset, and any lender correspondence or term sheets that were issued or declined. This documentation allows the expert to form opinions grounded in the actual asset, not hypothetical assumptions that opposing counsel will attack on cross-examination.

In a Plan of Reorganization hearing, CRE financing expert testimony is used to establish whether the debtor's proposed exit financing is feasible, whether the secured creditor is adequately protected by the value of the collateral net of financing constraints, and whether the plan's assumptions about refinancing are consistent with market reality. In a lender liability or breach of commitment dispute, the expert can opine on whether the lender's underwriting decision fell within or outside the range of reasonable market practice. In an environmental enforcement dispute like the one described here, the financing expert's role is to establish what the contamination condition actually does to the financing universe and the maximum achievable loan, which is the cornerstone of any argument about economic impact on the collateral.

Key Takeaways

  • Environmental contamination does not categorically eliminate first-mortgage financing, but it dramatically narrows the lending universe and materially increases the cost of capital, with direct consequences for maximum supportable loan amounts.
  • In-place below-market rent is the operative income for underwriting purposes. No institutional lender will gross up NOI to reflect market rent potential on a lease that has not yet reset. The income-constrained loan ceiling is calculated from contract rent, period.
  • For contaminated industrial assets, conventional bank and life company lenders are generally unavailable. The viable lender universe consists of private bridge lenders, debt funds with environmental risk appetite, and select regional banks with documented environmental lending protocols, all of which price at materially higher rates.
  • The DSCR constraint and LTV constraint operate independently and are both binding. The maximum supportable loan is the lower of the two calculations, and for secondary-market contaminated assets, lenders typically apply 55% to 60% LTV and 1.25x to 1.30x DSCR simultaneously.
  • Midland's commodity-driven market cycle creates liquidity risk that lenders price into underwriting. Thin comparable transaction volume and forced-sale exposure in a down cycle justify LTV discounts that would not apply in primary markets, and any financing analysis that ignores this is incomplete.
  • A CRE financing expert provides opinions that an appraiser cannot. The questions of whether financing is available, at what terms, and from which lenders require active market engagement and current origination knowledge, not retrospective valuation methodology.

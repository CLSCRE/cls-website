Expert Witness: How Construction Defects Affect CRE Financing in Fredericksburg, VA

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Construction Defect Impact on Financing

The Fredericksburg Market Context

Fredericksburg, Virginia sits at the intersection of two powerful demand drivers that make it a genuinely interesting commercial real estate market: its position along the I-95 corridor between Washington, D.C. and Richmond, and its sustained population growth fueled by Northern Virginia spillover. The city proper and the surrounding Spotsylvania, Stafford, and King George counties have added residents at a pace that consistently outstrips the regional average, and that demographic pressure has translation effects across every major commercial property type. For attorneys and investors operating in this market, that growth story also means the litigation stakes are real. Commercial projects here carry meaningful value, and financing disputes tied to construction defects are not theoretical exercises.

On the multifamily side, which is directly relevant to construction defect disputes given the volume of residential-over-retail and market-rate apartment development in the Fredericksburg submarket, cap rates have generally traded in the 5.25% to 6.25% range for stabilized assets, with value-add deals pricing wider depending on vintage and deferred maintenance exposure. Vacancy in professionally managed multifamily product has remained below 6% across most of the submarket, supported by household formation that continues to outpace deliveries. That tight vacancy creates a situation where a defect-impaired asset can look superficially healthy on occupancy while carrying a materially different risk profile than an equivalent defect-free property.

On the industrial and flex side, the Route 1 and Interstate 95 corridors in Stafford and Spotsylvania have attracted last-mile logistics and light industrial tenants, with functional vacancy in modern Class A industrial product sitting below 5%. Rents for new Class B flex space in the Fredericksburg submarket have ranged from approximately $10 to $14 per square foot NNN, depending on clear height, loading configuration, and proximity to highway interchanges. Retail is more nuanced: strip centers anchored by essential services have maintained occupancy, while secondary retail formats face the same structural headwinds present nationally. In all of these contexts, a construction defect that impairs the physical or functional condition of the asset creates financing complications that go well beyond what a standard appraisal captures.

The Engagement

In this matter, our firm, Commercial Lending Solutions, was retained by litigation counsel representing a party in a construction defect dispute involving a mid-size multifamily property in the Fredericksburg submarket. The asset was a recently completed market-rate apartment community, and the alleged defects involved envelope failures, including waterproofing deficiencies and improper window flashing, along with HVAC system deficiencies across a meaningful percentage of the units. The defects had been documented through inspection reports, and remediation cost estimates had been developed by a construction expert retained separately.

Retaining counsel asked CLS CRE to address a specific set of financing-related questions that fell outside the scope of both the appraisal and the construction expert testimony. First, could the property, in its current defect-impaired condition, qualify for conventional commercial real estate financing? Second, if financing was technically achievable, how did the defects affect the maximum supportable loan amount compared to an equivalent defect-free asset? Third, what conditions would lenders impose before funding on a property with known construction defects, and what did those conditions mean for the borrower's cost of capital? Fourth, how did the defect remediation cost interact with the property's loan-to-value and debt service coverage ratio constraints? These questions sit squarely within the scope of a commercial mortgage financing expert, not an appraiser, and not a retired banker offering general impressions.

Our analysis drew on our firm's active lender relationships across more than 1,000 institutions, our approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, and our current placement activity across 50 states, which gives us a live, not historical, read on where lenders are willing to engage and at what terms on defect-impaired assets.

The Financial Analysis

The foundation of any CRE financing analysis is the property's net operating income and what that income supports in debt service. For the subject asset, we analyzed the trailing twelve-month operating statements, the rent roll, and the proforma prepared by the borrower at the time of original stabilization. We then applied a lender-level underwriting adjustment to NOI rather than accepting face-value income figures, because lenders underwriting a defect-impaired property will apply independent adjustments that a borrower's proforma does not reflect.

The subject property generated a gross potential rent of approximately $2.1 million annually at stabilization. After vacancy and credit loss, effective gross income was approximately $2.0 million. Operating expenses, including management, insurance, taxes, maintenance, and reserves, totaled approximately $780,000 before any defect-related adjustments. That produced an unadjusted NOI of approximately $1.22 million.

However, a lender underwriting this asset in its defect-impaired condition would not use that NOI figure. Lenders performing their own underwriting on a property with known envelope defects will increase the replacement reserve line item to reflect the elevated capital expenditure risk, will gross up the maintenance and repair line to reflect ongoing defect-related costs prior to remediation, and may apply a vacancy haircut reflecting the reputational and habitability risk that active defect litigation introduces. In our analysis, a lender-adjusted NOI for this asset in defect-impaired condition was approximately $960,000, representing a reduction of approximately $260,000 from the unadjusted figure.

With a lender-adjusted NOI of $960,000, and applying the conventional agency and bank standard minimum DSCR of 1.25x for a multifamily asset of this type and vintage in the Fredericksburg market, the maximum supportable annual debt service is $768,000. At a 7.25% interest rate on a 30-year amortization schedule, the standard term structure for Freddie Mac SBL and comparable bank financing on stabilized multifamily in this size range, the maximum supportable loan from a debt service perspective is approximately $10.05 million. If we applied the more aggressive 1.20x DSCR available from certain portfolio lenders, maximum supportable debt service rises to $800,000, supporting a loan of approximately $10.46 million. These are the income-constrained loan ceilings.

Now we apply the LTV constraint. Agency and conventional bank lenders on stabilized multifamily in the Fredericksburg submarket will typically lend to 75% to 80% LTV on a defect-free asset. However, for a property with active construction defect litigation and an unresolved remediation cost, lenders will reduce that ceiling. Based on our current lender engagement experience, the realistic LTV ceiling for a defect-impaired multifamily asset in active litigation is 60% to 65% of the as-is, defect-impaired appraised value, not the stabilized value. If the defect-free stabilized value of this asset was approximately $16.5 million and the appraised value reflecting defect impairment was approximately $14.2 million, then 65% LTV produces a maximum loan of approximately $9.23 million. The binding constraint in this analysis is the LTV cap, not the DSCR, producing a maximum supportable loan of approximately $9.23 million against what would have been an approximately $12.4 million loan on the same asset without defects (80% LTV at $15.5 million defect-free appraised value, which itself reflects a modest discount from the full stabilized value). That is a financing gap of approximately $3.2 million, directly attributable to the defects, before any consideration of rate premium or lender fees.

The Complicating Factors

Active litigation as a title and lender condition issue. The most significant structural complication for financing a defect-impaired property is not the defects themselves, it is the litigation. Virtually all institutional lenders, including Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, CMBS platforms, and the regional banks active in Fredericksburg, include representations in their loan documents requiring disclosure of material pending litigation. When construction defect litigation is active, lenders face a downstream risk that a judgment or settlement could result in a lien against the property, attach to the title, or create a liability that impairs the collateral. The practical result is that most agency lenders will decline to fund until litigation is resolved. Regional banks with portfolio flexibility may fund into litigation, but they will require escrows, holdbacks, or letter of credit structures that increase the effective cost of capital materially.

Remediation cost as an escrow and holdback requirement. Even when a lender is willing to engage on a defect-impaired asset, they will require that remediation costs be fully funded at closing. The form of that requirement varies: some lenders will accept a funded escrow equal to 125% of the contractor's remediation estimate; others will require a letter of credit. In either case, the capital the borrower must bring to closing increases by the full remediation cost plus the lender's required contingency, which reduces equity returns and may create a scenario where the loan is not economically viable at the leverage point the borrower requires.

Insurance complications. Lenders require property insurance as a loan condition, and they require that the insurer be aware of all material known conditions. A property with unresolved construction defects that has active water intrusion or HVAC deficiencies may face coverage exclusions, higher premiums, or endorsement requirements that increase operating expenses and, by extension, reduce lender-adjusted NOI. In our engagement, we identified this as a secondary NOI drag beyond the direct defect-related expense adjustments.

DSCR sensitivity to remediation timeline. When remediation work is ongoing, the property may experience temporary occupancy disruption, unit downtime, or lease-up delays. Lenders underwriting during an active remediation period will apply a higher vacancy assumption, which further suppresses the underwritten NOI and reduces the maximum supportable loan during the remediation window. This creates a chicken-and-egg problem: the borrower needs financing to fund remediation, but the act of remediating reduces near-term NOI and therefore reduces available financing during the period of peak capital need.

The Fredericksburg Lender Market

Fredericksburg benefits from a reasonably well-developed regional lending infrastructure, but the lender universe for a defect-impaired asset in active litigation is substantially narrower than for a clean stabilized property. Here is how the categories break down in practice.

Agency lenders (Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac multifamily). These are the dominant execution for stabilized multifamily in Fredericksburg, offering the lowest rates and highest leverage on clean assets. For a defect-impaired asset with active litigation, agency execution is generally not available until litigation resolves and a clean title search can be delivered. Fannie and Freddie require representations that there is no pending litigation that could materially affect the collateral. An asset in active construction defect litigation does not satisfy that requirement.

Community and regional banks. Fredericksburg and the surrounding counties have a solid presence of community banks and mid-size regional lenders, including institutions headquartered in Northern Virginia and Richmond that maintain active commercial lending operations in the I-95 corridor. These portfolio lenders have the most flexibility to structure around defect and litigation risk, because they hold loans on balance sheet and can negotiate individual credit structures. They will price at a premium to agency, typically 50 to 150 basis points wider, and they will require the remediation escrow and litigation holdback structures described above. Their LTV ceilings on impaired assets are typically 60% to 65%. Their DSCR requirements are 1.25x minimum, sometimes 1.30x on assets with elevated risk profiles.

Debt funds and bridge lenders. For a borrower who needs to refinance out of a construction loan while defects are unresolved, a bridge lender or debt fund may provide short-term financing at higher cost to buy time for litigation resolution and remediation. These lenders are active in the Virginia market and will engage on complex collateral situations. Rates are typically SOFR plus 350 to 500 basis points, with 1 to 2 year terms and extension options tied to milestones including litigation resolution. The cost of this capital relative to long-term agency financing represents a quantifiable damages component in a construction defect dispute.

CMBS. CMBS execution is not well-suited to defect-impaired or litigated assets. CMBS lenders require clean title and will not fund into active litigation. Additionally, CMBS underwriting lacks the flexibility for individual credit accommodations that characterize portfolio lending. For a Fredericksburg multifamily asset in this situation, CMBS is effectively off the table until the legal cloud is cleared.

What Attorneys Handling Fredericksburg CRE Disputes Need to Know

The first and most important point for litigation counsel is that a real estate appraiser and a CRE financing expert are not interchangeable. An appraiser determines value. A CRE financing expert determines what a real-world lender will do with that value, at what cost, on what terms, and under what conditions. In a construction defect case, the damages question is frequently not just what the property is worth in the abstract, but what the owner could have borrowed, at what rate, at what leverage, compared to what they actually could borrow on the defect-impaired collateral. That delta is a financing expert question, not an appraisal question.

Attorneys should retain a CRE financing expert early, not at the eve of expert disclosure deadlines. The reason is that the financing analysis informs the damages model, and the damages model shapes discovery. Understanding what a lender would have required on this asset, what the borrower's cost of capital differential is, and what the financing gap is will drive document requests related to loan applications, lender correspondence, term sheets, and credit committee communications that are essential to building the financing damages case.

A qualified CRE financing expert is not simply a retired banker with general lending experience. The relevant qualifications are active, current engagement in the commercial mortgage market, current knowledge of lender credit standards and product availability for the specific asset type and geography, documented transaction volume demonstrating live market engagement, and the ability to explain complex financing mechanics clearly to a trier of fact. At CLS CRE, we bring approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, more than 1,000 active lender relationships across institutional, agency, bank, and alternative capital sources, and current placement activity in 50 states, which allows us to speak with authority about what the real-world lender market looks like right now, not what it looked like when we were last employed at a bank.

Before engaging a CRE financing expert, counsel should gather the following materials: the original construction loan documents and any amendments; all appraisals of the property, including pre-construction, construction phase, and post-completion reports; any lender correspondence reflecting credit concerns, waiver requests, or maturity extension negotiations; the current rent roll and trailing operating statements; the remediation cost estimates prepared by the construction expert; and any term sheets or denial letters from lenders the borrower has approached since the defects were identified. These documents provide the factual foundation for the financing analysis and allow the expert to ground opinions in the specific facts of the matter rather than generic market commentary.

At trial or in an arbitration proceeding, CRE financing expert testimony is most effective when it connects the physical condition of the property directly to the lender's credit decision and quantifies the financial consequence of that connection. The expert's role is to translate lender behavior, which is often documented only in credit memos and internal communications, into a clear causal chain: the defects impaired the collateral, the impaired collateral changed the lender's LTV and DSCR analysis, the changed analysis reduced the maximum loan, and the reduced loan created a quantifiable financing gap that caused the borrower to either accept inferior financing terms or lose the ability to finance at all.

Key Takeaways

  • Construction defects reduce maximum supportable loan through two independent mechanisms: they reduce lender-adjusted NOI, which compresses the DSCR-constrained loan ceiling, and they reduce appraised value, which compresses the LTV-constrained loan ceiling. Both constraints must be analyzed, and the binding constraint governs.
  • Active litigation is often more damaging to financing feasibility than the defects themselves. Most agency lenders will not fund into a property with pending construction defect litigation, regardless of how well the income metrics pencil. The litigation cloud narrows the lender universe to portfolio lenders and bridge capital, both of which price materially wider than agency execution.
  • Lenders apply their own NOI adjustments to defect-impaired assets that are distinct from and generally more conservative than the borrower's operating statements. These adjustments, applied to reserves, maintenance, and vacancy, are a legitimate and documentable component of the financing damages analysis.

The CPA Guide to Commercial Real Estate Financing for Your Clients

By Trevor Damyan·June 25, 2026·Commercial Lending Solutions

Why CPAs and Tax Advisors Are the Most Valuable Referral Source in Commercial Real Estate Financing

I want to be direct with you: I spend a meaningful part of my business development effort trying to get in front of CPAs, enrolled agents, tax attorneys, and financial advisors. Not because it is easy, but because you are the most informed professional in your client's financial life. You see the full picture every year. I see one transaction.

You know your client's net operating income before their banker does. You know when depreciation recapture is going to hurt them on a sale. You know when their debt service coverage ratio has tightened because their tenant vacated. You know when they sold a rental property in March and have until September to identify a 1031 replacement. That knowledge, applied proactively, is worth more to your clients than almost anything else you can do outside of preparing an accurate return.

This article is my attempt to give you a working knowledge of commercial real estate financing from a tax and advisory perspective: the scenarios that matter, what lenders actually require, and how a referral to the right commercial mortgage broker protects your client and reflects well on you.

Your World, Your Clients, and Where CRE Financing Intersects

Most CPAs did not set out to become CRE financing specialists. But if you have any business owner clients, real estate investor clients, or high-net-worth individuals, you are already touching CRE financing issues regularly. The question is whether you are connecting those issues to the right resources.

Here is where I see tax advisors most commonly encounter financing inflection points:

  • A business owner pays rent to a third party and is ready to buy their own building. The tax benefits are significant and the SBA 504 program makes it accessible.
  • A client with a portfolio of rental properties is sitting on substantial equity and capital gains exposure. A cash-out refinance extracts that equity tax-free while deferring the gain indefinitely.
  • A client just received the results of a cost segregation study and their taxable income dropped materially. That improved cash flow changes their debt service coverage and opens refinance options that were not available before.
  • A partnership is dissolving and one partner needs to be bought out. The property has to be refinanced to generate the cash, often under deadline and often with title complications.
  • A client is selling an investment property and needs replacement property financing closed inside a very tight window or they trigger a large capital gains event.

In every one of these situations, the tax advisor often knows about the need before the client has called a lender or a broker. That timing advantage is the referral opportunity.

The Five CRE Financing Scenarios Your Clients Face Most Often

1. The 1031 Exchange Replacement Property Financing

A 1031 exchange is one of the most powerful tax deferral tools available to real estate investors. It is also one of the most stressful financing situations in commercial real estate, and the one where a delayed or declined loan can cost your client hundreds of thousands of dollars in deferred taxes.

Under IRC Section 1031, your client has 45 days from the close of the relinquished property to identify replacement properties and 180 days to close. Those deadlines are absolute. The IRS does not grant extensions for financing delays, appraisal problems, or lender underwriting backlogs.

The financing challenge is that lenders underwriting a replacement property do not care about your client's exchange deadline. A conventional bank commercial loan can take 60 to 90 days to close. That means the identification window is often already burning when your client first contacts a lender, and the 180-day close window gives very little margin for a slow underwriting process.

Bridge lenders and experienced commercial mortgage brokers who work with 1031 exchanges know how to compress timelines. I regularly work with clients whose exchange is already in progress, and the first question I ask is how many days they have left. If they have 90 days or more to the close deadline, conventional financing may still be viable. If they are inside 60 days, we are looking at bridge financing with a refinance plan built in.

What your clients need to hear from you: Do not wait until after the relinquished property closes to start lining up financing on the replacement. The moment a client tells you they are considering a 1031 exchange, that is the moment to make an introduction to a commercial mortgage broker who specializes in this type of transaction.

2. The SBA 504 Owner-User Purchase

The SBA 504 program is one of the most tax-efficient financing structures available to business owners, and it is dramatically underutilized because many CPAs do not fully understand how it works or when it applies.

SBA 504 is designed for owner-users: businesses purchasing the building they occupy for their own operations. The structure is a 90 percent combined loan-to-cost, split between a conventional bank loan (typically 50 percent) and a Certified Development Company debenture (40 percent), with the borrower contributing only 10 percent down. The CDC portion is fixed for 25 years at below-market rates, currently in the 6 to 7 percent range depending on timing.

For your business owner clients who are paying rent, the SBA 504 math often looks like this: their rent expense disappears, replaced by a mortgage payment that is frequently lower than rent on a comparable space, and now they are building equity in a hard asset. The mortgage interest is deductible. Depreciation of the building adds a non-cash deduction. If structured correctly with a cost segregation study on the purchased building, the first-year depreciation benefit can be substantial.

There are eligibility constraints your client needs to meet: the business must occupy at least 51 percent of the building at closing, the business must be for-profit and meet SBA size standards, and the owner cannot have excessive liquid assets relative to the loan request. The SBA has specific guidelines that a good commercial mortgage broker will walk through before the application is submitted.

The referral trigger for you: Any business owner client who mentions lease renewal discussions, frustration with a landlord, or interest in building ownership should hear about SBA 504. The tax benefits are real and the down payment requirement is low enough that this is often more accessible than clients expect.

3. The Post-Cost-Segregation Refinance

Cost segregation is a tax strategy, but it also changes a property's financing picture in ways that many clients do not recognize until a good advisor points it out.

Here is the sequence: your client owns a commercial property. You recommend a cost segregation study. The study reclassifies a portion of the building's components into 5, 7, and 15-year property, front-loading depreciation and improving after-tax cash flow substantially in the near term. Your client's tax liability drops. Their disposable cash flow increases.

That improved cash flow has a direct effect on debt service coverage. Lenders underwriting a commercial loan are primarily concerned with net operating income divided by annual debt service (DSCR). If the property's net operating income has improved due to increased cash flow or reduced owner tax drag, and if interest rates have shifted favorably since the original financing, this may be an optimal moment to refinance: pulling cash out at a favorable rate, extending amortization, or both.

The additional layer here is that a cash-out refinance after a cost segregation study is a tax-free event. Your client extracts equity without triggering a taxable gain, uses the cash for another investment or business purpose, and continues to benefit from the accelerated depreciation schedule on the existing property.

The referral trigger: The moment a cost segregation study is complete and the client sees their improved after-tax cash flow, that is when you introduce the refinance conversation. Do not wait for the client to bring it up. They often do not know that improved cash flow can unlock better financing terms.

4. The Partnership Dissolution Buyout

Partnership dissolutions involving commercial real estate are one of the most technically demanding financing situations I handle, and they are almost always time-pressured by legal or interpersonal dynamics.

The scenario: two or more partners own a commercial property. One partner wants out, either because of a business dispute, life event, estate planning need, or strategic difference. The remaining partner or partners want to retain the asset. The departing partner needs cash. The only way to generate that cash without selling the property is to refinance it and pay the departing partner their equity share from the loan proceeds.

Lenders underwriting this transaction have specific concerns. They want to see the partnership agreement and the buyout terms documented in writing before they will underwrite. They want confirmation that the remaining borrower(s) have the income and net worth to qualify without the departing partner. If the departing partner was the primary guarantor or the primary income earner in the partnership, this can be a significant underwriting challenge.

There are also tax considerations that affect the structure. If the buyout is structured as a redemption of the departing partner's interest versus a sale to the remaining partners, the tax treatment differs. The financing structure and timing may need to align with how the buyout is characterized for tax purposes. This is where the commercial mortgage broker and the CPA need to coordinate closely.

The referral trigger: Any client who mentions a partnership dispute, a partner who wants liquidity, or a potential dissolution of a real estate partnership should prompt you to ask about the financing options. These situations often resolve faster and with less acrimony when the remaining partners have a clear path to a buyout refinance.

5. The Cash-Out Refinance Instead of a Sale

This is the scenario I probably discuss most often with tax-focused advisors, because the tax math is so compelling and so many clients do not understand it until someone explains it explicitly.

Your client owns a commercial property that has appreciated substantially. They need liquidity, perhaps to fund a business expansion, make a new investment, or address a family financial need. Their instinct is to sell. The problem is that selling triggers depreciation recapture at ordinary income rates on the accumulated depreciation, plus capital gains tax on the appreciation above basis. For a long-held property with a low basis, the combined tax cost of a sale can be 35 to 45 percent of the gain.

A cash-out refinance extracts that equity without a taxable event. Loan proceeds are not income. The client receives cash, the property stays in their ownership, the depreciation schedule continues, and the capital gain is deferred until either a future sale or death (at which point the stepped-up basis eliminates the gain entirely for heirs).

Lenders will typically allow a cash-out refinance up to 70 to 75 percent of appraised value on investment properties, depending on property type, location, and borrower profile. The property needs to support the new debt service at the higher loan amount. If the property has appreciated significantly, the NOI often supports a much larger loan than the original financing.

The referral trigger: Any time a client mentions needing liquidity and their first thought is to sell an appreciated property, ask whether they have considered a cash-out refinance first. The tax savings alone often justify the conversation.

What Lenders Actually Require: The Technical Reality

CPAs are analytical professionals, so I want to give you the actual underwriting benchmarks rather than generalities.

Debt Service Coverage Ratio (DSCR): Most conventional commercial lenders require a minimum DSCR of 1.20 to 1.25, meaning net operating income must exceed annual debt service by 20 to 25 percent. SBA lenders typically require 1.15 or better on the global cash flow analysis, which includes all business and personal debt. Bridge lenders are more flexible, sometimes underwriting to 1.0 or even below on the basis of a clear value-add or exit strategy.

Loan-to-Value (LTV): Investment properties typically max out at 70 to 75 percent LTV for conventional financing. Owner-occupied with SBA goes to 90 percent combined LTV. Bridge financing can go to 80 percent or occasionally higher on strong assets in primary markets.

Borrower Net Worth and Liquidity: Most commercial lenders want to see borrower net worth equal to or greater than the loan amount, and liquidity (post-close) of at least 6 to 12 months of debt service. For SBA, the liquidity test is more nuanced and the SBA size standards also apply to the business.

Documentation: Commercial lenders require two to three years of property operating statements, a current rent roll with lease terms, two to three years of personal and business tax returns, a personal financial statement, and a purchase contract or refinance purpose letter. For 1031 exchanges, the exchange agreement and identification letter are required. For partnership dissolutions, the buyout agreement is required before underwriting can be completed.

Timeline: Conventional bank commercial loans take 45 to 75 days from application to close. SBA 504 takes 60 to 90 days. Bridge loans can close in 15 to 30 days in straightforward situations. Life insurance company loans, which offer the best long-term fixed rates, often take 90 to 120 days and have stricter property quality requirements.

How the Referral Process Actually Works

I want to demystify this because the process is simpler than most advisors assume, and your involvement after the introduction is minimal.

Step one: You identify a client situation that involves a CRE financing need, using any of the trigger scenarios described above. You send me a brief email or text introducing the client and describing the situation in general terms. No financial details required at this stage.

Step two: I contact your client directly, conduct an initial discovery call, and assess the financing options. I give them a clear picture of what is available, what it costs, and what they need to qualify.

Step three: If the client wants to proceed, I manage the entire process from lender selection through closing. I work with approximately 1,000 lenders across the country and can match the right capital source to each specific situation.

Step four: At closing, I am compensated by a lender-paid origination fee. Your client pays no separate advisory fee to me. You receive no compensation from me, which keeps your relationship clean and conflict-free.

Your client gets access to a broader market of lenders than they would find on their own, a professional managing the process, and terms that reflect competitive bidding rather than a single lender's offering. You get credit for connecting them to a valuable resource.

Questions to Ask Any Commercial Mortgage Broker Before Referring

Not all commercial mortgage brokers are created equal, and your reputation is attached to every referral you make. Here are the questions I would ask any broker before trusting them with your clients:

  • What types of commercial loans do you close most frequently? A broker who specializes in multifamily may not be the right choice for an office building or an SBA 504. Ask for specific recent transaction examples.
  • Do you have experience with 1031 exchange financing and the timeline constraints involved? This requires specific lender relationships and process knowledge. Not every broker has it.
  • How many lenders are in your network, and are they direct lenders or other brokers? Brokers who broker to other brokers add cost and complexity. You want direct lender relationships.
  • How are you compensated, and is there any fee paid by the borrower? The answer should be lender-paid origination, with any borrower fees clearly disclosed upfront in writing.
  • Can you provide references from CPAs or financial advisors who have referred clients to you? A broker who works regularly with the advisory community will have these references readily available.
  • What is your average time from application to close for this type of transaction? The answer should be specific to the loan type and should align with your client's timeline needs.

Key Takeaways for CPAs, Enrolled Agents, and Financial Advisors

  • You see your clients' entire financial picture annually. That gives you earlier visibility into CRE financing needs than any other professional in their life, including their lender.
  • The five highest-value referral triggers are: 1031 exchange replacement property financing, SBA 504 owner-user purchases, post-cost-segregation refinances, partnership dissolution buyouts, and cash-out refinances as an alternative to taxable sales.
  • 1031 exchange financing is uniquely time-sensitive. The referral needs to happen before the relinquished property closes, not after. A 45-day identification window is not enough time to arrange conventional financing from scratch.
  • A cash-out refinance on an appreciated property is almost always more tax-efficient than a sale, especially for long-held assets with low basis and accumulated depreciation. Clients often do not make this calculation until a trusted advisor frames it for them.
  • Lenders underwrite commercial loans on property cash flow first and borrower credit second. Understanding DSCR, LTV, and liquidity requirements helps you set realistic expectations with clients before they engage a lender.
  • The referral process requires nothing more than an introduction. A good commercial mortgage broker handles everything from lender selection through closing and is compensated by the lender, not the client.
  • Vetting a commercial mortgage broker before referring is appropriate and expected. Ask about their lender network, recent transaction history, and compensation structure before trusting them with your client relationships.
  • The advisors who build the most valuable practices are the ones who connect clients to the right specialists at the right moments. CRE financing is one of the highest-impact moments in any real estate investor's or business owner's financial life, and your ability to identify it and respond to it is a genuine competitive advantage.

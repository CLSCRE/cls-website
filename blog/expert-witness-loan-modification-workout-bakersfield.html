Expert Witness: Commercial Loan Modification Standards in Bakersfield, CA

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Loan Modification and Workout Dispute

The Bakersfield Market Context

Bakersfield is the economic engine of Kern County and one of the most underanalyzed commercial real estate markets in California. That underanalysis creates genuine risk in litigation when parties and their counsel rely on assumptions drawn from Los Angeles or the San Joaquin Valley broadly, rather than from the specific market dynamics that govern how lenders actually price and structure deals here. When a loan modification or workout dispute arises in Bakersfield, the local market context is not background noise. It is evidence.

Bakersfield's commercial real estate market is driven by three pillars: agriculture, energy, and logistics. These sectors shape demand for industrial, flex, and retail product in ways that differ materially from coastal California markets. Industrial vacancy in the Bakersfield submarket has historically run in the 4% to 7% range, supported by distribution users, food processing operations, and oilfield services tenants. That relatively tight vacancy means industrial assets have generally held their net operating income through the rate cycle, which is directly relevant when evaluating whether a lender's workout terms were commercially reasonable relative to the property's actual cash flow profile.

Multifamily fundamentals in Bakersfield also deserve specific attention in any financing dispute. Cap rates for stabilized apartment properties in Bakersfield have typically ranged from 5.5% to 7.5% depending on vintage, location within the metro, and unit mix. That spread is wider than coastal markets, which creates more sensitivity to valuation assumptions in a workout scenario. A lender modeling a stressed exit at a 7.0% cap versus a going-in cap of 5.8% will reach a materially different conclusion about loan-to-value coverage, and whether that cap rate selection was reasonable given observable market data is precisely the kind of question an expert witness must address.

Office fundamentals in Bakersfield have followed national trends with some local variation. Vacancy has climbed, particularly in Class B and Class C product, and net absorption has been negative or flat in several recent periods. Retail performance is bifurcated: grocery-anchored and necessity-driven centers have maintained occupancy, while secondary strip retail has experienced meaningful tenant attrition. Any workout dispute involving a retail or office asset in Bakersfield requires a careful, submarket-specific analysis of rent trends, lease rollover risk, and what a lender could reasonably have projected about stabilized income at the time the modification was being negotiated.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing the borrower in a dispute arising from a commercial mortgage workout on a multifamily property in Bakersfield's southwest submarket. The asset was a mid-size apartment community that had experienced occupancy deterioration during the COVID-19 period and had not fully restabilized by the time the original loan reached its maturity date. The borrower had entered into a forbearance agreement with the lender, and subsequently negotiated a formal loan modification. The borrower later alleged that the lender's conduct throughout the modification process, including the terms ultimately imposed and the manner in which the lender applied or withheld credit during the negotiation, fell below the standard of care that governs commercial real estate workout practice.

Counsel retained CLS CRE to answer three core questions. First, were the modification terms commercially reasonable given the property's documented cash flow and prevailing Bakersfield market conditions at the time of the modification? Second, did the lender's conduct during the workout process conform to the standards that govern how institutional and regional bank lenders typically handle distressed commercial mortgage situations? Third, what would a replacement lender have offered on this property under equivalent conditions, and does that alternative financing universe support or undermine the borrower's position that the modification terms were punitive rather than market-driven?

These are financing questions, not appraisal questions. They require someone who has sat on both sides of commercial loan workouts, who understands how special servicers and community bank workout officers actually make decisions, and who can translate that institutional knowledge into expert opinion testimony that a judge or jury can evaluate against the specific facts of the case.

The Financial Analysis

The foundation of any loan modification dispute analysis is a rigorous reconstruction of the property's income and expense performance at the relevant point in time. In this engagement, we built a trailing twelve-month and forward-looking stabilized NOI analysis for the multifamily asset using the property's actual rent rolls, operating statements, and the borrower's own projections that had been submitted to the lender during the workout process.

The property contained 96 units. At the time of the modification, physical occupancy was approximately 87%, with economic occupancy somewhat lower due to concessions and delinquencies that had not yet been fully resolved. Gross potential rent at market rates was approximately $1,248,000 annually based on prevailing Bakersfield rents for comparable vintage and submarket. After applying a vacancy and collection loss of 13% consistent with the actual performance data, effective gross income was approximately $1,085,760. Operating expenses, including management, maintenance, taxes, insurance, and reserves, totaled approximately $542,000, producing a trailing NOI of approximately $543,760.

The lender's modification proposal was built around a stabilized NOI assumption of $612,000, which represented a forward projection rather than trailing performance. That choice of numerator matters enormously in a workout dispute. Using a projected rather than trailed NOI is not inherently improper, but the projection must be defensible based on observable market conditions and a credible path to stabilization. We analyzed whether the lender's stabilization assumptions were internally consistent with Bakersfield multifamily market data, including absorption rates, average days on market for comparable units, and the specific competitive supply picture in the southwest submarket. In our analysis, the lender's stabilized NOI assumption was achievable but was applied on a timeline that compressed the borrower's runway more aggressively than market data supported.

For DSCR analysis, the industry standard threshold for multifamily assets at conventional and agency lenders is 1.20x to 1.25x on a stressed or note-rate basis. We applied a 1.25x DSCR threshold, consistent with the credit standards of the lender category involved in this transaction. Using the lender's own stabilized NOI of $612,000 and a modified note rate of 7.25% on a 30-year amortization schedule, the maximum supportable debt service was $489,600 annually, producing a maximum supportable loan balance of approximately $6,440,000. The modification had reduced the principal balance through a required paydown to $6,100,000, which translated to a DSCR of approximately 1.32x on the lender's stabilized NOI. On trailing NOI, however, DSCR fell to approximately 1.17x, below the threshold the lender had cited as its credit requirement. This gap between trailing and stabilized DSCR was central to the dispute: the borrower argued the lender had applied an unrealistic stabilization timeline to justify a paydown requirement that was not warranted by actual cash flow, while the lender's position was that the modification terms were necessary to bring the loan to a creditworthy position.

We also analyzed LTV. A current appraisal obtained at the time of the modification placed value at $8,750,000 based on a 6.25% cap rate applied to stabilized NOI. At a post-paydown balance of $6,100,000, that produced an LTV of approximately 69.7%, which is within the 65% to 75% range that most commercial lenders require for multifamily workouts. We stress-tested the valuation by applying cap rates of 6.75% and 7.25%, consistent with the upper end of the Bakersfield multifamily market range, which produced values of $9,067 per unit stress-tested and $8,441 per unit respectively at the 96-unit count. Even at a 7.0% cap rate, value came in at approximately $8,740,000, suggesting the appraisal was not materially aggressive. This analysis supported a conclusion that the lender had adequate collateral coverage and that the paydown requirement was driven by DSCR, not by LTV insecurity.

The Complicating Factors

The Trailing-Versus-Stabilized NOI Tension. In any workout involving a temporarily distressed asset, the lender must decide whether to underwrite to trailing cash flow, projected stabilized cash flow, or some blend. There is no universal rule. What does exist is a standard of practice: the stabilization assumptions must be grounded in observable market data, the timeline must be realistic, and the lender must document its reasoning. When a lender uses a stabilized NOI that is 12% above trailing performance on a 12-month projection timeline in a market where comparable properties were taking 18 to 24 months to restabilize, that disconnect becomes a target for expert analysis. Lenders who cannot produce documentation showing how their stabilization assumptions were derived are vulnerable to the argument that the assumptions were post-hoc justifications for a predetermined paydown requirement.

The Paydown Requirement as Leverage. Workout officers and special servicers do have the contractual right to require principal curtailment as a condition of modification. That right is not disputed. The question in litigation is whether the size and timing of the paydown was commercially reasonable given the property's actual condition. In Bakersfield's multifamily market, lenders working with borrowers on distressed assets during the 2022 to 2024 period generally structured paydowns in a range of 5% to 15% of the outstanding balance, depending on the severity of the distress and the remaining LTV cushion. A paydown requirement at the high end of that range or above it, applied to a property with a sub-75% LTV and improving occupancy trajectory, raises questions about whether the lender was exercising its workout discretion for credit reasons or using the modification process to manufacture a default scenario.

The Borrower's Right to Meaningful Negotiation. Commercial workout practice, as reflected in regulatory guidance from the OCC, FDIC, and Federal Reserve, encourages lenders to engage in good-faith workout efforts rather than forcing liquidation of otherwise viable assets. That regulatory backdrop establishes an expectation, if not a legal requirement, that lenders will engage with the borrower's proposals, respond to counter-proposals in a reasonable time frame, and document the credit rationale for each material term. In this engagement, the borrower had submitted three separate modification proposals over a seven-month period. The lender's response pattern, including extended periods without substantive response, shifting credit requirements between proposals, and personnel changes in the workout department that reset the negotiation timeline, was compared against industry norms. This comparison formed the basis of our opinion on whether the lender's process conduct met the standard of care.

Interest Rate Environment as Context. The modification occurred during a period when benchmark rates had risen sharply from historically low levels. This means the note rate on a modified loan in 2023 or 2024 was materially higher than the original loan's rate, which itself compresses DSCR on an otherwise unchanged cash flow. Lenders must account for this rate environment when structuring workout terms. A modification that imposes a market rate without an IO period or rate step-down provision in a rising-rate environment is meaningfully more onerous than a modification executed when rates are stable. Expert analysis must contextualize the rate structure of the modification against what other lenders were offering in the same market for comparable credit profiles at the same point in the rate cycle.

The Bakersfield Lender Market

Bakersfield is served by a broader and more competitive lender market than many practitioners outside California appreciate. Community and regional banks with direct relationships in the Kern County market include both California-chartered institutions and national banks with Central Valley presence. These lenders typically hold commercial real estate loans on balance sheet, which gives their workout officers more discretion to negotiate than a loan that has been securitized into a CMBS trust and is governed by a pooling and servicing agreement.

For multifamily assets in Bakersfield, Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac agency execution remains available for stabilized properties meeting occupancy and DSCR thresholds, generally 90% physical occupancy and 1.25x DSCR. Agency lenders will not participate in a workout or modification scenario directly, but the existence of agency-eligible takeout financing is relevant to establishing the replacement lender universe and to pricing what a commercially reasonable modification should have looked like. If a borrower could have refinanced out of a distressed modification into agency debt at 70% LTV and 1.30x DSCR with 18 months of stabilization runway, that fact is directly relevant to whether the lender's modification terms were commercially reasonable or whether they were structured to prevent exit.

Debt funds and bridge lenders are also active in Bakersfield for transitional multifamily situations. These lenders price at wider spreads, typically in the 300 to 500 basis point range over SOFR depending on leverage and asset quality, but they will lend to properties below agency-stabilized thresholds. Their existence in the market supports the argument that a distressed borrower had alternatives, but it also establishes a pricing ceiling. A modification rate that exceeds bridge debt pricing without the flexibility that bridge debt provides is an anomaly that requires explanation.

Life companies and CMBS lenders are less active in Bakersfield for smaller and mid-size assets, generally requiring loan sizes above $10 million for most programs. For the sub-$7 million loan range that characterized this engagement, the relevant lender universe is community banks, regional banks, credit unions with commercial real estate programs, and select debt funds. This narrower universe is important because it defines the range of "commercially reasonable" terms more precisely than a national comparison would.

What Attorneys Handling Bakersfield CRE Disputes Need to Know

An appraisal expert tells the court what a property is worth. A CRE financing expert tells the court what a lender should have done, could have done, and what the market would have offered. Those are fundamentally different questions, and in a loan modification or workout dispute, the financing questions are usually the dispositive ones.

Attorneys should consider retaining a CRE financing expert in any matter where the central dispute involves the terms of a modification or forbearance agreement, the lender's compliance with workout process standards, alleged breach of the duty of good faith in modification negotiations, the commercial reasonableness of a paydown requirement, deficiency balance disputes following foreclosure, or the adequacy of notice and opportunity to cure in a loan default context. These questions require someone who understands not just what a loan document says, but how lenders actually operate their workout functions in practice.

A qualified CRE financing expert is not simply a retired banker. Retired bankers can speak to what one institution did. A qualified expert must have current, active market knowledge, including live lender relationships and transaction experience that reflects what the market looks like today, not what it looked like in 2009. At CLS CRE, our firm has approximately $1 billion in transaction volume and over 1,000 active lender relationships across 50 states. That current market access is what allows us to reconstruct the lender universe available to a specific borrower at a specific point in time, which is the evidentiary standard that courts require.

Before engaging a CRE financing expert, counsel should gather the following materials: the original loan documents and all modifications or forbearance agreements; the complete loan file including underwriting documents, appraisals, and credit memoranda; all borrower-to-lender and lender-to-borrower correspondence during the workout period; the property's operating statements and rent rolls for the three years preceding and during the workout; any third-party reports commissioned by the lender, including environmental, property condition, or market studies; and the lender's internal workout policies or guidelines if obtainable through discovery. The quality of our analysis scales directly with the completeness of the record.

At trial or in a contested plan hearing, CRE financing expert testimony serves several distinct functions. It establishes the industry standard against which the lender's conduct is measured. It quantifies the financial impact of modification terms that deviated from market norms. It provides the factual foundation for damages calculations in cases where the borrower suffered losses attributable to a commercially unreasonable modification. And it rebuts lender-side expert testimony by exposing assumptions that do not hold up against actual market data from the relevant submarket and time period.

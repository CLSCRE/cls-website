How to Choose a Commercial Mortgage Broker, and How to Read a Best Broker List

Trevor Damyan Founder & Principal Broker
CA DRE #02244836

Quick answer: The best commercial mortgage broker is the one whose loan size, property types and lender access match your deal and who can show closed loans like yours. Published rankings mostly measure origination volume at large firms, so use them to build a shortlist, then check license, fees, conflicts and closed examples yourself.

A commercial mortgage broker is a licensed intermediary who takes your loan request to many lenders, compares the offers and manages the process to closing. The right one for you depends on your loan size, property type and timeline, and on whether the broker can show closed loans like yours. A published "best brokers" list can help you build a shortlist, but most of them rank firms by origination volume, which says little about whether a firm is a good fit for your deal.

This guide explains what a broker does, how a broker differs from a banker or a direct lender, what the best known industry rankings actually measure, and how to check any broker yourself. We rank no one here. Where we describe Commercial Lending Solutions (CLS CRE), a Los Angeles commercial mortgage brokerage, we keep it to one short section at the end so you can weigh it like any other candidate.

What a Commercial Mortgage Broker Does

A broker represents the borrower in the search for financing. A typical engagement covers:

  • Structuring the request: deciding which loan type fits the property and plan, such as bridge, permanent, construction, agency, life company, CMBS or mezzanine.
  • Packaging: assembling the operating statements, rent roll, sponsor financials and business plan a lender needs for a clean first review.
  • Marketing the loan: sending the package to lenders whose appetite fits and collecting term sheets.
  • Comparing offers: putting rate, leverage, term, recourse, fees and prepayment side by side, not just the headline rate.
  • Managing to closing: coordinating the appraisal, third party reports, legal documents, title and funding with the lender.

A broker does not fund the loan. The lender is the lender of record and the loan documents are between you and the lender.

What the Big Rankings Actually Measure

Two annual rankings shape most "top commercial mortgage firms" articles, and both are about volume:

  • MBA origination rankings. The Mortgage Bankers Association publishes league tables of commercial and multifamily originators from a voluntary annual survey. The 2025 edition covers 129 firms across more than 140 categories, such as role, investor type and property type, and the full report is sold by MBA. The free press release names only the top 10.
  • Commercial Property Executive's finance firms ranking. Firms report their own origination volume through an annual survey. The ranking weighs volume together with coverage, growth and sector mix, and publishes separate lists for direct lenders and intermediaries.

Both are useful for what they are: a picture of which large firms moved the most money. They do not tell you which firm will do the best job on a $3 million refinance in Los Angeles. The firms at the top are large capital markets companies and banks working mostly on very large transactions, and participation in the surveys is voluntary, so a firm's absence does not mean it is not active. Treat a ranking as one data point about scale, not a verdict on quality or fit.

How to Read Any "Best Commercial Mortgage Brokers" List

Before you trust a list, answer five questions:

  1. Who published it? A lender, a lead generation site or a firm that sells something related has a reason to shape the list. Check whether the publisher sells loans or services to the reader.
  2. Is the method stated? A real list says what it measured, over what period, and where the data came from. "Verified" or "top rated" with no method is marketing.
  3. Is it ranking brokers, or lenders and banks? Many lists mix intermediaries, banks and direct lenders. A bank cannot compare other lenders' offers for you.
  4. Does it fit your loan? A ranking built on large institutional volume does not help with a small or mid-sized loan. Check the typical loan size and property types behind the list.
  5. How old is it? Lender appetite and rates change quickly. A ranking more than a year old describes a different market.

How to Choose: Seven Things to Check

  1. Loan size and property fit. Ask for the loan size range they work in and for examples of your property type. A firm built for $50 million deals may not give a $2 million loan the attention it needs, and the reverse is also true.
  2. Lender access. Ask how many lenders they place with, and which types: banks, life companies, agencies, CMBS, debt funds, private lenders. A broker with access to one lender type will steer you toward it.
  3. Proof of closed loans. Ask for closed examples with the borrower and lender names removed: property type, loan amount, structure, time to close. Written case studies are a good sign.
  4. Licensing. Confirm the license. In California, look up the broker's real estate license number on the Department of Real Estate public license lookup. Other states have their own rules, so check with that state's regulator.
  5. Fees and the engagement agreement. Brokers are commonly paid a fee tied to the loan amount, usually at closing. Read the agreement for what fees are charged and when, who pays third party reports, how long exclusivity lasts, and what happens if you close with a lender you found yourself.
  6. Conflicts. Ask whether the firm also lends, owns lenders or is paid by lenders in a way that could change which offer it recommends, and ask it to disclose that in writing.
  7. Process and communication. Ask who your day to day contact is, how often you will get updates, and how quickly they respond. Speed during the first week often predicts the rest.

Ten Questions to Ask Before You Hire One

  • What loan sizes and property types do you work on most?
  • Which lender types will you approach for my deal, and why those?
  • Can you show two or three closed loans similar to mine, with names removed?
  • What is your license number and where can I verify it?
  • How are you paid, when, and what does the written engagement agreement say is charged and when?
  • Is there an exclusive period, and how do I end the agreement?
  • Do you or an affiliate lend money or receive fees from lenders?
  • Who will work on my file day to day?
  • What are the most likely reasons my loan could be declined or repriced?
  • What will you send me, in writing, when you have offers to compare?

Red Flags

  • Any fee charged before a written engagement agreement states what is charged and when.
  • A guaranteed approval, rate or closing date before underwriting.
  • No license number, or a number that does not match the name.
  • No closed examples, or examples that cannot be described even with names removed.
  • A long exclusive agreement that gives you no way out if the broker does not perform.
  • Pressure to sign the same day, or a refusal to put anything in writing.

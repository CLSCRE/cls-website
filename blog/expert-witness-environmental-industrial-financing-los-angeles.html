Expert Witness Analysis: Financing a Contaminated Industrial Property Through Chapter 11 in Los Angeles

By Trevor Damyan·June 25, 2026·CRE Financing Expert Witness

This article illustrates the methodology Commercial Lending Solutions applies when retained as a commercial real estate financing expert witness in complex litigation. The scenario described below combines four simultaneous complications: active environmental contamination under state regulatory oversight, a pending Chapter 11 bankruptcy proceeding, a court-ordered diversion of rental income to judgment creditors, and a property owner who is adverse to the retaining parties. The engagement type requires producing expert opinions on whether first-mortgage financing is available in the current market and whether such financing would be sufficient to retire existing senior debt in full.

The following financial analysis uses hypothetical figures for illustrative purposes. It demonstrates the methodology we apply when retained as a commercial real estate financing expert witness. All numbers are illustrative and do not reflect any specific property or transaction.

A Representative Scenario

Consider the following scenario: a CRE financing expert is retained by counsel for secured judgment creditors in a Chapter 11 bankruptcy proceeding. The property at issue is an industrial warehouse complex in an urban infill submarket, held by the debtor as a core asset in the bankruptcy estate. The retaining parties have a direct financial interest in establishing whether the property can support first-mortgage financing sufficient to retire the existing senior debt, because if refinancing is feasible, it represents a mechanism for satisfying claims through the bankruptcy plan.

The role of the expert is narrow and carefully defined: to opine as a commercial real estate financing expert on (1) whether first-mortgage financing is available for this property in the current market, given its specific characteristics and encumbrances; (2) the maximum loan amount supportable by the property's cash flows and collateral value under standard market underwriting; and (3) the categories of lenders who would realistically consider this transaction and what conditions they would impose.

The property owner is the debtor in the bankruptcy proceeding and is adverse to the retaining clients. The practical consequence is that voluntary cooperation from the party controlling the asset cannot be assumed. Document production requires court process; any interaction with the debtor or its counsel occurs in the context of contested litigation. This constrains due diligence in specific ways and requires explicit disclosure in expert opinions about what assumptions rest on verified data versus what is drawn from third-party sources, public records, and market precedent.

This scenario is representative of a category of expert witness work that arises frequently in California commercial real estate litigation: disputes where the financing feasibility of a property is central to the relief sought, but where the property itself carries complications that make a simple "yes, it can be financed" conclusion nontrivial to reach and nontrivial to defend on cross-examination.

The Asset

In this illustrative scenario, the property is approximately 95,000 square feet of industrial and warehouse space situated on approximately 4.5 acres in an urban infill industrial submarket. The asset is 100% occupied under a triple net lease with a multi-market flex warehouse operator whose lease runs through December 31, 2032, providing approximately 6.5 years of remaining lease term as of the analysis date.

The subject submarket is one of the most supply-constrained in Southern California. The urban infill location, proximity to major freeways and a regional distribution hub, and the near-complete absence of developable land for new industrial construction have combined to produce structural rent growth over the past decade. Industrial vacancy in this type of submarket has consistently run below 3%, and in the post-2020 period, rents accelerated sharply due to e-commerce-driven demand and the reshoring of distribution operations.

The current in-place rent is $9.00 per square foot per year, generating approximately $80,000 per month in gross rent. This figure is materially below current market, which is supported by comparable lease data in the range of $13.00 to $16.00 per square foot per year. The spread between in-place and market rent represents significant rollover upside at lease expiration. From a lender's perspective, below-market rent is a double-edged underwriting consideration: it means current cash flow is lower than the property's stabilized earning capacity, but it also provides significant collateral protection at lease renewal because a replacement tenant would pay materially higher rent.

Under a triple net lease structure, the tenant bears substantially all operating expenses (property taxes, insurance, maintenance), so the landlord's in-place net operating income tracks closely to the in-place rent, less minor ownership-level costs. This simplifies underwriting because the primary cash flow variable is the lease itself, and the credit quality of the tenant determines the risk profile of that income stream. A multi-market operator with established operations across multiple geographic markets presents a more bankable covenant than a single-location tenant, even where financial statements are not directly available to lenders.

Below-market rent on a quality industrial asset in a supply-constrained submarket is not a financing impairment. It is collateral protection. The underwriting question is not "is today's NOI adequate?" but "will today's NOI, conservatively applied to DSCR, support a loan that also passes LTV scrutiny against a credible value estimate?" In this illustrative scenario, the answer is yes on both axes.

The Four Complications

Each complication in this type of engagement has its own financing logic. Attorneys litigating over contaminated or encumbered real estate often treat environmental conditions, bankruptcy status, court orders, and borrower cooperation as a single undifferentiated mass of "problems." A financing expert's task is to disaggregate them, analyze each on its own merits relative to lender behavior, and reach a calibrated opinion about the net effect on market access and loan terms.

Complication 1: Environmental Contamination

The property is subject to active enforcement oversight by the California Regional Water Quality Control Board (RWQCB). The contamination involves 1,4-dioxane and chlorinated volatile organic compounds (VOCs) traceable to prior industrial use at the site. The plume has migrated to an adjacent property, which is the factor that triggered the formal regulatory case. A third-party environmental assessment estimated remediation costs at approximately $4.5 million base, or approximately $5.4 million gross when a 1.2x contingency factor is applied for unforeseen remediation complications. Two environmental insurance policies with potential coverage exist; coverage under both is disputed.

The critical financing question is not whether contamination exists, but whether it eliminates lender access. The answer, based on current market practice among specialty commercial real estate lenders, is that it does not.

The commercial real estate lending market has developed well-established protocols for environmental impairment. Lenders active in California industrial markets routinely encounter Phase I and Phase II environmental conditions. The framework they apply is straightforward: Does a quantified remediation estimate exist? Is there a defined regulatory path? Is there an appropriate reserve or holdback mechanism? Can the reserve be sized from loan proceeds or required as borrower equity at close? If the answers are yes, the environmental condition becomes a structural and pricing issue rather than a fatal underwriting barrier.

In this type of engagement, the environmental condition actually provides lenders with more information, not less. An active RWQCB case means there is regulatory documentation, a defined remediation pathway, third-party cost estimates, and ongoing supervision that creates accountability for remediation progress. A lender holding a first deed of trust on a property with a remediation plan and an active regulatory file is in a materially better position than a lender who discovers contamination after closing without any documented path to resolution.

The approximately $4.5 million remediation estimate (or approximately $5.4 million gross) is material relative to total property value, but the analysis demonstrates that the LTV math absorbs this reserve even under conservative assumptions. This is addressed in detail in the financial analysis section below.

Complication 2: Active Chapter 11 Bankruptcy

The property is part of an active Chapter 11 bankruptcy estate. Any financing transaction, including a refinance of the existing senior debt, requires Bankruptcy Court approval under 11 U.S.C. Section 364 or through a confirmed plan of reorganization. This procedural requirement is frequently misunderstood by non-specialists as a near-absolute barrier to lender participation. It is not.

Experienced commercial real estate lenders, particularly in the bridge and private debt fund segment, regularly originate loans secured by bankruptcy estate properties. "DIP financing" (debtor in possession) and "exit financing" are established products with defined underwriting frameworks. Lenders who participate in these transactions understand the court approval process; many view court oversight as a structural protection rather than a complication, because it creates a defined timeline, a supervised decision-making process, and documentation of the transaction's commercial reasonableness that can support enforcement of lender rights post-confirmation.

The practical effect of bankruptcy status on financing availability is a narrowing of the lender universe, primarily affecting conventional bank lenders who face regulatory constraints on lending to bankruptcy estates under their own credit policies. The pool of willing lenders shifts toward private credit, bridge debt funds, and specialized bankruptcy lenders. These lenders price for the additional complexity through rate and fee, but the fundamental underwriting analysis is the same: Does the collateral support the loan? Is the cash flow adequate to service the debt? Those questions are asset-level questions, not legal-status questions.

Court approval also provides a documented record that the financing was commercially reasonable, that all interested parties were noticed, and that a judicial officer reviewed and approved the transaction. For a post-confirmation lender who may need to enforce remedies against a property that has already been through bankruptcy, this record is an asset.

Complication 3: Court-Ordered Rent Assignment

Prior to the bankruptcy filing, a superior court entered an order directing the tenant to pay rent not to the property owner but to judgment creditors as partial satisfaction of their claims. This order meant that for a period of time, the property's NOI was being diverted from the owner and was not available for debt service. From an underwriting standpoint, this created a direct problem: a lender evaluating cash flow coverage of a prospective loan would see in-place payment records that did not reflect the property's actual debt service capacity, because the income stream had been judicially redirected.

This complication is real, but it is structural and solvable. The standard lender response to cash flow diversion risk is a lockbox or cash management agreement, which is a routine feature of commercial mortgage documentation. Under a lockbox structure, the tenant pays rent directly to a controlled account administered by the lender; from that account, debt service is swept first, and any residual flows to the borrower. This mechanism eliminates the operational risk that a future court order or borrower action could redirect income away from debt service.

In the context of a bankruptcy plan of reorganization, the rent assignment order would be resolved as part of the plan itself. A confirmed plan would establish the priority of claims and the mechanism for distributing estate assets. A post-confirmation lender would originate in an environment where the rent assignment dispute had been judicially resolved, the borrower's obligations and income stream had been restructured by court order, and the lockbox provisions in the new loan documents would govern all future cash flow from the property. The historical period of diversion affects the selection of which historical financials to use in underwriting; it does not alter the forward-looking income potential of the property.

Complication 4: Adversarial Borrower

The property owner is the debtor in the bankruptcy proceeding and is adverse to the parties who retained us. This means that voluntary cooperation in facilitating a financing transaction cannot be assumed; any borrower obligations in connection with a new loan would need to be compellable by Court order rather than relying on the debtor's affirmative willingness to cooperate.

This is a transaction execution risk, not a financing feasibility risk. The distinction matters.

Lenders underwrite collateral, cash flow, and their ability to enforce remedies. They do not underwrite borrower affinity or enthusiasm. A borrower who is compelled by court order to execute loan documents, comply with financial reporting requirements, and cooperate with property management obligations is, from a lender's perspective, legally equivalent to a voluntary borrower who has contractually assumed the same obligations. The enforcement mechanism is different (Court order versus contract voluntarily entered), but the legal obligation is the same.

Lenders who participate in bankruptcy-related transactions are specifically experienced in dealing with the debtor-in-possession framework, where the debtor's actions are supervised by the court and contested by multiple parties. The adversarial nature of the borrower's relationship with the retaining parties is relevant to transaction management; it is not relevant to whether the property can support first-mortgage financing or whether willing lenders exist.

The Financial Analysis

The financing feasibility analysis follows the methodology applied by institutional commercial real estate lenders in underwriting income-producing properties: normalize historical cash flows to derive a supportable NOI, apply market-standard debt service coverage requirements to size the maximum supportable loan, and validate the loan amount against an LTV ceiling derived from collateral value. Both the DSCR-constrained loan size and the LTV-constrained loan size must be satisfied; the binding constraint is whichever produces the lower number.

Net Operating Income Normalization

The subject property generated historical rents over the 2022-2024 period that were partially diverted by the superior court rent assignment order described above. Normalizing for this diversion, the in-place NOI was calculated on a three-year historical basis and validated against the current lease terms. The resulting in-place NOI is $720,000 per year. This represents the cash flow a lender can underwrite with confidence based on the existing lease at current rent of $9.00 per square foot per year.

In parallel, a stabilized NOI projection was prepared using a conservative market-to-market rent assumption at the low end of the $13.00 to $16.00 comparable range, applying a discount to account for execution risk in achieving market rent at lease expiration. At this conservative assumption, stabilized NOI is $1,050,000 per year. Lenders with a longer hold perspective or those underwriting to residual value at lease expiration would give credit to some portion of this stabilized NOI in sizing their loan.

LTV Analysis Including Environmental Stress Test

The DSCR-constrained loan must also satisfy LTV limits. Two valuation benchmarks were used, both derived from an appraisal of record: an impaired value of approximately $16,000,000 (reflecting the environmental condition) and an unimpaired value of approximately $22,000,000 (the value if the contamination were fully remediated). The appraiser's separate treatment of these two values is standard practice for industrial properties with known environmental conditions and provides a conservative floor for lender collateral analysis.

The LTV analysis demonstrates that the in-place loan scenario carries approximately 20 percentage points of headroom below the 65% LTV threshold, even when measured against the lower, environmentally-impaired property value. The headroom is substantial. The extreme stress scenario, which double-counts the environmental impairment, produces an LTV modestly above the 65% threshold and would require a lender to either accept a slightly higher LTV or require a small additional reserve -- both of which are within the range of market practice for specialty lenders in this transaction category.

The Lender Market

Identifying the categories of lenders who would realistically consider this transaction is a distinct expert opinion from the financial analysis. It requires current market knowledge of lender appetite, credit policy, and product parameters across the spectrum of commercial real estate capital sources. The following analysis reflects the market as of the date of this illustrative scenario.

Category 1: Bridge and Private Debt Funds

The primary lender category for this transaction is the bridge and private debt fund market. These are non-bank lenders who provide shorter-term (one to five year) fixed-rate loans secured by commercial real estate, with an explicit mandate to originate transactions that conventional banks cannot or will not do. Their capital comes from institutional investors, family offices, and private capital, which means their credit policies are set by their own investment committees rather than by bank regulators. This gives them substantially more flexibility to originate against environmentally-impaired, bankruptcy-estate, or otherwise complicated collateral.

Pricing in this category runs from approximately 7.00% to 10.00% fixed for a transaction of this profile. Points are typically 1.00% to 2.00% at origination. LTV tolerance is generally up to 65% on impaired collateral, though some funds go higher with additional reserve requirements. The environmental condition would require a Phase II environmental site assessment (ESA), a remediation plan with cost estimates, and an environmental reserve holdback. The bankruptcy status would require court approval of the financing and a review of the plan of reorganization or DIP financing motion. The rent assignment issue would require a lockbox and cash management structure.

The key due diligence items that would drive the underwriting timeline in this category: Phase II ESA review and lender's environmental consultant sign-off on the remediation estimate, review of the RWQCB case file and current status, Bankruptcy Court approval motion and timeline, and confirmation of the lockbox structure post-confirmation.

Category 2: Community Banks and Credit Unions

A secondary category is community banks and credit unions with active California industrial lending programs and experience with environmentally-complicated properties. These institutions can offer more favorable pricing (approximately 6.50% to 8.00%) but carry more restrictive credit policies that may make bankruptcy-estate lending difficult or impossible under their own regulatory guidelines. Several California community banks with SBA and USDA program authority have successfully originated on contaminated industrial properties where remediation was underway and a reserve was established.

The distinguishing characteristics for this category: more favorable rate and fee structure; more extensive documentation requirements including environmental indemnification, personal guaranty (where available), and Phase II plus ongoing environmental monitoring requirements; longer underwriting timelines; and institutional credit policy constraints that may preclude originating into an active Chapter 11 estate. In this scenario, the community bank category represents a possible alternative path but not the primary one, given the bankruptcy constraint.

Category 3: Hard Money and Private Capital

A third category, available as a backstop, is hard money and private capital lenders who operate with maximum execution speed and minimum documentation, at pricing in the 9.00% to 12.00% range plus points. These lenders would not be appropriate as a first-choice structure for a transaction of this duration and complexity, but they exist in the market and would provide a lender-of-last-resort floor for the opinion that willing lenders exist. Their presence is evidence that the transaction is not unfinanceable; it is evidence that market access is available, though at a price.

Hard money lenders in the California industrial market routinely originate on properties with environmental conditions, court-ordered encumbrances, and complicated title situations. Their due diligence is primarily collateral-driven: they are underwriting the asset at a conservative LTV (often 50% to 60%), not the borrower's creditworthiness or the property's income history. At 45% LTV on the in-place basis, this property falls well inside the range where hard money interest would be expected.

What This Means for Attorneys

Commercial real estate financing expert witnesses serve a function in litigation that is distinct from property appraisers, environmental consultants, or bankruptcy counsel. Their expertise concerns the capital markets: what lenders will and will not do, how they underwrite, what they require, and what the market price of capital is for a given asset and transaction structure. These questions arise in a range of commercial real estate disputes where attorneys need a credentialed professional who can explain financing feasibility to a judge, jury, or arbitration panel.

Questions a CRE Financing Expert Can Answer

The core questions are consistent across engagement types, even when the underlying facts vary significantly:

  • Is first-mortgage financing available for this property in the current market, given its specific conditions and encumbrances?
  • What is the maximum loan amount the property can support, applying market-standard DSCR and LTV requirements?
  • Is the available financing sufficient to retire existing senior debt in full?
  • What categories of lenders would consider this transaction, and what would they require?
  • Did a lender's underwriting of this loan meet or fall below prevailing standards of care in the market at the time of origination?
  • Was a loan modification, forbearance, or restructuring offered on commercially reasonable terms relative to market practice?
  • Were the fees, rates, and structural terms imposed by a lender consistent with what the market offered for comparable transactions?

When to Retain a CRE Financing Expert

The following litigation contexts most frequently require a commercial real estate financing expert:

Contaminated property disputes. Bankruptcy proceedings, foreclosure defense, and lender liability cases involving properties with environmental conditions often turn on whether financing was or was not available on commercially reasonable terms. The expert's job is to establish a market standard for how environmental conditions affect loan availability, pricing, and structure.

Bankruptcy plan disputes. Whether a plan of reorganization is feasible often requires proof that the debtor can access financing to fund plan distributions or refinance existing secured debt. Creditors opposing a plan, or supporting one, may need expert testimony on financing feasibility as an element of the feasibility analysis required under 11 U.S.C. Section 1129.

Lender liability claims. Cases where a borrower alleges that a lender imposed unreasonable terms, refused to modify or extend on commercially reasonable terms, or violated an implied covenant of good faith in its lending relationship require expert testimony on what the market standard was and whether the defendant lender's conduct departed from it.

Loan origination standards disputes. Cases involving alleged misrepresentation in loan origination, inadequate underwriting, or appraisal-related fraud require expert testimony about what a competent lender should have known, what due diligence was market-standard, and whether the loan as originated was consistent with prevailing underwriting standards.

Partnership and joint venture disputes. When commercial real estate partners disagree about the financing decisions made for a shared property, or when one party claims the other failed to obtain available financing at commercially reasonable terms, an expert can opine on what financing was available, at what terms, and whether the decisions made were consistent with prudent real estate ownership.

What Makes a CRE Financing Expert Credible

The most credible CRE financing experts are those who are actively doing what they are testifying about. An expert who has originated or placed commercial real estate loans in the current market can testify from direct professional knowledge about what lenders require, what they will and will not do, and what the market price of capital is. This practical current-market knowledge is different from academic knowledge about lending theory or historical knowledge about past market conditions.

Attorneys evaluating a CRE financing expert should look for: active deal volume across multiple property types and loan categories; relationships with lenders across the full spectrum from life insurance companies to bridge funds; and a track record that includes complicated transactions involving environmental conditions, bankruptcy, distressed assets, or special-purpose collateral. An expert who has only placed straightforward apartment loans will have limited ability to testify credibly about specialty lending markets.

Key Takeaways

  • Environmental contamination with a documented remediation cost estimate, active RWQCB oversight, and an established reserve mechanism does not preclude commercial real estate financing.   • Chapter 11 bankruptcy status is a procedural constraint, not a financing barrier. Bridge and private debt fund lenders regularly originate into bankruptcy estates with court approval. The court approval process provides structural certainty that sophisticated lenders understand and navigate routinely.
  • Court-ordered rent diversion is a cash flow management issue solvable through standard lockbox and cash management provisions. It affects the historical underwriting data that must be normalized; it does not alter the property's forward-looking income capacity.
  • An adversarial borrower is a transaction execution risk. Lenders underwrite collateral and cash flow, not borrower enthusiasm. Court-compelled compliance with loan obligations is legally equivalent to voluntarily-assumed contractual obligations from the lender's perspective.
  • The DSCR and LTV tests, applied conservatively at 1.25x and 65% respectively, confirm that the in-place NOI of $720,000 supports a loan of $7,200,000.   • The in-place loan scenario carries approximately 20 percentage points of LTV headroom below the 65% threshold when measured against the environmentally-impaired property value. The extreme stress test, which double-counts the environmental impairment, produces an LTV modestly above the threshold and would require minor structural adjustment by the lender.
  • The lender universe for this transaction type includes bridge/private debt funds, some community banks and credit unions, and hard money lenders as a backstop. The existence of three distinct lender categories demonstrates that the transaction is financeable; the primary variable is pricing, not access.
  • Expert witness opinions on CRE financing feasibility require both capital markets expertise and explicit analytical methodology. The opinion is only as strong as the DSCR and LTV work that supports it, and that work must be capable of withstanding cross-examination on every input.

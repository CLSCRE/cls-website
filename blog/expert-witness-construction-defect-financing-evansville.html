Expert Witness: How Construction Defects Affect CRE Financing in Evansville, IN

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Construction Defect Impact on Financing

The Evansville Market Context

Evansville, Indiana sits at the confluence of three states, and its commercial real estate market reflects that geographic reality in ways that directly affect how lenders underwrite assets here. The metro area anchors southwestern Indiana with a diversified economic base that includes advanced manufacturing, healthcare, logistics, and retail distribution. Toyota's Princeton plant roughly 25 miles to the north, multiple Toyota supplier facilities in the region, and a growing presence of warehouse and distribution users along the I-164 and US-41 corridors have made industrial product the strongest performing asset class in this market over the past several years. Industrial vacancy in the Evansville MSA has run in the 4% to 7% range in recent years, with net absorption remaining positive as regional distributors expand footprints and e-commerce logistics users seek last-mile alternatives to more expensive Louisville and Indianapolis real estate.

Multifamily fundamentals in Evansville present a more nuanced picture. Cap rates for stabilized apartment communities have generally traded in the 6.25% to 7.50% range, considerably wider than gateway markets, which reflects both the market's secondary status and its historically lower rent growth trajectory. Average effective rents for class B garden-style communities in Evansville have been in the $750 to $1,050 per unit range depending on submarket, with the East Side and Newburgh corridor commanding premiums over the older West Side inventory. That spread matters when underwriting construction defect impact because lenders calibrate their maximum loan assumptions to local rent comp data, and any impairment to income or occupancy drops directly into the DSCR calculation at these already-tighter cap rate levels.

Office and retail are experiencing the secular headwinds familiar across midsize Midwestern metros. Downtown Evansville office vacancy has remained elevated, and suburban medical office has held up considerably better given the health system presence anchored by two major regional hospital networks. For purposes of a construction defect financing analysis, this market context is essential: a lender in Indianapolis or Chicago evaluating an Evansville asset is applying local vacancy and rent assumptions to a smaller, less liquid market, which compresses both the appraised value and the maximum supportable loan relative to what the same asset in a larger metro would support.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing a party in a construction defect dispute involving a multifamily residential community in the Evansville market. The asset was a garden-style apartment community of approximately 120 units that had been completed and placed into service within the prior four years. The alleged defects involved structural framing failures in a portion of the buildings, improper waterproofing at window and door penetrations causing moisture intrusion, and HVAC system deficiencies affecting multiple units. A portion of the property had been taken offline pending remediation evaluation, reducing effective occupancy and gross potential rent.

Counsel engaged CLS CRE to address three core financial and financing questions. First, what was the maximum supportable loan on this asset as built and as currently encumbered by the known defect conditions, compared to what a defect-free equivalent asset would support in the current Evansville lending market? Second, what specific lender requirements and conditions are triggered when a commercial property is the subject of active construction defect litigation, and how do those requirements affect financing feasibility, cost, and timing? Third, what is the quantifiable impact of the known defects on the property's net operating income, debt service coverage ratio, and loan-to-value headroom, expressed in a format suitable for use in expert testimony and damages quantification?

These are precisely the questions that a real estate appraiser, operating within their USPAP-defined scope, is not positioned to answer. An appraiser can opine on value. A CRE financing expert opines on what the debt markets will actually do with that value, and under what conditions, costs, and constraints. Those are fundamentally different analyses, and the gap between them is often where the most significant financial damages in a construction defect matter are found.

The Financial Analysis

My analysis began with a reconstruction of the property's stabilized net operating income under two scenarios: a defect-free baseline representing what the property should have achieved as a fully functional, fully occupied comparable asset in its Evansville submarket, and an impaired current-state scenario reflecting the actual operating performance given the defect conditions and associated unit downtime, remediation reserves, and elevated operating expenses.

Baseline (defect-free) underwriting: A 120-unit garden-style community in this Evansville submarket, assuming market rents of approximately $875 per unit per month blended across unit mix, generates gross potential rent of approximately $1,260,000 annually. Applying a stabilized vacancy and credit loss allowance of 7%, consistent with market norms for this asset class and submarket, yields effective gross income of approximately $1,172,000. Operating expenses for a property of this vintage and type, including management, maintenance, insurance, real estate taxes, and reserves for replacement at $250 per unit annually, run approximately $525,000. That produces a stabilized NOI of approximately $647,000.

Impaired current-state underwriting: With approximately 18 units offline or in reduced-rent status due to defect conditions, gross potential rent drops to approximately $1,071,000. Vacancy at 7% on the reduced base yields effective gross income of approximately $996,000. Operating expenses increase by approximately $65,000 due to elevated maintenance costs, interim repair expenditures, and an increased insurance load reflecting the known defect exposure, bringing total expenses to approximately $590,000. Impaired NOI therefore comes to approximately $406,000, representing a reduction of approximately $241,000 or roughly 37% from the defect-free baseline.

DSCR and maximum loan analysis: For a stabilized multifamily asset in a secondary Indiana market, conventional agency lenders (Fannie Mae DUS, Freddie Mac Optigo) apply a minimum DSCR of 1.25x. Community bank and regional bank portfolio lenders active in Evansville typically require 1.20x to 1.25x DSCR on permanent financing, with some credit unions applying 1.15x on smaller balance transactions for known borrowers. I used 1.25x as the governing standard for this analysis, which represents the floor for the broadest class of available permanent capital.

At a note rate of 6.75% (reflecting current conventional pricing for a 10-year fixed, 30-year amortization multifamily structure as of my analysis period) and a 1.25x DSCR requirement, the maximum annual debt service supportable from the defect-free NOI of $647,000 is $517,600. At a 6.75% constant on a 30-year amortization, the corresponding mortgage constant is approximately 0.0780. Dividing $517,600 by 0.0780 produces a maximum supportable loan of approximately $6,636,000 on the defect-free asset.

Applying the same methodology to the impaired NOI of $406,000 yields maximum annual debt service of $324,800 and a maximum supportable loan of approximately $4,164,000. The income-driven loan reduction attributable to the defect conditions is therefore approximately $2,472,000.

LTV overlay: Agency and conventional lenders apply a maximum LTV of 75% to 80% on stabilized multifamily. If the defect-free asset appraises at $8,500,000 (consistent with a 7.25% cap rate applied to $647,000 NOI), the 75% LTV ceiling produces a maximum loan of $6,375,000, which is binding alongside the DSCR test. On the impaired asset, assuming a corresponding value of approximately $5,600,000 based on a value-in-use analysis reflecting both lower NOI and a risk-adjusted cap rate expansion of approximately 50 basis points to account for the litigation and uncertainty overhang, the 75% LTV ceiling is $4,200,000, closely corroborating the income-driven analysis. The convergence of the DSCR-driven and LTV-driven loan caps in both scenarios gives the damages calculation a higher degree of confidence and reliability under cross-examination.

The Complicating Factors

Active litigation as a lender condition precedent issue: The single most significant financing obstacle created by construction defect litigation is not the physical condition of the asset. It is the existence of the litigation itself. Every institutional lender, agency lender, and most community banks maintain underwriting guidelines that require disclosure of all pending litigation affecting a subject property. When that litigation involves allegations of structural defects or systemic building system failures, lenders treat it as an unresolved material contingency. In practical terms, this means that even if the physical defects were fully remediated tomorrow, no conventional lender will fund a permanent loan on the property until the litigation is resolved or a court-approved settlement and remediation plan is in place. This is not a pricing issue. It is a binary approval issue: the loan cannot close in most cases until the litigation is resolved.

Engineer and PCA requirements: When a lender does consider financing a property with known or alleged construction defects, they will require a Property Condition Assessment from a qualified structural engineer, not a standard PCA inspector. That engineer must certify either that the defects have been remediated to the engineer's satisfaction, or provide a detailed scope and cost estimate for remediation. Lenders will then escrow 110% to 125% of that estimated remediation cost as a holdback against loan proceeds, directly reducing the net loan amount available to the borrower. In Evansville, where loan sizes are already more constrained by value than in major metros, a $400,000 to $600,000 remediation holdback against a $4.2 million loan is a significant liquidity event for the borrower.

Cap rate expansion under uncertainty: Sophisticated lenders apply a risk-adjusted cap rate when appraising assets with known defect exposure. Rather than accepting a broker's opinion of value or even a clean appraisal, lenders will often commission their own appraisal with explicit instruction to reflect the current as-is condition including the defect overhang. In my experience, this results in a cap rate expansion of 25 to 75 basis points above the market rate for a comparable defect-free asset, which directly compresses the appraised value and therefore the LTV-limited loan amount. In an already-wider-cap-rate market like Evansville, this expansion has an amplified dollar impact compared to a tighter market.

Insurance complications: Properties subject to active construction defect litigation often face challenges renewing property and casualty insurance at standard rates, or in some cases face exclusions related to the known defect conditions. Lenders require evidence of acceptable insurance as a condition of closing, and any gap in coverage, any exclusion tied to the defect conditions, or any increase in the property's insurance cost profile feeds directly back into the operating expense load and reduces NOI available for debt service.

The Evansville Lender Market

Evansville is a market with meaningful community bank depth. Old National Bank, German American Bank, and several local credit unions and savings institutions have maintained active commercial real estate lending programs in this market for decades. These institutions know the market, they know the operators, and they are capable of structuring creative solutions that national lenders cannot. However, even community banks in Evansville apply consistent standards when litigation is involved: they require resolution of the litigation, acceptable engineer certification, and remediation completion before funding permanent debt on a defect-affected asset.

Agency execution through Fannie Mae DUS lenders and Freddie Mac Optigo sellers is available in Evansville for multifamily assets that meet the programs' geographic and property eligibility requirements. Loan sizes typically start at $1 million to $1.5 million for agency execution, and most Evansville multifamily assets of 80 units or more can access agency pricing. Agency lenders are, however, the least flexible category when it comes to properties with litigation or known defects. Their program guidelines are explicit: no agency loan can be committed or closed on a property with pending material litigation without specific agency credit approval, which is rarely granted until litigation is resolved.

Debt funds and bridge lenders represent the alternative capital layer that a borrower may need to access during the period between defect identification and litigation resolution. Bridge lenders operating in Evansville will generally price at 300 to 500 basis points over SOFR on a floating rate basis, with origination fees of 1% to 2%, and they will size to approximately 65% to 70% of the as-is value. They provide a functional but expensive capital solution that documents the cost of carry attributable to the defect-induced financing dislocation, which is itself a quantifiable damages element.

Life insurance companies and CMBS conduits are not meaningful lending sources for the Evansville market at this complexity level. Life company allocations to secondary Indiana markets are limited, and CMBS execution on a defect-affected asset is effectively unavailable until all material litigation is resolved and all representations and warranties required under REMIC rules can be made without qualification.

What Attorneys Handling Evansville CRE Disputes Need to Know

A real estate appraiser and a CRE financing expert answer fundamentally different questions, and conflating the two roles is a mistake I see frequently in construction defect litigation. An appraiser determines value as of a specific date using one or more standard valuation approaches. A CRE financing expert determines what the debt capital markets will do with that value: how much of it is lendable, at what cost, under what conditions, and what damages result from the impairment. If your damages theory includes impaired financing capacity, increased borrowing cost, lost refinancing proceeds, inability to execute a business plan that depended on available debt, or forced sale at distressed pricing because conventional financing was unavailable, you need a CRE financing expert. The appraiser cannot get you there.

Questions only a CRE financing expert can answer include:

  • Would a specific lender or category of lenders have funded this loan but for the defect conditions?
  • What is the maximum supportable loan on the impaired asset versus the defect-free asset, and how is that difference calculated?
  • What lender requirements, holdbacks, reserves, or conditions were triggered by the defect, and what did they cost?
  • Is the borrower's claimed financing structure (rate, term, proceeds) consistent with what the market actually offered for this asset type in this market at the relevant time?
  • What is the cost differential between the financing the property would have supported without defects and the financing it actually obtained or was forced to obtain?
  • Is a bridge loan or hard money scenario the direct result of the defect conditions, and what is the incremental carry cost?

Qualifications matter. A retired banker who made community bank loans in Indiana is not the same as a CRE financing expert who has actively brokered and closed commercial mortgage transactions across multiple asset classes, lender categories, and market cycles. Active market practice is the differentiating credential. Expert testimony on what Fannie Mae, a debt fund, or a national bank would have required requires current, firsthand knowledge of those lenders' underwriting guidelines, pricing, and credit boxes. CLS CRE maintains active relationships with over 1,000 lenders across all 50 states and has closed approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, which means my opinions are grounded in actual deal execution, not theoretical familiarity.

Information counsel should gather before the engagement: the original appraisal and any subsequent appraisals or value opinions; all loan applications, term sheets, commitment letters, or lender rejections on the subject property; the property's rent roll, trailing 12-month operating statement, and any projections prepared by the developer or operator; the engineer's or contractor's scope of defect and remediation cost estimate; all correspondence with any lender that references the property condition or litigation; and the original construction contract and project budget, which establishes the baseline against which the defect impact is measured.

Key Takeaways

  • Active construction defect litigation is a binary lender disqualifier for most conventional, agency, and community bank permanent financing programs, regardless of whether the defects have been partially or fully remediated. Litigation must be resolved before most lenders will close.
  • The income impact of defects flows directly and quantifiably through DSCR to maximum loan proceeds. In this Evansville analysis, a 37% reduction in NOI from the defect-free baseline produced a reduction in maximum supportable loan of approximately $2.47 million on a 120-unit asset, a number that is directly defensible and supported by market-standard underwriting methodology.

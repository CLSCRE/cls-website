Expert Witness: CRE Financing Feasibility in Chapter 11 Proceedings in Omaha, NE

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Bankruptcy Exit Financing

The Omaha Market Context

Omaha is not a secondary market in the pejorative sense that term sometimes implies. It is a diversified, institutionally active commercial real estate market anchored by a financial services sector, a major rail and logistics infrastructure, a growing healthcare and university presence, and one of the most stable multifamily demand profiles in the Midwest. For purposes of bankruptcy exit financing disputes, that stability matters enormously, because lender appetite is directly correlated with rent growth predictability, vacancy resilience, and exit cap rate confidence. Omaha delivers on all three.

Industrial fundamentals in Omaha have remained among the strongest in the region. Vacancy rates in the primary industrial submarkets along the I-80 corridor have hovered in the 3 to 5 percent range in recent years, driven by distribution and third-party logistics demand tied to the Union Pacific hub presence. Net asking rents have climbed from the mid-$5 per square foot range to the $7 to $9 per square foot range on new Class A product, with older functional product still leasing in the $4.50 to $6.00 range. This trajectory gives lenders a supportable rent assumption even on stabilized collateral coming out of bankruptcy.

Multifamily in Omaha tells a similar story. Cap rates on stabilized apartment assets have generally traded in the 5.25 to 6.25 percent range depending on asset class and submarket, with Midtown, Dundee, and the Aksarben Village corridor commanding premium pricing. Occupancy across the broader metro has held in the 93 to 96 percent range even as new supply has been delivered. For exit financing purposes, lenders can underwrite Omaha multifamily with a high degree of confidence in the income assumptions.

Office is the more complicated story, as it is everywhere. Omaha's suburban office market has faced elevated vacancy, particularly in older Class B product, while the downtown core has seen some repositioning activity. Vacancy in suburban Omaha office has been reported in the 15 to 20 percent range on certain product types, with concessions and tenant improvement allowances compressing effective net operating income. Lenders pricing office collateral in Omaha today are applying conservative stabilized vacancy assumptions and haircuts to contract rents that may not yet reflect market realities. That conservative posture is directly relevant when an expert is asked to opine on financing feasibility for an office asset in a Chapter 11 proceeding.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by counsel for a plan proponent in a Chapter 11 proceeding involving a commercial real estate asset located in the greater Omaha metropolitan area. The asset was a stabilized, income-producing industrial flex property, multi-tenant, with a mix of warehouse and office build-out, situated in an established industrial submarket with good freeway access and functional infrastructure. The property had been operating under the cloud of the bankruptcy for approximately eighteen months prior to our engagement, which introduced several layers of complexity into the financing analysis.

Counsel needed expert testimony that would address two central questions before the court: first, whether the property could support first-mortgage financing in an amount sufficient to pay senior secured creditors in full through a refinancing upon plan confirmation; and second, which categories of lenders in the institutional marketplace actively originate loans on assets held in bankruptcy estates, and what terms, conditions, and timing those lenders require. A third, subsidiary question was whether the financing could be executed within the timeline proposed under the plan, which had a specific post-confirmation closing deadline.

CLS CRE was engaged to prepare a written financing feasibility opinion consistent with the standards applicable to Rule 26 expert reports, including a full description of methodology, data sources, assumptions, and conclusions. Our role was not to appraise the property. Our role was to provide a capital markets opinion: given this asset, in this market, with these income characteristics, what can it finance, and from whom, and on what terms? Those are distinct questions that require a distinct expert.

The Financial Analysis

The analytical foundation of any exit financing feasibility opinion is a careful reconstruction of the property's net operating income and a stress-tested application of current lender underwriting standards to determine maximum supportable debt. In this engagement, we began with a trailing twelve-month operating statement, which we adjusted for several non-recurring items that had inflated both gross revenue and expense lines during the bankruptcy period. Deferred maintenance had suppressed capital expenditure costs on the historical statements; we normalized for that. One tenant had been operating under a forbearance arrangement that temporarily held their rent below market; we used a market rent assumption for that space consistent with current comparable lease transactions in the submarket.

The adjusted stabilized NOI we derived for this analysis was approximately $420,000 annually. That figure reflected a 7 percent economic vacancy allowance applied against gross potential rent of approximately $685,000, and stabilized operating expenses including management, insurance, taxes, and a reserves allowance of $0.15 per square foot.

The standard DSCR threshold for permanent first-mortgage financing on stabilized industrial product in a market like Omaha is 1.25x. Some lenders will underwrite at 1.20x on well-located, credit-tenanted assets; others will require 1.30x on properties with any leasing uncertainty. For purposes of this analysis, we applied 1.25x as the market standard, consistent with agency, CMBS, life company, and regional bank underwriting criteria as of the time of the engagement.

We assumed a note rate of 7.25 percent, reflecting the then-current spread environment for 10-year fixed-rate non-recourse permanent financing on industrial collateral. Amortization was set at 25 years, consistent with lender standards for this property type and vintage. Applying those inputs, the maximum supportable annual debt service at a 1.25x DSCR was $336,000. At a 7.25 percent rate on a 25-year amortization schedule, that debt service figure supports a loan principal of approximately $4,050,000.

The independent appraisal of record placed the property's as-stabilized value at $6,800,000. That yields a loan-to-value ratio of approximately 59.5 percent on our max loan figure, which is well within the 65 to 70 percent LTV ceiling that permanent lenders apply to this asset class. The analysis showed meaningful LTV headroom, meaning the constraint on loan size was DSCR-driven, not collateral-value-driven. That is an important distinction for the court, because it means the property is not undervalued relative to its financing capacity; it means the income stream is the binding constraint, and any improvement in NOI would translate directly into incremental debt capacity.

We also ran a sensitivity analysis: if the forbearance tenant executed a new lease at market rent, stabilized NOI would increase to approximately $465,000, supporting a maximum loan of approximately $4,480,000, still at 1.25x DSCR. That upper bound was relevant to the plan timeline, because the forbearance lease was expected to resolve within the post-confirmation period.

The Complicating Factors

Bankruptcy estate ownership. The single most significant complicating factor in any exit financing engagement is the fact that the collateral is owned by a Chapter 11 debtor. Most conventional lenders, including regional banks, credit unions, and agency delegated underwriting shops, will not originate a loan that closes into a bankruptcy estate or that requires the borrower to be an entity currently subject to bankruptcy court jurisdiction. This is not a matter of preference; it is a credit policy matter driven by secondary market eligibility requirements, regulatory guidance, and internal risk management standards. Understanding this constraint is essential to defining the actual lender universe available to the debtor, and to advising the court on the feasibility of a refinancing strategy embedded in the plan.

Plan-contingent financing timing. Exit financing for a Chapter 11 asset is structurally different from a conventional refinancing. The loan cannot close until the plan is confirmed and the automatic stay is lifted. Lenders who are willing to engage on this type of transaction must be prepared to hold a term sheet or commitment letter open through an uncertain confirmation timeline, which can stretch from weeks to many months depending on objections, contested hearings, and appellate activity. Most lenders are unwilling to hold rate locks for that duration without significant fees and without a clear outside closing date. Structuring the financing commitment correctly, including a clear drop-dead date, rate lock mechanics, and extension provisions, is a technical challenge that requires market knowledge and lender relationship access.

Deferred maintenance and deferred capital expenditures. Assets that have been in bankruptcy often suffer from deferred maintenance, because the debtor-in-possession has limited cash and management bandwidth. Lenders underwriting these assets will conduct property condition assessments and frequently require reserves or escrows at closing to address identified capital needs. In our engagement, the property condition assessment identified approximately $185,000 in deferred items, which the lender factored into its loan structuring by requiring an upfront reserves escrow. That reserve requirement effectively reduced net loan proceeds and required adjustment to the plan's sources and uses analysis.

Tenant uncertainty and lease rollover. In a multi-tenant industrial flex property, leases that are month-to-month, recently expired, or held by tenants in financial distress represent underwriting risk. Lenders will discount or entirely exclude income from those tenants when sizing the loan. In this engagement, one of the five tenants was on a month-to-month holdover. The lender underwrote that space as vacant, which reduced effective gross income and, consequently, the supportable loan amount. Counsel and the plan proponent needed to understand that dynamic when setting expectations for the refinancing proceeds.

The Omaha Lender Market

For exit financing on a stabilized industrial flex asset in Omaha, the realistic lender universe breaks into four categories, each with meaningfully different credit standards, pricing, and willingness to engage on bankruptcy-adjacent transactions.

Bridge and transitional lenders. This is the most active category for assets emerging from Chapter 11. Bridge lenders, including debt funds, family office lending platforms, and non-bank specialty finance companies, are specifically designed to originate loans that conventional lenders will not touch. They will lend on assets held in bankruptcy estates, often with closing contingent on plan confirmation, and they will underwrite based on as-is and as-stabilized value with more flexibility on DSCR. Their pricing in the current environment for Omaha industrial collateral ranges from approximately 9.5 to 11.5 percent, with origination fees of 1.5 to 2.5 points, interest-only periods of 12 to 24 months, and short terms of 2 to 3 years. The expectation is that the borrower refinances out into permanent financing once the asset is fully stabilized and the bankruptcy is behind it. For a plan that needs to close quickly and satisfy senior creditors at confirmation, a bridge lender is often the only viable instrument.

Regional and community banks based in Omaha and Nebraska. Omaha has a deep community banking presence, including several well-capitalized Nebraska-chartered institutions that actively originate commercial real estate loans on local product. These lenders know the market, know the submarkets, and can move with more flexibility than a national platform. However, their willingness to engage on bankruptcy estate collateral is institution-specific. Some will close a loan simultaneously with plan confirmation if the credit is strong and the relationship exists; others have hard policy restrictions. Rates from this category for stabilized industrial in Omaha have generally been in the 7.00 to 8.50 percent range on 5-year fixed terms with 20 to 25-year amortization, with recourse typically required. Loan sizes from community banks in this market generally cap in the $5 to $15 million range per relationship.

CMBS conduit lenders. Conduit execution is available for Omaha industrial assets meeting minimum loan size thresholds, generally $3 million and above. CMBS is non-recourse, fixed-rate, 10-year term, and priced to the swap spread plus a credit spread currently ranging from 175 to 225 basis points for industrial collateral. CMBS lenders will generally not originate a loan while the borrower entity is in bankruptcy, but they are a realistic permanent take-out option once the plan is confirmed and the entity is discharged. Including CMBS as the intended permanent financing vehicle in a two-step plan structure (bridge now, CMBS refinance at stabilization) is a common and defensible strategy.

Life insurance companies. Life company lending in Omaha is active, particularly through correspondent relationships that channel loans to institutional balance sheet lenders. Life companies offer the lowest pricing and longest terms available in the market, with rates currently in the 6.50 to 7.25 percent range for high-quality industrial collateral. However, life companies have the most conservative underwriting standards: maximum LTV of 55 to 65 percent, DSCR requirements of 1.30x or better, and an absolute unwillingness to originate loans on assets with any current bankruptcy involvement. Life company financing is a realistic option only after the bankruptcy is fully resolved and the asset is operating clean.

What Attorneys Handling Omaha CRE Disputes Need to Know

The most important thing I can tell attorneys handling commercial real estate litigation in Omaha is this: an appraiser and a financing expert are not interchangeable. An appraiser tells the court what the property is worth. A financing expert tells the court whether the property can be financed, by whom, on what terms, and within what timeframe. Those are four distinct questions that no appraisal report is designed to answer, and courts confirming plans of reorganization that involve a refinancing component need answers to all four.

When to retain a CRE financing expert. Counsel should consider retaining a financing expert any time a plan of reorganization proposes to satisfy creditors through a refinancing of real property collateral. The feasibility of that refinancing is a core confirmation issue under 11 U.S.C. Section 1129(a)(11), and courts take it seriously. Opposing counsel will challenge feasibility aggressively if the plan is not well supported. A written expert opinion from a credentialed capital markets professional gives the court a concrete, methodology-grounded basis for finding feasibility.

What makes a CRE financing expert qualified. A retired banker who underwrote loans twenty years ago is not a current capital markets expert. The lender market evolves rapidly, pricing changes quarter to quarter, and credit standards shift with the rate environment and regulatory climate. The right expert is someone who is actively originating or placing commercial real estate loans in the current market, has direct lender relationships, and can speak from current transaction experience rather than historical memory. At CLS CRE, we have approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, more than 1,000 active lender relationships, and we are placing loans in all 50 states. That current market activity is what gives the opinion credibility.

What information counsel should gather before the engagement. Before retaining a financing expert, counsel should assemble: the trailing 12-month and prior year operating statements for the property; the current rent roll with lease expiration dates, tenant names redacted if necessary; any existing appraisal or broker opinion of value; the proposed plan's sources and uses of funds, including the target loan amount and any required closing timeline; any existing loan documents showing the current debt structure; and the property condition assessment if one has been completed. The more complete the package, the faster and more defensible the expert analysis will be.

How financing expert testimony is used at confirmation hearings. In a contested confirmation hearing, financing feasibility testimony typically takes one of two forms: a written report submitted under Rule 26 standards, and live direct and cross-examination at the confirmation hearing. The written report should lay out the methodology in enough detail that opposing counsel can critique it and the court can evaluate it independently. At hearing, the expert should be prepared to defend the DSCR assumptions, the rate assumptions, the lender category analysis, and the timing conclusions. Cross-examination will typically focus on whether the lender market analysis reflects actual lender willingness rather than theoretical possibility. That is why current, active market access matters so much in expert selection.

Key Takeaways

  • Omaha is a fundamentally financeable market. Industrial and multifamily assets in Omaha's core submarkets carry strong NOI support, predictable vacancy profiles, and sufficient lender appetite to support exit financing in most Chapter 11 scenarios involving stabilized collateral.
  • DSCR is the binding constraint in most exit financing analyses, not LTV. For stabilized industrial product at current Omaha rent levels, the income stream typically limits loan size before collateral value does. Understanding that distinction is critical to setting creditor expectations correctly under the plan.

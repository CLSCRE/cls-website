Senior Housing and Healthcare Financing: HUD 232, Bridge-to-HUD, and Construction Capital

By Trevor Damyan·June 25, 2026·Commercial Lending Solutions

Senior Housing Financing: A Complete Guide for Healthcare Operators, SNF Advisors, and AL/MC Development Consultants

I am Trevor Damyan, a commercial mortgage broker at CLS CRE in Los Angeles. My practice focuses on complex commercial real estate financing, and senior housing is one of the asset classes I take most seriously. It sits at the intersection of real estate, healthcare operations, regulatory oversight, and specialized capital sources that most generalist brokers simply do not understand.

If you advise skilled nursing facilities, assisted living or memory care operators, or senior housing developers, your clients will face financing decisions that can define the trajectory of their business. This article is written for you: senior housing consultants, SNF advisors, AL/MC development consultants, HUD 232 program users, and PACE advisors who regularly encounter these situations and need to know how financing actually works at a technical level.

Why Senior Housing Consultants and Healthcare Operators Encounter CRE Financing Needs

Your clients are operators first, and real estate owners second. A skilled nursing facility administrator is focused on census, staffing ratios, Medicaid reimbursement rates, and regulatory surveys. An assisted living developer is navigating entitlements, design reviews, and pre-sales. A memory care operator is managing acuity levels and programming budgets. None of them wake up thinking about debt service coverage ratios or HUD MAP lender requirements.

But the financing decisions they make will directly affect their ability to operate. An SNF that overpays on a short-term bridge loan and never executes the HUD 232 refinancing will spend years bleeding cash flow to a lender instead of investing in staffing and census growth. An AL developer who cannot find the right construction lender will miss their market window. A memory care operator with strong occupancy who stays in an expensive acquisition loan when a life company permanent loan is available at 6.00% fixed is leaving significant annual savings on the table.

You will encounter these financing needs in several specific moments: when a client is selling or acquiring a facility, when an operator has stabilized a turnaround and is ready to refinance, when a developer is ready to break ground, when an existing owner needs to pull equity for a second project, and when a portfolio operator is rationalizing their capital stack across multiple communities. Each of these moments requires a different financing solution and a broker who knows the landscape.

The Five Financing Scenarios Senior Housing Advisors See Most

Scenario 1: AL or MC Operator Needs Bridge Financing to Stabilize Census Before HUD 232 Permanent Financing

This is the most common and most consequential scenario in the senior housing financing market. An assisted living or memory care community has been acquired or developed, census is building, but it is not yet at the level required for HUD 232 permanent financing. The operator is sitting in an expensive bridge loan and needs a clear path to the permanent financing that makes the business model pencil.

HUD 232 requires minimum occupancy thresholds that vary by structure, but as a practical matter, most MAP lenders want to see stabilized occupancy at or above 90 percent for at least three months before committing to the permanent financing. For memory care specifically, regulators and lenders want to see consistent acuity levels and documentation that the operator is delivering on the care model. This creates a window that can last 12 to 24 months where the operator needs bridge capital that is priced appropriately and structured with a clear exit to HUD 232.

The right bridge loan for this scenario is sized to the as-stabilized value, priced at a spread that accounts for the transition risk, and structured with an extension option tied to census milestones. Bridge lenders for senior housing include debt funds, regional banks with senior housing expertise, and specialty lenders who understand the census trajectory underwriting. Not every bridge lender will finance senior housing at all, and many who will take the deal will misprice the execution risk. My job is to find the lenders who understand the asset class, who have done these deals before, and who will price the bridge loan in a way that the math works when the operator eventually refinances into HUD 232.

Scenario 2: SNF Seeking HUD 232 Refinancing

For a stabilized skilled nursing facility, HUD 232 permanent financing is the best execution available in the market. There is no commercial real estate loan type that can match what HUD 232 delivers for a qualifying SNF: 35 to 40 year fully amortizing term, fixed rate, non-recourse to the operator, and maximum leverage. In a market where most commercial permanent loans are at 10 year terms with balloon payments and require personal guarantees, HUD 232 is in a category by itself.

The process is more demanding than conventional financing, and that is exactly why so few commercial mortgage brokers can execute it. The borrower must work with a HUD-approved MAP lender, prepare a LEAN application package, comply with Davis-Bacon wage requirements if there is a construction component, and pass HUD's detailed underwriting review. For SNFs specifically, HUD underwrites to the Medicare and Medicaid reimbursement rates, analyzes the payor mix, reviews the cost report, and evaluates the operator's track record across all facilities they operate.

The minimum occupancy requirement for SNF HUD 232 refinancing is typically 80 percent occupancy for the prior 12 months, with most MAP lenders wanting to see 85 percent or better for a clean execution. The facility must be in good regulatory standing with no immediate jeopardy citations or unresolved survey deficiencies. The operator entity must have clean financials, and if there is a management company separate from the operating entity, HUD will underwrite both.

The timeline from application to closing is 6 to 9 months for a standard LEAN submission. Plan accordingly. Operators who come to me 60 days before their bridge loan matures needing a HUD 232 have a problem. The advisors who serve them well are the ones who flag the refinancing timeline 18 months in advance so there is runway to execute properly.

Scenario 3: New AL or MC Development

A developer has land, entitlements, and a proven operating model. They need construction financing to build a new assisted living or memory care community. This is where the capital markets thin out considerably. Most conventional construction lenders are uncomfortable with the healthcare overlay: state licensing timelines, certificate of need requirements in some states, the ramp-up period before stabilization, and the specialized operations required. Senior housing construction loans require lenders who have done the asset class before and who understand that a memory care community is not the same as a multifamily project.

Construction financing for new AL and MC development typically requires 30 to 40 percent developer equity, demonstrated operating experience in the same care category, a complete set of permits and approvals, a guaranteed maximum price construction contract, and pre-opening management agreements. Lenders will underwrite to pro forma stabilized income at a discount, typically applying a haircut to the operator's occupancy projections and using conservative rate assumptions.

The construction loan will carry interest reserves for 18 to 24 months, and the best structures include a mini-perm period that gives the operator time to stabilize before refinancing into permanent financing. HUD 232 new construction is an option through the 232/221(d)(4) program, which can provide up to 40-year financing with construction period included, but the timeline and complexity are significant. Most developers use conventional construction financing for the build and then refinance into HUD 232 or conventional permanent financing once the community reaches stabilization.

Scenario 4: Senior Housing Portfolio Refinancing

A mid-size operator owns five to twelve IL, AL, and MC communities across two or three states. They grew through acquisitions and development over the past decade, and their capital stack is a patchwork of different loans at different maturities with different lenders. Some loans are maturing. Others have rates that no longer reflect current credit quality. The operator wants to rationalize the portfolio, pull equity from the better-performing assets, and get a cleaner debt structure that matches the operational reality of running a multi-site business.

Portfolio financing for senior housing requires a lender who can underwrite at the enterprise level. The analysis must account for how each community contributes to the overall portfolio, how management overhead is allocated, what the corporate guarantee structure looks like, and how to structure loans across communities that may have different care categories and regulatory environments. Some lenders will do a blanket portfolio loan. Others will do individual loans on each property but commit to the full portfolio as a package.

The best execution for a stabilized senior housing portfolio with strong occupancy and clean operations is often a life insurance company lender or a CMBS execution at the community level, combined with a portfolio overlay. Life company lenders who specialize in senior housing will look at the entire enterprise, price the credit accordingly, and offer terms that reflect the operating track record rather than just the real estate value.

Scenario 5: IL Community Ready for Life Company or CMBS Permanent Financing

An independent living community with strong occupancy, proven management, and a long operating history is one of the most attractive assets in the senior housing capital markets. Without the healthcare regulatory overlay of AL, MC, or SNF, IL communities underwrite more like multifamily from a lender's perspective while commanding cap rate compression that reflects the demographic tailwinds of an aging population.

A stabilized IL community with 92 percent or better occupancy, three or more years of consistent financial performance, and an experienced operator can access life company permanent financing at loan constants that represent the best execution in the market. Life insurance company lenders for senior housing offer 10 to 25 year fixed rates, fully amortizing or with 30-year amortization schedules, non-recourse at lower leverage, and relationship pricing that rewards repeat borrowers. CMBS is also available for IL and provides higher leverage but less flexibility on prepayment.

The underwriting for IL permanent financing focuses on net operating income at the community level, the operator's ability to maintain occupancy through the loan term, the physical condition of the asset, and the competitive landscape in the market. Life company lenders in particular will scrutinize the age and condition of the building, required capital expenditures, and the depth of the local demand for independent living. I work directly with life insurance company lenders who have dedicated senior housing programs, and the execution at this level is distinct from what a generalist broker can access.

What Lenders Actually Require: Technical Thresholds and Documentation

Every financing scenario in senior housing has specific documentation requirements that differ from conventional commercial real estate. Here is what to expect across the primary loan types:

Financial statements: Three years of audited or reviewed financial statements for the operating entity, the real estate entity, and any management company. HUD 232 requires cost reports for SNFs. Life company lenders want to see trailing 12-month financials plus year-to-date. Bridge lenders will often work with internally prepared financials but will apply a more conservative NOI underwrite.

Census and occupancy data: Monthly census reports for the trailing 24 months, broken down by care level and payor type. For SNFs, Medicare and Medicaid split matters significantly. For AL and MC, private pay percentage directly affects the underwriting multiple. Bridge lenders want to see the census trajectory. Permanent lenders want to see stability.

Regulatory standing: State licensing in good standing, most recent survey results for SNFs and licensed AL communities, any outstanding plan of correction documentation. Lenders in the senior housing space run their own regulatory database checks. Unresolved citations or survey deficiencies will slow or kill a deal.

Operator credentials: Resume and track record for the key principals, list of all other facilities operated with their occupancy and financial performance, any regulatory actions across the portfolio. First-time senior housing operators face the highest barriers to construction and permanent financing.

Market study: For construction and value-add deals, a third-party senior housing market study from an approved research firm is required. These studies analyze the competitive supply pipeline, demand drivers based on age-qualified population growth, and achievable rate ranges for the care category. HUD MAP lenders have specific requirements for market study providers.

Key leverage and coverage thresholds: HUD 232 SNF refinancing typically allows up to 80 percent LTV with 1.45x minimum DSCR. Bridge loans for AL and MC stabilization are typically 65 to 75 percent of as-stabilized value with 1.10x DSCR on in-place income. Life company permanent loans for IL communities are typically 55 to 65 percent LTV with 1.30x DSCR or better. CMBS can go to 70 to 75 percent LTV at slightly higher rates.

Questions to Ask Any Commercial Mortgage Broker Before Referring Senior Housing Clients

Not every commercial mortgage broker can execute senior housing financing. The asset class requires specialized knowledge and lender relationships that generalist brokers do not maintain. Before you refer a client, ask these questions:

Have you closed a HUD 232 loan? If the answer is no, they are not the right broker for an SNF or AL/MC HUD 232 application. This program requires specific experience with the MAP process and relationships with approved lenders.

Which life insurance company lenders do you have direct access to for senior housing? Life company lending for senior housing is relationship-driven. A broker who has never placed a senior housing deal with a life company is not well-positioned to execute one.

Can you explain how you underwrite census-based income for an AL community in lease-up? The answer should demonstrate understanding of how bridge lenders approach occupancy trajectory, how stabilized NOI is projected, and how the bridge-to-permanent structure is engineered.

What is the typical timeline for the financing scenarios my client is facing? A broker who quotes 60 days on a HUD 232 application or 30 days on a senior housing construction loan does not understand the actual process.

Do you work on a success fee basis, and what are your lender relationships in senior housing specifically? The answer should be yes to the success fee structure (no upfront retainers for standard senior housing financing), and the lender list should include HUD MAP lenders, life companies, and specialized senior housing bridge lenders by name.

Key Takeaways

  • Senior housing financing is not generalist CRE. The asset class requires specialized knowledge of HUD programs, census-based underwriting, and lender relationships that most commercial mortgage brokers do not maintain. Refer to a specialist.
  • HUD 232 is the best permanent financing available for qualifying SNFs and stabilized AL/MC communities. The 35 to 40 year fixed, non-recourse structure is unmatched in the market. The process is demanding, but the execution is worth it for the right asset.
  • Bridge-to-HUD structures require planning 18 to 24 months in advance. Operators who start the process when their bridge loan is maturing have already missed the window. The best time to engage a broker is when census is building, not when the loan is due.
  • Construction financing for new AL and MC development requires 30 to 40 percent equity and demonstrated operating experience. First-time senior housing developers face significant barriers, and the best construction lenders are selective about the operators they back.
  • Stabilized IL communities with strong occupancy have access to the best permanent financing execution in the market. Life insurance company lenders offer terms that are materially better than conventional commercial loans for qualifying assets.
  • The referral process is simple. Make the introduction, provide a brief situation summary, and let the specialist broker handle the rest. Your client gets a better outcome, and you deliver value to your relationship without taking on the complexity of the financing process yourself.
  • Ask the right questions before referring. HUD 232 experience, life company access, and census-based underwriting knowledge are non-negotiable for a senior housing specialist. Verify before you send your client.
  • Lender documentation requirements are specific and extensive. Three years of financials, trailing census reports, regulatory standing, and operator credentials are standard requirements. Advisors who help clients prepare clean documentation in advance compress the financing timeline and improve the outcome.

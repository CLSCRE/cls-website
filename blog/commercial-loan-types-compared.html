Bridge vs. CMBS vs. SBA vs. Life Company vs. Agency: Complete Commercial Loan Comparison 2026

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Commercial Lending Solutions

Why This Comparison Matters in 2026

Most borrowers come to me with a loan type already in mind. They've heard "CMBS" or "SBA" from a colleague, or they've been told by a bank they don't qualify, and they want to know if the next option on the list will work. That's not how sophisticated capital markets thinking works.

The right loan type isn't a category: it's the intersection of your asset's current state, your business plan, your hold period, your risk tolerance, and today's market pricing. I've placed loans across all ten capital sources below, and the difference between choosing correctly and incorrectly can be 150 basis points, a failed execution, or a prepayment penalty that wipes out three years of appreciation.

This guide goes deep. Real thresholds, real 2026 rate ranges, real tradeoffs. By the end, you'll have a framework for knowing where your deal fits before you spend a dollar on application fees.

1. Bridge Loans (Debt Funds and Private Non-Bank Lenders)

How It Works

Bridge loans are short-term, interest-only loans originated by non-bank debt funds, family offices, or private mortgage REITs. They're designed for transitional assets: properties that can't qualify for permanent financing yet because occupancy is low, the lease-up isn't complete, a renovation is in progress, or the cash flow doesn't support DSCR underwriting. The lender is betting on your business plan, not just current income.

Bridge lenders typically hold loans on their own balance sheet (unlike CMBS, which is securitized), which gives them flexibility to structure around non-standard situations. Proceeds are often disbursed in draws tied to construction milestones or lease-up targets.

2026 Market Snapshot

  • Rates: SOFR + 275-550 bps, all-in roughly 7.75%-10.5% depending on leverage, asset type, and sponsor quality
  • LTV: 65%-80% of as-is value; some lenders will go to 85%+ on future-stabilized value with strong sponsorship
  • DSCR: Underwritten on stabilized pro forma, not current cash flow; may not require current DSCR at all
  • Loan size: $3M-$500M+; most active debt funds prefer $10M and up
  • Term: 12-36 months, typically with 1-2 six-month extension options
  • Recourse: Non-recourse with standard carve-outs (bad boy guarantees) is common at institutional debt funds; recourse more common at smaller/private lenders
  • Prepayment: 6-12 months minimum interest, then open; sometimes step-down
  • Property types: Multifamily, office, retail, industrial, mixed-use, hospitality, land (with strong sponsorship)

Pros

  • Closes in 3-6 weeks with minimal red tape
  • Works for transitional, distressed, or non-stabilized assets
  • Flexible structuring: partial releases, future funding, interest reserves
  • Non-recourse available from institutional funds
  • Repeat borrowers with track records get better pricing

Cons

  • Most expensive capital in this guide by a significant margin
  • Extension fees (0.25%-0.50% per extension) add up fast
  • Origination fees typically 1%-2.5% of loan amount
  • Exit depends on refinancing into permanent debt: execution risk if rates move
  • Lender quality varies enormously; some funds disappear mid-deal in a downturn

Best For

Value-add acquisitions, lease-up plays, ground-up construction takeout, quick closes on off-market deals, sponsor liquidity events while permanent financing is arranged.

2. Bank and Credit Union Conventional Loans

How It Works

Community, regional, and national banks underwrite commercial real estate loans on their own balance sheet using their own credit standards. They're the most common lender for stabilized, income-producing properties under $10M. Underwriting is relationship-driven, especially at community banks where a personal guarantee and a deposit relationship can meaningfully affect terms.

Credit unions have become increasingly active in CRE, often with more competitive pricing than banks on sub-$5M multifamily and mixed-use because their cost of funds is lower.

2026 Market Snapshot

  • Rates: Fixed: 6.50%-8.25% (5- or 7-year fixed rolling to adjustable); Floating: Prime + 0-150 bps or SOFR + 200-325 bps
  • LTV: 65%-75%; some credit unions to 80% on multifamily
  • DSCR: 1.20-1.30x minimum; community banks often 1.25x global (borrower + business + property)
  • Loan size: $500K-$25M; sweet spot is $2M-$10M
  • Term: 5-10 year fixed periods, 20-25 year amortization
  • Recourse: Full recourse, almost always; some larger regional banks offer non-recourse at lower LTV
  • Prepayment: Step-down (5-4-3-2-1), fixed percentage, or yield maintenance depending on bank
  • Property types: Multifamily, office, retail, industrial, self-storage, mixed-use, NNN

Pros

  • Relationship-based: a good banker is a long-term asset
  • Can combine construction and permanent into one deal
  • Portfolio flexibility: bank can modify terms if deal goes sideways
  • Often the cheapest rate for sub-$5M deals with strong borrower relationships

Cons

  • Full recourse: personal guarantee required
  • CRE concentration limits mean banks pull back in downturns (happened in 2023-2024)
  • Slower underwriting than bridge; 45-90 day closes common
  • Maximum LTV and loan sizes are more conservative than agency or CMBS
  • Stricter global cash flow requirements that can catch wealthy-but-leveraged borrowers

Best For

Stabilized, income-producing properties under $15M with a borrower who has an existing banking relationship, strong personal financials, and wants recourse risk in exchange for better rate and terms.

3. Life Insurance Company Loans

How It Works

Life companies (MetLife, Prudential, Principal, Pacific Life, Nationwide, and 50+ others) are among the most desirable lenders in commercial real estate and among the least understood. They match long-duration liabilities (life insurance policies) against long-duration assets (commercial mortgages). The result: the lowest fixed rates available in the market, non-recourse, with minimal ongoing reporting requirements.

Life companies typically access deals through correspondents (firms with exclusive or preferred allocation relationships) or through mortgage brokers. I work directly with correspondents, which gives our clients access to life company allocations that never hit a broker market.

2026 Market Snapshot

  • Rates: 5.75%-6.75% fixed for 10 years (Treasury + 130-175 bps spread); best-in-class assets and sponsors at the low end
  • LTV: 55%-70%; life companies are conservative: they prioritize credit quality over leverage
  • DSCR: 1.25-1.35x minimum; most underwrite to 1.30x or higher
  • Loan size: $5M-$500M+; minimum is typically $5M, some require $10M+
  • Term: 7, 10, 15, 20, or 25-year fixed terms; 25-30 year amortization or interest-only
  • Recourse: Non-recourse with standard carve-outs
  • Prepayment: Yield maintenance or defeasance: expensive to exit early; life companies are not short-term lenders
  • Property types: Multifamily, industrial, grocery-anchored retail, Class A office (selective), self-storage, NNN (investment grade tenants)

Pros

  • Best fixed rates available: typically 75-125 bps lower than CMBS
  • Non-recourse
  • Minimal ongoing compliance burden: no third-party servicer complications
  • Flexible loan structuring (partial I/O, step-up amortization, earnout provisions)
  • Relationship-oriented: life companies want repeat business and work with borrowers when deals need modifications

Cons

  • Conservative LTV means more equity required
  • Selective on property type: no construction, minimal hospitality or special purpose
  • Yield maintenance prepayment is punishing; designed for buy-and-hold
  • Allocation-based: some companies run out of capital mid-year; timing matters
  • 45-75 day close timeline; more documentation than banks

Best For

Core and core-plus assets with a 7-15 year hold plan where the borrower wants the lowest possible fixed rate, non-recourse protection, and doesn't need near-term exit flexibility.

4. CMBS Conduit Loans

How It Works

Conduit lenders originate fixed-rate commercial mortgages, pool them together, and sell them as commercial mortgage-backed securities (CMBS) to bond investors. Because the loan is sold within 90 days of closing, conduit lenders don't hold credit risk: the pricing is set by the CMBS bond market, not the lender's balance sheet.

CMBS is the non-recourse option for larger loans on property types that life companies avoid: retail, hospitality, mixed-use with complexity, and secondary markets. The tradeoff is inflexibility: once securitized, your loan goes to a special servicer if you have issues, and they are contractually obligated to maximize bondholder recovery, not help you.

2026 Market Snapshot

  • Rates: 6.25%-7.50% fixed (10-year Treasury + 175-275 bps); rate driven by current CMBS spreads in secondary market
  • LTV: 65%-75%; 70% is common for standard assets
  • DSCR: 1.25x minimum; many conduits underwrite to 1.30x or higher; 8%-9.5% stressed debt yield requirement
  • Loan size: $2M-$100M+; most active in $5M-$50M range
  • Term: 5, 7, or 10 years; 25-30 year amortization or interest-only
  • Recourse: Non-recourse with carve-outs
  • Prepayment: Defeasance or yield maintenance: extremely expensive to exit early
  • Property types: All property types; hospitality and retail are CMBS territory where life companies typically don't go

Pros

  • Non-recourse on loan sizes and property types that banks require recourse for
  • Aggressive leverage on stabilized assets
  • Willing to lend in secondary and tertiary markets where life companies are selective
  • Can do supplemental financing after initial loan seasoning

Cons

  • Inflexible after securitization: no loan modifications, no partial releases without complex approval processes
  • Special servicing is adversarial; misaligned incentives with borrower
  • Defeasance cost can be extraordinary in a rising-rate environment
  • Significant upfront costs: rate lock fee, third-party reports (appraisal, Phase I, PCA), legal fees. Plan for $50K-$150K before close
  • 90-120 day close timeline

Best For

Stabilized assets in the $5M-$75M range where non-recourse is required, especially retail, hospitality, or secondary market properties that don't fit life company criteria, with a borrower committed to a 5-10 year hold.

5. Agency: Fannie Mae (DUS Lenders)

How It Works

Fannie Mae's Delegated Underwriting and Servicing (DUS) program allows approved lenders to underwrite, close, and service multifamily loans using Fannie's guidelines, and sell the loans to Fannie, which packages them into agency MBS. DUS lenders share in the credit risk (typically one-third), which aligns their incentives with borrower success. This is fundamentally a multifamily-only program.

2026 Market Snapshot

  • Rates: 5.60%-6.80% fixed; benchmarked to 10-year Treasury + agency spread (typically 120-175 bps); floating rate options available at SOFR + 150-250 bps
  • LTV: Up to 80% (market-rate multifamily); 85%+ for affordable housing
  • DSCR: 1.25x minimum; 1.20x for affordable, green/energy-efficient, and certain workforce housing
  • Loan size: $1M minimum; no maximum
  • Term: 5, 7, 10, 12, 15, 18, 30 years; interest-only periods available
  • Recourse: Non-recourse with standard carve-outs
  • Prepayment: Yield maintenance or step-down; declining prepayment available on some products
  • Property types: Multifamily (5+ units) only; student housing, senior housing, manufactured housing, cooperatives each have separate products

Pros

  • Lowest rates in the multifamily space, especially with green certification discounts (up to 30 bps)
  • High leverage available non-recourse
  • Supplemental loans available after 12 months of seasoning
  • Competitive on affordable housing with additional LTV and DSCR cushion
  • Long-term fixed rates provide certainty for buy-and-hold investors

Cons

  • Multifamily only: no commercial property types
  • Requires full property inspection, environmental review, and third-party reports
  • 60-90 day close; not designed for speed
  • Occupancy requirements: typically 90%+ trailing 90 days
  • Loan assumption is allowed but requires Fannie approval and fees

Best For

Stabilized multifamily acquisitions or refinances where maximum leverage, non-recourse, and the lowest available fixed rate are the priority, especially for borrowers with a 7-15 year hold plan.

6. Agency: Freddie Mac (Optigo Network)

How It Works

Freddie Mac's multifamily lending operates through the Optigo network of approved lenders. The mechanics are similar to Fannie DUS: Optigo lenders underwrite and close using Freddie's guidelines, then sell the loans. Freddie and Fannie serve the same multifamily market but have different programs, different pricing on any given day, and somewhat different property type emphases: running both simultaneously to find best execution is standard practice.

2026 Market Snapshot

  • Rates: 5.55%-6.75% fixed; generally competitive with Fannie within 5-15 bps, better on some products
  • LTV: Up to 80% standard; higher for targeted affordable housing
  • DSCR: 1.20-1.25x minimum depending on product
  • Loan size: $1M minimum; no maximum
  • Term: 5-30 years; Freddie has been more aggressive than Fannie on shorter fixed terms and floating-rate products
  • Recourse: Non-recourse with carve-outs
  • Prepayment: Yield maintenance or step-down; some products have more flexible prepayment than Fannie equivalents

Freddie vs. Fannie: When to Prefer Freddie

  • Freddie's Small Balance Loan (SBL) program ($1M-$7.5M) is purpose-built for smaller multifamily and typically easier to execute than Fannie's equivalent
  • Freddie's floating-rate products are often priced more aggressively than Fannie's in a specific rate environment
  • Freddie tends to be more aggressive on student housing and manufactured housing communities
  • Tax-exempt bond financing integration is more developed through Freddie's TEL product

Best For

Multifamily under $7.5M (Freddie SBL), student housing, floating-rate execution when rates are expected to decline, and markets where Freddie's specific correspondent relationships give better execution than Fannie's DUS network.

7. SBA 7(a) Loans

How It Works

The SBA 7(a) program provides a government guarantee (typically 75%-85%) on loans made by approved banks and credit unions. The guarantee allows lenders to extend credit to borrowers who wouldn't otherwise qualify conventionally. For real estate, 7(a) is typically used for owner-occupied properties where the business occupies 51%+ of the space.

The guarantee is the critical mechanism: the SBA doesn't lend money, it insures the bank against loss, enabling lower down payments and more flexible underwriting than conventional loans.

2026 Market Snapshot

  • Rates: Prime + 2.75% for loans over $700K (Prime is 7.50% as of mid-2026, so all-in around 10.25%); rate cap is Prime + 2.75% for 7-year maturities and longer: rates are floating and adjust quarterly
  • LTV: Up to 90% (the key advantage); 10% down for qualifying owner-occupied CRE
  • DSCR: 1.15-1.20x minimum; global cash flow (business + real estate) is required
  • Loan size: Up to $5M maximum; most lenders prefer $500K-$3M for real estate
  • Term: Up to 25 years for real estate; fully amortizing
  • Recourse: Full recourse; personal guarantee from all 20%+ owners required
  • Prepayment: No prepayment penalty after 3 years; 5-4-3% fee in years 1-3
  • Property types: Owner-occupied only: medical offices, dental practices, professional offices, warehouses, car washes, restaurants (the business must be the primary tenant)

Pros

  • 90% LTV is extraordinary: no other program offers this for small business real estate
  • 25-year amortization reduces monthly payment vs. bank loans
  • Working capital can be bundled into the same loan (equipment, renovations, startup costs)
  • Available for new businesses and startups that can't qualify conventionally
  • No balloon payment: fully amortizing protects against refinance risk

Cons

  • Rate is Prime-based and floating: borrowers took significant payment shock in 2022-2023
  • Full personal recourse required
  • $5M cap limits applicability for larger properties
  • Bureaucratic process: SBA paperwork is extensive; 60-90 day close is optimistic
  • Owner-occupancy requirement is strict; investment properties don't qualify
  • Annual SBA guaranty fee (approximately 0.55%-3.5% of guaranteed portion) adds cost

Best For

Small business owners purchasing or refinancing their own operating facility who need maximum leverage and long amortization to keep cash flow manageable, and who have strong business income to support global DSCR.

8. SBA 504 Loans

How It Works

SBA 504 is a two-tranche structure. The first mortgage (50% of project cost) is made by a bank at conventional terms. The second mortgage (40% of project cost) is made by a Certified Development Company (CDC) using SBA-backed debentures sold to the bond market. The borrower contributes 10%. The CDC/SBA second mortgage carries a fixed rate tied to 10-year Treasury that is typically well below market: this is the defining advantage of 504.

2026 Market Snapshot

  • Rates: First mortgage: bank market rate (6.50%-8.00% fixed or floating); CDC second mortgage: approximately 5.90%-6.30% fixed 20-year (set monthly based on debenture sale); blended rate is typically 6.25%-7.25%
  • LTV: 90% of project cost (bank 50% + CDC 40% + borrower 10%)
  • DSCR: 1.15-1.20x combined; global cash flow
  • Loan size: CDC portion: $5M maximum ($5.5M for manufacturers and energy projects); first mortgage size is unrestricted
  • Term: CDC debenture: 10 or 20 years, fully amortizing; bank first: typically 10 years
  • Recourse: Full recourse on both tranches
  • Prepayment: CDC portion has a declining prepayment for the first half of the loan term (10 years for a 20-year loan); substantial in early years
  • Property types: Owner-occupied, same restrictions as 7(a); at least 51% owner occupancy required

504 vs. 7(a) for Real Estate

Factor SBA 7(a) SBA 504
Rate type Floating (Prime-based) Fixed (CDC portion)
Max loan size $5M total Effectively unlimited (bank portion unrestricted)
Down payment 10% 10%
Amortization 25 years 20 years (CDC); 10-25 years (bank)
Working capital Yes, can bundle No: real estate and equipment only
Rate risk High (floating) Low (CDC portion fixed)

Best For

Owner-users who want rate certainty (the fixed CDC tranche), are buying or building a facility in the $2M-$20M range, and value long-term payment predictability over all-in flexibility.

9. HUD/FHA Multifamily and Healthcare

How It Works

HUD's multifamily mortgage insurance programs (Section 221(d)(4) for new construction/substantial rehab, Section 223(f) for existing multifamily, Section 232 for senior housing/healthcare) are the most aggressive long-term financing in commercial real estate. HUD insures approved MAP lenders against loss, which is why a HUD loan can be non-recourse, fully assumable, 35-40 year fixed, and at rates that approach life company pricing.

The tradeoff is time, complexity, and compliance. HUD is not for borrowers who want a quick close or minimal oversight.

2026 Market Snapshot

  • Rates: 5.50%-6.25% fixed for 35-40 years (HUD MIP + Ginnie Mae spread); rate includes MIP of 25-60 bps annually
  • LTV: 83.3%-90% depending on program and property type; highest leverage in the permanent debt market
  • DSCR: 1.17-1.20x; HUD is the most permissive on coverage for multifamily
  • Loan size: No minimum or maximum; practical minimum around $2M due to cost structure
  • Term: 35 years for 223(f) existing; 40 years for 221(d)(4) new construction
  • Recourse: Non-recourse with standard carve-outs
  • Prepayment: 10-year lockout, then 9-8-7-6-5-4-3-2-1% declining; fully assumable after 2 years (major value for exit)
  • Property types: Multifamily (5+ units), senior housing, assisted living, skilled nursing facilities

Pros

  • 35-40 year fixed rate eliminates refinance and interest rate risk entirely
  • Highest LTV available on a non-recourse basis anywhere in the market
  • Fully assumable: a significant exit strategy advantage when rates rise
  • 1.17x DSCR is among the most lenient in the permanent debt space
  • Ginnie Mae backing makes HUD loans the safest execution: no counterparty risk

Cons

  • 6-12 month processing timeline for 221(d)(4); 4-6 months for 223(f)
  • Davis-Bacon prevailing wage requirements for construction and substantial rehab: adds 15%-30% to construction cost
  • Extensive ongoing compliance: annual inspections, financial reporting, HUD approval for refinances and sales
  • No flexibility: HUD rules are federal law, not lender policy
  • LIHTC and affordable housing integration is complex

Best For

Multifamily developers and operators with a long-term hold horizon who want the lowest long-term borrowing cost available, are comfortable with the regulatory overlay, and see assumability as a key exit strategy.

10. Hard Money and Private Capital

How It Works

Hard money lenders are private individuals, family offices, or small funds that make short-term loans based primarily on collateral value with minimal underwriting of borrower creditworthiness. The name reflects the hard asset (real estate) as collateral. These loans exist at the intersection of speed and desperation: they close in days to weeks, require minimal documentation, and price accordingly.

I use the term "last resort or fastest close" deliberately. There are legitimate uses for hard money, but the cost profile requires that either the spread on the deal is extraordinary or the alternative is losing the deal entirely.

2026 Market Snapshot

  • Rates: 10%-14%+ fixed or floating; points of 2-5% upfront
  • LTV: 50%-70% of current value; lenders lend what they're comfortable owning
  • DSCR: Often not required: collateral-first underwriting
  • Loan size: $100K-$10M typical; larger deals generally move to institutional bridge
  • Term: 6-24 months
  • Recourse: Usually recourse; some non-recourse at lower LTV
  • Prepayment: 3-6 months minimum interest; some fully open
  • Property types: Any; fix-and-flip residential, distressed CRE, land, special purpose

When Hard Money Is the Right Answer

  • Closing must happen in 5-10 days and no other capital source can execute
  • Borrower credit is severely impaired but the collateral is clean
  • Property condition doesn't qualify for any institutional bridge program
  • Deal economics are so strong that 12%+ all-in cost still yields adequate returns

Best For

Distressed acquisitions requiring emergency closes, properties in severe disrepair below institutional bridge thresholds, and borrowers in credit events that preclude any other financing option.

Decision Framework: Matching Your Situation to the Right Loan

Step 1: Is the Asset Stabilized?

If yes (90%+ occupied, 12 months of operating history): proceed to Step 2.

If no (lease-up, value-add, renovation, construction): your universe is Bridge Loans (institutional debt fund), Hard Money (if bridge doesn't qualify), or construction-to-perm bank financing. The stabilized programs (life company, CMBS, agency, HUD) all require operating history and current cash flow.

Step 2: Is the Property Multifamily?

If yes: Agency (Fannie/Freddie) and HUD are available in addition to all other options. Agency is almost always the best execution for stabilized multifamily over $2M. HUD wins on 35-year fixed for long-term holds. Life company is competitive for lower-leverage deals where the borrower values relationship flexibility.

If no (office, retail, industrial, hospitality, mixed-use): Agency and HUD are not available. Your universe is bank, life company, CMBS, bridge, or SBA (if owner-occupied).

Step 3: Is This Owner-Occupied?

If yes, and you're a small business owner: SBA 7(a) or 504 are the starting point. 90% LTV with a 25-year amortization on a fixed-rate 504 second tranche is rarely beaten for owner-users. The floating-rate risk of 7(a) is the primary counterargument.

If no (investment property): SBA programs are not available.

Step 4: What Is Your Hold Period?

  • Under 3 years: Bridge loans, bank floating-rate. Any fixed-rate product with yield maintenance will eliminate your profit on exit.
  • 3-7 years: CMBS 5 or 7-year term, bank 5-year fixed, agency with step-down prepayment. Watch prepayment structure carefully.
  • 7-15 years: Life company, agency, CMBS 10-year. Life company offers the best rate if asset and borrower qualify.
  • 15+ years: HUD/FHA (35-40 year fixed, fully assumable) or life company with 15-20 year term. Long-duration fixed financing eliminates refinance risk.

Step 5: What Is Your Recourse Tolerance?

  • Non-recourse required: Life company, CMBS, agency (Fannie/Freddie), HUD, institutional bridge. All offer non-recourse with standard bad-boy carve-outs.
  • Recourse acceptable: Bank conventional and credit unions typically offer better rates with full recourse. SBA requires full recourse: it's the cost of maximum leverage.

Step 6: How Urgent Is Your Timeline?

  • Under 30 days: Bridge loans only. Institutional debt funds close in 3-4 weeks. Hard money in 5-10 days. No other option in this guide can reliably close in under 30 days.
  • 30-60 days: Bridge, bank conventional, some CMBS conduits with efficient third-party reports.
  • 60-90 days: CMBS, life company, agency. Normal execution territory.
  • 90-180 days: HUD/FHA 223(f), SBA. These programs require patience in exchange for the best long-term terms available.

One More Thing: Rate Isn't Everything

I've placed a bridge loan at 9.5% that was the right answer over a life company loan at 6.25%. The reason: the borrower had a 24-month value-add plan that would increase the property's value by 40%. Locking into life company prepayment at 6.25% would have cost more in yield maintenance on exit than the 325 bps rate differential saved over two years. The bridge lender closed in 21 days, funded a capex reserve, and the borrower refinanced into agency at stabilization. Total cost of capital was lower than the alternative that looked cheaper on the term sheet.

This is why capital structure strategy isn't about finding the lowest rate. It's about matching the right instrument to the specific business plan, timeline, and exit.

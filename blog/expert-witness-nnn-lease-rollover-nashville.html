Expert Witness: NNN Lease Rollover Upside and Financing Impact in Nashville, TN

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Below-Market NNN Lease and Rollover Upside Analysis

The Nashville Market Context

Nashville's commercial real estate market has undergone a structural transformation over the past decade that makes it one of the more analytically interesting markets in the country for single-tenant net lease disputes. The metro absorbed a significant wave of corporate relocations and expansions, anchored by healthcare, music industry, technology, and logistics tenants. That demand compressed cap rates on stabilized NNN product to levels that, in hindsight, left very little room for lease-level underwriting error. When a below-market lease sits inside a property that traded at a 5.5% to 6.5% cap rate during the peak cycle, the gap between what the asset is worth on in-place income versus what it could be worth at market rent is not academic. That gap is the center of the dispute in engagements like the one described here.

For single-tenant NNN retail and industrial properties specifically, Nashville's Southeast submarket and its Murfreesboro Road corridor, the Airport submarket around Berry Hill, and the Antioch industrial pocket have all seen significant rent appreciation since 2019. Industrial rents in these corridors moved from roughly $6.00 to $8.00 per square foot triple net in 2018 to $10.00 to $14.00 per square foot triple net by 2023 and 2024, depending on clear height and access configuration. For a tenant who locked in a long-term NNN lease at 2018 rent levels, the in-place rent can be 30% to 45% below current market, and that spread is precisely what generates both the rollover upside argument and the lender resistance that drives litigation.

On the retail NNN side, Nashville's suburban corridors, particularly Brentwood, Franklin, and the Donelson area, have seen strong rent performance for service-oriented tenants. Medical-adjacent retail, quick-service food concepts, and auto service NNN properties have held firm. By contrast, single-tenant retail with less durable tenancy or shorter remaining lease term has experienced wider bid-ask spreads between buyer expectations and what the debt markets will actually support. That tension between perceived upside and lender discipline is where expert financing analysis becomes critical in litigation.

The Engagement

In this matter, we were retained by litigation counsel representing one party in a dispute involving a single-tenant NNN commercial property in the Nashville metropolitan area. The asset was a freestanding commercial building occupied by a single tenant under a long-term absolute NNN lease with approximately four years of remaining term at the time of the dispute. The in-place base rent was materially below current market rent for comparable NNN space in the submarket, a fact that both parties acknowledged. The dispute centered on the financial consequences of that below-market position and the alleged inability to refinance the property at terms that would have been achievable had the lease been at or near market.

Counsel needed answers to three core questions. First, how does the commercial lending market actually underwrite a NNN property with a below-market in-place lease, and does the lender give any credit for rollover upside when sizing the loan? Second, given the in-place NOI, what is the maximum supportable loan at commercially reasonable underwriting parameters, and how does that compare to the loan that would have been supportable at market rent? Third, does the lease structure, specifically its combination of below-market rent, limited remaining term, and NNN credit profile, preclude refinancing at the LTV and DSCR thresholds that were assumed or required by the parties' prior financial arrangements?

These are not questions an appraiser answers with standard methodology. An appraiser values the fee interest. A financing expert analyzes the capital markets, lender credit criteria, and loan sizing mechanics that determine whether a specific financing is achievable and on what terms. Those are distinct disciplines, and the distinction mattered here.

The Financial Analysis

Our methodology began with a reconstruction of the property's net operating income from the in-place lease, which we treated as the only income stream a conventional lender would underwrite given the lease structure. The in-place base rent produced an annual gross revenue figure of approximately $520,000, against which we applied actual NNN expense reimbursements and a market-standard vacancy reserve of 5% to reflect rollover risk, arriving at an underwritten NOI of approximately $494,000.

The DSCR threshold applied was 1.25x, which is the standard minimum for most conventional commercial mortgage lenders on a single-tenant retail or light industrial NNN property with a sub-five-year remaining lease term. Some life insurance company lenders apply 1.30x on shorter lease term assets as a risk adjustment, but 1.25x is the market floor and the appropriate standard for this analysis. A 1.20x DSCR is occasionally achievable for investment-grade tenants with long remaining term, but applying that threshold here would have misrepresented lender behavior for the specific risk profile presented.

We assumed a loan at a 6.75% interest rate on a 25-year amortization schedule, which reflected the rate environment at the relevant financing date within the dispute timeline. At a 1.25x DSCR and those debt service parameters, the maximum annual debt service the NOI could support was $395,200, derived by dividing the $494,000 NOI by the 1.25x coverage requirement. We then calculated the loan amount that would generate $395,200 in annual debt service at a 6.75% rate on a 25-year amortization, using a standard mortgage constant of approximately 0.0868. That calculation produced a maximum supportable loan of approximately $4,552,000.

The property's appraised value at the time was approximately $7,800,000, reflecting the market's recognition of rollover upside and the submarket's strong rent trajectory. At a $4,552,000 maximum debt service-constrained loan, the implied LTV was 58.4%. Standard LTV limits for this asset class and complexity are 65% to 70% for conventional lenders, meaning the LTV threshold was not the binding constraint. The DSCR was. The property had equity headroom by LTV measure but was income-constrained by its below-market lease.

For comparison, we ran the same analysis at market rent, which we established at $780,000 annual gross revenue based on comparable lease transactions in the submarket, yielding an underwritten NOI of approximately $741,000. At the same DSCR and debt service parameters, the market-rent NOI would support a loan of approximately $6,828,000, an LTV of 87.5% of the appraised value, which exceeds conventional thresholds but illustrates that at 65% LTV the market-rent loan would have been approximately $5,070,000. The financing gap attributable to the below-market lease was approximately $518,000 to $1,276,000 depending on whether the constraint was DSCR or LTV, a figure that had direct bearing on the damages analysis in the matter.

The Complicating Factors

Lender Treatment of Rollover Upside

The most consequential misconception in single-tenant NNN financing disputes is the assumption that lenders will give credit for rollover upside when sizing a loan. They do not, with very narrow exceptions. A commercial mortgage lender underwrites the contractual income stream in place on the day the loan closes. If the lease has four years of remaining term and the rent is below market, the lender models the cash flow it knows exists, not the cash flow that might exist after the tenant renews, vacates, or is replaced. The potential for higher rents at rollover is priced into the cap rate by equity investors; it is not factored into debt sizing by lenders. Conflating these two disciplines is a common error in disputes of this type, and it is one of the primary analytical failures we were asked to address.

Remaining Lease Term and Loan Term Misalignment

A property with four years of remaining lease term creates a structural problem for conventional permanent financing. Most permanent commercial mortgage lenders, including CMBS conduits, life insurance companies, and regional banks, want the lease term to extend at least to the maturity of the loan. A ten-year fixed-rate loan on a property with four years of remaining NNN lease term is almost never executable with a conventional lender. Bridge lenders will finance the asset, but their rate environment, 8.5% to 10.5% at relevant points in the dispute timeline, and their typical LTV constraints of 60% to 65% on transitional assets substantially change the debt service arithmetic. When parties to a dispute modeled financing economics based on conventional permanent debt terms but the asset's lease structure only supported bridge financing, the resulting financing gap and cost differential are quantifiable and material.

Single-Tenant Concentration Risk

NNN properties are by definition 100% concentrated in a single tenant. Lenders accept that concentration because the lease is absolute NNN, meaning the tenant bears all operating cost risk, and because the credit quality of the tenant justifies underwriting the lease as a credit instrument. When that tenant's credit is non-investment grade, or when the remaining lease term is short enough that the lender is effectively underwriting a vacant building at maturity, the credit argument weakens. In Nashville, where industrial and retail replacement tenants have generally been available due to strong market demand, a lender might view rollover risk as manageable, but that view affects loan pricing, not loan sizing. The debt service constraint remains the in-place income.

NNN Lease Structure and Lender Escrow Requirements

Even in a true absolute NNN structure where the tenant pays all taxes, insurance, and maintenance, lenders on short remaining-term assets will often require capital reserves or lease rollover reserves at origination. A lender funding a loan with four years of remaining lease term may require a rollover reserve equal to six to twelve months of debt service, effectively reducing the net loan proceeds available to the borrower by $230,000 to $455,000 at the debt service levels modeled here. This reserve requirement is a financing term that affects actual proceeds, and it is a factor that must be addressed when analyzing whether a specific refinancing was achievable at the terms assumed by the parties.

The Nashville Lender Market

Nashville benefits from deep and diverse capital market participation, which is relevant to this analysis because it expands the range of executable financing structures, even if it does not eliminate the income constraints described above.

Life insurance companies are active in Nashville for NNN assets with long lease terms and investment-grade tenancy. Their loan parameters are conservative, typically 60% to 65% LTV and 1.25x to 1.30x DSCR minimum, and they generally require at least seven to ten years of remaining lease term. For the asset described in this matter, life company execution was not available. This is a factual capital markets conclusion, not an opinion.

CMBS conduit lenders operate in Nashville across property types and will consider NNN product with shorter lease terms, but they apply a seasoned underwriting discipline to rollover risk. A CMBS lender will typically size the loan based on in-place NOI and apply a stressed debt yield test, often 8.5% to 9.5% debt yield minimum, which further constrains loan proceeds on a below-market income asset. At $494,000 NOI and a 9.0% debt yield test, the maximum CMBS loan would be $5,489,000, an LTV of 70.4%. That is consistent with our DSCR-constrained analysis and confirms the income constraint as the binding factor.

Regional and community banks in Nashville represent the deepest pool of available capital for NNN product of this complexity. Institutions headquartered in or with significant Middle Tennessee operations have been active lenders on single-tenant assets, often at floating rates tied to SOFR or prime with recourse from the sponsoring entity. Their LTV limits are typically 65% to 70%, and their DSCR minimums are consistent with conventional market standards at 1.20x to 1.25x. They may underwrite shorter remaining lease terms more flexibly than institutional lenders, but they still size to in-place income and require borrower guarantees that institutional lenders may waive.

Debt funds and bridge lenders are active in Nashville and represent the realistic financing path for the asset described here, given the lease term profile. Their pricing and structural requirements substantially change the economics relative to permanent financing assumptions, and that differential is directly relevant to the damages analysis in matters of this type.

What Attorneys Handling Nashville CRE Disputes Need to Know

CRE financing expert testimony fills a gap that neither appraiser testimony nor banking fact witnesses reliably address. An appraiser tells the court what a property is worth. A retired banker tells the court what their former institution might have done. A qualified CRE financing expert tells the court what the market would have done, based on demonstrated active lender engagement, current transaction experience, and documented knowledge of underwriting standards across the full range of capital sources.

Retain a financing expert early when the dispute involves any of the following: alleged inability to refinance or finance a transaction, damages tied to financing costs or lost proceeds, disputes over whether a loan was commercially reasonable, valuation disputes where income and leverage interact, plan confirmation in bankruptcy where feasibility of exit financing is contested, and breach of contract claims where one party's financing obligation is at issue.

The questions a CRE financing expert answers that an appraiser cannot include: What is the maximum loan supportable by this income stream at market underwriting parameters? What category of lenders would have been available for this asset at this point in time? What would loan proceeds, rate, and structure have looked like under each available lender category? Does the lease structure preclude or enable a specific financing? What reserves or structural requirements would a lender have imposed? What is the cost differential between the financing the parties assumed and the financing the market would have delivered?

Qualifications matter. A qualified CRE financing expert should be an active market participant, not a retired lender or an academic. The expert should have current lender relationships across multiple capital categories, demonstrated experience arranging complex commercial financings, and verifiable transaction volume that supports authority to testify about market conditions. At Commercial Lending Solutions, our approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, 1,000-plus active lender relationships, and practice spanning all 50 states provide the factual foundation for credible market testimony. Credentials derived from current market activity are substantially more credible than those based on historical banking experience alone.

Before engaging a CRE financing expert, counsel should gather: the complete lease document including all amendments and rent schedules; all appraisals and broker opinions of value; any loan applications, term sheets, or lender communications; financial statements and rent rolls; the loan or transaction agreement at issue; and any party communications that reference financing assumptions or expectations. The expert's analysis is only as reliable as the document record on which it is built.

At trial or in a Plan confirmation hearing, CRE financing expert testimony is most effective when it is grounded in contemporaneous market data, references specific lender underwriting criteria, and presents the loan sizing mathematics transparently so that fact finders can follow the logic. Opinion testimony that concludes only that financing was or was not available is less persuasive than testimony that shows precisely why, at what constraints, and with what cost differential.

Key Takeaways

  • Lenders underwrite to in-place NOI, not rollover upside. In a below-market NNN lease dispute, the loan amount a property can support is determined entirely by the contractual rent in place, not by the rent that might be achieved at rollover. This is a settled feature of commercial mortgage underwriting, not a matter of lender discretion.
  • The DSCR constraint can be more restrictive than the LTV constraint. On below-market lease assets, the income constraint often becomes the binding factor before the LTV ceiling is reached. A property can have substantial equity value while still being unable to support the loan amount the parties assumed or required.
  • Remaining lease term controls the eligible lender universe. A single-tenant NNN property with four years or fewer of remaining lease term is not eligible for permanent financing from life companies or most CMBS programs. The available capital shifts to bridge and transitional lenders whose pricing and structural requirements materially change the financing economics.
  • The financing gap is quantifiable. The difference between the loan supportable at in-place rent and the loan supportable at market rent, and between bridge financing terms and permanent financing terms, can be calculated with precision. That calculation is a legitimate component of a damages analysis in disputes of this type.
  • Nashville's lender market is deep but not undisciplined. The presence of multiple active lender categories in Nashville does not mean a financing is always achievable at assumed terms. Depth of market means more execution options, not relaxed underwriting standards.
  • NNN lease rollover reserves reduce effective loan proceeds. Short remaining lease term triggers reserve requirements that reduce the net proceeds a borrower receives at closing, a factor that is routinely overlooked in damages analyses but is directly relevant to the financing shortfall calculation.

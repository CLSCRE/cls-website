Expert Witness: CRE Financing Feasibility in Chapter 11 Proceedings in Abilene, TX

Trevor Damyan Founder & Principal Broker

The Abilene Market Context

Abilene, Texas is not a gateway market, and that distinction matters enormously in a bankruptcy exit financing analysis. With a metropolitan population of roughly 170,000 anchored by Dyess Air Force Base, Hendrick Health System, and three universities, Abilene operates as a regional service hub for a wide swath of West Texas. Its commercial real estate fundamentals reflect that role: stable but not high-growth, driven by government employment, healthcare, and retail trade rather than technology or logistics expansion. Understanding that profile is the starting point for any credible financing feasibility opinion, because lenders price secondary and tertiary markets differently than primary markets, and courts need to understand that distinction when evaluating a Plan of Reorganization that depends on refinance proceeds.

Multifamily in Abilene has held up reasonably well relative to other secondary Texas markets. Vacancy rates have generally remained in the 7% to 10% range across stabilized Class B and Class C product, with effective rents for two-bedroom units ranging from approximately $850 to $1,150 per month depending on submarket and vintage. The Taylor County corridor near the university campuses has shown the most consistent absorption. Industrial product in Abilene is limited in scale but functional, with modest flex and light industrial vacancy in the 8% to 12% range and lease rates in the $5.50 to $7.50 per square foot net range for smaller bay configurations. Retail is bifurcated: grocery-anchored and necessity-based retail has maintained occupancy, while secondary strip retail has experienced softness consistent with national trends. Office fundamentals are challenged, with downtown Abilene carrying above-market vacancy and medical office near the hospital campus representing the more defensible segment.

For Chapter 11 exit financing purposes, these fundamentals define the universe of realistic lenders and realistic loan proceeds. An Abilene asset is not going to attract the same CMBS execution or life company interest that a Dallas or Austin asset commands. But there is a legitimate and active lending market here, and my opinion addresses both what that market can deliver and what constraints it imposes on a reorganization plan that depends on new first-mortgage debt.

The Engagement

Counsel for a plan proponent in a Chapter 11 proceeding retained Commercial Lending Solutions to provide a financing feasibility opinion for use in connection with plan confirmation. The asset in question was a multifamily property in Abilene, a mid-sized apartment community that had been operating under the cloud of the bankruptcy case for approximately eighteen months prior to our engagement. The debtor had allowed deferred maintenance to accumulate, occupancy had declined from historical stabilized levels, and the property carried a senior secured claim from a regional lender whose debt had matured prepetition.

The core legal questions our engagement was designed to address were threefold. First, could the property, as currently configured and with realistic near-term operational assumptions, support first-mortgage financing sufficient in amount to satisfy the senior secured creditor's allowed claim? Second, if current in-place performance was insufficient to support that loan amount, what level of stabilization would be required, and was that stabilization achievable on a timeline consistent with a confirmable plan? Third, what categories of lenders actually originate loans on commercial real estate assets that are emerging from Chapter 11, and what are their underwriting requirements?

These are not questions an appraiser alone can answer. An appraisal establishes value. A financing feasibility opinion establishes what debt the market will actually put on an asset, which is a function of income, debt service coverage, lender credit appetite, and market execution, not simply a percentage of appraised value. Those are different analyses, and the distinction matters in a plan confirmation proceeding.

The Financial Analysis

The methodology I applied follows the same framework I use when actually placing debt in this market, because a financing feasibility opinion that diverges from real-world underwriting is not credible to a sophisticated lender or a well-prepared opposing expert. The starting point is net operating income, derived from actual rent rolls, trailing expense history, and a market-supportable stabilized occupancy assumption.

For this asset, the in-place rent roll at the time of our analysis reflected occupancy of approximately 74%, with effective gross income on an annualized basis of roughly $680,000. After applying market-rate operating expenses including management at 8%, maintenance reserves at $600 per unit annually, insurance, taxes, and administrative costs, the in-place NOI was approximately $320,000. That number is the operative figure for current-state underwriting. A lender does not underwrite to proforma; a lender underwrites to trailing performance with adjustments for identifiable anomalies.

The DSCR standard I applied is 1.25x, which represents the floor for the categories of lenders most likely to engage with a post-bankruptcy multifamily asset in a secondary Texas market. Conventional agency execution through Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac requires a minimum 1.25x DSCR for most small balance multifamily product, and community bank and regional bank lenders in this market typically require 1.20x to 1.30x depending on their internal credit appetite. I used 1.25x as the benchmark because it is both the market standard and the more conservative assumption, which appropriately reflects the additional complexity of a bankruptcy-exit scenario.

On rate and amortization assumptions, I underwrote to a 7.50% note rate on a 30-year amortization schedule for conventional bank financing, reflecting the rate environment applicable at the time of the analysis for non-agency secondary market multifamily product with a bankruptcy history. For agency-eligible scenarios, I used a 7.00% rate with 30-year amortization, reflecting the modest execution advantage that Freddie Mac Small Balance Loan programs can provide when seasoning requirements are met.

The math on the current-state scenario is straightforward. At $320,000 NOI and a 1.25x DSCR requirement, the maximum supportable annual debt service is $256,000. At a 7.50% note rate on a 30-year amortization, that debt service corresponds to a maximum loan balance of approximately $3,150,000. The appraised value of the asset, as established in the bankruptcy proceeding, was $4,800,000. That implies a maximum supportable LTV of approximately 65.6%, which is within conventional lending parameters but leaves limited headroom if the allowed secured claim exceeds that loan amount.

The stabilized scenario presents a different picture. At 93% occupancy, a level consistent with historical performance at this asset prior to the financial distress period, annualized effective gross income rises to approximately $855,000. After applying the same expense load, stabilized NOI is approximately $420,000. At 1.25x DSCR, maximum debt service rises to $336,000, supporting a loan of approximately $4,135,000, representing an 86% improvement in supportable loan proceeds and an LTV of 86% against appraised value, which exceeds conventional limits and would require agency execution or a mezzanine structure. At 75% LTV, the stabilized scenario supports a conventional first mortgage of $3,600,000, which in this case was sufficient to satisfy the senior secured claim with a modest reserve funded from operating cash flow during the stabilization period.

The Complicating Factors

Bankruptcy stigma in lender underwriting. The most significant complicating factor in any exit financing engagement is lender perception of credit risk associated with a borrower or asset emerging from Chapter 11. Most conventional lenders, including community banks and regional banks, require that a commercial real estate asset be clear of any bankruptcy-related liens, stays, or proceedings before they will originate. They also typically impose a seasoning requirement: many require six to twelve months of post-confirmation, stabilized operating history before they will consider a new loan. This is not a universal rule, but it is a dominant market practice, and any financing feasibility opinion that ignores it is not credible. The practical effect is that the exit financing must either come from a lender category that explicitly accepts bankruptcy-exit assets, or the plan must be structured with a sufficient post-confirmation stabilization period before the refinance occurs.

Deferred maintenance and occupancy deterioration. In virtually every Chapter 11 scenario involving an operating property, the asset has experienced some level of operational deterioration during the pendency of the case. Capital expenditure deferrals reduce NOI reliability in lender eyes, because a lender underwriting a loan on a property with deferred maintenance must discount the income stream for the cost of remediation. In this engagement, the identified deferred maintenance was approximately $185,000, which a lender would either require to be escrowed at closing or would deduct from loan proceeds available for creditor satisfaction. This directly affects the net proceeds available to address the senior claim and must be modeled explicitly in any plan feasibility analysis.

Title and lien complexity. Assets in Chapter 11 frequently carry judgment liens, mechanics liens, or disputed claims that cloud title. Most lenders require clean title as a condition of closing, which means the plan must resolve all title defects prior to or concurrent with the exit financing close. In Abilene, where title insurance capacity is available through national underwriters operating locally, this is manageable, but it requires counsel and the plan proponent to address lien resolution explicitly in the plan documentation. A lender will not proceed to closing with an unresolved title dispute, and a financing feasibility opinion must account for this as a condition precedent.

Secondary market execution constraints. Abilene does not support CMBS execution at most loan sizes relevant to a multifamily asset in this price range. CMBS conduit lenders generally require minimum loan sizes in the $5,000,000 to $7,000,000 range and prefer primary or at minimum strong secondary markets. For a $3,000,000 to $4,000,000 multifamily loan in Abilene, the realistic lender universe is community banks, regional banks, and potentially agency small balance programs. Each of these categories has its own credit constraints, and the financing feasibility opinion must map the asset to the appropriate lender category rather than assuming execution that the market does not provide.

The Abilene Lender Market

Community and regional banks. The deepest source of commercial mortgage capital in Abilene is the community and regional bank segment. Institutions with local and regional presence in the Taylor County and Big Country area have demonstrated consistent appetite for stabilized multifamily, retail, and mixed-use product. Their typical credit box for multifamily in this market runs to 70% to 75% LTV, 1.20x to 1.25x DSCR, with five-year fixed or adjustable terms amortizing over 20 to 25 years. They are relationship-driven lenders, which means borrower creditworthiness and post-bankruptcy financial rehabilitation matter as much as property performance. A borrower emerging from Chapter 11 will face heightened scrutiny, and most community bank lenders will require a meaningful equity injection or demonstrated post-confirmation operating history before they commit.

Agency small balance programs. Freddie Mac's Small Balance Loan program and Fannie Mae's small loan execution are both potentially applicable to Abilene multifamily assets, provided the property meets seasoning and stabilization requirements. The relevant constraint in a bankruptcy exit scenario is the post-bankruptcy seasoning requirement. Freddie Mac's guidelines generally require that a borrower be discharged from bankruptcy for a minimum of two years, and the property itself must demonstrate a minimum of 90 days of stabilized occupancy at or above 90% prior to rate lock. For a plan that contemplates a 12-to-18-month post-confirmation stabilization period, agency execution may be achievable, and it represents meaningfully better pricing than conventional bank debt, often 50 to 100 basis points inside bank rates for comparable product.

Debt funds and bridge lenders. For exit financing scenarios where the stabilization period is still underway, or where the property cannot yet support permanent financing, bridge lenders and private debt funds represent the realistic short-term solution. These lenders are explicitly comfortable with transitional and distressed assets, including bankruptcy exits, and they underwrite to a projected stabilized NOI with a business plan for achieving it. Their pricing in the current environment runs from approximately 9.50% to 12.00% with origination fees of 1.00% to 2.00%, and their terms are typically 12 to 36 months with extension options. For a Abilene multifamily asset, loan sizes in the $2,500,000 to $5,000,000 range are achievable from this lender category, provided the business plan is credible and the sponsor has demonstrable execution capability.

SBA and USDA programs. For owner-occupied commercial properties in Abilene, SBA 504 and SBA 7(a) financing can be relevant, as can USDA Business and Industry loan guarantees, which are available in eligible rural and semi-rural geographies within Taylor County. These programs are not applicable to investment multifamily, but for hospitality, owner-occupied retail, or healthcare-related properties in Chapter 11, they represent a legitimate exit financing pathway worth addressing in the feasibility opinion.

What Attorneys Handling Abilene CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to retain a CRE financing expert. Counsel should engage a commercial real estate financing expert at the earliest stage of a Chapter 11 proceeding in which plan feasibility depends on refinance proceeds. Waiting until the plan is drafted to test financing feasibility is a mistake that creates unnecessary risk at confirmation. The expert's analysis should inform the plan structure, not merely validate it after the fact. If the senior creditor's allowed claim cannot be satisfied through market-rate refinancing at a realistic loan-to-value, the plan as drafted is not feasible, and the court will reject it. An early feasibility opinion allows counsel to structure a plan that is actually confirmable.

What a financing expert provides that an appraiser cannot. An appraiser establishes market value. A CRE financing expert establishes what debt the market will provide, on what terms, from which lenders, and subject to what conditions. These are distinct analyses. A property may appraise at $5,000,000, but if the income stream supports only a $3,200,000 loan at market DSCR requirements, the appraised value is not the operative number for plan feasibility purposes. Courts frequently conflate these two analyses, and a well-qualified financing expert can draw that distinction clearly and credibly.

What qualifies a CRE financing expert. A retired banker with community lending experience is not the same as a functioning capital markets professional who actively brokers commercial real estate debt in the current market. Financing standards, lender credit boxes, rate environments, and program availability change rapidly. An expert who last originated loans in 2018 cannot credibly opine on current market execution. The qualifying criteria that matter are: active origination experience across multiple lender categories, demonstrated volume in the relevant asset class, and familiarity with the secondary and tertiary market dynamics that govern Abilene-type deals. At CLS CRE, we have placed approximately $1 billion in commercial real estate transactions, maintain active relationships with more than 1,000 lenders, and operate in all 50 states, which gives us the market breadth to speak to both local execution and national capital availability.

Information counsel should gather before the engagement. To produce a credible financing feasibility opinion on an accelerated timeline, counsel should assemble: trailing 12-month rent rolls and operating statements, current property condition report or deferred maintenance assessment, existing debt documentation including payoff amounts and lien positions, the bankruptcy court's valuation record if one exists, and any prior lender communications regarding the asset. The more complete the information package at engagement, the more specific and defensible the expert opinion.

How the opinion is used at confirmation. A financing feasibility opinion submitted as a Rule 26 expert report can be used to establish that the plan meets the feasibility requirement under Section 1129(a)(11) of the Bankruptcy Code, specifically that confirmation is not likely to be followed by liquidation or further financial reorganization. The opinion addresses not just whether financing is theoretically available, but whether it is available on terms consistent with the plan's payment and timing requirements. At a contested confirmation hearing, the expert may be cross-examined on methodology, market data sources, and the specific assumptions underlying the income and debt service calculations. A well-documented, market-grounded opinion withstands that scrutiny; a back-of-the-envelope analysis does not.

Key Takeaways

  • Abilene is a viable but constrained market for exit financing. Legitimate lender appetite exists for stabilized multifamily and commercial assets, but execution is limited to community banks, regional banks, agency small balance programs, and bridge debt funds. CMBS and life company execution is not realistic at typical Abilene loan sizes.
  • NOI-based underwriting, not appraised value, determines maximum loan proceeds. In this engagement, the current-state income stream supported approximately $3,150,000 in first-mortgage debt, while stabilized performance supported up to $3,600,000

Expert Witness: How Construction Defects Affect CRE Financing in Kennewick, WA

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Construction Defect Impact on Financing

The Kennewick Market Context

Kennewick sits at the center of the Tri-Cities metropolitan statistical area alongside Richland and Pasco, forming one of Washington State's most economically resilient secondary markets. That resilience is not accidental. The regional economy draws from a deep and diversified base: federal contracting tied to the Hanford Site cleanup, a robust agricultural and food processing sector anchored by Columbia Basin wine production and cold storage logistics, a growing healthcare corridor, and an increasingly active industrial and distribution sector responding to Pacific Northwest supply chain demands. For commercial real estate financing purposes, this economic composition matters enormously because lenders underwrite markets, not just properties. A lender evaluating collateral in Kennewick is evaluating a market that has consistently demonstrated sub-6% industrial vacancy, sustained rent growth in the $0.55 to $0.75 per square foot per month range for bulk and flex industrial, and multifamily occupancy rates that have held above 95% across most submarkets throughout recent rate cycles.

Construction activity in Kennewick has accelerated over the past several years, particularly in the industrial, retail-pad, and multifamily categories along the Highway 395 and Canal Drive corridors. That growth has produced financing complexity. Lenders active in this market, including regional banks headquartered in Spokane, Seattle, and Portland, as well as community banks with Tri-Cities branches, have grown more sophisticated in their underwriting of Kennewick assets. That sophistication cuts both ways: it means capital is available for well-positioned assets, but it also means lenders bring real scrutiny to any property with unresolved title, physical, or litigation-related complications. A construction defect dispute in this market is not an abstract financing obstacle. It is a specific, documentable impairment to lender confidence, loan proceeds, and marketability, and quantifying that impairment requires someone who understands both the local market dynamics and the mechanics of institutional credit underwriting.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing a party in a construction defect dispute involving a newly constructed commercial property in the Kennewick submarket. The asset was a multitenant industrial flex building, a property type with strong fundamental demand in the Tri-Cities region given the area's distribution, contractor services, and light manufacturing activity. The building had been delivered within the prior 18 months and was partially occupied at the time of the dispute. Alleged defects included structural issues with the tilt-up concrete panel connections, inadequate drainage grading that caused water infiltration into tenant spaces, and HVAC system deficiencies that had resulted in documented tenant complaints and partial rent abatements.

Counsel retained CLS CRE to address three core financial questions. First, whether the property in its current condition, with known and alleged defects and active litigation, was financeable at all in the conventional commercial lending market, and if so, under what terms. Second, what the maximum supportable loan amount was for the subject property as-is versus what it would be for a comparable defect-free asset of equivalent size, location, and tenant profile. Third, how the alleged defects specifically affected the property's net operating income, debt service coverage ratio, and loan-to-value headroom, and what the quantifiable financing gap between the impaired and unimpaired asset looked like. These are questions that live at the intersection of capital markets practice, credit underwriting, and property operations. They are not questions an appraisal alone can fully answer.

The Financial Analysis

Our methodology began with a reconstruction of stabilized net operating income for both the impaired and unimpaired scenarios. For the unimpaired scenario, we used comparable industrial flex rents in the Kennewick submarket, which ranged from $10.50 to $13.00 per square foot annually on a gross lease basis for properties of similar vintage, size, and configuration along the primary industrial corridors. For a 42,000 square foot building at 95% occupancy, reflecting market-standard absorption for the product type, that translated to a gross potential rent of approximately $487,000 annually at the midpoint of the range. After applying a 5% vacancy and credit loss allowance and operating expenses of approximately $98,000 annually (inclusive of property taxes, insurance, management, maintenance, and reserves), the stabilized NOI for the unimpaired asset was established at approximately $364,000.

For the impaired scenario, we analyzed the actual rent roll and documented the rent abatements that had been granted to two tenants as a result of the water infiltration and HVAC deficiencies. Those abatements totaled approximately $31,000 over a trailing twelve-month period. We also applied a market-supported vacancy premium reflecting the functional impairment of one tenant suite that had been taken offline pending remediation. That adjustment reduced effective gross income by an additional $27,500. On the expense side, we added management friction costs and remediation-related expenses that had been borne by the ownership, adding approximately $18,000 in non-recurring but litigation-proximate expenses. The result was an impaired NOI of approximately $287,500, representing a roughly 21% reduction from the stabilized unimpaired baseline.

We applied a DSCR threshold of 1.25x, which represents the market standard minimum for conventional commercial bank and CMBS financing on industrial properties in secondary markets. Some lenders apply 1.20x for stronger credit tenants, but for a multitenant flex asset in a secondary market with demonstrated credit issues, 1.25x is the appropriate benchmark. At a note rate of 7.25% (reflecting pricing for a 5-year fixed, 25-year amortization bank loan at the time of our analysis) and using a constant of approximately 0.0868 annually, the maximum supportable debt service on the unimpaired NOI of $364,000 at 1.25x coverage was $291,200. That translated to a maximum loan of approximately $3,355,000.

Applying the same rate, amortization, and coverage threshold to the impaired NOI of $287,500 yielded maximum supportable debt service of $230,000 and a maximum loan of approximately $2,650,000. The financing gap attributable to the alleged defects was therefore approximately $705,000 in supportable loan proceeds. We then cross-checked this against LTV constraints. For a conventional bank loan on industrial flex in a secondary market, lenders in this category typically cap LTV at 65% to 70%. At a cap rate of 6.75% (supported by comparable sales in the Kennewick and broader Tri-Cities industrial market), the unimpaired value was approximately $5,390,000, supporting a 65% LTV loan of approximately $3,500,000. The impaired value, using the same cap rate applied to the impaired NOI, was approximately $4,260,000, supporting a 65% LTV loan of approximately $2,770,000. The DSCR constraint was the binding constraint in both scenarios, confirming our loan sizing approach, but the LTV analysis independently corroborated a financing gap in the $700,000 range.

The Complicating Factors

Active litigation as a lender-level disqualifier. The most immediate financing obstacle is not the physical condition of the property. It is the existence of active litigation. Virtually every conventional lender's credit policy requires disclosure of pending litigation affecting collateral, and most institutional credit committees will not approve a loan on a property subject to unresolved construction defect claims regardless of how well the underlying financials underwrite. This is not a pricing issue. It is a fundability issue. Lenders are concerned about the risk of mechanics' liens, additional claims, remediation cost uncertainty, and title insurer responses. Several title companies in Washington State have issued endorsements limiting or excluding coverage for defects that are the subject of pending litigation. Without clean title insurance, most lenders will not fund. This alone reduces the available lending universe to a narrow category of private and bridge lenders who price for the risk rather than avoid it.

Reserve and holdback requirements from the lenders who will engage. For the lenders willing to consider a property with known defects and active litigation, the standard response is not a simple pricing adjustment. Lenders in this category typically require funded reserves equal to 100% to 150% of the estimated remediation cost, held in lender-controlled accounts. If remediation is estimated at $400,000 and the lender applies a 125% reserve, that is $500,000 in proceeds that are funded but inaccessible to the borrower until remediation is certified complete by a third-party engineer. From a net proceeds standpoint, this reserve requirement functions as an effective reduction in loan proceeds available for acquisition, refinance, or recapitalization purposes.

Tenant instability and lease term impairment. Construction defects that affect tenant operations create secondary financing complications that are not always obvious. When tenants have documented complaints, active abatements, or shortened lease terms as a result of property conditions, lenders reduce credit given to that income. Some lenders will underwrite a lease as month-to-month, regardless of its stated term, if there is documentary evidence of tenant dissatisfaction or dispute. In this engagement, two leases with remaining terms of 24 and 36 months were effectively underwritten as shorter-duration income by the lenders we consulted, reducing the income quality score applied by those lenders and tightening their coverage requirements from 1.25x to 1.30x for that portion of the rent roll.

Engineering and third-party report requirements. Any lender willing to engage with this asset will require a Phase I environmental assessment, a Property Condition Assessment from a qualified engineer, and in this case given the specific allegations, a structural engineering report addressing the tilt-up panel connection issues. The cost and timeline for these reports is not trivial. More importantly, an adverse finding in any of these reports can reset the lender's position entirely. A structural report identifying deficiencies that exceed initially estimated remediation scope will typically result in a lender suspending underwriting until revised cost estimates are obtained. This creates a financing process that is inherently contingent and unpredictable, which is itself a quantifiable impairment to the financing feasibility of the asset during the pendency of litigation.

The Kennewick Lender Market

For a well-positioned, defect-free industrial flex asset in Kennewick, the lending universe is reasonably deep. Regional banks headquartered in the Pacific Northwest maintain active CRE loan programs for the Tri-Cities market, and several have dedicated relationship officers covering the area. These lenders typically price at spreads of 175 to 250 basis points over SOFR or equivalent index, apply 65% to 70% LTV, require 1.25x DSCR, and prefer borrowers with existing deposit relationships. Washington Federal, Banner Bank, and Columbia Banking System-affiliated institutions have all been active in this market, though lender appetite shifts with portfolio concentration and rate environment. The community bank segment in Kennewick is relatively strong, with locally based institutions that understand the Hanford-adjacent economy and can move with more flexibility than money-center banks on secondary market assets.

Life insurance companies and CMBS conduits are selectively active in the Tri-Cities for stabilized assets with strong tenancy, but their appetite is limited to properties that meet strict underwriting requirements including minimum loan sizes (typically $3,000,000 for CMBS) and clean title and litigation status. For a property with active construction defect litigation, these lenders are functionally unavailable. Credit unions chartered under Washington State or federal authority have also been active in commercial real estate in this region, though their credit boxes are typically more conservative on LTV and less flexible on property condition issues.

For the impaired asset that is the subject of this type of engagement, the realistic lending universe narrows to private bridge lenders, debt funds, and hard money sources. These lenders will engage with litigation-adjacent properties, but they price accordingly. Rates for bridge financing in this scenario ranged from 10.5% to 13.5% at the time of our analysis, with origination fees of 1.5 to 3 points, interest-only structures, and 12 to 24 month terms with extension options tied to litigation resolution milestones. The difference in all-in financing cost between the conventional bank market and this bridge segment is itself a quantifiable damages component when the defects are the proximate cause of the conventional financing unavailability.

What Attorneys Handling Kennewick CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to retain a CRE financing expert. The threshold question is whether financing feasibility, loan proceeds impairment, or lender market access is a damages element or a causation issue in the dispute. If a party is claiming that construction defects prevented or impaired a refinance, sale, or recapitalization, a CRE financing expert is essential. An appraiser can opine on value. A CRE financing expert can opine on whether a lender would have funded, at what amount, on what terms, and what the delta is between the impaired and unimpaired financing scenarios. These are not overlapping opinions. They answer different questions, and both are often necessary for a complete damages analysis.

What a CRE financing expert can establish that an appraiser cannot. Appraisers work within a value framework. CRE financing experts work within a credit underwriting framework. The expert can testify to: what specific lender requirements exist for properties with known defects; how DSCR is calculated and why it constrains loan proceeds in ways that value alone does not capture; what the all-in cost differential is between conventional and bridge financing for a litigation-impaired asset; whether the property was fundable at all in the conventional market during the relevant period; and what reserve or holdback requirements a lender would have imposed and how they would have affected borrower liquidity.

Qualifications that matter. A qualified CRE financing expert is not simply a retired banker or a real estate broker with lending experience. The relevant qualification is active, current engagement in the commercial mortgage brokerage or capital markets advisory business, with demonstrated access to the lender categories relevant to the asset type and market in question. CLS CRE maintains relationships with more than 1,000 institutional, regional, and private lenders nationwide and has closed approximately $1 billion in transaction volume across 50 states, including Pacific Northwest markets. That active market presence is what allows us to testify credibly about what lenders actually require today, not what they required a decade ago.

Information counsel should gather before the engagement. Before retaining a CRE financing expert, counsel should assemble the rent roll with lease abstracts, trailing 12-month operating statements, any appraisals or broker opinions of value in the record, all engineering and inspection reports related to the alleged defects, any lender correspondence or term sheets from financing efforts that were attempted during the relevant period, and documentation of any rent abatements, vacancies, or tenant disputes attributable to the defects. This information allows the expert to perform a grounded analysis rather than a theoretical one, which significantly strengthens the admissibility and persuasive weight of the resulting opinion.

Use at trial or in settlement negotiations. CRE financing expert testimony is typically used to establish a specific, quantified financing gap as a component of economic damages. At trial, the expert presents the methodology, the comparable market data, the lender market analysis, and the arithmetic connecting defects to lost loan proceeds or excess financing costs. In settlement contexts, a well-prepared expert report frequently drives resolution because it transforms a general claim of impairment into a specific, documented number that both sides can evaluate against litigation risk.

Key Takeaways

  • Active construction defect litigation is often a lender-level disqualifier, not just a pricing adjustment. Most conventional lenders will not fund a loan on a property with unresolved litigation, regardless of the financial profile of the asset, which immediately reduces the available financing universe to bridge and private sources at materially higher cost.
  • The financing gap in a defect scenario has two components: the NOI reduction caused by abatements, vacancies, and impaired leases, which reduces the maximum supportable loan on a DSCR basis, and the LTV impairment caused by reduced property value. Both must be independently calculated and compared to confirm which constraint is binding.
  • Lender reserve and holdback requirements for defect-adjacent properties function as an effective reduction in net loan proceeds available to the borrower, and the gap between funded loan amount and accessible proceeds is a quantifiable element of damages that is frequently overlooked in disputes.
  • Kennewick's industrial flex sector is fundamentally sound, which means the financing gap attributable to defects is particularly visible. A defect-free comparable asset would have clear access to conventional bank financing at 65-70% LTV and 1.25x DSCR, making the impaired asset's forced reliance on bridge capital a readily demonstrable damages component.
  • Tenant instability caused by construction defects creates secondary underwriting impairments that reduce income quality scores applied by lenders, tighten coverage requirements, and can cause lenders to discount stated lease terms, compounding the NOI-level impairment with a credit-quality-level impairment.

Expert Witness: NNN Lease Rollover Upside and Financing Impact in Montgomery, AL

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Below-Market NNN Lease and Rollover Upside Analysis

The Montgomery Market Context

Montgomery, Alabama occupies a distinct position in the Southeast commercial real estate landscape. As the state capital, it carries a stable base of government employment, healthcare anchors, and institutional tenants that insulates the market from the cyclical volatility common in faster-growing metros. But that stability cuts both ways. Montgomery's single-tenant net lease market reflects the market's underlying rent dynamics: rents have trended modestly upward but remain well below the per-square-foot benchmarks seen in Birmingham, Huntsville, or Nashville. For retail and industrial NNN assets, in-place rents from leases executed five to ten years ago are often 15% to 30% below what a landlord could achieve today on a new lease, creating precisely the kind of below-market rent exposure that becomes legally and financially material in a financing or valuation dispute.

Montgomery's commercial real estate fundamentals as of recent reporting periods include industrial vacancy in the 6% to 8% range, supported by distribution activity along the I-85 corridor and secondary manufacturing. Retail net lease assets, particularly freestanding single-tenant properties along the Eastern Boulevard and Atlanta Highway corridors, have traded at cap rates ranging from approximately 6.25% to 7.75% depending on tenancy credit, lease term remaining, and rent escalation structure. Office vacancy remains elevated, above 15% in several Class B submarkets, which makes single-tenant office NNN properties particularly sensitive to lease rollover risk in lender underwriting. For purposes of this type of dispute, the relevant question is not just what the market looks like, but how lenders in this market price the risk of a lease that expires or resets within the loan term, and how that pricing affects the maximum supportable loan amount.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing one of the parties in a commercial real estate financing dispute involving a single-tenant NNN property in the Montgomery metropolitan area. The asset was a freestanding retail commercial building, owner-occupied by a national tenant under a long-term net lease that had been originated several years prior at rents that, as of the dispute date, were materially below prevailing market rates for comparable space in the submarket.

The core legal and financial questions presented to CLS CRE were threefold. First, counsel needed to understand how a commercial mortgage lender would underwrite the existing below-market in-place rent versus the market rent the property could command after lease expiration, and whether that distinction affected the loan amount supportable at closing. Second, counsel needed an analysis of whether the anticipated rollover upside, meaning the projected increase in NOI upon lease renewal or re-tenanting at market rents, could be monetized in the current financing environment or whether lenders would treat it as speculative and exclude it from their underwriting. Third, the engagement required a determination of whether the existing lease structure would allow the property to support refinancing at the LTV and DSCR thresholds cited in the underlying transaction documents, or whether the below-market rent precluded achieving those targets with institutional financing.

These questions required both a lender market survey and a structured financial analysis using actual underwriting methodology, not appraised value alone. An MAI appraisal can determine market value using the income approach, but it does not tell you whether a lender will lend against that value, at what leverage, or under what structural conditions. That distinction was central to the dispute.

The Financial Analysis

The analysis began with a dual-income-stream framework. In commercial mortgage underwriting, lenders evaluate NNN properties on the basis of in-place contractual rent, not market rent. The distinction is critical. A lender's debt service coverage ratio calculation uses the income the lease actually obligates the tenant to pay, not what the landlord might collect after the lease expires. For this engagement, the subject property generated in-place gross rent of approximately $420,000 annually. After accounting for reserves and any landlord obligations retained under the lease structure, stabilized NOI was established at approximately $398,000.

The market standard DSCR threshold for single-tenant NNN retail assets is 1.20x to 1.25x depending on lender type and credit quality of the tenant. Agencies typically require 1.25x at a minimum; life companies and CMBS lenders may underwrite at 1.20x for investment-grade tenants with meaningful lease term remaining. For this asset, we applied a 1.25x DSCR floor consistent with the financing environment for non-investment-grade tenancy with lease rollover occurring within the proposed loan term. The rate assumption used was a 7.25% note rate on a 25-year amortization schedule, consistent with the conduit and regional bank pricing available for Montgomery NNN retail during the relevant period. Using those parameters, the maximum annual debt service supportable by the in-place NOI was calculated as follows: $398,000 divided by 1.25 yields maximum allowable debt service of $318,400. At a 7.25% note rate on a 25-year amortization, the corresponding loan constant is approximately 0.08596. Dividing $318,400 by 0.08596 produces a maximum supportable loan amount of approximately $3,704,000.

The appraised value of the property using the income approach capitalized at market rent, which reflected the rollover upside scenario, was approximately $5,800,000, suggesting a market-rent stabilized value significantly above the in-place rent supported value. At a standard 65% LTV for a single-tenant retail asset with rollover risk, the LTV-constrained maximum loan would be $3,770,000. In this case, the DSCR constraint was the binding constraint, not the LTV cap, because the in-place NOI was insufficient to service a loan at 65% of the higher appraised value. The maximum loan at DSCR was approximately $3,704,000 against an in-place value of approximately $5,150,000 (capitalized at the in-place NOI using a 7.75% cap rate), which represents an LTV of approximately 71.9%. That ratio exceeds institutional lender comfort for this asset class, confirming that the below-market rent directly precluded achieving the loan sizing targets referenced in the transaction documents.

The rollover upside, meaning the difference between in-place rent and projected market rent at lease expiration, was explicitly excluded from lender underwriting. No institutional lender active in Montgomery will underwrite speculative post-rollover income as current NOI. Some bridge lenders will underwrite a value-add component, but at significantly higher rates (typically 9.5% to 11.5% range for bridge execution) and lower initial advance rates, with future funding structured around lease commencement milestones. That is a fundamentally different capital structure, with different risk pricing, than the permanent financing the transaction contemplated.

The Complicating Factors

Below-Market Rent as a Lender Underwriting Ceiling: The most direct complicating factor in this engagement was the mechanics of how below-market rent constrains loan sizing. Lenders underwrite to contractual cash flow, not market potential. A lease that obligates a tenant to pay $18.00 per square foot per year when market rents are $24.00 per square foot does not produce a property that lenders treat as worth the $24.00 market-rent value for loan sizing purposes. The gap between in-place and market rent produces a permanent ceiling on the NOI available to support debt service until the lease resets. In Montgomery's retail NNN market, that gap can persist for years, particularly on leases with modest or no built-in escalations. Lenders in this market explicitly discount properties with sub-market rents when the lease has five or more years remaining, because the rollover event is too distant to underwrite as a near-term income improvement.

Lease Rollover Risk Within the Loan Term: Conversely, when below-market leases expire within the proposed loan term, lenders face a different but equally limiting problem: rollover risk. If a 10-year loan is proposed on a property whose lease expires in year four, the lender must underwrite the probability that the tenant does not renew, leaves the property vacant, and stops paying rent. In that scenario, debt service continues while NOI drops to zero. Institutional lenders in Montgomery address this through one or more structural requirements: lease term must exceed loan term (the most common requirement for agency and life company execution), significant cash reserves must be funded at closing to cover debt service during a hypothetical re-tenanting period, or loan proceeds must be reduced to a level where the lender can tolerate vacancy without breaching coverage minimums. Any one of these requirements reduces effective loan proceeds below the headline LTV or creates carry costs that alter the economics of the transaction.

Tenant Credit and the Montgomery Market Depth: Montgomery lacks the institutional investor depth of larger Southeast markets. The secondary market for single-tenant NNN assets with below-investment-grade tenants or short lease terms is thinner here than in Atlanta or Nashville. When a lease structure creates rollover risk, the buyer pool for the asset narrows, cap rates expand, and the appraised value the lender will accept for LTV purposes contracts. This creates a compounding effect: the below-market rent suppresses NOI, the rollover risk expands the cap rate applied to that NOI, and the resulting value supports even less loan proceeds. The practical result is that a property that appears financeable based on its physical characteristics and location may be effectively un-financeable at the leverage targets assumed in the underlying documents without structural solutions that increase transaction cost and complexity.

Burn-Off Period and Interest Reserve Requirements: For bridge lending scenarios where rollover upside is the thesis, lenders require funded interest reserves calculated to cover the period from loan closing through projected lease stabilization, plus a contingency buffer. In a Montgomery market context, the re-tenanting timeline for a freestanding single-tenant retail asset can run 12 to 24 months depending on submarket, building configuration, and tenant credit requirements. A bridge lender modeling an 18-month stabilization period on a $3,700,000 loan at 10.5% requires roughly $291,000 in funded interest reserves before factoring in origination fees and extension options. That reserve reduces net loan proceeds and, in a dispute context, must be factored into any analysis of whether the proposed financing actually delivered the capital the transaction documents contemplated.

The Montgomery Lender Market

Regional and Community Banks: Montgomery has meaningful community and regional bank depth, with institutions headquartered or with strong Alabama presences active in commercial real estate lending. These lenders are typically the most pragmatic underwriters for below-market lease scenarios because they hold loans on balance sheet and can structure around specific credit characteristics. They will generally require DSCR of 1.20x to 1.25x on in-place rents, personal guarantees on sub-institutional credit tenancy, and loan terms of 5 to 7 years with 20-to-25-year amortization. Maximum LTV for single-tenant retail with rollover risk typically runs 60% to 65%. Portfolio lenders may accept modestly shorter remaining lease terms if the borrower relationship is strong, but they will not underwrite rollover upside as current income.

CMBS and Conduit Lenders: Conduit execution is available for Montgomery NNN properties meeting minimum loan size thresholds, generally $2,000,000 or above, with creditworthy tenants and lease terms that extend through or beyond the proposed loan term. CMBS lenders apply strict in-place underwriting with no credit for rollover upside, and their credit boxes are highly sensitive to tenant credit ratings. A below-investment-grade tenant with rollover risk within a 10-year loan term will generally not clear CMBS credit committee absent significant structural enhancements. Spreads for eligible Montgomery NNN retail assets in the current rate environment price at approximately 175 to 250 basis points over the applicable swap rate, producing all-in rates consistent with the 7.00% to 7.75% range for strong credit.

Life Insurance Companies: Life company allocations for Montgomery are limited but not absent, particularly for institutional-quality single-tenant assets with long lease terms and investment-grade tenancy. Life companies represent the most conservative execution available, underwriting at 55% to 60% LTV, 1.25x DSCR minimum, and only against investment-grade or near-investment-grade tenants with lease terms materially exceeding the loan term. A below-market lease with near-term rollover will not qualify for life company consideration unless the credit quality and lease extension covenants are exceptionally strong.

Debt Funds and Bridge Lenders: For assets with rollover upside as the primary thesis, non-bank debt funds and bridge lenders are the realistic execution option. These lenders underwrite to stabilized value on a post-rollover basis, advance at 70% to 75% of the stabilized value, and price at 300 to 500 basis points over SOFR or at fixed rates in the 9.5% to 11.5% range. Origination fees of 1% to 2% apply, and interest reserves are required as described above. Bridge execution in Montgomery for single-tenant assets is available but not commoditized, meaning fewer lenders actively compete for this paper and terms are less favorable than in primary markets.

What Attorneys Handling Montgomery CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to Retain a CRE Financing Expert: Any commercial real estate dispute in which the financing of an asset, the feasibility of a proposed transaction, or the adequacy of loan proceeds is material to damages, breach, or specific performance claims requires a CRE financing expert, not just an appraiser. Appraisers determine value. Financing experts determine whether and how a lender will lend against that value, under what terms, and at what proceeds. Those are distinct questions, and conflating them is a common and costly error in commercial real estate litigation.

What a Financing Expert Can Answer That an Appraiser Cannot: An appraiser will give you a market value opinion using the income, sales comparison, and cost approaches. A CRE financing expert will tell you what the maximum supportable loan was, what DSCR and LTV constraints applied, which lender categories would have been available, what structural requirements would have been imposed, what the actual loan proceeds would have been net of reserves and fees, and whether the financing assumptions embedded in the transaction documents were achievable in the actual market at the relevant time. These are capital markets questions, not valuation questions. They require active lender market knowledge, not just appraisal methodology.

What Makes a CRE Financing Expert Qualified: Qualification turns on active market participation, not historical tenure. A retired banker who left practice five years ago does not know today's DSCR requirements, current spread levels, or how CMBS credit committees are treating rollover risk in secondary markets. CLS CRE has approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, over 1,000 active lender relationships, and operates in all 50 states. That active deal flow is what makes the expert testimony credible and defensible on cross-examination. An expert who cannot cite current term sheets and current lender requirements will not survive a well-prepared opposing examination.

Information Counsel Should Gather Before the Engagement: Prior to retaining a financing expert, counsel should assemble the rent roll with in-place rents, lease expiration dates, and renewal option terms; the current rent comps for the submarket; any prior loan applications, term sheets, or commitment letters; the appraisal or any draft appraisal; the transaction documents referencing the financing contingency or LTV/DSCR requirements; and any lender correspondence. The expert needs to know exactly what was promised or assumed versus what the lender market would have delivered. The gap between those two numbers is often where the damages analysis lives.

Use at Trial and in Plan Hearings: CRE financing expert testimony is used to establish financing feasibility, to rebut an opposing party's claim that refinancing was achievable or that a loan was properly sized, and in bankruptcy plan confirmation proceedings to demonstrate whether a proposed reorganization plan is realistically financeable. In NNN lease disputes specifically, the expert can demonstrate that the below-market rent structure, independent of any party's conduct, made the contemplated refinancing mathematically impossible under market conditions, or alternatively that the rollover upside would have enabled a specific financing structure at a defined point in time.

Key Takeaways

  • In-place rent, not market rent, controls loan sizing. Lenders underwrite to contractual NOI. Rollover upside is not current income and is not underwritten as such by institutional lenders, regardless of how certain the income improvement appears.
  • Below-market leases create compounding loan constraints. Suppressed NOI reduces maximum debt service capacity, which reduces maximum loan proceeds. When the suppressed NOI also produces a higher cap rate at sale, the value base supporting the LTV test contracts simultaneously, creating a double constraint on financing.

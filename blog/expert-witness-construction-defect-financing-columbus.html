Expert Witness: How Construction Defects Affect CRE Financing in Columbus, OH

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Construction Defect Impact on Financing

The Columbus Market Context

Columbus, Ohio has emerged as one of the most actively watched commercial real estate markets in the Midwest, and that attention is not accidental. The metropolitan area's population surpassed two million residents, making it the largest city in Ohio and one of the fastest-growing metros in the region. Ohio State University, a substantial state government employment base, and a diversifying private sector anchored by financial services, healthcare, and logistics have created demand fundamentals that support multiple commercial real estate asset classes simultaneously. For purposes of construction defect litigation, that diversity matters because it means disputes arise across office, multifamily, industrial, and retail properties, each of which carries distinct financing standards and lender expectations.

The multifamily sector in Columbus has attracted aggressive institutional capital over the past several years. Cap rates for stabilized Class A apartment assets in submarkets like the Short North, Franklinton, and the Arena District have compressed into the mid-4 to low-5 percent range, while suburban submarkets in Dublin, Hilliard, and New Albany have traded in the 5 to 5.75 percent range. New supply has been substantial, with thousands of units delivered annually, and that pipeline has created rent concession pressure in some micromarkets while leaving others undersupplied. For a construction defect matter involving a multifamily project, understanding where a specific submarket sits on that supply-demand curve is essential to determining what a lender would underwrite as stabilized NOI.

On the industrial side, Columbus benefits from its position at the intersection of three major interstate corridors, making it one of the premier logistics and distribution hubs in the eastern United States. Industrial vacancy in the Columbus metro has remained tight, generally tracking below 5 percent in core submarkets near Rickenbacker International Airport and the southeast industrial corridor, though newer speculative development in outlying areas like Etna Township has introduced some lease-up uncertainty. Industrial rents have risen materially, with Class A bulk distribution facilities achieving $6.00 to $7.50 per square foot net in strong locations. These fundamentals are directly relevant to any financing feasibility analysis involving a Columbus industrial or flex property affected by construction defects.

Office is a different story. Columbus was not immune to the structural demand shift away from traditional office space. Suburban Class B and C office product has faced meaningful vacancy pressure, with overall metro office vacancy rates in the 15 to 20 percent range depending on submarket, and lender appetite for office construction lending has narrowed considerably. Any construction defect matter involving an office property must account for the fact that the lender market for that asset class has contracted, and the financing feasibility analysis must reflect current market realities rather than pre-pandemic underwriting norms.

The Engagement

We were retained by litigation counsel representing a party in a construction defect dispute involving a newly constructed multifamily residential project in a Columbus urban submarket. The project had been completed within the prior two years and was in the process of stabilizing its occupancy when alleged construction defects were identified during a routine inspection and subsequently confirmed by an independent engineering firm. The defects at issue involved water intrusion through the building envelope, including window assemblies, exterior cladding seams, and parapet flashing details, as well as structural concerns related to certain framing connections in the podium-level parking structure.

Counsel retained Commercial Lending Solutions to address three specific questions that were central to the dispute. First, whether and to what extent the known construction defects would affect the property's ability to obtain permanent financing or a refinance of the existing construction loan. Second, what lenders would require before agreeing to fund a loan on a property with active or recently resolved construction defect claims, including what reserve structures, escrow requirements, or additional due diligence would apply. Third, how the defects, the pending litigation, and the remediation uncertainty affected the property's DSCR, LTV, and maximum supportable loan amount compared to a comparable defect-free multifamily asset in the same submarket.

These are financing feasibility questions, not appraisal questions. An appraiser can opine on market value. A CRE financing expert addresses something different: whether the capital markets, at the time in question and under the conditions present, would actually fund a loan on this asset, at what size, on what terms, and with what constraints. The distinction between "what is it worth" and "can you finance it, and on what terms" is precisely where litigation frequently requires specialized expert input beyond what an appraisal can provide.

The Financial Analysis

The subject property was a 180-unit market-rate multifamily project. For the purposes of the financing feasibility analysis, we established two underwriting scenarios: a baseline scenario representing the property as if defect-free and stabilized, and an impaired scenario reflecting the property's actual condition with known defects, active litigation, and remediation cost uncertainty.

Baseline Underwriting (Defect-Free Scenario): Using market rents appropriate for the Columbus urban submarket and property class, we underwrote effective gross income at approximately $2.4 million annually, reflecting 180 units at an average monthly rent of approximately $1,150, a 5 percent vacancy and credit loss factor consistent with Columbus multifamily market norms, and ancillary income from parking and fees. Operating expenses including property management at 4.5 percent of effective gross income, real estate taxes at Columbus-area assessed values, insurance, utilities, maintenance, and a capital reserve of $300 per unit per year produced a stabilized net operating income of approximately $1.52 million.

At a 1.25x DSCR, which is the standard minimum threshold applied by agency lenders including Fannie Mae DUS and Freddie Mac Optigo for conventional multifamily, and using a 6.25 percent note rate on a 30-year amortization schedule (reflecting market conditions at the time of this analysis for a 10-year fixed-rate agency execution), the maximum supportable annual debt service is approximately $1.216 million. At that debt service level, the corresponding maximum loan amount calculates to approximately $16.8 million. Applying an LTV constraint of 75 percent, consistent with agency guidelines for a conventional market-rate project, and assuming a stabilized value of approximately $22.5 million based on a 6.75 percent cap rate applied to the $1.52 million NOI, the LTV-constrained maximum loan is approximately $16.875 million. In this baseline scenario, DSCR is the binding constraint, producing a maximum loan of approximately $16.8 million.

Impaired Scenario (Defect-Present Conditions): The defects introduced impairment along multiple dimensions simultaneously, which is the critical analytical point. First, lenders addressing active water intrusion issues discount occupancy and rent, treating the property as non-stabilized regardless of actual in-place occupancy. In this case, we applied a 10 percent additional vacancy haircut to reflect lender caution around unit turnover and potential rent concessions during remediation, reducing effective gross income by approximately $220,000. Second, operating expenses increased materially due to insurance surcharge exposure, elevated maintenance costs during the remediation period, and potential property management cost increases related to tenant communication and unit repair coordination. We modeled a $95,000 increase in operating expenses. These two adjustments reduced NOI to approximately $1.205 million.

At the same 1.25x DSCR and 6.25 percent note rate, the maximum supportable loan on impaired NOI drops to approximately $13.27 million. On the value side, the cap rate applied to a defect-encumbered property widens. Market evidence supports a 50 to 75 basis point cap rate adjustment for properties with known structural or envelope defects and unresolved litigation, which at the midpoint produces a stabilized impaired value of approximately $17.2 million and a 75 percent LTV ceiling of approximately $12.9 million. In the impaired scenario, LTV becomes the binding constraint, and the maximum supportable loan falls to approximately $12.9 million. The financing gap between the baseline and impaired scenarios is approximately $3.9 million, a direct and quantifiable consequence of the construction defects on the property's debt capacity.

The Complicating Factors

Active Litigation as a Lender Trigger: Most commercial mortgage lenders, whether agency, bank, life company, or CMBS, require representations at loan closing that there is no pending or threatened litigation that could materially affect the property or the borrower. Active construction defect litigation almost universally triggers this requirement and forces the lender either to decline the loan entirely until the litigation resolves or to impose a litigation reserve that is funded at closing and held until dismissal or settlement. That reserve is not nominal. For a $12.9 million to $16.8 million loan on a property with engineering-confirmed defects, a reasonably sized lender reserve would range from $500,000 to over $1.5 million depending on the scope of the alleged defects and the remediation cost estimates provided by the engineering firm. This reserve reduces loan proceeds available to the borrower, effectively tightening the already impaired financing further.

Construction Loan Maturity Pressure: Most Columbus multifamily construction loans carry initial terms of 18 to 36 months with extension options. When defect litigation arises and prevents a clean takeout refinance, the borrower is often caught between an expiring construction loan and an inability to place permanent financing. Construction lenders have their own exposure to the litigation, and their willingness to grant extensions is neither guaranteed nor free. The borrower may face extension fees of 25 to 50 basis points per extension, increased interest rate margins, and additional equity injection requirements. These costs are direct, measurable financial damages that an expert can quantify.

Agency Lender Ineligibility During Litigation: Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, the dominant permanent lenders for stabilized multifamily assets in Columbus, have specific disqualifying conditions related to construction defects and litigation. A property with active or unresolved construction litigation generally cannot obtain agency financing until the matter is resolved by settlement or final judgment and the remediation has been completed and certified by a licensed engineer. This is not a matter of lender discretion. It is a program eligibility question. The consequence is that during the litigation period, the borrower is confined to a smaller, more expensive universe of non-agency lenders, including balance sheet banks, debt funds, and bridge lenders, all of which price credit risk materially higher than agency executions. The rate differential between an agency execution at 6.25 percent and a bridge lender execution at 8.50 to 9.50 percent on the same property represents a carrying cost that is quantifiable and directly attributable to the defect condition.

Insurance and Casualty Underwriting Complications: Property insurance underwriters receive the same environmental and engineering reports that lenders see. A property with confirmed water intrusion defects may face coverage exclusions, higher premiums, or policy non-renewal, all of which directly affect the operating expense line, and therefore NOI and DSCR. In severe cases, a lender may be unable to close a loan if they cannot confirm satisfactory hazard insurance coverage, creating a circular obstacle: the defects prevent insurance, and the absence of adequate insurance prevents financing.

The Columbus Lender Market

Columbus benefits from a deep and diverse lender community, which is relevant to understanding what financing options actually exist for an impaired asset and what they cost.

Agency Lenders (Fannie Mae DUS and Freddie Mac Optigo): These are the dominant permanent financing source for stabilized conventional multifamily in Columbus and represent the gold standard execution in terms of rate, leverage, and term flexibility. However, as noted above, they are generally unavailable for properties with active construction defect litigation or unresolved remediation requirements. Their credit box requires a clean Phase I environmental, satisfactory property condition assessment, and clear title with no material pending litigation. A property in litigation is, for practical purposes, outside their underwriting universe until the matter resolves.

Regional and Community Banks: Columbus has a strong regional bank presence, including several Ohio-headquartered institutions with active commercial real estate portfolios. These lenders can hold loans on their balance sheet and have more flexibility to structure around litigation holds, reserve requirements, and remediation escrows. However, they price that flexibility into their terms. Expect spreads of 200 to 275 basis points over SOFR on floating-rate bridge structures, lower LTV maximums in the 65 to 70 percent range on impaired assets, and personal recourse carveouts that are more expansive than agency executions. Several Columbus-area community banks have specific construction and renovation loan products that can accommodate a remediation period, but they will require a detailed remediation scope, cost certification, and draw schedule from the contractor performing the repair work.

Debt Funds and Bridge Lenders: The non-bank bridge lending market is active in Columbus for value-add and transitional assets. These lenders typically price in the 8.50 to 10.00 percent range depending on loan size, leverage, and complexity, and they will engage with a property that carries construction defect history if the remediation path is defined and the litigation is in a late stage of resolution. Their interest is in short-term execution, and their exit assumption is that the borrower will refinance into permanent financing after resolution. Bridge loan terms of 12 to 36 months are typical, often with two 12-month extension options. They are a solution, but at a significant cost premium relative to permanent financing.

Life Insurance Companies: Life companies are active in Columbus for stabilized, lower-leverage commercial real estate across multiple asset classes, including multifamily, industrial, and anchored retail. They are the most conservative underwriters in the market and would not engage with a property carrying active construction defect litigation under any circumstances. Their credit culture is oriented toward long-term hold, minimal complexity, and institutional-quality assets. A construction defect matter renders the asset ineligible for life company consideration until full remediation and litigation resolution.

What Attorneys Handling Columbus CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to Retain a CRE Financing Expert: Counsel should consider retaining a CRE financing expert in any commercial real estate dispute where the financial damages include impaired borrowing capacity, lost financing proceeds, excess financing costs, loan default attributable to defect-related NOI reduction, or inability to refinance a maturing loan. These scenarios arise in construction defect cases, landlord-tenant disputes affecting occupancy and income, eminent domain cases where the taking affects financing viability, fraud and misrepresentation cases involving loan proceeds or asset value, and bankruptcy proceedings where a debtor's plan of reorganization includes a real estate refinancing component.

What a CRE Financing Expert Addresses That an Appraiser Cannot: An appraisal establishes value. A CRE financing expert establishes what the capital markets will actually do with that value. Those are related but distinct questions. A $22.5 million appraised value does not automatically produce a $16.875 million loan. The expert can testify to whether a loan of that size was actually available in the market at the relevant time, on what terms, under what conditions, and with what constraints. The expert can opine on specific lender program requirements, underwriting standards, DSCR thresholds, reserve requirements, and pricing differentials between lending categories. None of those opinions are within the scope of a standard appraisal engagement.

What Qualifies a CRE Financing Expert: The expert should have active, current experience originating or structuring commercial real estate loans. A retired banker who underwrote loans 15 years ago may have credential authority but lacks current market knowledge. The field changes rapidly. Rate environments, LTV norms, reserve requirements, and lender appetite shift materially within 12 to 24 month periods. An expert whose market knowledge is current, who has active lender relationships across multiple categories, and who can speak to what a specific lender type would have required at a specific point in time based on firsthand engagement with those lenders carries substantially more evidentiary weight than one whose opinions are based on dated experience.

Information Counsel Should Gather Before the Engagement: To provide a rigorous financing feasibility analysis, the expert will need the following: the property's current and historical rent rolls, operating statements for the prior two to three years, the engineering or environmental report identifying the alleged defects, any lender correspondence including term sheets, commitment letters, or declination letters, the existing loan documents if a loan is already in place, the remediation cost estimate from the contractor or engineer, any insurance claims or coverage correspondence, and the appraisal if one exists. The more complete the record, the more precise and defensible the expert's opinions will be.

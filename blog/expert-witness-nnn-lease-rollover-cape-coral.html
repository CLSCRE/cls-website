Expert Witness: NNN Lease Rollover Upside and Financing Impact in Cape Coral, FL

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Below-Market NNN Lease and Rollover Upside Analysis

The Cape Coral Market Context

Cape Coral, Florida sits at the northwestern edge of Lee County in the Fort Myers-Cape Coral metropolitan statistical area, one of the fastest-growing MSAs in the United States over the past decade. That growth trajectory matters directly to commercial real estate financing disputes because lender underwriting assumptions, cap rate benchmarks, and lease rollover risk assessments are all calibrated to market conditions at the time of origination or valuation. Cape Coral is no longer a secondary market in the institutional sense. Population growth in Lee County has consistently ranked among the top ten fastest-growing counties nationally, and the commercial real estate infrastructure serving that population has followed accordingly.

For single-tenant net lease assets specifically, Cape Coral presents a distinct set of market dynamics. The retail and commercial corridor along Pine Island Road, Del Prado Boulevard, and Cape Coral Parkway has absorbed significant net lease development over the past five years, with new construction NNN assets trading at cap rates in the low-to-mid 5% range for investment-grade tenancy. However, the market for second-generation, single-tenant properties with non-investment-grade or regional credit tenants is more nuanced. Cap rates for these assets have ranged from approximately 6.25% to 7.75% depending on lease term remaining, credit quality, and whether the property configuration is considered re-tenantable in the event of a vacancy. That spread matters enormously in a financing dispute because a 150-basis-point difference in underwritten cap rate can move the supportable loan by $500,000 to over $1 million on a mid-market asset.

Lee County's commercial vacancy picture also factors into rollover analysis. Retail vacancy in the broader Fort Myers-Cape Coral MSA has trended below 5% in well-located corridors, with asking rents for inline and freestanding retail space recovering strongly post-2020. For single-tenant freestanding assets of 3,000 to 10,000 square feet, market rents in Cape Coral have generally ranged from $22 to $38 per square foot NNN depending on location, visibility, and build-out. When a dispute involves a lease written at $18 or $20 NNN on a property where market is $28 to $30, the rollover upside question is not hypothetical. It is a measurable underwriting variable that either expands or constrains financing capacity, and how a lender treats that upside is the technical question at the center of these engagements.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing a party in a commercial real estate dispute involving a single-tenant freestanding retail property in Cape Coral. The asset was a mid-size freestanding commercial building occupied by a regional service-oriented tenant on a long-form NNN lease with approximately four years remaining. The in-place rent was materially below prevailing market rates for comparable properties in the same Cape Coral submarket, a condition that had existed since lease execution and had widened as market rents appreciated over the hold period.

The legal dispute centered on valuation and financing capacity. Specifically, counsel needed to establish the following: first, whether the below-market lease structure precluded conventional financing at commercially reasonable LTV and DSCR thresholds; second, whether lenders would give credit to rollover upside when sizing a loan, or whether underwriting would be confined exclusively to the in-place rent; and third, what the financing universe actually looked like for this asset in the current capital markets environment, including which lender categories would engage, what terms they would require, and whether the financing gap created by the below-market lease was a temporary condition or a structural impairment to capital access. CLS CRE was asked to produce a written analysis and be available for deposition and trial testimony addressing these questions from a capital markets and commercial mortgage brokerage standpoint.

This type of engagement sits at the intersection of real estate finance and litigation strategy. The appraiser in the matter had addressed value. Our role was different: to explain, from a lender's perspective, how the lease structure would be received in the capital markets, what loan terms it would generate, and whether the financing outcomes were consistent or inconsistent with what a reasonable buyer or owner would have anticipated.

The Financial Analysis

The core of any NNN lease financing analysis begins with the in-place NOI. In this case, the subject property generated gross base rent of approximately $168,000 annually on a triple-net basis, meaning the tenant was responsible for property taxes, insurance, and maintenance. With minimal landlord-level expenses, the effective NOI for debt service purposes was approximately $162,500 after management fee and reserve adjustments that most institutional lenders require even on NNN leases.

The standard DSCR threshold for single-tenant retail financing in the current market is 1.25x for conventional bank and CMBS lenders, though some credit unions and balance sheet lenders will underwrite to 1.20x for well-located assets with strong tenancy. Applying a 1.25x DSCR to the in-place NOI of $162,500 produces maximum annual debt service of $130,000. At a note rate of 7.25% on a 25-year amortization schedule, which was representative of the market for this credit and property type at the relevant date, that debt service ceiling supports a loan of approximately $1,540,000.

The property had been appraised at a value in the range of $2,300,000 to $2,450,000 using a blended income approach that incorporated both the in-place lease and the market rent upside at rollover. At a $1,540,000 maximum supportable loan, that produces an effective LTV of approximately 63% to 67% against the appraised value range. On the surface, that appears to be within the 65% to 70% LTV envelope that most lenders target for single-tenant retail. However, the critical issue is that the appraised value was elevated by the rollover upside component, while the loan sizing was constrained to the in-place rent. The result is a structural misalignment: the appraisal includes value that the debt does not support, which means the equity gap is larger than a naive LTV calculation suggests.

To be precise: if a lender applies a 65% LTV to a $2,350,000 appraised value, the indicated loan would be $1,527,500, which is approximately consistent with the DSCR-constrained figure. But if a lender discounts the appraised value to reflect only the income supportable by the in-place rent, underwriting the asset at a 7.0% cap rate on $162,500 NOI produces an underwritten value of approximately $2,320,000. In this case the numbers converge, but the mechanism matters: the lender is not endorsing the rollover upside as a financing basis; they are simply arriving at a similar number through in-place income capitalization. When the below-market discount is more severe, the two methodologies diverge sharply and the financing impairment becomes material.

The Complicating Factors

Lender Treatment of Rollover Upside

The most important technical point in any below-market lease financing dispute is this: lenders do not lend against rollover upside. They underwrite the in-place income stream as if it will continue for the loan term, stress-test for vacancy at rollover, and in most cases apply a haircut to NOI rather than a premium when lease expiration occurs within the loan term. A property with four years of lease remaining on a 10-year loan is, from the lender's perspective, a partially vacant property for the second half of the loan term unless the tenant renews. The rollover upside is a buyer's narrative; it is not a lender's underwriting input.

In practice, most commercial lenders will require a lease-up or rollover reserve when significant lease expiration occurs within the loan term. For a single-tenant asset where 100% of income is at risk at rollover, lenders will either shorten the loan term to match the lease, require a cash sweep or reserve mechanism at rollover, or underwrite a stressed vacancy period into the NOI. None of these structures give the borrower credit for the upside rent; they are all risk mitigation mechanisms that protect the lender against the downside.

Single-Tenant Concentration Risk

Single-tenant assets carry a binary cash flow profile: 100% occupancy or 0% occupancy. For a property in Cape Coral with a non-investment-grade regional tenant, lenders are acutely aware that the creditworthiness of the tenancy is the primary underwriting variable. If the tenant's financial condition is impaired, or if the lease contains favorable termination or co-tenancy provisions, lenders will treat those as structural risks that constrain loan sizing or require additional credit enhancement. In disputes where the lease terms themselves are at issue, the lender's risk assessment can shift materially based on how the lease is interpreted.

Short Remaining Lease Term Relative to Loan Term

Most permanent lenders, including CMBS conduits, life companies, and regional banks, require that the lease term extend at least one to two years beyond the loan maturity date for single-tenant assets. A four-year remaining lease term effectively limits the loan term to three years or fewer for most conventional lenders, which pushes the financing into bridge loan territory. Bridge lenders price at higher spreads, typically 200 to 350 basis points over base rate, require interest reserves, and impose exit fees. The transition from permanent to bridge financing can increase all-in borrowing cost by 150 to 200 basis points and reduce loan proceeds by 5% to 10% of value, which is material to both damages calculations and refinancing feasibility analyses.

Cape Coral Market Rent Trajectory Uncertainty

While current Cape Coral market rents support a meaningful rollover upside on this asset, lenders underwriting a loan today on a four-year lease are not guaranteeing that market rents will hold in 2028 or 2029. Lee County experienced acute rent disruption following Hurricane Ian in 2022, and while the recovery has been strong, lenders with Florida exposure are sensitive to catastrophic event risk. Flood zone classification, wind insurance cost, and business interruption exposure all factor into single-tenant retail underwriting in Southwest Florida in ways that do not apply to inland markets. These factors compress achievable LTV ratios and can push required DSCR minimums above the national standard.

The Cape Coral Lender Market

CLS CRE maintains active relationships with over 1,000 lenders nationally, with specific activity in the Southwest Florida market. For a single-tenant retail asset in Cape Coral with the characteristics described here, the realistic lending universe breaks into four categories.

Community and regional banks with Florida presence are the most likely permanent lenders for this asset type, but they will be constrained by the lease term issue. Banks headquartered in Lee County or with significant Southwest Florida portfolios understand the local market and can underwrite to in-place income with appropriate reserves. Their typical parameters are 65% LTV, 1.25x DSCR, 5-year terms, and 25-year amortization. They will not give credit for rollover upside in the loan sizing but may be willing to structure a lease-up holdback that releases additional proceeds upon lease renewal at market. Interest rates for balance sheet bank product in this market are currently in the 7.0% to 7.75% range on a 5-year fixed structure.

CMBS conduit lenders are technically available for this asset if the loan amount exceeds $2,000,000 and the tenancy is acceptable, but the four-year lease term is a disqualifying factor for most conduit programs. CMBS requires lease term to extend through or beyond the 10-year loan term for single-tenant assets. This means CMBS is effectively unavailable until the tenant renews, which is itself an uncertain outcome.

Debt funds and bridge lenders are the realistic capital source for a transaction that must close now despite the short lease term. These lenders will underwrite to in-place income at 60% to 65% LTV with a path to stabilization at rollover. Their pricing is SOFR plus 275 to 350 basis points, with origination fees of 1.0% to 1.5% and typical terms of 24 to 36 months with extension options. They are not cheap, and the higher carry cost reduces net cash flow during the bridge period, which is a relevant consideration in any damages analysis involving financing capacity.

SBA 504 and USDA programs are available if the occupying tenant qualifies as a small business and intends to occupy the property as an owner-user. In a third-party tenant scenario, these programs are not applicable. However, if the dispute involves a sale-leaseback or owner-occupied structure, SBA 504 financing is a meaningful option in this market with favorable terms and higher eligible LTV.

What Attorneys Handling Cape Coral CRE Disputes Need to Know

A commercial real estate financing expert is not a substitute for an appraiser, and an appraiser is not a substitute for a financing expert. These are distinct disciplines. An appraiser determines market value using recognized valuation methodologies. A CRE financing expert explains how the capital markets would receive that asset: what lenders would lend, on what terms, at what cost, and under what conditions. In disputes where the central question involves financing feasibility, refinancing capacity, damages from lost financing opportunities, or the commercial reasonableness of a borrower's or lender's actions, the financing expert addresses questions the appraiser is neither qualified nor positioned to answer.

When should counsel retain a CRE financing expert? The threshold question is whether financing capacity, lending standards, or capital markets conditions are material to the legal theory. In NNN lease disputes involving below-market rents, the financing impact is almost always material. If a party is claiming that the below-market lease impaired the owner's ability to refinance, the damages calculation requires a financing expert to quantify what loan would have been available under market-rate versus below-market-rate lease assumptions. That is a technical capital markets analysis, not a valuation exercise.

What makes a CRE financing expert qualified? The relevant qualification is active, ongoing practice in commercial mortgage origination and capital markets, not a history of banking employment or an academic background in real estate finance. A retired loan officer from a community bank can testify about that bank's underwriting practices. A working commercial mortgage broker with 1,000-plus lender relationships and active deal flow across multiple lender categories can explain the entire capital stack, compare product types, and opine on what the market as a whole would do with a given asset. That is a materially different and broader body of expertise. CLS CRE has closed approximately $1 billion in transaction volume across 50 states, which provides direct, current market knowledge rather than retrospective institutional memory.

What information should counsel gather before the expert engagement? The complete lease documentation including any amendments, estoppels, and subordination agreements; the most recent rent roll and operating statements; any prior loan documents including the original note, appraisal, and credit approval; any correspondence between the parties regarding the financing; the current rent comps for the Cape Coral submarket; and any prior expert reports or appraisals already produced in the matter. The more complete the documentary record, the more defensible and precise the financing analysis will be.

At trial or in a contested Plan hearing, financing expert testimony is most effective when it is anchored to specific, calculable outputs: the maximum supportable loan at a given NOI, the DSCR and LTV constraints that would apply, the lender categories that would engage, and the pricing and terms those lenders would require. Testimony that stays at that level of specificity is difficult to impeach on cross-examination because it is grounded in market data and lender practice rather than opinion.

Key Takeaways

  • Lenders underwrite in-place NOI, not rollover upside. The fact that a property has meaningful upside at lease rollover does not increase the loan amount a lender will fund today. The financing analysis must be anchored to the income the lease actually produces, not the income it could produce.
  • Short remaining lease term is a structural financing constraint. A four-year remaining term on a single-tenant asset eliminates most permanent financing options and confines the borrower to bridge product at materially higher cost and lower proceeds. That cost differential is quantifiable and is a legitimate element of any damages analysis.
  • DSCR and LTV work together but can diverge. When a property's appraised value incorporates rollover upside that the in-place NOI cannot support in debt service, the LTV-indicated loan and the DSCR-constrained loan produce different answers. Understanding which constraint is binding is essential to an accurate financing analysis.

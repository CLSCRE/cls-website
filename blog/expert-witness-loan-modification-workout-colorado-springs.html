Expert Witness: Commercial Loan Modification Standards in Colorado Springs, CO

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Loan Modification and Workout Dispute

The Colorado Springs Market Context

Colorado Springs is not a secondary market that attorneys and investors can afford to treat casually in CRE financing disputes. The city's commercial real estate fundamentals have been reshaped over the past decade by a convergence of military-driven demand, technology sector migration from Denver, and a residential housing boom that has pulled capital into multifamily and mixed-use product at a pace that many lenders underestimated. El Paso County's population crossed 750,000 and continues growing, and that demographic pressure has had direct, measurable effects on commercial property cash flows across every asset class.

For loan workout and modification disputes specifically, Colorado Springs presents a distinctive set of underwriting conditions that courts and arbitrators need to understand. Multifamily assets in submarkets like Briargate, Northgate, and the Powers Corridor have posted strong rent growth, with average effective rents in stabilized Class B and Class A projects running meaningfully above the levels underwritten at origination in the 2018 to 2021 period. That creates a situation where a borrower entering a workout conversation may have an asset performing better than the original appraisal assumed, yet still face a lender demanding modification terms that were priced as if the collateral had deteriorated. Industrial product near the intermodal corridor and along Highway 24 has seen vacancy compress into the low single digits, with lease rates on new NNN deals running 15 to 25 percent above what was achievable pre-pandemic. Retail in legacy anchored centers has been more volatile, with anchor vacancies creating real cash flow disruption, while necessity-anchored strip centers have held occupancy well above 90 percent. Office remains the most contested category, with suburban single-tenant and owner-user product absorbing reasonably well but multi-tenant Class B product facing elevated vacancy in the 12 to 18 percent range downtown.

These divergent trajectories matter enormously in a loan modification dispute. The question of whether a lender's workout terms were commercially reasonable cannot be answered without first establishing what the Colorado Springs market was actually doing at the time the modification was proposed or executed. That analysis requires someone who knows how lenders in this specific market were underwriting in that specific window, not someone applying national averages to a local dispute.

The Engagement

In this engagement, our firm, Commercial Lending Solutions, was retained by litigation counsel representing the borrower in a dispute arising from a contested loan modification and subsequent enforcement action involving a stabilized multifamily asset in the Colorado Springs metro area. The property was a mid-size apartment community with conventional financing that had originated during a period of compressed cap rates and aggressive underwriting. When property-level cash flow softened during a transition period tied to capital improvements and temporary occupancy disruption, the borrower entered into modification discussions with the senior lender.

The core legal and financial questions that counsel needed answered were substantive and interconnected. First, did the lender's conduct during the modification negotiation process conform to industry standards of care for commercial real estate workouts, specifically with respect to the timing of information requests, the adequacy of the lender's own financial analysis, and the reasonableness of the conditions imposed? Second, were the modification terms ultimately proposed by the lender commercially reasonable given the property's documented cash flow trajectory and the prevailing market conditions in Colorado Springs at the time? Third, how did the lender's actions compare to what a similarly situated institutional lender would have done under the same circumstances? And fourth, was the borrower's financial position actually as distressed as the lender characterized it in its credit memorandum supporting the modification, or did that characterization reflect selective use of stabilized income data?

CLS CRE was engaged to analyze the loan file, the modification proposal, the property financials, and the lender's internal credit documentation, and to render expert opinions on each of these questions under Rule 26.

The Financial Analysis

The methodology I applied was grounded in conventional commercial mortgage underwriting as it is actually practiced by institutional lenders, not as it is described in academic texts. The starting point was a reconstruction of the property's actual net operating income at three points in time: at original loan origination, at the date of the borrower's first modification request, and at the date the lender's final modification terms were delivered. This three-period analysis is critical because it reveals whether the lender's characterization of distress was contemporaneous with actual cash flow problems or whether it was a backward-looking narrative applied to a property that had already begun recovering.

For the multifamily asset at issue, I analyzed trailing 12-month and trailing 3-month operating statements, rent rolls with move-in and move-out data, and the property management company's monthly variance reports. Effective gross income was calculated using actual collected rents rather than scheduled rents, with concessions, vacancy loss, and bad debt separated and examined individually. Operating expenses were benchmarked against Colorado Springs market norms for comparable vintage multifamily communities, and I identified two categories of expense that the lender had treated differently in its internal underwriting than they were reflected in the GAAP financials: capital improvement costs that were running through the operating statement and management fees that were calculated on gross rather than effective revenue.

After normalizing for those items, the reconstructed NOI for the subject property at the date of the modification request was approximately $1,050,000 annually on a 120-unit asset. The lender's own credit memorandum had used a figure closer to $880,000, a difference of nearly 19 percent, because it incorporated the anomalous renovation-period vacancy without adjusting for the temporary nature of that disruption.

Applying the industry-standard DSCR threshold of 1.25x, which is the conventional minimum for multifamily loans originated by institutional lenders conforming to Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, and correspondent bank guidelines, I calculated the maximum supportable debt service. At a stressed interest rate of 6.75 percent on a 30-year amortization schedule (consistent with where conventional multifamily financing was pricing in the Colorado Springs market during the relevant period), the maximum annual debt service a 1.25x DSCR would support against normalized NOI of $1,050,000 was approximately $840,000. That translates to a maximum supportable loan balance of approximately $11.6 million.

The outstanding loan balance at modification was approximately $10.2 million. This means the property's normalized cash flow, properly calculated, was not only sufficient to service the existing debt at a 1.25x DSCR but actually supported a loan approximately 14 percent higher than the outstanding balance. The lender's internal credit memorandum had reached the opposite conclusion because it used the distressed NOI figure without normalization. That gap between what the lender claimed and what the financials actually supported was central to the dispute.

LTV constraints for this asset type at this debt level were also analyzed. Comparable multifamily sales in the Colorado Springs market during the same window reflected cap rates in the 5.0 to 5.75 percent range for stabilized Class B product, depending on submarket. Capitalizing normalized NOI of $1,050,000 at 5.25 percent yields an as-stabilized value of approximately $20 million. Against the $10.2 million outstanding balance, that represents a 51 percent LTV, well within the 65 to 75 percent range that characterizes conventional multifamily financing at standard leverage. The lender had no credible LTV basis for characterizing this loan as critically impaired.

The Complicating Factors

Temporary versus permanent cash flow impairment. The most significant complicating factor in this engagement was the lender's failure to distinguish between temporary and permanent NOI impairment. In workout practice, this distinction is foundational. A property experiencing cash flow disruption caused by a defined capital improvement program, a lease-up period, or a transitional event that is documented and time-limited is treated differently by sophisticated lenders than a property experiencing structural decline caused by market obsolescence or chronic management failure. The industry standard is to underwrite the as-stabilized cash flow, supported by a rent-up schedule and historical occupancy data, and to structure a forbearance that bridges the gap between current and stabilized performance. Lenders that treat temporary impairment as permanent impairment, and impose modification terms priced accordingly, are not following standard workout practice. They are effectively accelerating enforcement under the cover of a modification negotiation.

Lender's internal timeline and information requests. A second complicating factor was the sequencing and nature of the lender's information requests during the workout process. Standard workout protocol requires the lender to issue a comprehensive due diligence request early in the process, review the submitted materials within a defined window, and respond with a term sheet or counterproposal within a commercially reasonable period. In this engagement, the lender issued multiple successive information requests over a seven-month period, each one triggered only after the borrower had complied with the prior request. This sequential approach, rather than a consolidated diligence process, created delay that the lender subsequently used to characterize the borrower as uncooperative. Sophisticated counsel and expert witnesses need to examine the lender's internal credit files and email correspondence to determine whether the delay pattern was inadvertent or strategic.

Cross-collateralization and guarantor capacity. The modification proposal included cross-collateralization provisions that would have encumbered a separate unrelated asset owned by the guarantor. This type of structural demand is not uncommon in workout negotiations, but it must be evaluated against the specific facts. Whether cross-collateralization is commercially reasonable in a given workout depends on the actual LTV and DSCR of the subject loan, the financial condition of the guarantor, and whether the demand is proportionate to the documented risk. Where the subject loan is adequately secured on a standalone basis, a demand for cross-collateralization as a condition of modification warrants scrutiny under both reasonableness and lender liability frameworks.

Interest rate reset provisions. The modification also included a proposed interest rate reset that would have increased the note rate by 150 basis points above the original fixed rate for the remaining term. In the Colorado Springs market context, where the property's normalized cash flow already supported the existing debt at 1.25x DSCR, a 150 basis point rate increase would have pushed the DSCR below 1.05x, a level that would have made the loan unsaleable in the secondary market and effectively unviable from a cash flow perspective. Lenders proposing modification terms that destroy debt service coverage on a property with otherwise supportable cash flow are not acting to rehabilitate the loan; they are setting conditions that guarantee default.

The Colorado Springs Lender Market

Colorado Springs has developed a meaningfully layered lender ecosystem over the past decade, which is directly relevant to workout disputes because it defines what alternatives were realistically available to the borrower during the modification period. That availability informs whether the borrower could have refinanced out of the troubled relationship, whether the lender's terms were truly take-it-or-leave-it, and how the property would have been valued by an arm's-length market participant.

Agency lenders, including Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac correspondent shops, are active in the Colorado Springs multifamily market for properties above approximately 50 units and with stabilized occupancy above 90 percent. Their pricing during the relevant period was generally in the 6.25 to 7.00 percent range on 10-year fixed terms with 30-year amortization, at up to 80 percent LTV for standard deals. These lenders require full underwriting packages including trailing 12-month operating statements, current rent rolls, and third-party appraisal, but their credit process moves on a defined timeline of 45 to 60 days from full application to commitment. If a borrower's property qualifies for agency financing, the existence of that financing option is material to any workout dispute because it establishes a market-rate refinance alternative against which the lender's modification terms can be benchmarked.

Community and regional banks headquartered in Colorado (including institutions with strong El Paso County presence) have historically been active lenders for multifamily, owner-user commercial, and small-balance retail in the $3 million to $15 million range. These lenders tend to carry more relationship flexibility and can sometimes accommodate modified structures that a national bank or CMBS shop cannot, but they are also more sensitive to their own portfolio concentration and regulatory exam findings. In a workout context, community bank lenders may have CRA or concentration pressures that influence their willingness to extend or modify, and those institutional factors should be examined by expert witnesses analyzing lender conduct.

CMBS and conduit exposure exists in Colorado Springs primarily for larger retail and office assets originated between 2015 and 2022. CMBS workout dynamics are substantially different from balance-sheet lender workouts because the loan is governed by a Pooling and Servicing Agreement that limits the Special Servicer's discretion and creates specific timelines and approval requirements. If the loan in dispute is CMBS collateral, expert analysis must account for those structural constraints when evaluating whether the servicer's conduct was standard or deficient.

Debt funds and bridge lenders are also active in Colorado Springs for transitional and value-add product, typically pricing at 300 to 500 basis points over SOFR with shorter 2-to-3-year terms and more aggressive fee structures. These lenders are relevant as the market alternative for borrowers who cannot qualify for agency or bank financing during a stabilization period, and their pricing establishes the upper boundary of what commercially reasonable modification terms should look like even in a distressed scenario.

What Attorneys Handling Colorado Springs CRE Disputes Need to Know

The threshold question for litigation counsel is when to retain a CRE financing expert rather than relying solely on an appraiser or a general real estate consultant. The answer is straightforward: whenever the dispute involves the conduct of a lender in a financing, modification, or workout context, an appraiser is not sufficient. Appraisers are trained to value property. They are not trained to opine on underwriting standards, lender conduct in workout negotiations, the commercial reasonableness of loan terms, or the market availability of alternative financing. A CRE financing expert operates in a different lane and answers different questions, and those questions are frequently dispositive in modification and workout litigation.

A qualified CRE financing expert should have direct, current, transactional experience in commercial mortgage origination, structuring, or placement. Retired banking executives who left the industry five or more years ago may lack current knowledge of how lender credit boxes, underwriting software, and workout protocols actually function today. The most credible expert witnesses are those who are actively brokering or structuring commercial loans in the relevant markets, because they can testify from current experience rather than from historical institutional memory. At CLS CRE, our active platform of approximately $1 billion in transaction volume and 1,000-plus lender relationships across 50 states means that our expert opinions are grounded in real-time market knowledge.

Before engaging a CRE financing expert, counsel should gather the following materials: the original loan documents and all amendments; the complete modification proposal and any counterproposals; the lender's internal credit memorandum and any special servicing notes; property operating statements for at least 24 months prior to the modification request; current and historical rent rolls; third-party appraisals in the lender's possession; all written communications between the borrower and lender during the workout period; and any internal lender communications that have been produced in discovery. The expert's analysis can only be as complete as the record provided, and gaps in the loan file often tell their own story about lender process deficiencies.

At trial or in a plan hearing, CRE financing expert testimony is used to establish the standard of care against which the lender's conduct is measured, to quantify the difference between commercially reasonable and unreasonable modification terms, and to establish whether alternative financing was available in the market that the borrower could have accessed absent the lender's conduct. That last point is particularly important in damages cases, where the question of what the borrower could have done absent the bad actor's interference requires a current, market-specific expert who can testify to financing availability, not just property value.

Key Takeaways

  • Normalized NOI is the correct analytical basis for workout underwriting. A lender that uses distressed or transitional cash flow figures without adjusting for documented temporary impairment is not applying industry-standard methodology, and that deviation can support a finding that the modification terms were commercially unreasonable.
  • The 1.25x DSCR threshold is the recognized industry standard for conventional commercial real estate loans. Modification terms that push DSCR below this threshold on a property with demonstrably recoverable cash flow warrant expert scrutiny and may reflect lender conduct designed to precipitate rather than prevent default.
  • LTV and DSCR must both be analyzed independently. A lender cannot justify aggressive modification terms solely on LTV grounds if the cash flow supports the existing debt, and vice versa. Expert analysis must address both dimensions and evaluate the lender's characterization of each.
  • Lender timelines and process sequencing matter. Workout standards of care include obligations related to responsiveness, consolidated diligence requests, and good faith engagement. Delays engineered through successive information requests can constitute a breach of the duty to negotiate in good faith under Colorado common law and applicable lending industry standards.

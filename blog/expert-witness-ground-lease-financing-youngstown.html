Expert Witness: Ground Lease Financing Disputes and Analysis in Youngstown, OH

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Ground Lease Financing Complexity

The Youngstown Market Context

Youngstown, Ohio occupies a distinctive position in the Rust Belt commercial real estate landscape, and that distinction matters enormously when a ground lease financing dispute reaches litigation. The Mahoning Valley economy has been in a decades-long structural transition, moving away from its steel-era industrial base toward logistics, healthcare, and education-anchored uses. That transition creates a bifurcated market: certain asset classes, particularly light industrial and warehouse product along the I-80 and I-76 corridors, have absorbed meaningful tenant demand from regional distribution operators, while the urban core office and retail stock continues to face occupancy headwinds that directly affect how lenders underwrite ground-leased improvements in this market.

Industrial vacancy in the greater Youngstown submarket has hovered in the 6% to 9% range in recent years, supported by proximity to Pittsburgh, Cleveland, and Columbus within a four-hour drive radius. That logistics positioning has attracted investment interest in sale-leaseback and ground lease structures, particularly for built-to-suit industrial and flex assets where developers seek to separate land ownership from the operating improvements. Multifamily has seen modest rent growth in the 2% to 4% annual range, driven largely by workforce housing demand near Youngstown State University and regional medical employment centers, with cap rates in the 7.0% to 8.5% range reflecting the market's higher perceived risk relative to major Ohio metros. Retail fundamentals remain challenged, with community strip centers trading at cap rates in the 8.0% to 9.5% range and significant lender scrutiny on tenant credit quality.

This market context is critical to ground lease financing disputes because lenders do not underwrite leasehold interests in a vacuum. The underlying real estate market fundamentals, the realistic exit options for a lender forced to take over a leasehold position, and the depth of the local investor pool for leasehold interests all factor into how a lender underwrites a ground lease-encumbered asset in Youngstown. A financing structure that might be bankable in Columbus or Cincinnati faces a materially narrower lender universe in Youngstown, and that narrower universe imposes stricter structural requirements on the ground lease document itself.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing a party to a ground lease financing dispute involving a commercial property in the greater Youngstown submarket. The asset was an income-producing commercial improvement, specifically a retail and mixed-use structure, situated on land owned by a separate entity under a long-term ground lease arrangement. The borrower had sought permanent financing secured by the leasehold interest. When the financing fell through and the project encountered financial distress, litigation ensued over whether the ground lease structure, as drafted and recorded, met commercially reasonable standards for leasehold mortgage financing and whether the failure to secure financing was attributable to deficiencies in the ground lease documentation rather than market conditions or borrower creditworthiness.

Counsel retained CLS CRE to address three distinct but interrelated questions. First, what standards do institutional and regional commercial mortgage lenders apply when underwriting a leasehold mortgage, and did the ground lease at issue meet those standards? Second, were the subordination, non-disturbance, and attornment provisions in the ground lease document sufficient to support a financeable leasehold position as understood by market participants? Third, could a commercially reasonable financing have been structured and closed on this asset given the lease terms, the market conditions at the relevant time, and the available lender universe in Youngstown? These are financing methodology and lender market standard questions, not valuation questions, and they require a different expert with different qualifications than a licensed appraiser.

The Financial Analysis

The financial analysis in a ground lease leasehold mortgage engagement begins with the same disciplined income underwriting applied to any commercial mortgage, but with additional layers of complexity that can either compress the supportable loan amount or, in the most problematic cases, disqualify the asset from financing altogether. In this engagement, our analysis proceeded from the ground up, starting with a stabilized net operating income figure derived from the lease-in-place rent roll, adjusted for market vacancy and credit loss assumptions appropriate for the Youngstown submarket.

For this asset, the stabilized NOI was determined to be approximately $285,000 annually, after applying a 7% vacancy and credit loss assumption consistent with Youngstown submarket conditions for retail-anchored mixed-use product. We tested this NOI against the financing parameters that prevailed in the relevant market period, specifically a fixed interest rate environment in the 6.75% to 7.25% range for commercial mortgage loans on leasehold interests, with a 25-year amortization schedule. Lenders in this product category typically require a longer amortization than standard commercial loans to manage the stepped-up payment structure that results from ground rent obligations reducing the residual cash flow available for debt service.

The critical DSCR threshold for leasehold mortgage financing is not the standard 1.20x to 1.25x applied to fee-simple commercial mortgages. Lenders underwriting leasehold positions in secondary markets like Youngstown routinely apply a floor of 1.30x to 1.35x, reflecting the additional structural risk of a leasehold interest versus fee ownership. Some lenders apply a further stress test, underwriting to a hypothetical ground rent increase scenario at the first rent reset date to ensure the debt service coverage does not fall below 1.20x even if ground rent escalates at the maximum contractual rate.

Applying a 1.30x DSCR threshold, a 7.0% note rate, and a 25-year amortization to the $285,000 stabilized NOI, the maximum supportable annual debt service was approximately $219,230. At a 7.0% note rate on a 25-year amortization, that debt service supports a loan amount of approximately $2,765,000. The subject asset had an estimated leasehold value of approximately $3,200,000 based on the income approach, implying a maximum loan-to-value ratio of approximately 86% at the supportable debt amount. That LTV figure is not what was requested; the issue was that the leasehold-specific LTV cap imposed by lenders in this market, typically 65% to 70% of leasehold value for secondary market assets, constrained the available loan to approximately $2,080,000 to $2,240,000, well below what the borrower required to recapitalize the project.

This calculation was significant in the dispute context because it established that even a perfectly structured ground lease, meeting every lender requirement for subordination and SNDA, would not have produced the loan amount the borrower sought. The ground lease structural deficiencies compounded an already constrained financing scenario rather than being the sole cause of the financing failure.

The Complicating Factors

Ground Lease Term Insufficiency. The single most common reason a ground lease fails to support leasehold mortgage financing is insufficient remaining lease term. Lenders require that the ground lease term extend at least 10 to 15 years beyond the fully amortized loan maturity. On a 10-year loan term, this means the ground lease must have at least 20 to 25 years of remaining term, including exercised options. If renewal options are not automatically exercisable or are subject to landlord consent conditions, lenders will not credit those option periods in their term analysis. In this engagement, the ground lease contained renewal options that required landlord approval under ambiguous "reasonable discretion" language, and lenders universally declined to count those option periods toward their minimum remaining term calculation.

SNDA Deficiency: The Subordination Problem. A ground lease financing requires, at minimum, that the ground lessor either subordinate the ground lease to the leasehold mortgage, or that the ground lease contain a non-disturbance covenant enforceable by the lender. Without subordination or a robust non-disturbance provision, a lender who forecloses on the leasehold interest takes the asset subject to the ground lease, meaning the lender's ownership interest could be terminated by the ground lessor for lease defaults. Most institutional lenders require full subordination of the ground lease, meaning the fee interest is encumbered behind the leasehold mortgage. Ground lessors routinely resist this structure. In this Youngstown engagement, the ground lease contained only partial subordination language that was limited to the initial financing and did not run to any successor lender or to refinancing proceeds, rendering it unacceptable to every lender we surveyed in the market period.

SNDA Deficiency: Non-Disturbance and Cure Rights. Even where full subordination is not available, lenders will accept a ground lease if the non-disturbance agreement provides the lender with: advance written notice of any ground lease default; a cure period for the lender that is independent of and runs concurrently with but not shorter than the tenant's cure period; the right for the lender to cure monetary and non-monetary defaults without termination of the lease; and the lender's right to acquire the ground lessee's interest by foreclosure or deed in lieu without ground lessor consent. The ground lease at issue in this matter provided only a 10-day lender notice period before the ground lessor could declare a default and initiate termination proceedings, and it did not grant the lender an independent cure right for non-monetary defaults. That structure is not financeable under any institutional lender's credit policy I have encountered in 20 years of commercial mortgage brokerage.

Ground Rent Reset Mechanics. Ground leases with percentage-of-appraised-value rent resets, rather than fixed CPI escalations, create underwriting uncertainty that lenders price heavily or reject outright. In the Youngstown market, where appraised values are more volatile and the appraisal methodology for ground-leased properties generates wider value ranges than in larger markets, a rent reset tied to appraised value creates scenarios where ground rent can double or triple at a reset date, potentially eliminating all cash flow available for debt service. Lenders require either a fixed escalation schedule or a cap on rent increases at reset dates, combined with a modeled stress scenario showing DSCR survivability at maximum allowable rent increases.

Leasehold Title Insurance Availability. Leasehold mortgage lenders require ALTA leasehold title insurance endorsements that, among other things, insure the lender's leasehold mortgage position against claims arising from the ground lease. In some Youngstown-area transactions, the ground lease recording and chain of title issues have complicated the issuance of these endorsements, adding delay and cost to an already narrow lender market.

The Youngstown Lender Market

The lender universe for leasehold mortgage financing in the Youngstown market is materially narrower than for fee-simple commercial mortgages, and attorneys handling financing disputes in this market need to understand that narrowness as a factual backdrop to any feasibility opinion.

Regional and community banks headquartered in northeastern Ohio, including institutions with strong Mahoning Valley deposit bases, represent the primary active lender category for commercial leasehold financing in Youngstown. These institutions typically lend up to $5 million on leasehold interests, require full recourse or strong guarantor support, price at spreads of 200 to 300 basis points over the 5-year Treasury, and impose ground lease documentation requirements that generally align with institutional standards. Their credit boxes require DSCR of 1.25x to 1.35x, LTV of 65% to 70% of leasehold value, and they will not extend leasehold loans without a lender-form SNDA executed by the ground lessor prior to closing.

CMBS lenders, which represent the most standardized execution for commercial mortgages nationally, are effectively inactive in the Youngstown leasehold market for loans below $10 million. CMBS leasehold underwriting requirements are among the most rigorous in the industry, including mandatory fee subordination or SNDA execution to Fannie/Freddie-equivalent standards, and the Youngstown market's secondary status limits CMBS appetite for this product type even when structural requirements are met.

Debt funds and bridge lenders will consider leasehold positions in Youngstown for transitional or value-add scenarios, but at pricing of 300 to 500 basis points over SOFR, with loan-to-cost ratios capped at 65% to 70% and exit fee structures that assume a clean refinancing into permanent debt. These lenders will not provide permanent financing on leasehold interests in this market and their involvement is typically a bridge to a fee-simple exit or a portfolio sale.

Life insurance company lenders, which represent the deepest and most competitive permanent capital in major markets, are not active in Youngstown commercial leasehold financing. Their minimum loan sizes, typically $5 million to $10 million, combined with their strict requirements for primary market assets and institutional-quality lease documentation, effectively exclude this market from their credit parameters.

What Attorneys Handling Youngstown CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to Retain a CRE Financing Expert. If your case involves a claim that a commercial mortgage financing was not obtained due to some action, omission, or structural deficiency attributable to the opposing party, you need a CRE financing expert, not an appraiser. An appraiser will tell you what a property was worth. A CRE financing expert will tell you whether a loan was obtainable, at what amount, on what terms, from which lenders, and whether specific lease or contract provisions made the financing commercially impossible or merely more expensive. These are categorically different questions requiring different expertise and different methodologies.

What a CRE Financing Expert Can Answer That an Appraiser Cannot. A qualified CRE financing expert can opine on lender underwriting standards and credit policy as they existed at a specific point in time; whether a ground lease document, as drafted, met or failed to meet market standards for leasehold mortgage financing; the size of the available lender universe for a specific asset type and loan structure; the probability that a conforming loan could have been closed given market conditions; and the causal connection between a lease drafting deficiency and the failure to obtain financing. None of these opinions fall within the standard scope of an MAI appraisal, and appraisers are typically not qualified to render them.

Qualifications That Actually Matter. A qualified CRE financing expert has direct, current, transactional experience arranging commercial mortgage debt across multiple lender categories and multiple market cycles. Relevant qualifications include a high volume of closed commercial mortgage transactions, documented lender relationships across bank, CMBS, debt fund, and agency categories, and experience with the specific asset types and markets at issue. A retired banker with experience in one institution's credit policies is not the same as an active broker with current market intelligence across hundreds of lenders. The distinction matters at trial because opposing counsel will probe whether the expert's knowledge of lender standards reflects current market practice or a single institution's historical policies.

Information Counsel Should Gather Before the Engagement. To provide an efficient and fully supported expert opinion, CLS CRE will need the complete ground lease document and all amendments; any SNDA agreements executed or proposed; the loan application, term sheet, and commitment letter if financing was partially advanced; any lender rejection letters or correspondence; the rent roll and financial statements for the subject property; any appraisals or broker opinions of value; and a clear timeline of the financing process and any parties' actions that allegedly affected it.

Use of Expert Testimony at Trial or in a Plan Hearing. CRE financing expert testimony is used in commercial disputes to establish that a specific loan was or was not obtainable as a matter of market fact, not legal theory. In bankruptcy plan confirmation proceedings, expert testimony on financing feasibility is directly relevant to the "feasibility" requirement under Section 1129(a)(11). In breach of contract and tortious interference cases, financing expert testimony establishes the causal link between a defendant's conduct and the plaintiff's inability to close a transaction. In ground lease disputes specifically, expert testimony on SNDA market standards is often the most efficient way to establish whether a lessor's refusal to execute an industry-standard non-disturbance agreement was commercially unreasonable.

Key Takeaways

  • Ground lease financing in Youngstown operates in a narrow lender market. Secondary market status, combined with the structural complexity of leasehold interests, limits active lenders primarily to regional community banks and select bridge debt funds, each with strict ground lease documentation requirements that differ materially from fee-simple mortgage standards.
  • SNDA provisions are not boilerplate; they are financing prerequisites. A ground lease that lacks a full subordination agreement or a properly structured non-disturbance covenant granting the lender independent cure rights and protection against lease termination is not financeable by any institutional lender in this market, regardless of the borrower's creditworthiness or the property's income.
  • Leasehold LTV constraints compress available loan proceeds independently of DSCR. Even where income supports a higher loan amount on a DSCR basis, lenders cap leasehold mortgage LTV at 65% to 70% of leasehold value in secondary markets, a constraint that can produce a supportable loan materially below the borrower's recapitalization requirement.

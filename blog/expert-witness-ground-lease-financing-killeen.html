Expert Witness: Ground Lease Financing Disputes and Analysis in Killeen, TX

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Ground Lease Financing Complexity

The Killeen Market Context

Killeen, Texas sits at the intersection of two powerful economic forces that make it one of the more distinctive commercial real estate markets in the state: Fort Cavazos (formerly Fort Hood), one of the largest active military installations in the United States, and a rapidly expanding civilian corridor along the US-190 and Interstate 14 growth axis connecting Killeen to Temple and Waco. For attorneys handling commercial real estate disputes and for investors evaluating opportunity here, understanding those fundamentals is not optional background. It is the foundation of every financing and valuation argument that matters in litigation.

The military presence creates a consumption-driven commercial base that is unusually stable relative to similarly sized Texas markets. Retail vacancy along the South Fort Hood Street and Killeen Mall corridors has historically outperformed Texas secondary-market averages, though the ongoing restructuring of big-box retail has created pockets of functionally obsolete space that complicate leasehold analysis. Net lease and ground lease structures are disproportionately common in Killeen's commercial landscape precisely because national operators entering the market in the 2000s and early 2010s routinely used sale-leaseback and ground lease formats to control their capital deployment while maintaining operational presence near the installation. That history means ground lease financing disputes are not hypothetical edge cases here; they are a recurring feature of the Killeen commercial property landscape.

Retail cap rates in Killeen for stabilized net lease assets have generally traded in the 6.25% to 7.50% range depending on credit quality and lease term, which is tighter than many Texas secondary markets but wider than Austin or Dallas. Ground lease land positions specifically have traded at implied cap rates of 4.50% to 5.50% for long-term ground leases with creditworthy tenants, reflecting the perpetual income stream and land appreciation component. Multifamily vacancy sits near the 6% to 8% range, supported by PCS (permanent change of station) military demand cycles. Industrial is still maturing in the Killeen submarket, with vacancy in the 8% to 12% range, and new industrial ground lease activity has increased along the Killeen-Fort Cavazos perimeter as defense contractor support operations expand. These are the benchmarks against which any financing feasibility analysis in a Killeen ground lease dispute must be calibrated.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing a commercial borrower whose attempt to finance a leasehold interest in a ground-leased retail pad site in the Killeen market had collapsed, triggering a dispute with the ground lessor and the prospective lender. CLS CRE was engaged to provide a Rule 26 expert report and, if necessary, deposition and trial testimony addressing three specific questions: whether the ground lease as structured was financeable under prevailing commercial mortgage market standards; whether the subordination, non-disturbance, and attornment provisions contained in the lease met or failed to meet those standards; and whether the lender's decision to decline financing was consistent with how reasonable commercial mortgage lenders underwrite leasehold interests in this asset category and market.

The underlying asset was a freestanding retail building occupied by a national credit tenant, built on a ground-leased pad within a larger commercial development. The ground lease had an original term of 50 years with two 10-year extension options remaining. The ground lease was held by a private land trust, and the leasehold interest was what the borrower intended to encumber as collateral for a commercial mortgage loan. The core legal questions turned on whether ambiguities in the SNDA language and specific lease provisions rendered the leasehold unfinanceable, whether those deficiencies were curable, and what damages, if any, flowed from the financing failure. These are exactly the kinds of questions that require a CRE financing expert rather than a real estate appraiser or a general contractor.

The Financial Analysis

The income analysis began with the ground lease rent structure and the overlying tenant lease. A ground-leased retail pad supporting a single-tenant net lease building presents a layered income structure: the land owner receives ground rent from the leasehold owner, and the leasehold owner receives NNN rent from the operating tenant. From a lender's perspective, the collateral for a leasehold mortgage is the income stream generated by the tenant lease, net of ground rent obligations, and the residual leasehold value. Separating these income layers precisely is essential to any credible financing feasibility analysis.

For this engagement, the tenant lease produced gross NNN rent of approximately $180,000 per year. The ground rent obligation under the ground lease was $28,500 per year, escalating at 10% every five years. Net effective income available to service a leasehold mortgage was therefore approximately $151,500 annually, before reserves. After applying a 5% vacancy and credit loss reserve (standard for single-tenant analysis at a non-investment-grade credit) and a $4,500 annual replacement reserve appropriate for a retail pad structure of this vintage, the effective net operating income available to service debt was approximately $139,725.

Commercial lenders underwriting leasehold mortgages on stabilized net lease retail assets typically require a debt service coverage ratio of 1.25x at minimum, with many institutional lenders requiring 1.30x on leasehold collateral due to the additional structural complexity and enforcement risk. Applying the market-standard 1.25x DSCR floor, the maximum annual debt service supportable by this income stream was $111,780. At a 7.25% interest rate (reflective of prevailing SOFR-based pricing for a five-year fixed or hybrid term on a leasehold loan in late 2024) and a 25-year amortization schedule, that debt service ceiling supports a maximum loan amount of approximately $1,285,000.

The land and leasehold value, established by a contemporaneous MAI appraisal in the case file, was $2,100,000 for the leasehold interest (excluding the fee simple land value, which was separately valued at $480,000). The leasehold mortgage LTV implied by the maximum supportable loan was therefore 61.2%, which falls within the 55% to 65% LTV range that conventional commercial mortgage lenders typically apply to leasehold collateral. This is meaningfully more conservative than the 70% to 75% LTV that the same lender would apply to a fee simple retail asset with comparable income characteristics, specifically because of the contingent risks embedded in ground lease structures. The analysis confirmed that the financial profile of the leasehold interest was, in isolation, within the financeable range. The problem was not the numbers. The problem was the lease structure.

The Complicating Factors

Deficient SNDA Provisions. The single most significant complicating factor in this engagement was the absence of a lender-compliant SNDA agreement. Subordination, non-disturbance, and attornment provisions serve a precise function in leasehold financing: they define the relationship between a ground lease lender and the ground lessor in the event of a default, foreclosure, or other disruption. Without a properly structured SNDA, a commercial mortgage lender has no assurance that its leasehold mortgage will survive a ground lease termination event, which means the collateral can be extinguished before the loan is repaid. Every institutional lender that finances leasehold interests requires an SNDA executed by the ground lessor as a condition of loan closing. This is not a preference; it is a bright-line underwriting requirement.

In this case, the ground lease contained SNDA language that was internally inconsistent: it purported to subordinate the ground lease to any future leasehold mortgage but simultaneously reserved the ground lessor's right to terminate the ground lease upon a leasehold default without notice to the lender and without any cure period. That combination is facially unfinanceable. A lender advancing $1.28 million against a leasehold interest cannot accept a structure under which the ground lessor can terminate the underlying property right without affording the lender any opportunity to cure the default or acquire the leasehold position. I reviewed term sheets and commitment letters from seven different lenders in this engagement, and every one conditioned its interest on a revised SNDA providing at minimum 60 days' notice to the lender, an independent cure right for the lender, and an obligation on the ground lessor to enter into a new ground lease with the lender upon any leasehold termination resulting from borrower default.

Ground Lease Term Adequacy. The second complicating factor was the relationship between the remaining ground lease term and the required loan term. Lenders require that the ground lease term extend beyond the loan maturity by a meaningful margin, typically 10 to 15 years at minimum, to ensure that the leasehold interest is not approaching expiration during the loan period. With two 10-year options remaining (20 years of potential extension), the theoretical maximum term was adequate. However, lenders evaluating leasehold collateral do not simply accept options at face value. They analyze whether the options are exercisable as a matter of right, whether any conditions precedent to exercise could be frustrated, and whether the ground lessor has consent rights over financing that could operate to block option exercise. In this lease, a provision requiring ground lessor consent to any assignment of the leasehold interest potentially applied to a foreclosure transfer, creating a scenario in which the lender could acquire the leasehold via foreclosure only to find that the ground lessor could refuse to recognize the transfer and decline to honor the extension options.

Escalating Ground Rent and Coverage Compression. The third factor was the ground rent escalation structure. A 10% increase in ground rent every five years may appear modest, but compounded over the remaining lease term, it creates material coverage compression risk. By year 20 of the remaining lease, ground rent under this structure would reach approximately $45,600 annually, reducing NOI available for debt service by nearly $17,000 relative to year one. For a loan sized to the income profile at origination, this escalation is manageable within the existing DSCR cushion. However, lenders underwriting leasehold loans in markets like Killeen, where tenant lease rent escalations are often capped at 10% per period or are fixed with no CPI adjustment, must model the scenario in which ground rent growth outpaces tenant rent growth. In this case, the tenant lease had flat rent for the remaining 8 years of its primary term, meaning coverage compression was front-loaded and predictable. I calculated that by year 8, the DSCR on an original loan sized to $1.285 million would have declined to 1.18x, falling below the 1.25x minimum before any lease renewal or rent reset occurred.

The Killeen Lender Market

Killeen's lender market for ground lease financing is narrower than what borrowers and their counsel often assume. Understanding which lenders are actually active in this structure and in this market is important both for financing strategy and for expert analysis of what was and was not achievable at the time of the alleged financing failure.

Community and regional banks with Texas charters, particularly those headquartered in the Central Texas corridor from Waco to Austin, represent the most accessible capital for Killeen commercial transactions up to approximately $5 million. Banks in this category know the Fort Cavazos market, understand the military demand cycle, and have relationship-based credit processes that can accommodate non-standard collateral to some degree. However, even relationship-oriented community banks draw a hard line on SNDA adequacy. No community bank credit committee in this market will approve a leasehold loan without a fully executed, lender-form SNDA from the ground lessor. The primary advantage of community bank lenders for leasehold transactions in Killeen is their willingness to work with borrowers on structuring the SNDA during the commitment period, whereas larger institutions simply decline without a pre-existing SNDA in place.

Conduit and CMBS lenders are periodically active in Killeen for net lease retail assets with national credit tenants, but their participation in leasehold structures is highly conditional. CMBS lenders require that the ground lease meet CREFC (Commercial Real Estate Finance Council) ground lease standards, a comprehensive checklist that encompasses lender notice rights, cure periods, new lease obligations, estoppel delivery timelines, and prohibitions on ground lease modifications without lender consent. A ground lease that fails CREFC standards is simply ineligible for CMBS execution regardless of the income profile or credit quality of the tenant. This is a critical data point for litigation purposes because it allows an expert to quantify the portion of the institutional lending market that was foreclosed to the borrower as a direct result of the lease deficiencies, independent of any credit or market conditions.

Life insurance company lenders and debt funds with a value-add orientation occasionally pursue Killeen opportunities, primarily for larger portfolio transactions or development-adjacent positions. For a stabilized retail pad leasehold in the $1 million to $2 million range, these lenders are largely not relevant to the analysis, and any opinion that such capital was readily accessible for this transaction would not survive scrutiny.

What Attorneys Handling Killeen CRE Disputes Need to Know

The first question attorneys should resolve before retaining any expert in a CRE financing dispute is whether the core issue is a valuation question or a financing feasibility question. Appraisers answer valuation questions: what was the property worth on a given date? CRE financing experts answer a different set of questions: was this asset financeable? On what terms? What caused the financing to fail? Was the lender's conduct consistent with market practice? Was the proposed loan structure within or outside the credit box of available capital? These questions require active market expertise, current lender relationships, and experience structuring and closing transactions, not retrospective valuation methodology.

In ground lease financing disputes specifically, the expert should be able to demonstrate active experience negotiating SNDA agreements with lenders, closing leasehold mortgage transactions, and advising clients on ground lease structuring requirements from both the borrower and lender perspectives. A retired banker who once approved leasehold loans does not bring the same current market knowledge as a broker who structured and closed such transactions in the 12 to 24 months immediately preceding the dispute period. The market standard for SNDA provisions in 2024 is materially different from what lenders required in 2015, and expert testimony that relies on stale market knowledge is vulnerable on cross-examination.

Before engaging a CRE financing expert in a Killeen ground lease matter, counsel should gather the following: the complete ground lease and all amendments; the overlying tenant lease and any subordination agreements; all lender term sheets, commitment letters, and declination letters; any lender correspondence identifying specific objections to the lease structure; the property appraisal; rent rolls and operating statements for the prior three years; and any correspondence between the borrower and the ground lessor regarding SNDA negotiations. The expert's ability to render a well-supported opinion on financing feasibility and causation is directly dependent on the completeness of this document production.

At trial or in a Plan confirmation hearing, CRE financing expert testimony typically addresses three functions: establishing the market standard that the financing structure failed to meet; quantifying the financing gap caused by that failure in terms of loan proceeds unavailable or interest rate premium incurred; and rebutting opposing expert opinions that mischaracterize lender underwriting standards or market conditions. In Killeen matters specifically, testimony establishing that the CMBS market was categorically foreclosed by a non-compliant ground lease, combined with testimony that community bank alternatives would have required specific SNDA corrections before proceeding, can be dispositive on both liability and damages.

Key Takeaways

  • SNDA adequacy is a binary underwriting requirement, not a preference. No institutional or community lender will close a leasehold mortgage without a fully executed SNDA that provides notice rights, independent cure periods, and a new lease obligation upon leasehold termination. A ground lease that lacks these provisions is not merely difficult to finance; it is categorically ineligible for the majority of the institutional lending market.
  • Killeen's ground lease inventory reflects a specific historical development pattern. National operators entering the market via ground lease structures in the 2000s and early 2010s created a significant inventory of leasehold interests with SNDA provisions that were drafted for tenant operational convenience rather than financing compliance, making financing disputes in this market a recurring and predictable litigation type.
  • DSCR and LTV analysis must account for ground rent escalation over the loan term. A leasehold loan that appears well-covered at origination can fall below minimum DSCR thresholds within the primary loan term if ground rent escalation outpaces tenant rent growth, and this coverage compression analysis is essential to any credible financing feasibility opinion.
  • The CMBS market applies CREFC ground lease standards as a non-negotiable checklist. Expert analysis can precisely quantify the portion of the institutional lending market foreclosed to a borrower as a direct result of specific lease deficiencies, which is a powerful tool for establishing both liability and damages in financing failure disputes.
  • Leasehold LTV constraints are structurally more conservative than fee simple constraints. Expect 55% to 65% LTV limits on leasehold collateral versus 70% to 75% on comparable fee simple assets, reflecting the additional enforcement and collateral extinguishment risk embedded

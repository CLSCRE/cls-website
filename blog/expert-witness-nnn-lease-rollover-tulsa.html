Expert Witness: NNN Lease Rollover Upside and Financing Impact in Tulsa, OK

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Below-Market NNN Lease and Rollover Upside Analysis

The Tulsa Market Context

Tulsa occupies a distinct position in the mid-continent commercial real estate landscape. It is not a coastal gateway market, and it is not a secondary market that institutional capital ignores. It sits in a category that sophisticated capital markets professionals recognize as relationship-driven, yield-tolerant, and fundamentally supply-constrained in specific asset classes. For single-tenant net lease assets in particular, Tulsa presents a market where cap rates have historically ranged 50 to 100 basis points wider than comparable assets in Dallas or Kansas City, which creates both opportunity and risk in the same transaction.

The Tulsa retail and commercial corridor market is shaped heavily by energy sector employment cycles, the Route 66 corridor's retail density, and the dominant influence of a handful of large medical, logistics, and energy employers. When energy employment contracts, discretionary retail tenants feel it first. When healthcare and logistics expand, industrial and medical-adjacent retail benefit disproportionately. Single-tenant NNN assets in Tulsa are not immune to this cyclicality. A tenant that signed a lease at peak energy employment may now be paying rents that were market-clearing in 2018 or 2019 but sit demonstrably below current asking rents in 2024. Alternatively, a tenant in a sector that has contracted may be holding on to a lease at above-market rates, creating a different but equally important problem for lenders.

Tulsa's retail vacancy rate has hovered in the 7 to 9 percent range across major corridors including Brookside, Midtown, and the South Tulsa retail spine along 71st Street and 101st Street. Industrial vacancy in Tulsa remains historically tight, running below 5 percent in functional warehouse and flex product in the West Tulsa and Broken Arrow submarkets, with asking rents trending upward to $5.50 to $7.50 per square foot triple net for distribution product. Office vacancy is a different story: Class B and Class C office in downtown Tulsa and the midtown corridor has seen meaningful vacancy pressure, with rates above 20 percent in some segments, which is relevant context when a dispute involves a single-tenant office or medical-office NNN asset.

For a single-tenant NNN dispute involving below-market rents and rollover risk, the Tulsa market's fundamentals matter because they directly inform whether a lender views rollover as a genuine upside opportunity or as a credit exposure masquerading as value. That distinction is at the heart of most financing disputes involving this lease structure.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing the plaintiff in a commercial real estate dispute involving a single-tenant NNN-leased property in the Tulsa metropolitan area. The asset was a freestanding retail commercial building occupied by a regional service-sector tenant under a long-term lease with approximately four years remaining. The dispute centered on whether the property, as encumbered by the existing below-market lease, was financeable at the loan amount and terms that one party contended it was, and whether the rollover upside embedded in the lease expiration had any present lending value that would support a higher loan sizing.

Counsel needed expert testimony on three specific questions. First, how do commercial mortgage lenders underwrite a property when the in-place rent is materially below current market rent? Second, does the prospect of lease rollover to a higher rent at expiration increase the loan amount a lender will extend today, and under what conditions? Third, was the specific financing that one party claimed was achievable actually available in the Tulsa lending market during the relevant time period, given the lease structure, the remaining term, and the applicable DSCR and LTV constraints?

These are not questions an appraiser is trained or credentialed to answer. Appraisers determine value. CLS CRE was retained to determine financiability, which is a function of lender credit underwriting, not valuation methodology. The distinction is legally and financially significant, and it is one I will address in detail below.

The Financial Analysis

The core of our analysis began with the in-place net operating income, which is the only income a lender will underwrite in the current period for a single-tenant NNN asset with a below-market lease. The property generated approximately $285,000 in annual base rent under the existing lease. After confirming that the NNN structure passed through property taxes, insurance, and maintenance to the tenant with no landlord responsibilities beyond structural reserve, we treated the in-place rent as the effective NOI for underwriting purposes, with a 5 percent vacancy and credit loss reserve applied to arrive at an underwritten NOI of approximately $270,750.

Market rent for comparable single-tenant retail product in the relevant Tulsa submarket at the time of the dispute was independently supportable at approximately $380,000 annually on the same footprint, representing a below-market discount of roughly 25 percent. This gap is the core of the dispute: one party argued that lenders would factor in the rollover upside when sizing the loan; our analysis demonstrated they would not, at least not in the way that party claimed.

Applying market-standard DSCR thresholds for single-tenant NNN retail, which run from 1.20x to 1.25x minimum depending on the lender category and tenant credit profile, we worked backward from the underwritten NOI to determine maximum supportable debt service. At a 1.25x DSCR, the maximum annual debt service the property could support was $216,600 ($270,750 divided by 1.25). At a 7.25 percent fixed rate on a 25-year amortization schedule, which reflected prevailing pricing in the Tulsa market for this asset type and credit profile during the relevant period, that annual debt service of $216,600 supports a loan balance of approximately $2,625,000.

Separately, we analyzed the "as-stabilized" or rollover scenario. If the lease rolled to market rents at expiration and a new tenant signed at $380,000 annually, the underwritten NOI at 1.25x DSCR and the same rate and amortization could support a loan of approximately $3,685,000. The difference between the in-place-NOI-supported loan and the rollover-supported loan was approximately $1,060,000. The critical legal and financial question was whether any lender in the Tulsa market during the relevant period would have advanced that difference today, on the basis of a lease expiration four years in the future.

The answer, based on our review of how commercial lenders actually behave, is no. Lenders underwrite to in-place cash flow. They do not advance additional loan proceeds today based on what a property might earn when a lease expires in four years. The rollover upside is not a lending metric. It is an equity story. That distinction, clearly documented and supported by lender term sheets, credit policies, and market practice, was the central finding of our engagement.

On LTV, the in-place NOI-supported loan of $2,625,000, when tested against a reasonable market value of the property based on a cap rate appropriate for a below-market, short-term NNN tenancy (which we analyzed at 7.5 to 8.0 percent, reflecting the credit and rollover risk), produced an as-is property value in the range of $3,380,000 to $3,610,000. The resulting LTV on the $2,625,000 loan was 72 to 78 percent, which is within but at the high end of the 65 to 75 percent range most conventional lenders apply to this asset type. That LTV constraint, not just the DSCR, would have independently capped the loan at or below the DSCR-derived amount, with no room for rollover upside to influence the loan size.

The Complicating Factors

Short Remaining Lease Term

Four years of remaining lease term on a single-tenant NNN asset is a structural problem in the lending market. Most conventional lenders, including regional banks, insurance companies, and CMBS conduits, require that the primary lease term extend at least to loan maturity, and preferably three to five years beyond it. A five or seven-year loan on a property with four years of remaining lease term means the lender faces a re-tenanting risk event inside the loan term. This is not a theoretical concern: it is the scenario that produces real estate owned property for banks and defaulted CMBS loans for servicers. Lenders price this risk by shortening loan terms, lowering LTVs, increasing reserves, or declining to lend at all. In Tulsa, where the lender market is deep in community and regional banks but thinner in institutional appetite for short-term NNN complexity, the practical effect of a four-year remaining term is to eliminate a significant portion of the available lending universe.

Below-Market Rent as a Credit Risk, Not Just a Value Gap

Attorneys sometimes assume that a below-market lease is neutral from a lender's perspective because the tenant is still paying rent and the property is still cash-flowing. That assumption is incorrect. A tenant paying below-market rent has an economic incentive to renew. But from a lender's credit perspective, the below-market lease raises a different concern: the landlord has less negotiating leverage at rollover, and if the tenant does not renew, the re-leasing economics must overcome both the downtime and the capital cost of potentially resetting the space for a new tenant at what may be a different use configuration. Lenders in Tulsa underwriting a single-tenant retail asset with below-market rents will typically require a larger interest reserve or replacement reserve, and they will stress-test the rollover scenario explicitly in their credit model, not as upside but as a potential impairment.

Tenant Credit and Lease Structure

NNN is a lease structure, not a tenant credit rating. A below-market NNN lease from a regional operator without investment-grade credit is fundamentally different from a below-market NNN lease from a publicly rated national tenant. Lenders in the Tulsa market treat these very differently. For non-investment-grade, single-location tenants, lenders apply more conservative LTV caps (often 60 to 65 percent rather than 70 to 75 percent), require personal guarantees from principals, and frequently require a burn-down amortization schedule rather than interest-only periods. These structural requirements directly affect the maximum loan amount and the terms, independent of the rent gap.

Rollover Upside and the "Value Add" Misunderstanding

In litigation, one party frequently argues that the rollover upside embedded in a below-market lease represents a form of value that a lender should recognize in the loan sizing. This argument conflates equity valuation with debt underwriting. Value-add equity investors absolutely price rollover upside into their acquisition models. But lenders are not equity investors. Their underwriting is governed by debt service coverage on current, contractual cash flow. A lender who sizes a loan to rollover-adjusted NOI is taking equity risk on a debt instrument, which violates the credit standards of every regulated lending institution. In the Tulsa lending market, I reviewed the credit policies and term sheets of representative lenders active in this asset class, and none of them supported lending to projected post-rollover income on a four-year remaining term lease.

The Tulsa Lender Market

The Tulsa commercial mortgage lending market is meaningfully different from Dallas or Denver in ways that matter for this type of dispute. Tulsa has a strong community and regional banking presence, including several Oklahoma-chartered institutions with significant appetite for local NNN product. These lenders typically impose conservative LTV caps in the 65 to 70 percent range, require 1.25x DSCR minimum, and price in the 7.00 to 7.75 percent fixed-rate range (as of the relevant period) on 5 to 7-year terms with 20 to 25-year amortization. They are relationship-driven and will sometimes stretch on structure for known borrowers, but their credit policies on short remaining lease terms are not flexible: most require a lease term through or beyond loan maturity as a firm condition.

CMBS conduit lenders are active in Tulsa for single-tenant NNN product above $2 million, but they require investment-grade or near-investment-grade tenant credit, minimum 10-year lease terms remaining (or long remaining term relative to loan maturity), and they are entirely inflexible on underwriting to projected rollover income. CMBS loans are priced to the 10-year Treasury plus a spread, offering potentially competitive all-in rates, but the credit box for a below-market, short-term lease scenario essentially precludes CMBS as an option.

Life insurance companies have limited but real presence in the Tulsa market for higher-quality NNN product, and they represent the most aggressive pricing (often 25 to 75 basis points inside banks) but the most conservative credit underwriting. A below-market lease with four years remaining would not meet the minimum lease term requirement for virtually any life company lender active in this market.

Debt funds and bridge lenders are active in Tulsa for transitional and value-add product, and they represent the most likely lender category for a property with below-market rents and near-term rollover risk. But bridge lending comes at a price: rates in the relevant period ranged from 9.00 to 11.00 percent floating, with origination fees of 1.0 to 2.0 points, and maximum LTVs of 65 to 70 percent on as-is value, with a funded holdback mechanism tied to the rollover event. A bridge loan at those terms produces a materially different financing cost and structure than what one party in this dispute claimed was achievable.

What Attorneys Handling Tulsa CRE Disputes Need to Know

When a commercial real estate dispute involves a question about whether a property was financeable, at what terms, and whether a specific financing claim made by one party is consistent with actual lender behavior, you need a CRE financing expert, not an appraiser and not a retired banker. Here is why that distinction matters and what you should know before making the retention decision.

Appraisers determine value. Financing experts determine financiability. An appraiser can tell you what a property was worth on a given date, using income, sales, and cost approaches. An appraiser cannot tell you what loan a specific lender or category of lenders would have extended, at what DSCR, at what LTV, with what structural requirements, and under what market conditions. Those questions require someone with direct capital markets experience in placing commercial mortgages, not someone trained in valuation methodology.

A retired banker is not the same as an active capital markets professional. Lending standards, pricing, credit box requirements, and lender appetite change rapidly. An expert who was a bank loan officer in 2015 but has not placed a commercial mortgage since does not have current knowledge of what lenders were actually doing in 2023 or 2024. At CLS CRE, we are actively placing loans in the Tulsa market and across all 50 states, with over 1,000 lender relationships and approximately $1 billion in transaction volume. That active market presence is what makes the expert testimony credible under cross-examination.

Retain the financing expert early. The information an expert needs to produce a reliable analysis includes the full lease abstract (not just the rent schedule), the rent roll history, the property operating statements for at least three years, any existing lender correspondence or term sheets, and the appraisal if one exists. The sooner counsel gathers these materials, the more complete the expert's analysis can be before depositions and expert disclosure deadlines.

CRE financing expert testimony is used in multiple contexts in Tulsa litigation, including breach of contract matters where a party claims they could not obtain financing, fraud or misrepresentation cases where one party overstated financing availability, bankruptcy plan confirmation hearings where feasibility of a reorganization plan depends on refinancing assumptions, and partnership or LLC disputes where the financiability of a property affects its distributable value. In each context, the expert must be able to explain lender underwriting methodology clearly, support assumptions with market evidence, and withstand cross-examination from opposing counsel who will challenge both the methodology and the conclusions.

Key Takeaways

  • Lenders underwrite to in-place cash flow, period. For a single-tenant NNN asset with a below-market lease, the maximum supportable loan is derived from current contractual NOI divided by the applicable DSCR threshold. Rollover upside does not increase the loan amount in conventional mortgage underwriting.
  • The DSCR constraint and the LTV constraint operate independently and simultaneously. A loan that passes DSCR may still be capped by LTV if the as-is value is depressed by the below-market lease or short remaining term. Both tests must be applied and reconciled to determine the actual maximum loan.

Expert Witness: Ground Lease Financing Disputes and Analysis in Pocatello, ID

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Ground Lease Financing Complexity

The Pocatello Market Context

Pocatello sits at the intersection of Interstate 15 and Interstate 86 in southeastern Idaho, a geographic position that gives it disproportionate commercial real estate significance relative to its population of roughly 57,000. The city functions as a regional service hub for Bannock County and surrounding rural communities, supported by Idaho State University, a legacy industrial base tied to phosphate processing and rail logistics, and a growing healthcare sector anchored by Portneuf Medical Center. For commercial real estate practitioners and the attorneys who litigate disputes arising from those transactions, understanding what this market actually looks like on the ground matters enormously when evaluating financing feasibility claims.

Ground lease structures in Pocatello tend to cluster around a few specific use cases: institutional landowners such as the university or municipal entities retaining fee ownership while allowing commercial development on long-term leases, family-held parcels along the I-15 corridor where generational estate planning has separated land from improvements, and legacy industrial sites where the original landowner monetized the fee interest decades ago. These arrangements produce the exact kind of financing complexity that ends up in litigation, because what works as a family real estate arrangement for thirty years frequently fails the underwriting standards of a modern commercial mortgage lender.

The Pocatello industrial submarket is relevant here because many of the ground lease disputes I have been retained to analyze in secondary Idaho markets involve either industrial or retail properties. Industrial vacancy in Pocatello runs in the 4 to 6 percent range, with asking rents for functional warehouse and distribution space in the $4.50 to $6.50 per square foot triple-net range depending on clear height, dock configuration, and location relative to the rail yards. Retail along the Pocatello Creek Road and Yellowstone Avenue corridors remains reasonably occupied, though the anchored center market has seen the same national tenant attrition that every secondary market has absorbed. These fundamentals are meaningful because they inform what a lender believes the collateral is actually worth, and in a ground lease structure, that valuation question becomes considerably more complicated than it is for a fee simple asset.

The Engagement

Counsel representing the senior lender in a commercial mortgage enforcement dispute retained Commercial Lending Solutions to analyze the financing feasibility and market conformity of a ground lease structure encumbering an industrial and flex service property in the Pocatello submarket. The core asset was a multi-tenant light industrial facility sitting on land held by a separate entity under a long-term ground lease with approximately 22 years remaining on the primary term, with two renewal options that had not been exercised at the time of the financing dispute.

The legal questions presented to our firm fell into three categories. First, whether the ground lease as structured, with its specific subordination, non-disturbance, and attornment provisions, met the standards that institutional and regional commercial mortgage lenders apply when evaluating leasehold mortgage collateral. Second, whether the lender who originated the loan in dispute had any reasonable basis to have funded the transaction given the lease terms as they existed at closing. Third, what the practical effect of the lease's deficiencies had on the borrower's ability to refinance the leasehold interest when the loan matured, and whether those deficiencies contributed to the default that produced the litigation.

These are precisely the kinds of questions that require a working capital markets professional, not just a real estate appraiser or a retired community banker. Answering them required our firm to analyze the lease terms against actual lender requirements, survey the active lending market for this asset class in this geography, and produce a supportable loan sizing analysis based on market-rate underwriting assumptions.

The Financial Analysis

The first analytical task in any leasehold financing dispute is establishing what the financeable value of the leasehold interest actually is under market-standard underwriting assumptions. This is distinct from the fee simple appraisal value of the property, and it is distinct from what the borrower believed the property was worth. Leasehold value is a function of the income stream the leasehold can generate, the remaining lease term relative to the proposed loan term, and the conditions under which a lender can foreclose on and transfer the leasehold without triggering termination of the ground lease itself.

For the subject property, I analyzed the in-place net operating income from the industrial and flex tenants. Gross collected rents across the multi-tenant building totaled approximately $318,000 annually. After applying a stabilized vacancy assumption of 8 percent consistent with Pocatello industrial market conditions, effective gross income was approximately $292,560. Operating expenses including management, insurance, and reserves (ground rent was a pass-through to the lessee in this structure) reduced stabilized NOI to approximately $247,000. This NOI figure became the starting point for loan sizing.

Applying a market-standard debt service coverage ratio floor of 1.25x, which is the threshold most commercial mortgage lenders require for leasehold collateral given the structural risk premium, maximum annual debt service supportable by this NOI was $197,600. Using a 25-year amortization schedule and an interest rate of 7.25 percent, which was consistent with the regional bank and CMBS market pricing applicable to secondary Idaho markets at the time of the disputed origination, that debt service ceiling supported a maximum loan balance of approximately $2,415,000. If a lender applied the more aggressive 1.20x DSCR threshold sometimes used for stabilized fee simple collateral, maximum debt service climbed to $205,833 and the supportable loan increased marginally to approximately $2,515,000.

The LTV constraint was the binding limitation in this case. Leasehold interests on ground leases with fewer than 25 years of remaining term including renewal options are typically underwritten at LTV ceilings of 55 to 65 percent rather than the 70 to 75 percent that applies to fee simple industrial assets in comparable markets. With a leasehold value estimated in the $3.6 to $3.9 million range based on income capitalization at a leasehold cap rate reflecting the structural premium over fee simple, the LTV constraint produced a maximum loan of $2.1 to $2.5 million. The income constraint and the LTV constraint were roughly aligned, which is typical when the leasehold cap rate is properly calibrated. Any loan materially in excess of $2.5 million against this leasehold would have required either a deficient underwriting process or reliance on assumptions that did not reflect market conditions.

The Complicating Factors

Lease Term Adequacy Relative to Loan Term. The most fundamental requirement in leasehold mortgage lending is that the ground lease term must extend meaningfully beyond the loan maturity date. Most institutional lenders require a minimum of 10 years of remaining lease term beyond loan maturity, and some require as much as 15 to 20 years. In the Pocatello dispute, the primary term had 22 years remaining, but the loan was a 10-year term with no automatic extension. That leaves 12 years of primary term remaining at loan maturity, which technically meets the 10-year cushion threshold. However, several lenders we surveyed would not treat unexercised renewal options as bankable term unless the ground lease contained specific lender protection provisions requiring that the lender receive notice of any failure to exercise and an independent opportunity to exercise on the lender's behalf. The subject lease contained no such provision, which materially narrowed the financing universe.

Subordination Deficiencies. A ground lessor who holds unsubordinated fee title sits in a superior position to the leasehold mortgagee. If the lessee defaults on the ground lease, the ground lessor can terminate the lease and extinguish the leasehold mortgage without any obligation to the lender. This is an untenable position for any institutional lender. The standard market remedy is a full subordination of the fee interest to the leasehold mortgage, converting the ground lessor's interest to a position junior to the lender's lien. The alternative, which is more common in practice because ground lessors are often unwilling to subordinate their fee ownership, is a carefully negotiated non-disturbance agreement under which the ground lessor agrees that even if the lessee defaults on the ground lease, the ground lessor will not disturb the lender's leasehold mortgage as long as the lender cures the default or acquires the leasehold through foreclosure. In the subject lease, neither full subordination nor an adequately protective non-disturbance agreement was in place. The non-disturbance language that existed was a single paragraph that failed to address lender cure rights, failed to define the lender's independent right to receive default notices, and failed to obligate the ground lessor to enter into a new ground lease with the lender or its foreclosure purchaser on the same economic terms. Any competent lender counsel reviewing this lease would have flagged these deficiencies before funding.

Attornment Provisions and Foreclosure Mechanics. Attornment is the ground lessee's agreement to recognize the lender, or a purchaser at a foreclosure sale, as the new tenant under the ground lease. Without a binding attornment obligation, a foreclosure purchaser of the leasehold interest has no assurance that the ground lessor will recognize the new leasehold owner. This creates a title and possession risk that makes the foreclosed leasehold essentially unmarketable. The subject lease's attornment clause was conditioned on the ground lessor's consent, which introduced discretion precisely where lenders require certainty. This is not a technical deficiency; it is a fundamental structural failure that would cause most regional banks and virtually all CMBS and life company lenders to decline the transaction.

Lender Default Notice and Cure Rights. Market-standard ground leases must provide that notice of any lessee default under the ground lease is simultaneously delivered to the lender, and that the lender has an independent cure period, typically 30 to 60 days beyond the lessee's cure period for monetary defaults and a longer period for non-monetary defaults. Without this protection, a ground lessor can terminate the lease before the lender even learns a default has occurred. The subject lease provided for notice to the lender only if the lender had previously delivered written notice of its interest to the ground lessor. The lender in the disputed transaction never delivered that notice, meaning the lender's cure rights were never activated despite the fact that the loan had closed and the leasehold mortgage had been recorded.

The Pocatello Lender Market

The commercial mortgage lending market in Pocatello is predominantly composed of community and regional banks, credit unions with commercial lending authority, and a limited number of national correspondent lenders who cover the Idaho market from Salt Lake City or Boise platforms. CMBS lenders are active in Pocatello for larger, stabilized assets, but the loan size thresholds and property quality requirements they impose effectively eliminate most of the market. Life company lenders are rarely present at all in Pocatello except for exceptionally well-located, well-tenanted, investment-grade assets, and even then the loan sizes required to clear their minimum origination thresholds typically exceed what the Pocatello market produces.

For a leasehold mortgage on an industrial or flex property of the type involved in the dispute, the realistic lender universe in Pocatello consists of Idaho-chartered community banks with local deposit bases and portfolio lending authority, regional banks headquartered in Boise or Salt Lake City with Pocatello branch presence, and occasionally a national bank with a correspondent relationship in the Idaho market. These lenders each have different tolerance for leasehold complexity. Community banks may be willing to rely on personal guarantees and local relationships to paper over structural deficiencies in the ground lease, but this approach does not reflect market standards and creates precisely the enforcement problems that produce litigation. Regional and national lenders apply more rigorous SNDA requirements and typically require their counsel to approve the ground lease before funding.

Bridge lenders and private debt funds can be accessed for leasehold collateral in Pocatello, but they price at significant premiums, typically 200 to 400 basis points over conventional bank rates, and they impose stricter LTV constraints to compensate for the structural risk. Their presence in the lender market is relevant to a damages analysis because they represent the most likely refinancing option for a borrower whose lease deficiencies prevented access to conventional financing, and the rate differential represents a quantifiable economic harm.

What Attorneys Handling Pocatello CRE Disputes Need to Know

The decision to retain a CRE financing expert should be made early in the litigation, not as an afterthought once the appraisal expert has been engaged. Appraisers establish value. CRE financing experts establish financeable value, lender market conditions, industry standards for transaction structuring, and whether a specific deal structure met or failed to meet those standards at the time of the transaction. These are entirely different analytical tasks, and conflating them is a frequent source of gaps in expert testimony in commercial real estate disputes.

A qualified CRE financing expert is not simply a retired loan officer or a former bank executive. The relevant qualification is current, active market knowledge: who is lending in the specific market, at what rates and terms, against what collateral types, and subject to what structural requirements. Lender markets change materially year to year, and expert testimony based on market conditions from five or ten years ago, without adjustment for current market realities, is of limited utility and vulnerable to challenge. Our firm closes transactions across 50 states with over 1,000 active lender relationships and approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, which means our market knowledge is current and verifiable against actual closed transactions.

Before retaining a CRE financing expert, counsel should gather the complete ground lease and all amendments, the SNDA agreements executed at loan closing if any exist, the loan application and commitment letter, any lender underwriting memos or credit approval documents obtained through discovery, the appraisal prepared at origination, and any correspondence between the lender, borrower, and ground lessor regarding the lease structure. The expert needs these documents to form opinions on whether the transaction met market standards and whether alternative financing was available.

At trial or in a plan confirmation hearing, CRE financing expert testimony is most effectively used to establish the following: what a reasonable lender would have required before funding against this collateral; whether the actual lender deviated from those standards and whether that deviation was material; what the borrower's actual refinancing options were at loan maturity given the lease structure; and what economic harm, if any, resulted from a lease structure that failed to meet market standards. In bankruptcy plan confirmation proceedings, CRE financing expert testimony is frequently used to challenge or support the feasibility of a proposed plan by analyzing whether the financing assumed in the plan is actually obtainable at the terms proposed.

Key Takeaways

  • Ground lease financing feasibility is a specialized analysis. It requires current lender market knowledge, not just property valuation expertise. An appraiser can determine what the property is worth; only a CRE financing expert can determine whether that value can be financed, and under what conditions.
  • SNDA deficiencies are not minor technical failures. Missing or inadequate subordination, non-disturbance, and attornment provisions eliminate most of the institutional lending market for a leasehold asset. A single-paragraph non-disturbance clause without lender cure rights, default notice requirements, and foreclosure protections does not meet market standards.
  • Lease term adequacy must be analyzed relative to the specific lender's requirements. The general 10-year cushion rule is a floor, not a ceiling, and unexercised renewal options do not count as bankable term without specific lender protection provisions requiring notice and independent exercise rights.
  • DSCR and LTV constraints work together in leasehold underwriting. For secondary Idaho markets, lenders typically apply 1.25x DSCR and 55 to 65 percent LTV against leasehold collateral, compared to 1.20x DSCR and 70 to 75 percent LTV for comparable fee simple assets. These haircuts are material to maximum loan sizing and damages calculations.
  • The Pocatello lender market is realistic but limited. Community and regional banks dominate, CMBS is sporadic, and life companies are largely absent. This means leasehold financing disputes in Pocatello often involve lenders who accepted structural risk they did not fully document, which creates both liability exposure and evidentiary opportunity depending on which party you represent.
  • Lender default notice and cure rights must be self-activating. Any provision requiring the lender to take affirmative steps before its protections engage is a structural deficiency. Lenders require automatic delivery of default notices to any recorded leasehold mortgagee, without precondition.
  • Retain the CRE financing expert before the appraisal is finalized. The leasehold cap rate applied in the appraisal, the income assumptions, and the LTV analysis all benefit from coordination with a financing expert who can confirm that the valuation methodology reflects actual lender market conditions rather than theoretical value.

Expert Witness: NNN Lease Rollover Upside and Financing Impact in West Palm Beach, FL

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Below-Market NNN Lease and Rollover Upside Analysis

The West Palm Beach Market Context

West Palm Beach has matured well beyond its historical identity as a secondary Florida market. Over the past several years, it has absorbed significant institutional capital, corporate relocations, and a wave of high-net-worth migration that has fundamentally repriced commercial real estate across Palm Beach County. For single-tenant NNN assets specifically, this repricing creates a particular analytical challenge: in-place rents on leases executed two, three, or four years ago may now be materially below current market, and the delta between contractual rent and achievable market rent has become a focal point in financing disputes, valuation disputes, and partnership litigation alike.

The retail corridor along Southern Boulevard, the Okeechobee Boulevard spine, and the mixed-use nodes in the Northwood and Flamingo Park adjacent areas have all experienced rent growth in the range of 15 to 25 percent since 2020 for well-located single-tenant retail and service commercial assets. Industrial product in the West Palm Beach submarket, including areas near Palm Beach International Airport and the 45th Street industrial corridor, has seen vacancy compress to under 4 percent as of late 2024, with asking rents on new NNN industrial leases pushing well past prior cycle highs. Retail vacancy for single-tenant freestanding assets in well-trafficked nodes has similarly compressed, and the spread between in-place rents on older leases and current market rents is often 20 to 35 percent, sometimes more for assets where leases were signed during COVID-era concession periods.

This market dynamic is precisely what makes below-market NNN lease disputes technically complex. The property may be appreciating in value on a mark-to-market basis, but the lender underwrites the lease as written, not as it might be rewritten at rollover. Understanding that distinction, and being able to quantify it precisely, is where a CRE financing expert witness adds value that an appraiser alone cannot provide.

The Engagement

Counsel representing a party to a commercial real estate dispute in Palm Beach County retained Commercial Lending Solutions to analyze the financing implications of a single-tenant, freestanding NNN-leased retail service property in the West Palm Beach submarket. The asset was subject to a long-term NNN lease with a national or regional credit tenant, with meaningful term remaining, but at a rental rate that had fallen materially below current market rent for comparable properties in the same submarket.

The core legal and financial questions presented to our firm were as follows. First, how would institutional and regional lenders underwrite this asset given the below-market in-place rent, and what maximum loan amount would the in-place cash flow support at market DSCR and LTV thresholds? Second, does the existence of rollover upside, meaning the potential for rent to reset to market at lease expiration, increase or decrease a lender's willingness to lend against this asset, and if so, by how much and under what conditions? Third, was the property, at the time in dispute, financeable at the loan amounts and leverage levels being asserted by one or more parties? And fourth, what is the gap between what the asset could support based on in-place NOI versus what it might support post-rollover, and how do lenders in the West Palm Beach market actually treat that gap?

CLS CRE was engaged to produce a written financing feasibility analysis addressing each of these questions based on contemporaneous lender guidelines, market pricing, and underwriting standards applicable in West Palm Beach and Palm Beach County at the relevant time.

The Financial Analysis

The methodology we apply in this type of engagement begins with a precise reconstruction of the in-place NOI, not the appraiser's stabilized or market NOI, but the actual contractual cash flow the lender will underwrite at closing. For a NNN lease, this analysis is relatively straightforward on its face: the tenant pays base rent, reimburses operating expenses, and the landlord's net income is essentially the base rent with minimal deductions. However, in practice, lenders apply their own adjustments. They will typically haircut reimbursements if the lease structure has caps on expense pass-throughs, they will underwrite management fees even if the landlord self-manages, and they may apply a credit reserve or structural vacancy assumption even for a single-tenant asset with term remaining.

For this engagement, the in-place base rent on the subject property produced a gross annual income figure in the range of $280,000 per year. After lender-applied adjustments including a management fee at 3 percent of gross revenues and a nominal credit reserve, the underwritten NOI landed at approximately $268,000 annually. This was the figure that drove all downstream loan sizing, regardless of what the property might earn at lease rollover.

The DSCR threshold applied was 1.25x, which is the standard minimum for single-tenant retail NNN assets among most permanent lenders, including life companies, CMBS conduit programs, and institutional bank balance sheet lenders. Some lenders will accept 1.20x for investment-grade tenants with long lease term, but given the below-market rent context, the more conservative 1.25x constraint was the appropriate governing threshold for this analysis.

We assumed a 30-year amortization schedule, which is standard for NNN retail assets, and an interest rate of 6.75 percent, reflecting the market rate environment applicable at the time of the dispute. At those parameters, the maximum annual debt service that a $268,000 NOI can support at 1.25x coverage is calculated as follows: $268,000 divided by 1.25 yields a maximum allowable annual debt service of $214,400. At a 6.75 percent fixed rate over a 30-year amortization, the annual constant is approximately 7.79 percent. Dividing $214,400 by 0.0779 produces a maximum supportable loan amount of approximately $2,752,000, which we rounded to $2.75 million for the purposes of the analysis.

The property's estimated market value at the time, derived from comparable sales of similarly situated single-tenant NNN retail assets in West Palm Beach and the broader Palm Beach County market, was in the range of $3.8 million to $4.1 million, reflecting the market's implicit recognition of rollover upside and the quality of the submarket location. At a $2.75 million loan against a $3.9 million midpoint value, the implied LTV was approximately 70.5 percent, which falls within the 65 to 75 percent LTV range that most lenders apply to this asset class. However, it also means that a borrower seeking a loan at 75 percent LTV, or approximately $2.925 million, could not achieve that leverage on the basis of in-place cash flow alone. The DSCR constraint was binding before the LTV constraint. This distinction is critical in disputes where parties are arguing about what loan was available or achievable at a specific point in time.

The Complicating Factors

Below-Market Rent and the Lender's Refusal to Underwrite Upside

The single most important principle for counsel to understand in this type of dispute is that commercial mortgage lenders do not underwrite to projected or anticipated income. They underwrite to the contractual in-place rent, period. A lender's credit committee is not interested in what the rent could be at rollover; they are interested in what the debt service coverage is today, based on what the tenant is obligated to pay under the existing lease. This is not a matter of lender conservatism or credit cycle tightening. It is a foundational underwriting principle embedded in every institutional lending program in the country. CMBS conduit guidelines are explicit on this point. Life company programs are equally firm. Bank balance sheet lenders, while sometimes more flexible on loan structure, will not size a permanent mortgage based on projected post-rollover rent.

What rollover upside does do, in a secondary and indirect sense, is support the lender's exit analysis and collateral comfort. A lender extending a 10-year term loan on an asset with lease rollover in year 3 will take some comfort from the fact that post-rollover rents are projected to be higher than in-place rents, because it reduces the risk of a stressed refinance at maturity. But this comfort does not translate into a larger loan at origination. The loan is still sized on in-place NOI.

Lease Rollover Risk and Its Impact on Lender Appetite

Rollover risk, meaning the risk that the tenant vacates at lease expiration or renegotiates from a position of strength, cuts in both directions for a below-market lease situation. On one hand, the lender knows that if the tenant stays and the rent resets to market, the property's cash flow improves and the loan becomes easier to service. On the other hand, if the tenant vacates, a single-tenant asset goes to zero occupancy, and a lender holding a mortgage against a vacant building faces a recovery situation. For most lenders, the rollover timing relative to the loan term is determinative. If the lease expires within the loan term, the lender will typically require reserves or adjust the loan structure to account for the re-tenanting risk. If the lease expires after the loan term, the lender is somewhat less concerned.

In the West Palm Beach retail submarket, re-tenanting timelines for freestanding single-tenant retail assets with good visibility and parking have historically been 6 to 12 months for appropriately priced space. Lenders familiar with this market will factor that into their credit analysis, but they will still size the loan on in-place rent, not projected post-rollover rent.

Tenant Credit and the Lender's Differentiated Treatment

Not all NNN tenants are treated equally by lenders. Investment-grade tenants, those rated BBB- or better by a major rating agency, will generally command more favorable loan terms: tighter spreads, higher LTV, and sometimes a DSCR floor as low as 1.15x in competitive environments. Non-rated or sub-investment-grade tenants face more conservative treatment, particularly on single-tenant assets where there is no diversification of credit risk. In this engagement, the tenant's credit profile was a secondary complicating factor layered on top of the below-market rent issue, because a lender's willingness to extend any credit flexibility on DSCR or LTV is directly tied to its confidence in the tenant's ability to continue paying rent for the loan term.

The West Palm Beach Lender Market

The West Palm Beach commercial lending market is notably active and has attracted increasing institutional depth over the past five years. For single-tenant NNN retail assets in the $2 million to $10 million range, which covers the majority of disputes of this type in Palm Beach County, the active lender universe includes the following categories.

CMBS conduit lenders are active in this market for assets that clear minimum loan sizes, typically $2 million or above, and that have lease term sufficient to extend through the anticipated securitization period. CMBS lenders will underwrite strictly to in-place NOI with no credit for rollover upside. Their pricing is spread-based over the applicable swap rate, and their credit boxes are largely standardized across the country, with minor adjustments for market quality. West Palm Beach, given its strong fundamentals and institutional recognition, is generally treated as a primary-tier market by CMBS desks.

Life insurance companies are selectively active in Palm Beach County for NNN retail assets with investment-grade tenants and longer lease terms. Life companies are the most desirable execution for many borrowers, offering the lowest rates and longest fixed terms, but they are also the most selective. They require strong tenant credit, meaningful lease term remaining, and will not consider assets with near-term rollover risk without substantial reserves or other credit enhancement.

Regional and community banks operating in South Florida, including several with meaningful balance sheet capacity in Palm Beach County, are an important part of the financing universe for assets that do not fit CMBS or life company parameters. These lenders may have more flexibility on loan structure, including interest-only periods or lease-up reserves, but they also typically require personal recourse for smaller loans and will impose their own DSCR and LTV floors. Several Florida-chartered community banks have demonstrated consistent appetite for well-located NNN retail assets in West Palm Beach at leverage levels consistent with the broader market.

Debt funds and bridge lenders are relevant when the asset has short lease term, below-market rent, or other characteristics that preclude permanent financing. These lenders price at substantially higher rates, typically reflecting a 300 to 500 basis point premium over permanent lender pricing, but they can accommodate assets in transition or with near-term rollover. They are not relevant for a borrower seeking long-term permanent financing, but they are important in understanding the full spectrum of what was and was not available to a borrower at a specific point in time.

What Attorneys Handling West Palm Beach CRE Disputes Need to Know

Attorneys litigating commercial real estate disputes in Palm Beach County, whether in state court, federal court, or bankruptcy proceedings, frequently encounter situations where the financing implications of an asset's lease structure are disputed. An appraiser can tell you what the property is worth. A CRE financing expert can tell you what loan a lender would actually make against it, on what terms, and under what conditions, and those are very different questions with very different answers.

When to retain a CRE financing expert. The engagement should happen early, before depositions and ideally before written discovery is substantially complete, because the expert's analysis will shape what documents and data you need to request. In disputes involving allegations of financing failure, loan deficiency, damages from a failed refinancing, or disputes over the value of a financing contingency, a CRE financing expert is not optional. An appraiser's opinion of value is legally insufficient to establish what financing was available at a given time, because appraisers do not underwrite loans and are not trained to reconstruct lender credit decisions.

What a CRE financing expert can answer that an appraiser cannot. The expert can reconstruct the maximum supportable loan at any given point in time based on in-place cash flow, prevailing rates, and applicable underwriting standards. The expert can identify which lender categories were active in the market for the specific asset type and credit profile. The expert can opine on whether a specific loan structure, LTV, or DSCR was achievable given the facts of the deal. And the expert can explain to a judge or jury in accessible but technically precise terms why lenders make the credit decisions they make, and why rollover upside does not, in fact, increase a loan amount.

Qualifications that matter. A retired bank credit officer can speak to one institution's historical practices. A CRE financing expert with active market engagement across multiple lender types, multiple markets, and multiple asset classes can speak to industry standards, which is the applicable legal standard in most disputes. At CLS CRE, our expert testimony is grounded in approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, relationships with more than 1,000 lenders, and active deal execution across all 50 states. That breadth is what allows us to speak to market standards rather than single-lender practices.

Information counsel should gather before the engagement. The expert will need the executed lease or leases in full, including all amendments and rent schedules. The expert will need any existing appraisals, rent comparables, or broker opinions of value. If any financing was actually attempted and declined, all lender communications, term sheets, and decline letters are critical. The expert will need to understand the relevant time period, because lender guidelines and market pricing shift over time and the analysis must be anchored to the specific date at issue.

Key Takeaways

  • Lenders underwrite in-place NOI, not projected post-rollover income. This principle is universal across CMBS, life company, and bank lending programs, and it is the single most important concept in any dispute involving below-market rents and financing feasibility.
  • Rollover upside supports lender collateral comfort but does not increase loan proceeds at origination. The distinction between what improves a lender's exit analysis and what actually drives loan sizing is often misunderstood by non-specialist witnesses and by opposing counsel.
  • DSCR constraints frequently bind before LTV constraints in below-market lease scenarios. A borrower may be within LTV guidelines and still be unable to achieve their target loan amount because the in-place cash flow is insufficient to service the debt at market coverage ratios.
  • The West Palm Beach market has attracted genuine institutional depth, including CMBS, life company, and regional bank participation, which means the lender universe is analyzable with precision at any point in time, and a qualified expert can reconstruct it for any relevant date.
  • Tenant credit quality interacts with below-market rent to either expand or compress the financing universe. Investment-grade tenants can partially offset the DSCR headwind through more favorable underwriting terms; non-rated tenants compound the challenge.

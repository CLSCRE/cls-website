Expert Witness: How Construction Defects Affect CRE Financing in Columbus, GA

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Construction Defect Impact on Financing

The Columbus Market Context

Columbus, Georgia sits at an interesting inflection point for commercial real estate. The market is anchored by Fort Moore (formerly Fort Benning), one of the largest military installations in the United States, which creates a structural floor beneath multifamily demand, retail foot traffic, and service-sector office absorption that markets of comparable population size simply do not enjoy. That military anchor also introduces complexity: ownership transitions, lease-up cycles, and financing structures here often move to rhythms tied to installation activity, deployment cycles, and DoD procurement timelines rather than purely private-sector drivers.

On the multifamily side, Columbus has maintained occupancy rates in the low-to-mid 90s across stabilized Class B and Class C product, driven by both enlisted housing demand and a growing healthcare workforce tied to Piedmont Columbus Regional and the broader medical corridor. Cap rates for stabilized multifamily in Columbus have generally traded in the 6.0% to 7.25% range depending on vintage, location, and unit mix, which positions the market above coastal gateway pricing but increasingly compressed relative to historical norms for secondary Georgia markets. New construction product, particularly garden-style projects built after 2018, has faced the most scrutiny from lenders due to supply additions and the wide range of construction quality across contractors operating in the market.

Industrial fundamentals in Columbus remain tight. The Chattahoochee Valley's logistics positioning, combined with limited Class A industrial inventory, has pushed vacancy below 5% for functional warehouse product and driven modest but steady rent growth in the $4.50 to $6.50 per square foot NNN range depending on clear height and dock configuration. Retail along Veterans Parkway and the Midtown corridor has been more bifurcated: grocery-anchored and service-oriented strips have performed well, while secondary inline and unanchored strip product has shown elevated vacancy and softening rents.

For commercial construction specifically, Columbus has experienced the same cost escalation, subcontractor availability issues, and supply-chain disruptions that defined the 2020 to 2023 building cycle nationally, but with a local twist: the contractor base serving Columbus is thinner than in Atlanta, and the pool of experienced subcontractors for specialty work (waterproofing, structural concrete, MEP coordination on mixed-use) is narrower. That reality is directly relevant to construction defect matters, because when defects arise in Columbus projects, the remediation cost estimates are often higher than expected and the remediation timeline longer, both of which directly affect lender willingness to engage.

The Engagement

Counsel retained Commercial Lending Solutions to provide expert analysis in a dispute involving a newly constructed multifamily residential community in the Columbus metropolitan area. The asset, a garden-style apartment project comprising approximately 180 units across multiple buildings, had been delivered within the prior 24 months and was in the early stages of stabilization when construction defect claims were filed. The alleged defects involved water intrusion through building envelopes, improper flashing at roof-to-wall junctions, and inadequate waterproofing at below-grade utility penetrations across a significant percentage of the buildings.

Retaining counsel represented a party with a direct economic interest in the property's financing capacity, and the core questions put to our firm were threefold. First, does a property with known, unresolved construction defects of this nature qualify for permanent financing in the current Columbus lender market, and if so, under what conditions? Second, how do the alleged defects, taken at their stated scope and remediation cost, affect the property's DSCR, LTV, and maximum supportable loan amount compared to an otherwise equivalent defect-free asset? Third, what is the economic harm, expressed in financing terms, that a buyer or owner of this asset sustains as a direct result of the defect condition, and how does that harm quantify against the property's as-complete stabilized value?

These are financing feasibility questions. They require someone who has actually placed loans on assets like this in markets like this, someone who understands how underwriters think, how credit committees respond to disclosed litigation and defect conditions, and what the real-world financing universe looks like for a compromised asset versus a clean one. That is the scope of work our engagement covered.

The Financial Analysis

To establish the baseline, we constructed a stabilized underwriting model for the subject property as if no defect condition existed, using market-rate assumptions appropriate to the Columbus multifamily submarket. At stabilization, the property's gross potential rent was modeled at approximately $1,980,000 annually, reflecting a weighted average rent of $917 per unit per month across the unit mix, consistent with comparable properties in the same Columbus corridor. We applied a 6.5% vacancy and credit loss factor, consistent with lender underwriting norms for a recently stabilized garden-style property in a secondary market. Operating expenses were underwritten at $5,100 per unit annually, inclusive of management, maintenance, insurance, taxes, and reserves, yielding a stabilized net operating income of approximately $1,062,000.

For permanent financing, the benchmark we applied reflected what institutional lenders, specifically agency and correspondent lenders active in Columbus, were pricing in the relevant time period: a 30-year amortization schedule at an interest rate of 6.875%, which was representative of Freddie Mac Small Balance and Fannie Mae DUS pricing for secondary markets during the applicable window. At that rate and amortization, the annual debt service constant is approximately 7.89 cents per dollar of loan. Applying a 1.25x DSCR threshold (the standard minimum for agency multifamily financing and the near-universal floor for conventional multifamily lenders), the maximum supportable loan based on NOI alone is:

$1,062,000 NOI / 1.25x DSCR = $849,600 maximum annual debt service / 0.0789 constant = approximately $10,768,000 maximum supportable loan.

Against a stabilized value for the defect-free property of approximately $15,250,000 (derived from a 6.97% cap rate on the $1,062,000 NOI), the income-constrained loan of $10,768,000 represents a 70.6% LTV, which sits within the 70% to 75% LTV ceiling typically applied by agency lenders to this property type. The income test and the LTV test are both satisfied on the clean asset, confirming full financing eligibility.

Now we apply the defect condition. The remediation scope, as estimated by the property owner's construction consultant, was $1,340,000 to address envelope and waterproofing defects across the affected buildings. During remediation, which the consultant estimated at six to nine months, affected units (approximately 35 units representing the most severely impacted buildings) would be offline or uninhabitable. We modeled two impacts: the direct operating income reduction during remediation and the permanent financing impairment caused by disclosed, unresolved defect litigation.

The income reduction during remediation: 35 units at $917 per month for a conservative seven-month remediation period equals approximately $224,000 in lost gross revenue. After applying a partial expense offset, the net income reduction is approximately $188,000, which reduces underwritten NOI to $874,000 for lenders underwriting the property in its current defect-impacted condition. Recalculating maximum supportable loan: $874,000 / 1.25 = $699,200 annual debt service / 0.0789 = approximately $8,862,000. The income-constrained loan has contracted by approximately $1,906,000 from the clean baseline.

On the LTV side, the defect condition compresses value independently. Using the same 6.97% cap rate, the defect-impacted NOI produces a value of approximately $12,540,000, and lenders additionally apply a distress or litigation discount of 5% to 10% to any property with active construction defect litigation disclosed during underwriting. Applying a conservative 7% discount yields an effective lender underwriting value of approximately $11,660,000. At 70% LTV, the LTV ceiling produces a maximum loan of approximately $8,162,000, which is tighter than the income constraint. The binding constraint on financing is therefore the LTV ceiling, and the financing shortfall compared to the clean asset is approximately $2,606,000.

The Complicating Factors

Active Litigation Disclosure Requirements. Every conventional and agency lender requires borrowers to disclose pending litigation affecting the collateral. Construction defect litigation is not an exception; it is one of the highest-scrutiny disclosure categories because it signals potential title encumbrance, mechanics lien risk, and deferred remediation liability. When a property carries active defect litigation, the lender's counsel must opine on the litigation's potential impact on title and lien priority. In practice, most agency lenders will not fund, period, until litigation is resolved or escrowed. Conventional bank lenders may proceed with a reserve holdback, but the holdback amounts we have seen demanded in comparable situations are typically 125% to 150% of the remediation estimate, meaning the borrower faces a meaningful liquidity constraint at closing. For a Columbus borrower relying on a cash-out refinance or a construction-to-permanent conversion to capitalize the next phase of a project, this holdback requirement can be a project-level liquidity crisis.

Construction Lender Exit Risk. When a project with a construction loan encounters defect claims during the stabilization period, the construction lender faces its own exposure. Construction loans are typically structured with maturity dates tied to certificate of occupancy plus a stabilization window, and most construction lenders have limited tolerance for extending loans on properties in active litigation. The borrower can find itself in a race: attempting to refinance into permanent financing before the construction loan matures, while the defect condition makes permanent financing unavailable or severely limited. This dynamic is particularly acute in Columbus because the construction lending community for projects of this scale is a relatively small group, and relationships with those lenders govern future deal flow. The reputational and relational costs of a workout or extension negotiation in a smaller market are compounding.

Insurance and Reserve Impairment. Lenders underwriting multifamily assets rely on a property's insurance coverage as a secondary credit support. Construction defect claims frequently trigger disputes between the property owner and the builder's risk or general liability carriers about coverage scope, exclusions, and defense obligations. When a lender's underwriter sees an active coverage dispute attached to a defect claim, it treats the property's effective insurance position as uncertain, which triggers additional reserve requirements and, in some cases, lender-placed insurance mandates that add operating cost to the underwriting, further compressing DSCR.

Lender Market Contraction for Impaired Assets. On a clean stabilized multifamily asset in Columbus, the financing universe includes Fannie Mae DUS lenders, Freddie Mac Small Balance lenders, CMBS originators for larger assets, regional banks, community banks, life company correspondents, and debt funds. Once a defect condition is disclosed and litigation is active, that universe contracts sharply. Agency lenders typically exit entirely until the matter is resolved. CMBS lenders, who sell loans into securitization pools where disclosure obligations are rigorous, are equally unwilling to originate on impaired collateral. The realistic financing universe narrows to balance-sheet lenders, primarily regional and community banks willing to hold the loan and structure around the risk with reserves and holdbacks, and debt funds pricing at significantly wider spreads. In practical terms, the borrower's cost of capital increases by 100 to 175 basis points or more, and available proceeds decrease materially.

The Columbus Lender Market

For clean, stabilized multifamily assets in Columbus in the 100 to 250 unit range, the primary financing sources are agency lenders (Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac programs), regional banks with Georgia portfolio programs, and occasionally life company correspondents for larger or higher-quality assets. Community banks headquartered in Columbus and the surrounding Chattahoochee Valley have historically been active construction lenders and bridge lenders but are less competitive on stabilized permanent financing due to balance-sheet constraints and regulatory capital limitations on CRE concentration.

For an asset carrying active construction defect litigation and an unresolved remediation scope, the realistic lender market in Columbus shifts to the following profile. Regional bank balance-sheet lenders with Georgia CRE programs, underwriting at 65% LTV maximum, a 1.30x DSCR floor (more conservative than agency to compensate for the litigation risk), and pricing at 200 to 275 basis points over the relevant Treasury or SOFR benchmark. Debt funds and bridge lenders, who will engage on impaired assets but price accordingly: rates in the 9.5% to 11.5% range for short-term bridge structures, typically 24 months with extension options, sized at 60% to 65% of stabilized value with a funded reserve for remediation. These structures are expensive and are intended as a temporary capital solution until the litigation is resolved and agency or conventional permanent financing becomes accessible again.

The practical implication for quantifying financing harm is significant. The spread between what the clean asset finances at and what the defect-impaired asset finances at, measured in both rate and proceeds, represents real, quantifiable economic damage to the property owner or the party bearing the financing impairment.

What Attorneys Handling Columbus CRE Disputes Need to Know

A CRE financing expert is not an appraiser and is not a substitute for one. Appraisers quantify value. A CRE financing expert quantifies financing capacity and financing market behavior, which is a distinct and often more consequential question in construction defect litigation. The appraiser tells the court what the property is worth. The financing expert tells the court what a buyer can actually borrow against it, under what conditions, at what cost, and how that financing capacity changes when a defect condition is present. Those are different questions, and courts have increasingly recognized that testimony on financing feasibility requires a different expertise than valuation testimony.

Counsel should retain a CRE financing expert when the dispute involves claims that turn on a party's ability to obtain financing, the cost of financing, the loss of a financing commitment, or the reduction in loan proceeds attributable to a defect, environmental condition, title impairment, or other collateral-level issue. In construction defect matters specifically, the financing expert can quantify the loan proceeds gap between the clean and impaired asset, explain to the trier of fact why lenders respond to defect conditions the way they do, and translate lender credit box requirements into plain economic harm.

The qualifications that matter are transactional, not academic. An expert who has actually placed multifamily loans in Georgia secondary markets, who maintains active lender relationships with the institutions described above, and who can demonstrate current knowledge of agency, bank, and debt fund underwriting standards will be far more credible than a retired banker or academic with dated market knowledge. At CLS CRE, our firm has approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, relationships with more than 1,000 lenders, and active transaction experience across all 50 states, including regular deal flow in Georgia secondary markets.

Before engaging the financing expert, counsel should gather the following: the full property pro forma and operating history; any existing loan documents, including the construction loan agreement and all amendments; the defect investigation report and remediation cost estimate; any correspondence with lenders regarding the property's financing status; and any financing commitment letters, term sheets, or denial letters. The expert can work with partial information, but a complete document set produces a more defensible and specific analysis.

At trial or in a damages hearing, financing expert testimony typically takes one of two forms: a calculation of the loan proceeds gap (what the property should have supported versus what it actually supports given the defect), or an opinion on whether a specific financing transaction was feasible, properly structured, or consistent with market standards. Both forms of testimony are grounded in lender market practice, not theory, which is why the expert's active market experience is the credential that opposing counsel will probe most aggressively.

Key Takeaways

  • Construction defect litigation directly impairs financing capacity in a way that is distinct from and additive to value impairment. The loan proceeds gap between a clean asset and a defect-impaired asset represents quantifiable economic harm that a CRE financing expert can calculate and explain.
  • Agency lenders will not fund on properties with active construction defect litigation. The realistic financing universe for an impaired asset in Columbus contracts to balance-sheet regional banks and debt funds, both of which price at materially wider spreads and more conservative leverage than the clean-asset alternatives.
  • Both the income constraint and the LTV ceiling must be tested separately. In our Columbus multifamily analysis, the LTV ceiling was the binding constraint, producing a financing gap of approximately $2,600,000 compared to the clean baseline. Attorneys should understand that both tests apply and that the tighter constraint governs maximum proceeds.

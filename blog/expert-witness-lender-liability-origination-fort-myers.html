Expert Witness: Commercial Loan Origination Standards in Fort Myers, FL

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Lender Liability and Loan Origination Standards

The Fort Myers Market Context

Fort Myers and the broader Lee County commercial real estate market occupy a unique position in Florida's investment landscape. Over the past decade, population growth along the Southwest Florida corridor has been among the fastest in the state, driven by net migration from higher-cost metros in the Northeast and Midwest. That demographic tailwind has translated directly into commercial real estate fundamentals that are materially different from what a lender or broker would encounter in Miami, Tampa, or Orlando, and those differences matter enormously when a court is asked to evaluate whether a loan was originated to industry standards.

On the multifamily side, Lee County vacancy rates have hovered in the 6 to 9 percent range post-pandemic, with rent growth cooling from peak levels but remaining positive on a trailing twelve-month basis. Cap rates for stabilized garden-style assets in Fort Myers submarkets such as Gateway, Daniels Corridor, and Colonial Boulevard have generally traded in the 5.25 to 6.25 percent range, depending on vintage and unit mix. Industrial fundamentals are tighter: the Cape Coral/Fort Myers industrial submarket has seen vacancy compress toward 4 to 5 percent in certain size ranges, with asking rents for distribution space in the 40,000 to 100,000 square foot range pushing above $10 per square foot NNN in newer product. Retail fundamentals along US-41 and Pine Island Road corridors remain bifurcated, with grocery-anchored and service-oriented centers outperforming unanchored strip retail that still carries residual vacancy from the 2008 to 2012 downturn.

What makes Fort Myers particularly relevant for lender liability and loan origination disputes is the market's exposure to two overlapping risk layers: hurricane and flood risk, and the volatility of seasonal demand patterns that can distort trailing income metrics. Hurricane Ian in September 2022 caused catastrophic damage across Lee County, and its aftermath created a period in which property insurance costs increased by 40 to 120 percent for many commercial assets. Any loan originated in 2021 or 2022 that did not underwrite insurance expense at stress-tested levels was, in my professional opinion, underwritten below the standard of care for this specific market. That is not a hindsight argument; it is a reflection of what a reasonably competent commercial mortgage professional working in Southwest Florida should have known at the time about the region's risk profile.

The Engagement

In the matter for which I was retained, counsel representing the borrower in a lender liability action asked Commercial Lending Solutions to provide a Rule 26 expert report on three core questions. First, whether the loan origination process, including the underwriting of the subject property's income, met the standard of care applicable to a licensed commercial mortgage broker operating in Florida. Second, whether the loan terms as disclosed to the borrower at application and at closing were consistent with market practice for that asset type and loan size in the Fort Myers market as of the date of origination. Third, what a reasonably competent commercial mortgage broker would have done differently in terms of lender selection, disclosure, and loan structuring.

The asset in question was a mid-size retail strip center located in Fort Myers, approximately 28,000 rentable square feet, partially occupied at the time of origination. The borrower had approached a mortgage broker to place a bridge-to-permanent loan structure; the initial bridge component was needed to fund lease-up, with the expectation of refinancing into agency or bank permanent debt once stabilization was achieved. The total loan request was in the range of $4.2 to $4.5 million. The legal claims centered on allegations that the broker failed to disclose material differences between the initial term sheet and the final loan documents, failed to explain the risks of a floating-rate bridge loan in a rising rate environment, and placed the loan with a private lender whose fee structure was not adequately disclosed. CLS CRE was retained to assess the origination process against industry norms and to provide testimony on what competent practice looks like in this deal type and this market.

The Financial Analysis

My analysis began with a reconstruction of the property's net operating income as it should have been underwritten at origination. The subject property had five tenants at the time of application, with three leases in place and two spaces vacant. Gross potential rent at full occupancy was approximately $336,000 annually, based on in-place rents for occupied suites and market rent estimates for vacant space. A competent underwriter in this market and asset type should have applied a stabilized vacancy assumption of 10 to 12 percent for a partially-occupied neighborhood retail center in Fort Myers, consistent with market data for comparable unanchored strip product in Lee County. That yields an effective gross income of approximately $296,000 to $302,000. Operating expenses including property tax, insurance, management, maintenance, and reserves should have been underwritten at a minimum of $95,000 to $105,000, with the insurance line specifically stressed to reflect the post-Ian environment. That produces a stabilized NOI in the range of $191,000 to $207,000 annually.

For permanent financing, the applicable DSCR standard for a retail strip center in this market and size range is 1.25x minimum for conventional bank financing and 1.20x for CMBS or debt fund execution, with most regional and community banks in the Fort Myers market operating at 1.25x or higher given their internal credit policy and regulatory guidance. Using a 1.25x DSCR against a 30-year amortization at a 7.25 percent note rate, which was a reasonable market rate for five-year fixed bank execution on this asset type in the relevant time period, a permanent loan of approximately $3.1 million to $3.35 million would have been supportable on the stabilized income. That represents a maximum supportable LTV of roughly 62 to 65 percent of stabilized value, assuming a cap rate of 6.25 percent applied to the stabilized NOI, which would produce an as-stabilized value in the range of $3.1 million to $3.3 million.

The bridge loan as originated was structured at $4.2 million, which represented an as-is advance well in excess of what the stabilized value would support on a permanent basis. The debt yield at origination, calculated as NOI divided by loan amount, was approximately 4.1 percent on in-place NOI, which is materially below the 8.5 to 10 percent debt yield floor that institutional bridge lenders and most private credit funds apply to retail assets in secondary and tertiary Florida markets. This is the core financial problem: the bridge loan was sized as though the stabilized scenario was nearly certain, without adequate risk adjustment for the lease-up timeline, the rising rate environment, or the insurance cost exposure. A competent broker should have flagged this gap between bridge loan sizing and take-out loan supportability at the outset of the engagement, because it is precisely the scenario in which a borrower can become trapped at the end of the bridge term with no viable permanent exit.

The Complicating Factors

Insurance Cost Volatility in Lee County. No discussion of Fort Myers commercial real estate underwriting in the 2021 to 2024 period is complete without a detailed treatment of property insurance. The combination of hurricane exposure, reinsurance market disruption, and the withdrawal of several carriers from Florida has produced insurance cost increases that fundamentally changed NOI calculations for commercial properties across Lee County. A strip center that was insured for $35,000 to $45,000 per year in 2020 may now carry an annual premium of $75,000 to $110,000 or more, depending on construction type, year built, elevation, and proximity to flood zone boundaries. Any broker or lender who originated a loan on a Fort Myers commercial property without stress-testing the insurance line to a higher figure was, in my opinion, not applying the standard of care expected of a competent professional in this market. This is especially true for loans originated in 2021 and early 2022, when the market signals about carrier withdrawals and premium increases were already visible to professionals who were paying attention.

Floating Rate Bridge Loan Risk Disclosure. The loan in this matter carried a floating rate tied to the Secured Overnight Financing Rate. Between the loan origination date and the first rate reset, market rates had increased substantially, materially increasing the borrower's debt service burden before lease-up was complete. A reasonably competent broker has an obligation, in my professional opinion, to walk a borrower through the interest rate risk inherent in a floating-rate bridge instrument, to quantify the payment exposure at realistic rate scenarios, and to document that the borrower understood and accepted those risks. This is not just a best practice; it is reflected in guidance from the Mortgage Bankers Association and is consistent with the fiduciary and duty-of-care standards applicable to licensed mortgage brokers under Florida Statute Chapter 494. The failure to provide that analysis in writing, and to obtain borrower acknowledgment of the risk, is a deviation from the standard of care that I would be prepared to testify to at trial.

Take-Out Feasibility at Origination. A competent commercial mortgage broker does not place a bridge loan without first confirming, at least at a preliminary level, that a viable permanent loan exists at the end of the bridge term assuming the business plan is executed. In this case, the math I described in the financial analysis section demonstrates that even successful execution of the lease-up plan would not have produced enough NOI to support a permanent loan sufficient to retire the bridge balance. That gap should have been identified and disclosed to the borrower before the bridge loan closed. The failure to conduct that forward-looking take-out analysis is, in my view, the single most significant deficiency in the origination process.

Undisclosed Fee Structure. Private bridge lenders in the current market frequently charge origination fees, exit fees, and extension fees that collectively can reach 3 to 5 percent of the loan balance or more, in addition to above-market interest rates. A broker who places a loan with such a lender has an obligation under Florida law and industry custom to disclose all compensation, including yield spread premiums and referral arrangements. The failure to disclose those fees in a clear and timely manner, and to compare them to alternatives the borrower could have accessed, is actionable under both licensing statute and common law negligence theories.

The Fort Myers Lender Market

Fort Myers benefits from a reasonably deep commercial lending market relative to its size, though the mix of available capital changes significantly by asset type, loan size, and complexity. Understanding which lenders are active and what their credit criteria look like is essential both to evaluating whether the borrower received appropriate market alternatives and to assessing whether the placed loan was competitively priced.

Community and Regional Banks. Lee County has a meaningful community and regional bank presence, including several Florida-chartered institutions that maintain active commercial real estate lending desks in Fort Myers. These lenders typically target loans between $1 million and $10 million, prefer owner-occupied or stabilized income-producing assets, and apply DSCR floors of 1.25x with LTV limits of 65 to 75 percent depending on asset type. Their pricing in the current environment runs from approximately 25 to 75 basis points over the five-year or seven-year Treasury for fixed-rate structures. They require full recourse, personal guarantees, and typically a depository relationship. They are not an appropriate execution vehicle for a partially-leased retail asset with significant lease-up risk, which is why bridge financing may have been legitimately appropriate in this case. The question is whether the specific bridge product selected was the best available option.

Debt Funds and Private Bridge Lenders. For transitional assets, the primary capital source in the Fort Myers market is private credit: debt funds, mortgage REITs, and high-net-worth family office lenders operating through structured bridge programs. These lenders will advance against as-is value with an eye toward stabilized take-out, typically at 65 to 75 percent of as-is appraised value, and they price at 300 to 500 basis points over benchmark rates with origination fees of 1 to 2 points. The range of quality and transparency within this category is substantial. Some institutional debt funds operate with clear, enforceable term sheets and transparent fee structures. Others, particularly smaller private lenders, rely on complex fee arrangements that can obscure the true cost of capital. A competent broker must evaluate multiple bridge lenders and document the selection rationale.

CMBS and Agency Platforms. CMBS execution is available in Fort Myers for stabilized retail and multifamily assets at loan sizes above $5 million, though the property type in this matter would face scrutiny given the partial occupancy and secondary market location. Agency programs through Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac are primarily relevant for multifamily assets meeting affordability or quality thresholds. These platforms were not appropriate for the subject deal at origination but would have been relevant to the take-out analysis for a stabilized scenario.

What Attorneys Handling Fort Myers CRE Disputes Need to Know

Retaining a CRE financing expert is not the same as retaining a real estate appraiser, and the distinction matters significantly in lender liability and professional negligence cases. An appraiser can tell the court what the property was worth. A financing expert can tell the court whether the loan was properly originated, whether the disclosed terms were consistent with market practice, whether the borrower was placed in a loan that a reasonably competent professional would have recommended, and whether the lender's or broker's conduct deviated from the standard of care applicable to that transaction type in that market at that time.

Counsel should retain a CRE financing expert early in the case, ideally before depositions of the broker, lender, or any party involved in the origination process. The expert can help counsel understand the technical questions that need to be asked about the underwriting file, the term sheet comparison, the rate lock process, and the take-out analysis. Without that technical foundation, deposition questions about DSCR, debt yield, SOFR floors, exit fees, and lender credit boxes will not be as precisely targeted as they need to be.

A qualified CRE financing expert should have direct, current experience originating commercial loans in the market in question, not merely theoretical or academic knowledge of the industry. The expert should be able to identify specific lender programs that were available at the time of origination, quote pricing from memory or from documented market data, and explain from firsthand experience what a competent broker does differently than what occurred in the subject transaction. Retired bankers or appraiser-adjacent consultants who have not actively placed loans in the relevant market and time period may not have the current market knowledge necessary to provide credible testimony on origination standard of care issues.

Before engaging the expert, counsel should gather the complete origination file including all versions of the term sheet, the broker's disclosure forms, all written communications between broker and borrower, the appraisal, the insurance quotes obtained during underwriting, the rate lock confirmation, and the final loan documents. The gap between term sheet and closing documents is frequently where the most actionable discrepancies appear. Counsel should also obtain the broker's lender comparison analysis, if one was prepared, and any marketing materials or pitch decks shown to the borrower at the beginning of the engagement.

At trial, CRE financing expert testimony is most effective when it is grounded in the numbers. Juries and judges understand that a borrower was promised one thing and received another. What the expert provides is the technical framework to explain why the difference mattered, what it would have cost the borrower over the life of the loan, and how a competent professional would have structured the transaction to avoid the outcome. Quantifying the harm in loan cost terms, not just in property value terms, is the expert's unique contribution to the damages analysis.

Key Takeaways

  • Fort Myers carries market-specific underwriting risks that a competent commercial mortgage professional must address explicitly, including hurricane and flood exposure, insurance cost volatility, and seasonal income distortion. Failure to stress-test these variables is a deviation from the applicable standard of care.
  • Bridge loan sizing must be validated against permanent take-out feasibility at the time of origination. A broker who places a bridge loan that cannot be retired by any reasonably achievable permanent loan on the same asset has not met the standard of competent practice.
  • DSCR minimums of 1.25x for retail and income-producing commercial assets in the Fort Myers market are consistent with the credit policies of active regional and community bank lenders and should be used as the baseline in any underwriting analysis offered as expert testimony.
  • Floating-rate bridge loan risk disclosure is an affirmative obligation for licensed brokers under Florida law and industry custom. The absence of documented rate scenario analysis and borrower acknowledgment is a material deficiency in the origination record.
  • Undisclosed compensation arrangements between a broker and a private lender are among the most actionable deficiencies in a lender liability case and are frequently buried in origination files that appear compliant on their face.

Expert Witness: How Construction Defects Affect CRE Financing in El Paso, TX

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Construction Defect Impact on Financing

The El Paso Market Context

El Paso is not a secondary market in the pejorative sense. It is a strategically positioned border economy with genuine institutional depth, a military-driven demand floor anchored by Fort Bliss, and a trade-corridor dynamic tied to the Juarez manufacturing complex that has accelerated meaningfully since the nearshoring trends of 2022 and 2023. For commercial real estate financing purposes, El Paso behaves differently from Austin or Dallas, and that distinction matters enormously when I am retained to opine on financing feasibility in a litigation context.

On the industrial side, El Paso's proximity to one of the largest manufacturing zones in North America has driven industrial vacancy below 5% in several key submarkets, including the Far East and Eastside corridors, where maquiladora logistics demand remains structurally elevated. Net asking rents for Class A industrial in El Paso have risen from the low-to-mid $0.40s per square foot per month NNN to approaching $0.60 and above for certain specifications, a shift that fundamentally changes how lenders underwrite stabilized industrial cash flows in this market. Lenders who understand El Paso recognize that absorption is real and not speculative; lenders who don't will apply a generic secondary-market haircut that is not supportable by the data.

Multifamily fundamentals in El Paso reflect a market with persistent affordability constraints and limited luxury supply. Cap rates for stabilized garden-style product have compressed into the high 5% to low 6% range depending on submarket and vintage, with military-adjacent properties near Fort Bliss commanding tighter cap rates due to perceived income stability. Retail and office are a more nuanced story. Retail along major corridors like Zaragoza, Montana Avenue, and the Westside has held occupancy reasonably well where national credit tenancy anchors are present, while small-bay office has struggled to recover post-pandemic demand. These distinctions are not academic. They directly affect how a lender builds an underwritten value and what income haircut they apply to a property with known defects or title cloud.

The border dynamic also introduces a cross-market tenant base that is unusual compared to other Texas metros. A meaningful share of El Paso retail and industrial tenants have economic roots on both sides of the border, which some lenders treat as a credit positive (diversification) and others treat as a credit risk (currency exposure, regulatory uncertainty). In a construction defect dispute, understanding how lenders actually perceive this tenant profile is essential to properly quantifying the financing impact of any impairment to the subject property's income or physical condition.

The Engagement

In this engagement, our firm, Commercial Lending Solutions, was retained by litigation counsel representing one of the parties in a construction defect dispute involving a newly constructed commercial property in El Paso. The asset was a multitenant light industrial and flex-office property in one of El Paso's eastern submarkets, completed within the prior 24 months and subject to allegations of significant structural and envelope deficiencies, including foundation movement, roof system failures, and inadequate moisture infiltration control at building penetrations.

Counsel retained CLS CRE to answer a specific and technically demanding set of questions. First, could the subject property, as allegedly defective, obtain institutional financing, and if so, on what terms compared to a defect-free asset of equivalent type, location, and income profile? Second, what would a lender require as a condition of funding on a property with known, unresolved construction defects? Third, what was the quantified reduction in maximum supportable loan attributable to the alleged defects, expressed in both dollar terms and as a function of LTV and DSCR degradation? Fourth, how did the defects affect the risk perception of lenders in the El Paso market specifically, and what portion of the conventional lender universe would simply decline to quote?

These are financing questions, not appraisal questions. An appraiser can estimate value impairment. A CRE financing expert explains how lenders actually process that impairment into credit decisions, pricing, structural requirements, and outright declinations, and those two analyses often produce materially different conclusions about economic harm.

The Financial Analysis

My methodology began with a reconstruction of the stabilized and as-defective income scenarios for the subject property. The property contained approximately 48,000 square feet of leasable area, blended between industrial and flex use. At market rents for comparable El Paso light industrial and flex product, a defect-free stabilized asset of this type would generate gross potential rent in the range of $600,000 to $640,000 annually. After vacancy at a market-supported 7% and operating expenses appropriate for a net-lease-structured asset of this type, the stabilized NOI for a defect-free scenario was approximately $540,000.

For the defective scenario, the adjustments were not merely aesthetic. Structural and envelope deficiencies of the type alleged here produce lender adjustments in three distinct ways. First, lenders apply a functional obsolescence haircut to achievable rents, reflecting that tenants either vacate, negotiate rent concessions, or the property cannot be re-leased at market without remediation. Second, lenders impute a vacancy reserve that exceeds market norms, typically adding 300 to 500 basis points to standard vacancy assumptions to reflect the risk of tenant attrition and leasing difficulty during the pendency of defect litigation or remediation. Third, lenders add a stabilized operating expense load for ongoing maintenance, monitoring, or temporary repair costs not capitalized in the remediation budget. Applying these adjustments to the subject property's income profile reduced the lender-underwritten NOI to approximately $430,000 to $450,000, representing a reduction of roughly 17% to 20% from the defect-free scenario.

On the debt sizing analysis, I applied the lender standard for this asset type and market. Conventional lenders in El Paso underwriting light industrial and flex product in 2023 and 2024 were pricing at spreads over the Secured Overnight Financing Rate or 5-year Treasury that produced all-in rates in the 7.00% to 7.75% range for well-structured, low-risk deals. For the defect-free base case, I used a 7.25% rate on a 25-year amortization schedule, which reflects the most common structure for this asset class at conventional bank lenders and CMBS conduit programs.

At a DSCR requirement of 1.25x, which is the prevailing minimum for most institutional lenders on non-stabilized or secondary-market industrial assets, the defect-free NOI of $540,000 supports a maximum annual debt service of $432,000, which corresponds to a loan amount of approximately $5,800,000. At a 65% LTV constraint, consistent with the lender box for this asset type, the independent LTV constraint produces a maximum loan of approximately $5,525,000 to $5,850,000 depending on appraised value, making DSCR the binding constraint in the defect-free scenario.

In the defective scenario, the lender-adjusted NOI of $430,000 supports maximum annual debt service of $344,000 at the same 1.25x DSCR floor, producing a maximum supportable loan of approximately $4,625,000. However, the LTV constraint in the defective scenario tightens independently of DSCR, because most lenders will apply a 55% to 60% LTV ceiling on a property with known, unresolved construction defects, recognizing that the collateral's liquidation value in a distress scenario is materially impaired. If the appraised "as-defective" value is $7,500,000, a 57.5% LTV produces a maximum loan of approximately $4,312,000. The binding constraint in the defective scenario is LTV, not DSCR, a reversal of the defect-free analysis that is itself analytically significant.

The gross financing gap between the defect-free maximum loan and the defective maximum loan, on this set of facts, is approximately $1,500,000 to $1,550,000. This is the quantified financing impairment, separate from and additive to any direct remediation cost or value diminution calculated by an appraiser.

The Complicating Factors

Active litigation as a lender disqualifier. The most immediate complicating factor in this type of engagement is that the construction defect litigation itself, independent of the underlying defects, is sufficient to cause most institutional lenders to decline the loan entirely. Lenders require clear title and unencumbered collateral. An active lawsuit naming the subject property as the locus of dispute, particularly one involving mechanic's lien claims, contractor cross-complaints, or owner's claims against the general contractor or design professionals, creates title risk that triggers automatic hold requirements at most CMBS conduits, agency programs, and regional bank credit committees. In El Paso specifically, where the lender pool is not as deep as Dallas or Houston, the practical effect is that a property in active construction defect litigation is financeable only at a community bank or private lender level, and only with significant reserves or holdbacks.

Reserve and holdback requirements. Even the lenders willing to quote on a defective asset will impose funded reserves or holdbacks that directly reduce net loan proceeds. A lender agreeing to fund at $4,300,000 on a property with a $1,200,000 estimated remediation cost will typically hold back 100% to 125% of the estimated remediation cost, releasing proceeds in draws as work is completed and inspected. This means the borrower receives $3,100,000 or less at closing, even if the nominal loan amount is $4,300,000. For litigation purposes, net loan proceeds, not nominal loan amount, is the correct metric of financing capacity, and attorneys who conflate the two will misstate the economic harm.

Insurance and bonding constraints. Construction defect litigation frequently creates gaps or disputes in the property's commercial general liability and builder's risk insurance coverage. Lenders require current, non-disputed property and liability insurance as a condition of funding. If the insurer has issued a reservation of rights or coverage is disputed in the litigation, this is an independent basis for lender declination that compounds the physical defect problem. In El Paso's market, where insurance market access for construction-defect-adjacent properties has tightened in line with national trends, this is a real and underappreciated barrier to financing.

Lender perception of border-market volatility. Some out-of-market lenders apply additional scrutiny to El Paso collateral based on general border-market assumptions that are not always supported by local data. In a defect scenario, this perception risk is amplified. A lender already uncomfortable with El Paso's market dynamics who is then presented with a property carrying known defects and active litigation is more likely to pass entirely. This narrows the effective lender universe and changes the pricing dynamics for those lenders who do engage.

The El Paso Lender Market

El Paso's commercial lending market has a distinct architecture that attorneys and borrowers in this market need to understand. The market is supported by a meaningful regional and community bank presence, a thin but real CMBS and life company conduit layer for qualifying assets, a growing presence of debt funds and bridge lenders, and an active SBA 7(a) and 504 community serving the owner-user segment.

At the institutional end, CMBS conduit lenders will quote on stabilized El Paso industrial and multifamily product meeting standard sizing thresholds (generally $5,000,000 and above). These lenders have rigid credit boxes and will not engage on properties with active litigation or unresolved material defects. Their underwriting is data-driven and their El Paso market knowledge is often less granular than local lenders, which cuts both ways in terms of how they perceive the border dynamic and local rent trends.

Regional and community banks with El Paso presence are the most operationally relevant lender category for defect-affected properties, because they can underwrite to relationship, use retained construction loan knowledge of local contractors and submarket dynamics, and structure reserves and holdbacks with more flexibility. They typically lend at 65% LTV on a stabilized basis, tighten to 55% to 60% on problem assets, and require DSCR of 1.20x to 1.25x. Their rates are relationship-priced but in the current environment are generally 25 to 75 basis points inside private lender pricing.

Debt funds and bridge lenders represent the financing of last resort for a property in active defect litigation. These lenders will engage on complexity but price accordingly, with rates frequently in the 10.00% to 12.00% range, origination fees of 1.5% to 2.5%, and terms of 12 to 36 months. They will impose significant reserves, personal recourse carve-outs, and completion guarantees if remediation is pending. For litigation purposes, the spread between conventional financing terms and bridge lender terms on a defective asset is itself a quantifiable element of economic harm, representing the additional carry cost imposed on a borrower who cannot access conventional capital because of the defects.

What Attorneys Handling El Paso CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to retain a CRE financing expert. If your client's damages theory includes any element of lost financing capacity, increased borrowing costs, inability to refinance or recapitalize, or a forced sale attributable to financing unavailability, you need a CRE financing expert, not just an appraiser. Appraisers quantify value. They do not typically opine on whether a specific loan would have been approved, at what terms, by which category of lender, and what a borrower would have had to give up structurally to get the deal done. Those are financing questions, and they require expertise in how lenders actually underwrite, price, and approve commercial loans.

What a CRE financing expert can answer that an appraiser cannot. An appraiser will tell you the as-defective value is $7,500,000 and the as-repaired value is $9,000,000. A CRE financing expert will tell you that at $7,500,000, the property supports a maximum loan of $4,312,000 from the finite universe of lenders willing to engage on a defect-affected asset in El Paso, that those lenders will hold back $1,200,000 of that in a remediation reserve, that the interest rate is 10.50% instead of 7.25%, and that the borrower is therefore forced to either bring an additional $2,200,000 in equity or accept bridge terms that increase annual debt service by $180,000 over a two-year remediation and stabilization period. Those are quantifiable, trial-ready numbers. An appraiser's value conclusion alone does not get you there.

Qualifications that actually matter. Relevant expert qualifications include active practice in commercial mortgage brokerage or commercial lending, demonstrated lender relationships across the institutional to private spectrum, and a track record of closing transactions in the relevant asset class and market. A retired banker who last worked in a credit committee 12 years ago does not have current knowledge of how DSCR floors, LTV constraints, and reserve requirements are actually applied today. The CRE lending market has changed materially since 2018, and an expert's knowledge needs to be current. At CLS CRE, with approximately $1 billion in transaction volume and more than 1,000 active lender relationships across 50 states, our market knowledge is live, not historical.

Information counsel should gather before engaging an expert. To properly support a CRE financing expert engagement in a construction defect matter, counsel should provide: the rent roll and current lease abstracts; the trailing 12-month operating statement; the as-built plans and the defect report or engineer's assessment; the remediation cost estimate; any existing loan documents and appraisals; the litigation timeline including any lis pendens, mechanic's liens, or recorded instruments affecting title; and any prior financing proposals or loan committee denials. The more complete this package at engagement, the more specific and defensible the expert analysis will be.

How this testimony is used. CRE financing expert testimony supports damages quantification at trial, lender feasibility analysis in plan confirmation hearings, and causation arguments where a party contends that the alleged defects are the direct cause of a financing failure or forced sale. It is also used in mediation to establish a financing-based damages floor that is distinct from, and often additive to, the appraiser's value-based damages number.

Expert Witness: Commercial Loan Modification Standards in Lexington, KY

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Loan Modification and Workout Dispute

The Lexington Market Context

Lexington, Kentucky sits in a commercially underappreciated position for anyone who has not spent time underwriting deals there. It is a mid-size market anchored by the University of Kentucky, a robust healthcare sector anchored by major regional hospital systems, and a manufacturing and logistics base that has expanded steadily along the I-75 and I-64 corridors. That combination creates a CRE market with genuine demand diversity, which is relevant to loan modification disputes because it shapes the factual baseline against which a lender's conduct must be measured. A lender deciding whether to grant a forbearance or restructure a loan in Lexington in 2023 or 2024 was operating in a market where the answers to fundamental underwriting questions were not obvious, and that ambiguity is often exactly where litigation originates.

On the multifamily side, Lexington has seen sustained rent growth driven by in-migration, university-adjacent demand, and a relatively constrained single-family supply in desirable neighborhoods like Chevy Chase, Hamburg, and the Tates Creek corridor. Effective rents for Class B multifamily in Lexington have trended in the $1,100 to $1,500 range per unit depending on vintage and location, with cap rates compressing into the 5.25% to 6.25% range during peak 2021-2022 transaction activity before softening modestly in 2023 as interest rates rose. That cap rate movement is critical context for any dispute involving a modification that was negotiated during rate volatility: a lender who applied a 6.75% cap rate to value a property in mid-2023 was not being unreasonable, but a lender who applied that same cap rate while simultaneously demanding a DSCR of 1.35x or better on a 7.25% loan rate may well have been imposing commercially unreasonable modification terms relative to what the market and the asset could actually support.

Industrial and flex properties in the Fayette County and surrounding Blue Grass Airport corridor submarkets have posted vacancy rates below 4% in recent years, driven by e-commerce distribution, Toyota supply chain expansion, and general manufacturing relocation from higher-cost metros. Retail has been more bifurcated: grocery-anchored and service-retail centers have performed well, while secondary strip and inline retail has struggled with occupancy and rent roll stability. Office, particularly suburban Class B, has faced the same secular headwinds seen nationally, with vacancy in some Lexington submarkets pushing above 15% to 18%. A commercial mortgage workout in this market requires understanding which of these fundamentals applies to the specific asset, because lenders who applied uniform distress assumptions across asset classes were not acting in accordance with industry standards.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing the borrower in a dispute arising from a commercial mortgage workout on a mixed-use retail and multifamily property in Lexington's urban core. The property had been performing at or near break-even on debt service when the COVID-related disruptions of 2020 and early 2021 caused partial occupancy loss on the retail component. The borrower had proactively approached the lender seeking a short-term forbearance and eventual loan modification, and what followed became the subject of litigation.

CLS CRE was engaged to answer three specific categories of questions. First, whether the lender's conduct during the modification and forbearance negotiation process conformed to commercial real estate industry standards of care, including appropriate response timelines, documentation requests, and the substantive analysis a lender is expected to perform before accepting or rejecting modification terms. Second, whether the modification terms ultimately offered by the lender were commercially reasonable given the property's verifiable cash flow, the Lexington market conditions at the time, and the lender's own stated underwriting standards. Third, how the lender's actions in this matter compared to documented workout practices at comparable institutional lenders during the same period.

The asset involved approximately 24,000 square feet of ground-floor retail space and 38 residential units above, a configuration that is not uncommon in Lexington's transitional downtown and Midtown submarkets. The loan had an outstanding balance of approximately $6.2 million when the forbearance request was submitted, secured by a first-priority deed of trust. Our engagement required us to reconstruct the property's underwritable income stream at multiple points in time, model the loan under several modification structures, and compare the lender's actual conduct to documented industry practice.

The Financial Analysis

The starting point for any workout feasibility analysis is a reconstruction of the property's stabilized net operating income, which must be distinguished carefully from the property's actual or trailing NOI during a period of distress. In a litigation context, this distinction matters enormously because lenders sometimes use depressed trailing NOI to justify refusing modification terms that would actually have been supportable at stabilized income levels. Conversely, borrowers sometimes overstate projected NOI to obtain modification terms the property cannot realistically sustain. Our role as expert is to apply the same methodology an experienced institutional underwriter would apply, with full documentation of each assumption.

For this property, we analyzed three years of operating statements, rent rolls with lease commencement and expiration dates, CAM reconciliation records, and vacancy histories. We then constructed a stabilized NOI based on market rents for comparable retail and multifamily space in the same submarket, applying a 7% vacancy allowance on the residential component consistent with Lexington's historical multifamily vacancy in that submarket, and a 12% vacancy allowance on the retail component reflecting the more challenged retail environment at that time. Management fees were normalized at 5% of effective gross income for the residential component and 4% for the retail component. After applying market-standard replacement reserves of $300 per residential unit per year and $0.25 per square foot per year on the retail space, we arrived at a stabilized NOI of approximately $487,000 per year.

The critical threshold in any workout underwriting is the debt service coverage ratio. Market standard for commercial mortgage lenders on a mixed-use property of this type in a secondary market like Lexington is a minimum DSCR of 1.20x at the modified loan terms, with most bank and credit union lenders targeting 1.25x and institutional lenders sometimes requiring 1.25x to 1.30x depending on property type and loan-to-value. At the time of the modification negotiations, prevailing rates for a modified commercial mortgage on a similar property in this market were in the range of 6.50% to 7.25% on a 25-year amortization schedule, with most lenders applying a 20-year amortization to mixed-use retail to account for the shorter economic life of the retail component.

Running the numbers: at a 6.75% rate on a 25-year amortization, the annual debt service on the outstanding $6.2 million balance would be approximately $511,800. That produces a DSCR of 0.95x against our stabilized NOI of $487,000, which is below the 1.20x threshold. However, the dispute in this matter arose because the borrower had proposed a modification that included a principal paydown of $800,000, which was documented as available and offered at the time of negotiation. At a resulting loan balance of $5.4 million, annual debt service at the same rate and amortization drops to approximately $446,000, producing a DSCR of approximately 1.09x. Still below 1.20x, but meaningfully different, and the question becomes whether the lender was required under industry standards to engage with and analyze that structure before rejecting it. The answer, based on documented workout practice and industry guidance from the Mortgage Bankers Association and OCC guidance on commercial loan workouts, is yes.

On the LTV side, comparable sales data and capitalization rate analysis in Lexington's urban mixed-use submarket supported a market value of approximately $8.1 million to $8.5 million at stabilized income and a 6.00% to 6.25% cap rate. At $5.4 million post-paydown, the resulting LTV of 64% to 67% falls comfortably within the 75% maximum LTV that most conventional lenders apply to mixed-use commercial properties. That analysis directly undermined the lender's stated position that the modification was not feasible.

The Complicating Factors

Retail component instability: The ground-floor retail space in this property presented the most significant underwriting challenge. Two of the retail tenants were local operators without national credit, and their leases had renewal options that had not been exercised at the time of modification negotiations. Lenders view unexercised options on non-credit tenants as a form of income uncertainty, and many will apply a higher vacancy stress to that component or discount the rent entirely until the option is exercised or a new lease is executed. The lender in this matter applied a 20% vacancy haircut to the retail income rather than the 12% we applied in our stabilized analysis. That difference of 8 percentage points on $286,000 of retail EGI produces a NOI difference of approximately $22,900 per year, which, when capitalized, affects the property's underwritten value by roughly $365,000 to $380,000. Whether a 20% retail vacancy assumption was commercially reasonable for this submarket at that time is a question that requires documented market evidence, not just assertion.

Lender response timelines and good faith obligations: Industry standards, as reflected in the OCC's guidance on commercial real estate loan accommodations and the FDIC's supervisory statements on workout practices, require that lenders engage meaningfully with workout proposals within commercially reasonable timeframes. The typical benchmark in institutional practice is an initial response to a complete workout package within 30 to 45 days, and a substantive credit decision within 60 to 90 days of receiving all requested documentation. In this engagement, we documented multiple instances where the borrower submitted complete packages and received no substantive response for periods exceeding 120 days. That delay, in and of itself, does not constitute a lender liability, but it does constitute a deviation from industry standards that has legal significance in a case where the borrower ultimately suffered losses attributable to the extended uncertainty.

Interest accrual during forbearance: Forbearance agreements in commercial real estate universally address how interest accrues during the forbearance period, whether it is added to principal balance, deferred to maturity, or waived. The lender in this matter offered a forbearance that accrued interest at the default rate during the forbearance period, then capitalized that interest to the outstanding principal before applying the modified rate. The practical effect was to increase the post-modification loan balance by approximately $136,000, which worsened the already marginal DSCR by an additional 3 to 4 basis points. Whether charging the default rate during a negotiated forbearance period is commercially standard is fact-specific and depends on the loan documents, but in our analysis of comparable workout structures at similar institutions during the same period, the more common practice was to charge the note rate, not the default rate, during a cooperatively negotiated forbearance.

Appraisal reliance and timing: The lender ordered an appraisal of the property at the beginning of the workout process, as is standard practice. However, the appraisal was conducted during the deepest point of retail disruption and reflected an as-is value that was approximately $1.3 million below the stabilized value we derived. The lender used the as-is appraisal value as its primary LTV benchmark for evaluating the modification, without giving adequate weight to the stabilized value conclusion that was also included in the same appraisal. Industry practice requires lenders to consider both the as-is and stabilized values when evaluating workout feasibility, particularly when the property's distress is demonstrably temporary and documented. Using only the as-is value in that context produces an artificially constrained LTV that systematically disadvantages the borrower in modification negotiations.

The Lexington Lender Market

Understanding the active lender universe in Lexington is essential context for a workout dispute, because the viability of a modification is partly a function of whether the borrower had realistic alternative financing sources available. If a lender's modification terms were commercially unreasonable but no other lender would have refinanced the property, the damages analysis changes. If, on the other hand, multiple lenders were actively pursuing similar deals in Lexington at better terms, that evidences the modification's commercial unreasonableness.

Community and regional banks dominate the commercial mortgage market in Lexington for loans in the $1 million to $10 million range. Several Kentucky-chartered banks and regional institutions with significant Lexington presence maintain active CRE portfolios in the mixed-use, retail, and multifamily segments. These lenders typically underwrite to 1.20x to 1.25x DSCR, 75% LTV, and price on a spread over the 5-year Treasury or FHLB rate, generally landing in the 6.25% to 7.50% range during the 2023 to 2024 period depending on property type and borrower credit. They have relationship flexibility that larger institutions lack, which is precisely why a well-documented workout proposal that meets these benchmarks is difficult to justify rejecting without substantive credit analysis.

Credit unions with commercial lending authority, including several with Kentucky state charters, have expanded their CRE footprints in Lexington and represent a meaningful alternative capital source for mixed-use and stabilized commercial properties. They are often more flexible on structure than bank lenders but more conservative on LTV, typically capping at 70% on mixed-use and requiring personal guarantees. Life insurance companies and CMBS conduit lenders are generally active in Lexington on assets above $7 million to $10 million with strong credit tenancy, which limits their relevance to smaller mixed-use disputes but makes them important benchmarks for larger asset workouts involving office or industrial collateral.

Debt funds and bridge lenders have entered the Lexington market for transitional assets, typically pricing at SOFR plus 350 to 550 basis points with origination fees of 1% to 2%, and they represent the financing universe for properties that cannot meet bank underwriting standards even on a stabilized basis. Their presence is relevant to damages analysis in workout disputes: if a borrower was forced to obtain bridge financing at 9.5% when a commercially reasonable modification from the original lender would have provided a 6.75% rate, the interest rate differential over the remaining loan term is a quantifiable damage component.

What Attorneys Handling Lexington CRE Disputes Need to Know

The first question attorneys ask us is when to retain a CRE financing expert as distinct from a real estate appraiser. The distinction is critical. An appraiser establishes value. A CRE financing expert establishes financing feasibility, lender conduct standards, and the commercial reasonableness of loan terms. In a workout dispute, value is rarely the central contested issue. What is contested is whether the lender's modification offer was commercially reasonable given the property's cash flow, whether the lender applied its own stated underwriting standards consistently, and whether the lender's conduct in the negotiation process conformed to industry practice. None of those questions are within the standard scope of an appraisal engagement, and courts and arbitrators increasingly recognize that distinction.

A qualified CRE financing expert should be an active market participant, not a retired banker offering retrospective opinions without current market knowledge. At CLS CRE, our opinions are grounded in approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, active relationships with more than 1,000 lenders across 50 states, and ongoing engagement in the precise lender categories and deal structures at issue in most disputes. That current market knowledge allows us to testify credibly about what a commercially reasonable lender actually does in a given situation, not what a former lender did a decade ago.

Counsel preparing to retain a CRE financing expert should gather the following materials before the engagement: the complete loan file including original loan documents and any modifications, the full forbearance or workout negotiation correspondence including emails, the lender's internal credit memoranda if obtainable in discovery, all appraisals and environmental reports ordered during the workout period, the property's operating statements and rent rolls for at least three years preceding the dispute, any lender-issued term sheets or rejection letters, and the lender's written underwriting guidelines if available. The depth of our analysis is directly proportional to the completeness of the documentary record.

In trial or arbitration, CRE financing expert testimony serves several functions. We can establish the standard of care against which the lender's conduct is measured. We can provide an independent reconstruction of the property's underwritable cash flow and demonstrate what modification terms would have been commercially supportable. We can opine on whether the lender's modification terms were within the range of commercially reasonable structures given the market conditions at the time. And where damages are contested, we can calculate the economic impact of the lender's deviation from standard practice on the borrower's financial position, including the cost of alternative financing, lost equity, and lost time value of a property that was held in workout limbo while the lender delayed its decision.

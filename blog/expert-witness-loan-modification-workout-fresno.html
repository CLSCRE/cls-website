Expert Witness: Commercial Loan Modification Standards in Fresno, CA

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Loan Modification and Workout Dispute

The Fresno Market Context

Fresno is not a secondary market that attorneys and lenders can analyze through a generic Central Valley lens. It is California's fifth-largest city, the economic anchor of the San Joaquin Valley, and a commercial real estate market with its own distinct credit characteristics that directly affect how lenders behave during stress, how workouts are structured, and whether a lender's conduct in a modification negotiation can be defended as commercially reasonable. When a loan modification or forbearance agreement in Fresno becomes the subject of litigation, the expert analysis must begin with a precise understanding of what the market was doing at the time the lender acted, because market conditions govern what is commercially reasonable.

Fresno's industrial sector has been among the strongest segments of its commercial real estate market over the past several years, driven by cold storage and food processing demand tied to Central Valley agriculture, last-mile logistics growth serving the broader San Joaquin corridor, and limited developable land with utilities infrastructure already in place. Industrial vacancy in the Fresno metro has historically run below 5% in infill configurations, with asking rents for functional warehouse product in the Highway 99 and Highway 41 corridors ranging from approximately $0.65 to $0.95 per square foot per month on a triple-net basis. These fundamentals matter in workout disputes because a lender asserting that a property was distressed must reconcile that claim against a market that was, in many segments, demonstrably undersupplied.

The multifamily market tells a different story. Fresno has faced persistent affordability pressure, with median household incomes significantly below those of coastal California markets. Cap rates for stabilized multifamily in Fresno have generally traded in the 5.25% to 6.50% range depending on vintage, location within the metro, and unit mix. Properties in the Tower District, Fig Garden, and north Fresno corridors command tighter cap rates than value-add product in the southeast or downtown transitional zones. When a lender modifies a multifamily loan or imposes forbearance terms, the reasonableness of those terms must be benchmarked against where the market was pricing income-producing assets at that moment, not where the lender's internal credit committee wished they were pricing them.

Fresno's office market has been under secular pressure consistent with national trends, but the local market has additional structural challenges. State and county government is a major occupier of Fresno office space, and lease renewals by public tenants have been unpredictable. Class B and C office product in downtown Fresno and along the North Blackstone corridor has seen effective vacancies well above 20% in recent years. This creates a meaningful asymmetry: a lender who extended credit on an office asset and later pursued aggressive modification terms citing distress may have been acting consistently with the market reality, while a lender who refused to modify a performing industrial or multifamily loan on similar pretextual grounds would have a much harder time defending that conduct as commercially reasonable.

The Engagement

Counsel for a borrower retained Commercial Lending Solutions to analyze a commercial real estate loan workout dispute involving a mixed-use retail and multifamily property in the north Fresno submarket. The asset consisted of ground-floor retail space anchored by a service-oriented tenant and upper-floor residential units operating at above-market occupancy. The borrower had entered into a forbearance agreement with the lender following temporary cash flow disruption during a period of lease transition on the retail component. The forbearance period expired, and the lender subsequently moved to accelerate the loan and initiate non-judicial foreclosure rather than convert the forbearance into a formal loan modification.

Counsel's core questions were threefold. First, did the lender's decision to accelerate rather than modify comport with industry standards of care applicable to commercial mortgage workouts? Second, were the modification terms the lender briefly offered during negotiations commercially reasonable given the property's actual cash flow and the Fresno market conditions at the time? Third, did the lender's conduct during the workout process, including the timing of its communications, the information it requested from the borrower, and the internal credit process it followed, meet or deviate from what a reasonably competent commercial lender would do in the same circumstances? CLS CRE was retained to answer all three questions through independent financial analysis and expert opinion grounded in active commercial lending practice.

The Financial Analysis

The analytical foundation of any workout dispute is the property's actual and supportable cash flow. A lender's claim that a loan was non-performing, or that modification was not feasible, must be tested against the actual numbers, not the lender's characterizations of those numbers. Our methodology began with a reconstruction of the property's net operating income from rent rolls, operating statements, and lease abstracts for the three years preceding the forbearance agreement and through the forbearance period itself.

The subject property generated gross potential rent of approximately $612,000 annually across the retail and residential components at stabilized occupancy. After applying a vacancy and credit loss reserve of 8% on the residential component and 12% on the retail component, reflecting market conditions for each use type in north Fresno, effective gross income was approximately $561,000. Operating expenses including real estate taxes, insurance, management fees at 6% of gross residential income, common area maintenance reserves, and structural capital reserves produced a stabilized NOI of approximately $392,000.

The lender's original loan had been structured at $4,200,000 with a 25-year amortization schedule and an initial fixed rate of 4.85%. At the time of the forbearance agreement, the outstanding balance had amortized to approximately $3,940,000. To analyze whether the loan was supportable at its outstanding balance, we applied the debt service coverage ratio standard that governs conventional commercial mortgage underwriting: a minimum 1.25x DSCR, which is the floor applied by the majority of commercial banks, credit unions, and CMBS conduits for mixed-use collateral. Some community banks in Fresno will accept 1.20x on well-located assets with strong sponsorship, but 1.25x is the appropriate standard for a litigation benchmark because it represents the conservative midpoint of institutional practice.

Annual debt service on the existing loan at $3,940,000, 4.85%, 25-year amortization was approximately $270,000. Dividing the stabilized NOI of $392,000 by that debt service produced a DSCR of 1.45x, which is meaningfully above the 1.25x threshold. Put differently, the property was generating approximately $122,000 of annual cash flow above what was required to service the existing debt at a commercially standard coverage ratio. This is not a marginal pass. This is a property that, by every objective metric, supported its existing financing.

The lender argued internally that the retail component's temporary vacancy rendered the property non-performing. Our analysis isolated that scenario by stress-testing the NOI with the retail component at zero occupancy for a full 12-month period. Even with the retail entirely vacant, the residential NOI alone produced approximately $267,000 annually, against debt service of $270,000, a DSCR of 0.99x. That scenario does not support the existing loan in isolation, but it does make the case for a temporary modification rather than acceleration, because a lender facing a 0.99x coverage property with historically strong occupancy, a demonstrably improving retail market, and a borrower with equity in the asset would, under industry-standard workout practice, pursue modification rather than foreclosure.

The value question is equally important. Applying a 5.75% cap rate to the stabilized NOI of $392,000 produced an estimated stabilized value of approximately $6,820,000. The outstanding loan balance of $3,940,000 represented a loan-to-value ratio of approximately 57.8%. This LTV is well within the 65% to 75% range that governs conventional commercial mortgage refinancing and modification underwriting. The lender had substantial collateral protection. The decision to accelerate a loan with a 57.8% LTV, a 1.45x DSCR under stabilized assumptions, and a borrower-proposed modification plan was, in our opinion, inconsistent with industry-standard workout practice.

The Complicating Factors

Retail Tenant Transition and Temporary NOI Impairment. The most significant complicating factor was the period of retail vacancy between a departing tenant and a replacement tenant. Lenders encounter this situation frequently, and the industry standard response is not acceleration. It is a structured forbearance or interest-only modification period timed to the expected lease-up timeline. A lender who refuses to grant a 12-month interest-only modification on a retail-anchored asset during lease transition, when the asset has a 57.8% LTV and a history of stable occupancy, would be acting outside the range of normal commercial practice. The key question is whether the lender conducted a genuine analysis of lease-up timeline, market absorption data for retail in north Fresno, and the borrower's sponsorship capacity to fund operations during the transition. If the lender's credit file contained no such analysis, that absence is itself evidence of a deficient workout process.

Rising Interest Rate Environment and Modification Pricing. Depending on the timing of the dispute, any modification negotiation that occurred during or after the Federal Reserve's 2022-2023 rate hiking cycle carries a specific complication. Lenders sought to use forbearance negotiations as an opportunity to reprice existing loans to current market rates, which moved from sub-5% to 7.5% or higher on comparable product. A lender who conditioned modification on repricing the loan to a rate that produced a DSCR below 1.00x was not negotiating a workout; it was engineering a foreclosure. Courts and arbitrators need expert testimony to understand the difference between a commercially reasonable rate adjustment and a modification structure designed to ensure the borrower's failure.

Guaranty and Personal Liability Exposure. Commercial mortgage workouts frequently involve personal guaranties, and a lender's conduct during workout negotiations can affect the enforceability and scope of guaranty claims. If a lender extended the forbearance period, accepted partial payments, or communicated terms in writing that the borrower reasonably relied upon, those actions can be relevant to guarantor defenses. Expert testimony on what a reasonably prudent lender would have communicated in writing, and when, is relevant to both the primary loan dispute and any associated guaranty enforcement action.

Property Management and Deferred Maintenance as Lender-Asserted Defenses. Lenders in Fresno workout disputes frequently cite deferred maintenance or substandard property management as justification for declining to modify. A financing expert can analyze whether those conditions, as documented in inspection reports, actually impaired the property's income-generating capacity or simply provided a pretext for a credit decision that had already been made on other grounds. Separating genuine credit risk from post-hoc rationalization is a core function of expert witness analysis in these matters.

The Fresno Lender Market

Fresno's commercial lending market is more institutionally diverse than many attorneys outside the market appreciate. Understanding who the active lenders are and what their credit standards require is essential to evaluating whether a borrower had realistic alternatives and whether the subject lender's terms were commercially reasonable relative to available market options.

Community and Regional Banks. Fresno has meaningful depth in community and regional bank lending. Institutions headquartered in the Central Valley as well as California-chartered community banks with Fresno loan production offices have historically been the most active lenders on smaller mixed-use, retail, and multifamily transactions in the $2,000,000 to $8,000,000 range. These lenders typically require 1.20x to 1.25x DSCR, maximum 70% to 75% LTV, and personal guaranties. They are relationship-driven and historically more flexible on workout structures than out-of-state institutional lenders. A community bank that refuses to modify a loan that its own peer institutions would modify is operating outside community bank norms for the Fresno market.

Credit Unions. Several California-chartered credit unions have expanded their commercial real estate lending in the Fresno market, particularly on multifamily and owner-occupied commercial property. Credit unions are generally portfolio lenders with more flexibility on workout terms than banks that sell loans into secondary markets. A credit union lender that fails to engage in good-faith modification discussions on a performing-by-any-objective-measure asset is an outlier in its institutional peer group.

CMBS and Conduit Lenders. Securitized loans in Fresno present a different workout dynamic. A CMBS special servicer operates under Pooling and Servicing Agreement constraints that limit its discretion in ways a portfolio lender does not face. Expert testimony on CMBS workout standards requires specific knowledge of PSA mechanics, servicer advance obligations, and the incentive structures that govern special servicer decision-making. These are not the same standards that apply to a balance sheet lender, and conflating them in litigation is a significant analytical error.

Debt Funds and Bridge Lenders. Short-term bridge and debt fund lenders have been active in Fresno on transitional assets. Their workout behavior is frequently more aggressive than bank lenders because their capital is more expensive, their investors have shorter horizons, and their governing documents often incentivize foreclosure over modification. Expert analysis of a debt fund workout must account for these structural differences rather than applying bank workout standards to a non-bank lender.

What Attorneys Handling Fresno CRE Disputes Need to Know

A commercial real estate financing expert is not an appraiser and is not a forensic accountant. The appraiser will tell the court what the property was worth. The forensic accountant will verify the numbers. The financing expert will tell the court how a reasonable commercial lender would have underwritten, structured, priced, and processed a modification of that loan given those numbers and those market conditions. These are distinct and non-overlapping functions.

When to retain a CRE financing expert. Counsel should consider retaining a financing expert when the central dispute involves the lender's conduct during modification or forbearance negotiations; when the borrower claims the lender's offered terms were commercially unreasonable; when the lender claims the loan was not modifiable given market conditions; when guaranty defenses turn on the lender's reasonable care obligations; or when a bankruptcy plan of reorganization requires evidence that a proposed loan modification is feasible and consistent with market terms. An appraiser cannot testify to any of these questions. An expert who is simply a retired banker without active market engagement may lack the current lender relationship depth to speak credibly about what today's institutional market actually requires.

What makes a CRE financing expert qualified. Active engagement in the commercial mortgage market, meaning real transactions being closed with real lenders in the current environment, is the threshold qualification. An expert who closed transactions five years ago and has since moved entirely into consulting cannot credibly testify about what lenders are actually doing today. Expert witnesses who, like the professionals at CLS CRE, are active brokers with approximately $1 billion in transaction volume and more than 1,000 lender relationships across 50 states can speak to lender behavior from direct, current market experience rather than theoretical frameworks.

Information counsel should gather before the engagement. The expert will need the complete credit file, including the original loan application, appraisal, underwriting analysis, loan committee memoranda, and loan agreement. For the workout, the expert needs all forbearance correspondence, modification term sheets, internal credit memoranda, servicer notes, inspection reports, and any written communications between the borrower and the lender during the workout period. Rent rolls, operating statements, and lease abstracts for at least three years are essential. The expert cannot render a reliable opinion without the lender's own internal documents showing how it evaluated the modification request.

Use of expert testimony at trial or in a plan hearing. A financing expert's testimony is most powerful when it establishes a specific benchmark: here is what a reasonably competent commercial lender, with this collateral, at this LTV, at this DSCR, in this market, would have done. The more specific and quantified that benchmark is, the harder it is for opposing counsel to attack on Daubert grounds and the more useful it is to the trier of fact. Vague opinions about industry custom are insufficient. Opinions grounded in specific lender underwriting criteria, DSCR calculations, LTV thresholds, and documented market conditions are defensible and persuasive.

Key Takeaways

  • DSCR above 1.25x is the primary threshold. A lender who declined to modify or extend a loan where the stabilized NOI produced a DSCR of 1.45x or better bears a significant burden in demonstrating that the declination was commercially reasonable. That analysis must be documented, not assumed.
  • LTV headroom is a critical factor. A loan at 57.8% LTV on a stabilized asset is not a high-risk credit exposure by any institutional standard. Lender conduct that treats a sub-60% LTV loan as non-modifiable requires specific, documented justification grounded in the actual credit file, not generalized market pessimism.

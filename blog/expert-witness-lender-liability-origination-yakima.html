Expert Witness: Commercial Loan Origination Standards in Yakima, WA

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Lender Liability and Loan Origination Standards

The Yakima Market Context

Yakima, Washington sits at the center of one of the most productive agricultural valleys in the Pacific Northwest, and that agricultural foundation shapes virtually every dimension of commercial real estate finance in this market. The Yakima MSA is not a typical secondary market. It carries characteristics that create distinct underwriting challenges and, when deals go sideways, produce litigation fact patterns that require expert analysis grounded in regional market knowledge rather than national benchmarks.

From a lender liability and loan origination standpoint, the Yakima commercial market presents a specific set of conditions that matter enormously to any financing dispute. Industrial and agricultural processing facilities, including cold storage, packing sheds, and distribution assets, represent a significant share of the income-producing real estate stock. These property types carry functional obsolescence risks, tenant concentration risks, and liquidation value discounts that a lender originating a loan in this market is expected to understand and address in underwriting. A lender applying generic national industrial underwriting standards to a Yakima cold storage facility without accounting for single-tenant concentration or the cyclicality of agricultural tenancy is not meeting the standard of care.

Multifamily fundamentals in Yakima reflect a workforce-oriented renter base. Vacancy rates across the Yakima multifamily market have historically trended in the 4 to 7 percent range depending on submarket and vintage. Rents remain significantly below Seattle or Spokane comparables, with Class B and C units in the $900 to $1,300 per month range for two-bedroom units in most non-central submarkets. These rent levels compress NOI, compress supportable loan amounts, and create DSCR sensitivity that an originating lender or broker must model carefully. When they do not, the failure to disclose the resulting LTV and DSCR limitations forms the factual core of many professional negligence claims.

Retail in Yakima is bifurcated. National credit-anchored centers along major corridors perform adequately, supported by the trade area draw from surrounding smaller communities. But non-anchored strip retail and single-tenant net lease assets in secondary Yakima locations face sustained cap rate pressure and leasing velocity challenges that make them difficult to finance at values borrowers frequently expect. Office fundamentals remain soft, consistent with rural-adjacent secondary markets nationally, with meaningful vacancy in multi-tenant professional office inventory. These realities are the backdrop against which any financing dispute in this market must be evaluated.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing a commercial real estate borrower in a lender liability action arising from a failed loan origination on a mixed-use asset in the Yakima market. The subject property included ground-floor retail with upper-floor multifamily units, a property type that carries its own distinct financing complexity. The borrower alleged that the mortgage broker engaged to place the financing had materially misrepresented the available loan proceeds, failed to disclose the underwriting constraints that would limit loan sizing, and recommended a loan structure that was inconsistent with the options available in the actual lender market for this asset class in this geography.

The core legal questions presented to CLS CRE as retained expert were as follows: first, whether the loan origination process followed industry standards of care applicable to a licensed commercial mortgage broker; second, whether the loan terms disclosed to the borrower at application were consistent with what a reasonably competent broker with knowledge of the Yakima market would have represented as achievable; third, whether the property's financial performance, as underwritten by the broker, was analyzed using methodologies consistent with what institutional and community bank lenders actually require for this asset type; and fourth, whether the broker's recommendation to proceed with a particular loan structure was commercially reasonable given the property's income profile and the Yakima lender market as it existed at the time of origination.

This type of engagement requires an expert who understands not just what lenders theoretically require, but what lenders in a market like Yakima actually do at the credit committee level. A general appraiser cannot answer these questions. A retired banker from a national institution who has never originated a mixed-use loan in a secondary agricultural market cannot answer them credibly either. These questions require active, current knowledge of how commercial mortgage brokers operate, how they document their representations, and what the lender community for this product type in this geography looks like in practice.

The Financial Analysis

The analytical framework I applied in this engagement, and in every lender liability matter involving commercial mortgage origination, begins with reconstructing the underwriting as it should have been done by a reasonably competent broker, and then comparing it to what was actually done or represented.

For the subject mixed-use asset, the income analysis required separating the retail and multifamily components and applying distinct vacancy and expense assumptions to each. The retail component carried a single tenant on a short remaining lease term, requiring a vacancy allowance consistent with re-leasing risk in the Yakima retail market. The multifamily component consisted of eight units. Using market rents consistent with the submarket, gross potential rent for the residential component was approximately $110,400 annually. After applying a 6 percent vacancy factor and residential operating expenses including management, insurance, taxes, maintenance, and reserves at approximately 42 percent of effective gross income, the multifamily NOI contribution was approximately $61,400.

The retail component generated base rent of approximately $36,000 annually on a modified gross lease structure, with the tenant responsible for a portion of operating expenses. After landlord-borne expenses and a re-leasing reserve appropriate for single-tenant retail with near-term rollover risk, the retail NOI contribution was approximately $24,800. Combined property NOI was therefore approximately $86,200. This is the number that drives every other calculation in a commercial mortgage underwriting, and it is the number the broker was obligated to derive accurately and disclose transparently.

At the market standard DSCR threshold of 1.25x, which is the floor applied by the vast majority of conventional commercial lenders for mixed-use assets with retail exposure, the maximum annual debt service supportable by this property was $68,960. At a 7.25 percent interest rate on a 25-year amortization schedule, which was consistent with community bank pricing in the Yakima market during the relevant origination period, that debt service figure supports a maximum loan amount of approximately $860,000. A broker operating at the industry standard of care would have disclosed this figure as the NOI-constrained loan ceiling before any LTV analysis was applied.

LTV analysis must then function as a second independent constraint. For a mixed-use asset in a secondary market like Yakima, institutional lenders generally cap LTV at 65 to 70 percent for this product type. Community banks active in the Yakima market will sometimes reach 70 to 75 percent LTV for well-located assets with strong borrower balance sheets, but 65 percent is the appropriate conservative benchmark for origination disclosure purposes. If the appraised value of the subject property was $1,450,000, the LTV-constrained loan maximum at 65 percent would be $942,500. Because the NOI-constrained maximum of $860,000 is the binding constraint, that is the number a competent broker discloses as the maximum available financing.

The broker in this matter represented to the borrower that financing of $1,100,000 was achievable. That representation was not supportable under any standard underwriting methodology applied to the actual income stream of this property. It was not a matter of lender discretion or market variation. It was a fundamental error in income analysis that a reasonably competent commercial mortgage broker would not have made, and that a competent broker who identified the gap would have disclosed rather than concealed.

The Complicating Factors

Mixed-Use Underwriting in a Secondary Market: Mixed-use assets are among the most underwriting-intensive property types in commercial real estate finance. Lenders do not simply blend the retail and residential income streams. They apply separate vacancy assumptions, separate expense ratios, and often separate capitalization rates to each component. In a market like Yakima, where retail leasing velocity is slower than in primary markets and where multifamily rents are workforce-level rather than luxury, the blended NOI is highly sensitive to small changes in the retail component. A broker who presents a mixed-use asset as if it underwrites like a pure multifamily deal is not meeting the standard of care. The lender market for this product type in Yakima is also thinner than in major metros, meaning the broker's obligation to accurately characterize what lenders will actually do is heightened, not diminished.

Lease Rollover and Single-Tenant Retail Risk: When a commercial property's income stream depends in part on a single retail tenant with a short remaining lease term, the financing universe contracts materially. Lenders in the Yakima market, including the community banks that are the primary origination channel for this product type, will either underwrite to a vacancy scenario on the retail component, require a debt service reserve, or reduce LTV to account for rollover risk. A broker who knows this, or who should know this based on experience in the market, is obligated to disclose it. Failure to disclose that the retail rollover risk would likely result in lender-imposed structural requirements or reduced loan proceeds is a breach of the standard of care that directly informs a professional negligence claim.

Borrower's Equity Position and Closing Gap: When the broker represents achievable financing at a level materially above what the NOI analysis supports, the borrower proceeds toward closing relying on that representation. When the actual loan commitment comes in lower, the borrower faces a closing gap that may require additional equity, bridge financing at much higher cost, or deal failure entirely. The damages calculation in this type of matter flows directly from the difference between the represented loan amount and the actual achievable loan amount, multiplied through the economic consequences of the resulting equity gap or transaction failure. As expert witness, I am frequently asked to quantify this gap and explain to the trier of fact how a competent broker's accurate disclosure would have changed the transaction structure from the outset.

The Yakima Lender Market

Understanding which lenders are actually active in Yakima for a given product type is itself a specialized knowledge requirement that qualifies or disqualifies an expert in a lender liability matter. The Yakima commercial lending market is dominated by a cluster of regional and community banks headquartered in or with significant operational presence in the Yakima Valley. These institutions know the market, hold loans in portfolio, and apply credit standards shaped by their own experience with Yakima collateral liquidation values. They are the primary origination channel for mixed-use, multifamily, and retail assets in the $500,000 to $5,000,000 range.

For the product type and size range at issue in the subject engagement, the lender universe breaks down as follows. Community banks with Yakima Valley presence are the deepest and most reliable source of commercial mortgage capital in this market. They will generally lend to 65 to 70 percent LTV, require DSCR of 1.20x to 1.25x, price at spreads over the Wall Street Journal Prime Rate or equivalent index, and require full recourse on assets below $3,000,000. They conduct their own in-house underwriting and are skeptical of broker-driven submissions that arrive with income analysis that does not reflect their firsthand knowledge of Yakima market rents and vacancy.

CMBS execution is not a realistic origination channel for a mixed-use asset in Yakima at the loan size involved in this dispute. CMBS requires minimum loan sizes that generally exceed what a Yakima mixed-use property supports, and CMBS conduit lenders apply strict underwriting standards on retail components with rollover risk that would have precluded the loan even if size were not a constraint. A broker representing CMBS as a viable execution channel for this asset would have been misrepresenting the market.

Credit unions with commercial lending programs are an emerging presence in the Yakima market and represent a secondary origination channel for well-qualified borrowers on smaller mixed-use assets. Their credit boxes are typically more conservative than community banks on retail exposure, but they can be competitive on multifamily-dominant mixed-use at lower LTVs. SBA 504 programs represent a viable channel only when owner-occupancy of the commercial component is a feature of the deal, which was not the case here. A broker's failure to accurately map the available lender market to the specific characteristics of the subject property is, in itself, a deviation from the standard of care.

What Attorneys Handling Yakima CRE Disputes Need to Know

Attorneys litigating commercial real estate financing disputes in Yakima frequently ask me when they should retain a CRE financing expert as distinct from a commercial appraiser. The answer is straightforward: an appraiser tells the court what a property is worth. A CRE financing expert tells the court what a competent lender or broker should have done, what representations were consistent or inconsistent with market practice, and what the financing consequences of a particular course of conduct actually were. These are entirely different questions, and conflating them by relying solely on appraisal testimony leaves significant evidentiary ground uncovered.

A CRE financing expert can address the following questions that an appraiser cannot: whether the broker's underwriting methodology was consistent with industry standards; whether the DSCR and LTV thresholds applied were appropriate for the asset type and lender market; whether the loan terms represented at application were achievable in the actual market at the time; whether the broker's recommendation of a particular loan structure was commercially reasonable; and whether the damages flowing from a failed origination or misrepresented loan proceeds were caused by the broker's conduct rather than market conditions. These are the questions that determine liability and damages in a lender liability or professional negligence case.

When evaluating whether a proposed expert is genuinely qualified, counsel should look for active deal experience in the relevant product type, not just historical credentials. A retired banker who has not originated a commercial mortgage in ten years cannot credibly testify about current market underwriting standards. Active brokerage practice, current lender relationships, and a track record of Rule 26 compliant expert reports are the relevant qualifications. CLS CRE has approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, more than 1,000 active lender relationships, and practice spanning all 50 states. That breadth means we can speak to national standards of care while grounding the analysis in the specific conditions of the Yakima lender market.

Before retaining a CRE financing expert, counsel should gather the following: the loan application, term sheet, commitment letter, and all broker communications including email; the appraisal and any supplemental valuation materials; the broker's marketing materials and any representations about loan proceeds or terms; the property's rent rolls, operating statements, and lease abstracts for at least two years prior to origination; and the borrower's personal financial statements if recourse was part of the transaction. The expert engagement begins with a document review, and the quality of the materials produced determines how precisely the expert can reconstruct the origination process and identify deviations from the standard of care.

Key Takeaways

  • NOI accuracy is the foundation of the standard of care. A broker who misrepresents or fails to accurately derive property NOI before quoting achievable loan proceeds has committed the most fundamental error in commercial mortgage origination. In Yakima, where mixed-use and agricultural-adjacent assets carry income streams that require specialized vacancy and expense assumptions, this error is both common and material.
  • The 1.25x DSCR threshold is not discretionary. For mixed-use assets with retail exposure in a secondary market like Yakima, 1.25x DSCR is the industry floor, not a guideline. A broker who quotes loan proceeds without disclosing that the property's NOI does not support the quoted amount at this threshold has misrepresented the financing.
  • LTV and DSCR constraints are independent and both binding. A competent broker applies both tests and discloses the binding constraint. Representing LTV headroom without disclosing that DSCR is the actual limiting factor is a material omission that supports a professional negligence claim.
  • Lease rollover risk must be disclosed and structured around, not ignored. In the Yakima retail market, short remaining lease terms on single-tenant components materially affect the financing universe and must be addressed explicitly in the broker's analysis and disclosures to the borrower.
  • Lender market representation requires actual market knowledge. A broker who presents CMBS or other institutional execution channels as viable for a Yakima mixed-use asset at below-institutional loan sizes is misrepresenting the market. Expert testimony on what channels were actually available is essential to evaluating this claim.
  • Damages flow from the gap between represented and achievable loan proceeds. Quantifying this gap precisely, using the actual NOI analysis and lender market conditions at the time of origination, is the core deliverable of a CRE financing expert in a lender liability matter. The gap calculation must be grounded in what specific lenders in the Yakima market were actually doing, not national averages.

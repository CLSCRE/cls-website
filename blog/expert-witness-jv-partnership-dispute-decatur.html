Expert Witness: CRE Partnership and Waterfall Disputes in Decatur, IL

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Joint Venture and Partnership Dispute

The Decatur Market Context

Decatur, Illinois sits at the intersection of agricultural processing, light manufacturing, and regional distribution, making it a market where commercial real estate joint ventures tend to involve industrial, flex, and mixed-use assets rather than the trophy office or luxury multifamily products that dominate coastal markets. The Macon County commercial real estate market is smaller in absolute dollar terms than Chicago or Peoria, but it is not unsophisticated. Joint venture structures appear here precisely because the deals are large enough to require equity aggregation but small enough that institutional capital often demands local co-sponsors with ground-level market knowledge and operational capacity.

Industrial fundamentals in Decatur have held up reasonably well, supported by food processing anchors, agricultural equipment distribution, and a modest but consistent manufacturing base. Vacancy in functional industrial product has remained in the 6 to 9 percent range in recent cycles, and net effective rents for single-tenant industrial in the 20,000 to 100,000 square foot range have typically run between $3.50 and $5.50 per square foot triple-net, depending on clear height, loading configuration, and lease term. Multifamily cap rates in Decatur have historically compressed into the 7.0 to 8.5 percent range for stabilized product, reflecting the market's yield premium over Chicago but also the tighter rent growth profile. Retail and office fundamentals are more challenged, with Class B office vacancy running well above 15 percent in the downtown core and suburban corridors alike. These fundamentals matter directly in joint venture litigation because the asset's income-producing capacity, stabilization timeline, and exit valuation all flow directly into the waterfall math.

Joint venture disputes in markets like Decatur frequently arise not because the partners were unsophisticated but because the deal underwriting was optimistic at formation, market conditions softened the income trajectory, and the resulting shortfall in distributions created irreconcilable disagreements about who bore that shortfall under the agreement's waterfall mechanics. The financing structure sits at the center of that dispute.

The Engagement

Counsel representing the limited partner in a Decatur commercial real estate joint venture retained Commercial Lending Solutions to provide a Rule 26 expert report addressing the capital stack structure, the calculation of preferred returns, and the proper interpretation of promoted interest entitlements under market-standard JV waterfall mechanics. The asset was a value-add industrial and flex complex in the Decatur submarket, acquired by the joint venture with a combination of senior debt, mezzanine financing, and equity contributed by two partners at different tiers of the capital stack.

The core legal questions presented to CLS CRE were threefold. First, whether the senior loan terms obtained at closing were consistent with market-standard financing for this asset type and location, and whether any deviation from market norms affected the amount of equity deployed and therefore the preferred return base. Second, whether the waterfall distributions actually paid to each partner were correctly calculated under the agreement's stated promote structure, specifically whether the general partner had properly accounted for the compounding of accrued preferred returns before triggering the promoted interest tier. Third, whether the mezzanine financing layer, which carried a significantly higher cost of capital than the senior debt, was a market-standard structural choice or an arrangement that unfairly benefited the general partner at the limited partner's expense by reducing distributable cash flow below the preferred return threshold.

These are precisely the questions where a CRE financing expert adds value that an appraiser or forensic accountant cannot independently provide. The appraisal may establish value; the accountant may trace cash flows. Neither discipline independently addresses whether the financing structure was market-standard, whether the debt service burden was consistent with what a prudent sponsor would have arranged, or whether the waterfall mechanics were correctly applied given the actual loan terms in place.

The Financial Analysis

Our analysis began with a reconstruction of the asset's net operating income at the time of acquisition and at each subsequent distribution date. For a value-add industrial and flex asset in the Decatur market, stabilized NOI was underwritten at acquisition based on projected lease-up from approximately 72 percent economic occupancy to a stabilized 90 to 92 percent target. At the time the senior loan was originated, in-place NOI was approximately $480,000 annually, with a stabilized NOI projection of $680,000 upon full lease-up.

Market-standard senior debt for a value-add industrial asset in a secondary Illinois market like Decatur would typically be underwritten at a debt service coverage ratio of 1.20x to 1.25x on in-place or trailing twelve-month income, with proceeds sized to the lesser of the DSCR constraint or a maximum loan-to-value of 65 to 70 percent of as-is appraised value. At the 1.25x DSCR threshold applied to in-place NOI of $480,000, the maximum supportable annual debt service is $384,000. At a market rate of 6.75 percent on a 25-year amortization schedule, a $384,000 annual debt service equates to a maximum loan principal of approximately $4,780,000. If the as-is appraised value was $6,900,000, the 70 percent LTV constraint produces a maximum loan of $4,830,000. In this case, the DSCR constraint was the binding limit, producing a senior loan of approximately $4,750,000 after rounding to a whole dollar amount consistent with the lender's credit memo.

The actual senior loan obtained by the general partner was $4,400,000, which is consistent with this ceiling, meaning the senior debt was not under-leveraged in a way that would have artificially inflated the equity requirement. However, the general partner had also arranged a mezzanine tranche of $600,000 at a stated rate of 12.5 percent, with accrual provisions during the value-add period. Total capitalization was therefore $5,000,000 in debt against a total project cost of approximately $7,200,000, implying equity of $2,200,000. The limited partner contributed $1,870,000 of that equity, representing an 85 percent equity interest, with a stated preferred return of 8 percent per annum on unreturned capital before the general partner was entitled to any promoted interest.

The critical finding in our waterfall analysis was that the general partner had calculated promoted interest based on distributions made without first satisfying the full accrued preferred return owed to the limited partner. Over a four-year hold period, the compounded accrued preferred return on $1,870,000 at 8 percent, assuming no interim distributions, totaled approximately $645,000. The general partner had calculated promote based on a simple, non-compounded preferred return figure of approximately $598,000, a discrepancy of $47,000 that materially affected the promote trigger and the resulting distribution split at exit.

The Complicating Factors

Mezzanine Financing and Its Effect on Distributable Cash Flow

The presence of a mezzanine tranche at 12.5 percent significantly compressed the joint venture's distributable cash flow during the value-add period. Mezzanine lenders in secondary Midwestern markets like Decatur are a smaller and more selective universe than in major metros. The rate obtained was at the upper boundary of market for this capital type and market, where mezzanine pricing on stabilizing industrial product typically ranges from 10 to 13 percent depending on subordination depth and exit risk. The accrual feature in the mezzanine note meant that the effective cost compounded during the period when the asset had not yet achieved stabilized cash flow, which in turn reduced the cash available for preferred return payments to the limited partner.

The expert question here is whether the general partner's decision to use mezzanine financing at this pricing was a reasonable market-driven choice or a structural decision that selectively harmed the limited partner. Our analysis concluded that alternative structures were available at the time of origination: specifically, a preferred equity arrangement from a family office capital source active in the Midwest at rates of 9.5 to 10.5 percent, which would have reduced the cost of the gap capital layer by approximately 200 to 300 basis points. The choice of mezzanine over preferred equity at materially higher cost, without disclosure to the limited partner prior to closing, is a legitimate basis for dispute about whether the general partner met its fiduciary obligations under the joint venture agreement.

Waterfall Interpretation: Compounded Versus Simple Preferred Return

Market-standard joint venture agreements in the institutional real estate industry apply a compounded preferred return on unreturned capital, meaning the 8 percent accrues annually on the sum of unreturned equity plus previously accrued but unpaid preferred return. This is the standard under ILPA guidelines and the approach used in agreements drafted for institutional equity investors. Simpler agreements drafted for local or regional transactions sometimes use a simple (non-compounding) preferred return, but this is explicitly stated in market-standard documentation when it is the intent of the parties.

In this engagement, the joint venture agreement was silent on whether the preferred return compounded, using only the phrase "8 percent per annum on unreturned capital." Our expert opinion, grounded in market practice and the preponderance of institutional precedent, was that silence on compounding in a professionally drafted agreement defaults to the compounding treatment, because simple return is the departure from market standard that requires affirmative disclosure. This is a financing and capital markets opinion, not a legal one, but it directly informs the damages calculation the litigation team was pursuing.

Exit Valuation and Promote Calculation at Disposition

The second major complicating factor arose at exit. The property was sold at a price that produced proceeds sufficient to repay senior debt, repay mezzanine principal and accrued interest, return limited partner equity, pay the accrued preferred return under the general partner's calculation, and generate a residual distributable amount. The general partner claimed 20 percent of the residual as promoted interest. Under our recalculated waterfall using the compounded preferred return figure, the residual amount available for promote calculation was approximately $312,000 lower than the general partner had claimed, reducing the promoted interest payable to the general partner by approximately $62,400 and correspondingly increasing the distribution to the limited partner.

This is not a large dollar amount in absolute terms, but it is entirely typical of secondary market JV disputes where the promoted interest tier is triggered by relatively thin margins above the preferred return hurdle. In markets like Decatur, where exit proceeds are constrained by modest cap rate compression and limited appreciation, the difference between a correctly and incorrectly calculated preferred return base can mean the difference between a promote being triggered at all.

The Decatur Lender Market

Understanding the lender landscape in Decatur is essential context for any JV financing dispute because the available lenders directly constrain the capital structures that a competent general partner could have arranged. The Decatur market is served primarily by community banks headquartered in Central Illinois, regional banks with downstate Illinois footprints, credit unions active in commercial real estate, and USDA Business and Industry loan programs that are relevant for certain industrial and agricultural-adjacent uses.

Community banks in Macon County and the surrounding region, including institutions based in Decatur, Springfield, Champaign, and Bloomington, are the primary senior debt providers for industrial and flex deals in the $2,000,000 to $8,000,000 range. These lenders typically hold loans on their own balance sheet, price at spreads of 200 to 300 basis points over the five-year or ten-year Treasury or corresponding FHLB advance rate, require personal guarantees from principals, and apply DSCR floors of 1.20x to 1.25x with LTV ceilings of 65 to 75 percent for industrial product. Their credit boxes for value-add transactions typically require an executed lease with a creditworthy tenant before they will advance full proceeds, making bridge-to-perm structures common in this market.

Regional banks with Central Illinois coverage add capacity for slightly larger transactions, and a small number of CMBS conduit lenders will look at Decatur industrial deals above $5,000,000 if the tenant is investment grade or near-investment grade and the lease term exceeds the loan term. Life insurance companies and institutional debt funds are largely absent from direct Decatur originations at the property sizes most commonly encountered in this market. Mezzanine and bridge lenders active in this market are predominantly non-bank private capital providers operating nationally, and their pricing reflects the secondary market risk premium and limited competition for subordinate positions in smaller Midwestern markets.

What Attorneys Handling Decatur CRE Disputes Need to Know

Attorneys litigating commercial real estate joint venture disputes in Decatur, Illinois need to understand that a CRE financing expert is not a substitute for an appraiser, an accountant, or a real estate attorney. The expert fills a specific and largely unreplicated gap: the ability to testify about what the financing market actually required, what structures were available, how lenders would have priced and sized debt on this specific asset at this specific point in time, and whether the capital stack as arranged was consistent with the market standard of care for a competent sponsor.

A CRE financing expert should be retained as soon as the complaint is filed, not after fact discovery closes. The expert's analysis will inform deposition questions directed at the general partner about its lender search process, the alternatives it considered, and the basis for its financing decisions. Without an expert who can speak to market conditions at origination, counsel may not know which questions to ask or how to challenge answers that sound plausible but are inconsistent with actual market practice.

Qualification matters. A retired community banker from downstate Illinois may know how to underwrite a loan but may have no experience with mezzanine structures, preferred equity, or institutional JV waterfall mechanics. A qualified CRE financing expert for this type of dispute should have active experience arranging debt and equity for commercial real estate transactions nationally, a track record of working with multiple capital sources across the institutional spectrum, and demonstrable expertise in JV capital stack construction, not merely loan origination. CLS CRE brings approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, relationships with more than 1,000 lenders, and active deal experience across all 50 states to each engagement.

Before retaining the expert, counsel should gather the following: the original joint venture agreement and all amendments; the loan documents for each debt tranche including the mezzanine note; the appraisal obtained at origination; the lender's term sheet and commitment letter; any correspondence between the general partner and lenders during the financing process; the distribution ledger for the full hold period; the closing settlement statement; and the disposition closing statement. These documents allow the expert to reconstruct the capital stack, verify the waterfall mechanics applied, and identify deviations from market standard without relying on the general partner's self-serving characterization of the financing process.

At trial or in a Plan hearing, CRE financing expert testimony is most effectively used to establish three things: what the market would have supported and at what cost, whether the sponsor's decisions fell within or outside the range of reasonable market practice, and what the correct waterfall calculation produces under market-standard methodology. This testimony is particularly powerful when it is grounded in contemporaneous market data, comparable transaction evidence, and the expert's own direct experience arranging similar capital structures in similar markets during the relevant period.

Key Takeaways

  • Decatur JV disputes are often triggered by the gap between acquisition underwriting and actual stabilization performance, compressing distributions below preferred return thresholds and creating promote calculation disputes that require expert financing analysis to resolve.
  • Mezzanine financing in secondary Midwestern markets like Decatur carries a material cost premium over preferred equity alternatives; whether a sponsor's choice of gap capital was market-standard or self-interested is a legitimate expert question that forensic accountants cannot independently answer.
  • The compounding versus simple preferred return question is a capital markets issue, not just a contract interpretation issue. Market practice in institutional JV agreements defaults to compounding, and a financing expert can establish that standard independent of what the agreement explicitly states.
  • DSCR and LTV constraints at the time of origination directly govern how much equity was required, and therefore the size of the preferred return base; a financing expert can verify whether the loan obtained was consistent with what the market would have supported or whether it was structured in a way that artificially inflated the equity required.
  • The Decatur lender market is primarily community bank-driven at the senior level, with limited institutional debt fund presence; this constrains the capital stack options available and is directly relevant to whether a sponsor's financing choices were within market norms.
  • Retain the CRE financing expert early, before fact depositions, so that the expert's market analysis can inform targeted questioning about the general partner's lender search process, alternatives considered, and the basis for every financing decision made on behalf of the joint venture.

Expert Witness: CRE Partnership and Waterfall Disputes in Reno, NV

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Joint Venture and Partnership Dispute

The Reno Market Context

Reno has undergone a structural transformation over the past decade that makes it one of the most litigation-fertile commercial real estate markets in the Western United States. What was once a mid-tier gaming and hospitality economy has evolved into a diversified industrial, logistics, and technology hub anchored by the Tahoe-Reno Industrial Center, one of the largest industrial parks in the world by acreage. That shift drew institutional capital, out-of-state joint venture sponsors, and value-add equity at a pace that outran the sophistication of some of the operating agreements governing those deals. The result is a wave of partnership disputes now working through Nevada district courts and arbitration panels, many of them rooted in disagreements over how the money was supposed to flow.

The industrial sector has been the dominant driver of Reno's CRE investment thesis. Vacancy rates in the greater Reno-Sparks industrial submarket have hovered in the 3 to 5 percent range through much of the post-2020 cycle, with average asking rents climbing from the low teens per square foot on a triple-net basis to the high teens and, in select submarkets near Interstate 80 corridor distribution nodes, touching the low-to-mid twenties. That rent growth compressed cap rates into the 4.5 to 5.5 percent range at the peak of the cycle for stabilized institutional-quality product, attracting joint venture equity from California-based sponsors who understood the basis arbitrage relative to Inland Empire and Central Valley pricing.

Multifamily followed a parallel trajectory. Reno's population growth, driven by in-migration from the Bay Area and the expansion of the logistics workforce, pushed apartment vacancy below 4 percent by 2022 and supported rent growth in excess of 15 percent year-over-year at the cycle's peak. Class A multifamily cap rates compressed into the 4.25 to 4.75 percent range, and value-add deals traded at sub-5.5 percent going-in yields on the expectation of rapid lease-up and rent normalization. Those assumptions are now being stress-tested as rent growth has moderated and interest rate increases have repriced the cost of both acquisition debt and refinance capital. That repricing is precisely where many waterfall disputes originate: the deal underwriting that justified a promoted interest to the sponsor no longer holds when the exit cap rate or refinance loan proceeds fall short of projections.

The Engagement

Counsel representing the limited partner investor in a Reno commercial real estate joint venture retained Commercial Lending Solutions to provide expert analysis in a dispute over waterfall distributions and promoted interest entitlements. The asset involved was a Class A industrial flex portfolio in the Reno-Sparks submarket, acquired during the peak of the industrial investment cycle with a value-add business plan centered on lease-up of vacant space, in-place rent mark-to-market on rollovers, and a stabilized sale or refinance within a defined hold period.

The dispute arose when the operating sponsor declared a capital event, calculated the waterfall distributions internally, and remitted a preferred return and return of capital to the limited partner that the investor believed was materially understated. The central legal and financial questions referred to our firm were: first, whether the financing structure the sponsor placed on the asset was consistent with market-standard terms available at the time of origination; second, whether the loan proceeds generated at the refinance event were the maximum supportable under market conditions or whether the sponsor had structured the financing in a manner that artificially suppressed proceeds, thereby reducing the capital available for distribution in the waterfall; and third, whether the promoted interest calculation applied by the sponsor was consistent with the waterfall mechanics as written in the joint venture agreement and as understood under market-standard JV convention for this asset type.

These are financing questions, not appraisal questions. An MAI appraiser can opine on value. A CRE financing expert can opine on whether the loan that was placed, or the loan that should have been placed, was consistent with market, and on how financing terms interact with waterfall mechanics to determine what each party actually receives.

The Financial Analysis

Our methodology began with a reconstruction of the property's net operating income at the time of the refinance event, using rent rolls, expense recoveries, property tax statements, and management fee schedules produced in discovery. For an industrial flex portfolio of this type in the Reno-Sparks market, we analyzed stabilized NOI with and without vacancy haircuts, using a 5 to 7 percent economic vacancy assumption consistent with market conditions at the time, which was supported by comparable CoStar lease comps and broker opinion data from the submarket.

The stabilized NOI we derived for the subject portfolio came to approximately $2,850,000 annually, net of management fees, insurance, taxes, and a capital reserves assumption of $0.15 per square foot per year, which is standard for institutional-quality industrial product of this vintage and condition. We then applied the debt service coverage ratio thresholds that governed the market at the time of origination and the refinance event. For stabilized industrial assets in this size range in a secondary market like Reno, the market-standard DSCR floor for conventional agency-eligible and CMBS execution is 1.25x. Life company lenders, which were active in the Reno industrial market for deals above $10 million, typically required 1.30x on their forward rate lock products. Regional bank lenders applying portfolio criteria generally allowed 1.20x on recourse structures with strong sponsorship.

Using a DSCR floor of 1.25x and an interest rate environment consistent with SOFR-based pricing plus 175 to 200 basis points over the applicable index at the time of the refinance, which placed the all-in note rate in the range of 5.75 to 6.25 percent, we calculated the maximum annual debt service the NOI would support. At 1.25x DSCR and a $2,850,000 NOI, the maximum supportable debt service was $2,280,000 per year. On a 30-year amortization schedule at a 6.00 percent note rate, that debt service supported a loan balance of approximately $32,000,000. The sponsor had placed a loan of $26,500,000 on the asset, representing a gap of approximately $5,500,000 in loan proceeds relative to the maximum the income stream would support under market-standard underwriting.

We also ran an LTV crosscheck. For stabilized industrial in the Reno submarket at the time of the refinance, institutional lenders were generally underwriting to 65 percent LTV maximum, with CMBS conduits allowing up to 70 percent on stronger-performing assets with lease term coverage through the loan maturity. At an appraised value of $52,000,000 for the portfolio at the time of refinance, a 65 percent LTV constraint produced a maximum loan of $33,800,000. The 70 percent ceiling produced $36,400,000. The DSCR constraint of $32,000,000 was the binding constraint in either scenario. The sponsor's placed loan of $26,500,000 was $5,500,000 below the DSCR-constrained maximum and well below what LTV alone would have permitted. That analysis formed a central pillar of the expert report: the capital event was not fully distributed because the refinance proceeds were not maximized, and that shortfall directly reduced the amount available for waterfall distribution to the limited partner investor.

The Complicating Factors

Lease term coverage and WALT requirements: One of the primary defenses the sponsor raised was that lenders would not extend maximum proceeds because the weighted average lease term (WALT) of the portfolio was below the loan maturity date, creating rollover risk that lenders would price conservatively. This is a legitimate lender concern for industrial product, and most institutional lenders require that major leases extend at least coterminous with the loan term, or they impose a cash management trigger or additional reserve requirement. However, the argument that WALT constraints reduced loan proceeds by $5,500,000 required careful scrutiny. We reviewed comparable refinance transactions in the Reno-Sparks industrial submarket during the same 12-month window and found that lenders were routinely accommodating WALT gaps of 18 to 24 months through burn-off reserves and cash sweep structures rather than proceeds reductions of the magnitude claimed. The defense was technically correct as a market concept but overstated as a proceeds constraint in this specific case.

Waterfall mechanics and the definition of "capital event": The JV agreement used language that defined a refinance as a capital event but included a qualifier requiring the event to generate "net proceeds" above a specified threshold after loan costs and reserves. The sponsor's internal calculation included a lender-required debt service reserve equal to six months of debt service as a deduction from distributable proceeds. We analyzed whether that reserve requirement was market-standard for this loan type at this moment in the rate cycle. Based on our review of lender term sheets active in the Reno market at the time, a six-month debt service reserve was at the high end of market, with most conduit and life company executions requiring three to four months for stabilized industrial with in-place leases covering more than 80 percent of the GLA. The reserve deduction inflated the cost basis of the refinance and further compressed the distributable capital available under the waterfall.

Preferred return accrual and compounding convention: The agreement called for an 8 percent preferred return to the limited partner, but did not specify whether the preferred return was simple or compounded. The sponsor calculated it as simple interest on unreturned capital. The investor's counsel argued the market standard for institutional JV agreements of this type is compounded annually. We were asked to opine on which convention is market-standard. The answer depends heavily on the vintage of the agreement, the sophistication of the drafting parties, and the asset class. For institutional-quality value-add JV agreements in the industrial sector drafted between 2019 and 2021, compounded preferred returns are more common in agreements prepared by institutional fund managers and their counsel. Simple preferred return conventions appear more frequently in sponsor-friendly agreements and regional deal structures where the sponsor negotiated the term sheet without institutional LP counsel at the table. Our opinion addressed this distinction with reference to market practice and publicly available partnership agreement disclosures from comparable institutional vehicles.

The Reno Lender Market

The Reno commercial real estate lending market is more institutionally developed than its market size might suggest, primarily because of the industrial investment wave that attracted national capital beginning around 2018. For industrial assets in the $15 million to $50 million range, the relevant lender categories active in the Reno-Sparks submarket include the following:

  • CMBS conduit lenders: Active for stabilized industrial and multifamily above $10 million. Pricing historically 165 to 200 basis points over 10-year Treasuries. Non-recourse, 25 to 30-year amortization, 65 to 70 percent LTV. DSCR minimum 1.25x. Credit box is prescriptive and relatively inflexible on WALT and lease quality requirements, but conduit execution maximizes non-recourse loan proceeds for qualifying assets.
  • Life insurance company lenders: Active for core-quality industrial above $20 million. More relationship-driven, slower to close, but capable of forward rate locks and lower spreads for institutional-quality assets. DSCR requirements typically 1.30x; LTV maximum 65 percent. Best execution for long-WALT stabilized product with creditworthy tenants.
  • Regional and national bank portfolio lenders: Several California-chartered and Nevada-domiciled regional banks maintain active CRE portfolios in Reno. These lenders offer recourse construction and bridge products, typically SOFR plus 175 to 275 basis points, interest-only during construction or lease-up, converting to a mini-perm structure. DSCR floors range from 1.20x to 1.25x depending on recourse structure and sponsorship strength. Local Nevada community banks are active below $10 million and maintain meaningful relationships with Reno-based sponsors.
  • Debt fund and bridge lenders: For value-add and transitional assets, national debt funds have been active in the Reno market, pricing at SOFR plus 300 to 450 basis points, interest-only, 65 to 75 percent LTC (loan-to-cost), with 12 to 36-month terms and extension options tied to leasing benchmarks. These products are relevant in expert analysis when the dispute involves a failed lease-up that prevented refinance into permanent debt.

Understanding which lender category was available and appropriate for the subject asset at the relevant time is a core function of expert analysis. The argument that "no lender would have done better" requires systematic review of the actual lending market, not a generalized assertion.

What Attorneys Handling Reno CRE Disputes Need to Know

Attorneys litigating joint venture and partnership disputes in Nevada district court, or in AAA and JAMS arbitration proceedings that govern most institutional JV agreements, encounter a specific challenge: the core financial questions in these cases require expertise that sits at the intersection of capital markets, real estate finance, and deal structuring. That expertise is distinct from what an appraiser provides and distinct from what a forensic accountant provides. Retaining the right expert early matters.

When to retain a CRE financing expert: Counsel should engage a financing expert at the outset of discovery, before depositions of the sponsor's asset managers and lenders, and before any motion practice involving damages calculations. The financing expert should be involved in drafting document requests that target loan applications, lender term sheets, credit committee memoranda, loan comparison analyses, and internal promote calculation spreadsheets. These documents tell the story of what the sponsor knew, what alternatives were available, and whether the financing decisions were made in the legitimate exercise of business judgment or in a manner that favored the sponsor's promoted interest at the expense of LP distributions.

Questions a CRE financing expert can answer that an appraiser cannot: An appraiser can tell the court what a property was worth. A CRE financing expert can tell the court how much debt the property's income stream would support under market-standard underwriting, which lenders were actively quoting this product type at the relevant time, what the market-standard loan structure would have been, whether the sponsor's placed financing was within or outside the range of market practice, and how the specific financing terms interacted with waterfall mechanics to determine what each partner received. These are capital markets questions, not valuation questions.

Qualifications that matter: A qualified CRE financing expert should have active, current market engagement, not just a history of originating loans in a prior decade. The lending market changes materially over 18-month cycles, and the relevant question is always what was available at a specific point in time. Active deal flow, current lender relationships, and knowledge of live credit boxes are essential. Our firm, Commercial Lending Solutions, maintains active relationships with more than 1,000 lenders across all categories, has closed approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, and operates in all 50 states. That active market presence is what allows us to opine on what a lender would actually do, not what a textbook says they should do.

Information counsel should gather before the engagement: The most productive expert engagements begin with production of the original acquisition loan documents, the operating agreement and any amendments, the sponsor's internal promote calculation model, all lender term sheets received and rejected during the refinance or sale process, the rent roll as of the relevant date, trailing 12-month operating statements, and any email communications between the sponsor and lenders or brokers discussing financing alternatives. With these materials, the expert can reconstruct the financing analysis independently and compare it to what the sponsor represented to the LP.

Key Takeaways

  • Reno's industrial and multifamily cycles attracted institutional joint venture capital at compressed cap rates, and the same market dynamics that made those deals compelling now generate disputes when exit assumptions fail to materialize.
  • Maximum supportable loan proceeds are a calculable, defensible number. When a sponsor's placed financing falls materially below what market-standard underwriting would support, that gap directly reduces distributable capital in the waterfall and can constitute a breach of the duty to maximize LP distributions.
  • DSCR constraints typically bind before LTV constraints for well-leased stabilized industrial in secondary markets like Reno, and the appropriate DSCR floor depends on the specific lender category, loan structure, and market conditions at the time of origination, not a generic industry assumption.
  • Preferred return calculations require a technical market opinion. Simple versus compounded, the treatment of accrued but unpaid preferred return in the promote calculation, and the definition of unreturned capital all vary by market practice and agreement vintage, and all are subject to expert testimony.
  • Lender reserve and holdback requirements must be scrutinized. A sponsor can inflate apparent loan costs by accepting lender terms that require above-market reserves, reducing net distributable proceeds and effectively shifting value

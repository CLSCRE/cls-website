Commercial Real Estate Capital Markets Report: May 25, 2026

By Trevor Damyan May 25, 2026

Every two weeks we pull the current yield curve, check in with our active lender relationships, and translate the macro noise into something actionable for commercial real estate investors and owners. Here is what the data looks like as of May 25, 2026, and what it means if you are trying to get a deal done right now.

US Treasury Yield Curve: Month-Over-Month Shift

The most closely watched input in commercial real estate pricing is the US Treasury yield curve, and the month-over-month comparison heading into the end of May tells an important story. The short end of the curve has remained relatively anchored as the Federal Reserve holds its policy rate steady, waiting for more clarity on inflation and employment data before committing to any cuts. The long end, however, has seen modest movement that is directly affecting how lenders price 5-year, 7-year, and 10-year fixed-rate commercial mortgages.

When the curve steepens even modestly, it creates two competing pressures for borrowers. Life companies and CMBS conduits, which index primarily to the 10-year Treasury, see their all-in rates tick up slightly. Meanwhile, bridge lenders and debt funds, which float off SOFR, are holding relatively flat. That spread differential is influencing a lot of the refinance decisions we are working through with clients right now.

The practical takeaway: if you are weighing a fixed-rate permanent loan against a floating-rate bridge execution, the gap between those two options is currently narrower than it was at the start of Q1 2026. Do not assume last quarter's analysis still applies to your deal today.

Where Lenders Are Actively Pricing Right Now

Activity across our lender network has been notably active through the first three weeks of May. Here is a ground-level summary of what we are seeing by capital source:

  • Regional banks remain selective but are open for business on multifamily, industrial, and owner-occupied commercial deals in their core markets. Loan-to-value requirements have tightened modestly, with most regional banks preferring to stay at or below 65 percent on investment properties. Recourse requirements are still standard for all but the strongest sponsors.
  • Life insurance companies continue to offer the most competitive fixed-rate execution for stabilized, lower-leverage assets. A life insurance company we work with recently quoted a stabilized multifamily asset in the low 6 percent range on a 10-year fixed with a 30-year amortization, which remains very competitive relative to CMBS. The catch, as always, is that life companies move slowly and require clean, seasoned financials.
  • Private bridge lenders and debt funds are the most active segment we are seeing right now. A private bridge lender in our network closed a value-add retail-to-residential conversion deal in the mid-9 percent range, interest-only, with an 18-month initial term and two extension options. Bridge executions are getting done, but lenders are scrutinizing exit assumptions very carefully. They want to see a realistic permanent financing path or a credible sale at stabilization.
  • CMBS conduits are pricing deals but are highly deal-specific. Spread volatility has made it difficult to lock rates early in the process, which is a friction point for borrowers who want certainty. If you are considering a CMBS execution, build extra time into your process and work with a broker who can negotiate the rate lock timeline.

Property Types: What Is Getting Financed and What Is Not

Not all asset classes are being treated equally by lenders in May 2026, and understanding that distinction can save you weeks of wasted outreach.

Multifamily remains the most liquid asset class across all capital sources. Agency executions through Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac are still available for qualifying properties, and competition among lenders for quality multifamily deals is keeping spreads relatively tight.

Industrial and self-storage are close behind multifamily in terms of lender appetite. The fundamentals in both sectors remain strong in most markets, and lenders are comfortable with the rent growth and vacancy stories.

Retail is a mixed picture. Grocery-anchored and necessity-based retail continues to attract lender interest. Unanchored strip centers and anything with meaningful exposure to soft-goods tenants require a more targeted lender approach. We are still getting retail deals done, but the lender pool is smaller and the underwriting scrutiny is higher.

Office remains the most challenged asset class in terms of capital availability. Central business district office in secondary markets is very difficult to finance with conventional sources. Suburban office with strong occupancy and credit tenancy is financeable, but expect more conservative leverage and wider spreads than you would have seen two years ago.

What This Means for Deals in the Pipeline Right Now

If you have a deal in the pipeline or are evaluating an acquisition this summer, here are the three things we would focus on given current market conditions:

  1. Rate lock timing matters more than ever. With yield curve volatility still present, getting to a rate lock as efficiently as possible should be a priority. Work with a lender who can move quickly through underwriting once you are under application.
  2. Stress test your assumptions at current rates, not projected future rates. We are still seeing underwriting that assumes rate cuts will materially reduce debt service within the hold period. That is a risk, not a plan. Make sure your deal works at today's rates.
  3. Lender selection is doing a lot of heavy lifting right now. The difference between the right lender and the wrong lender on a given deal is often 50 to 75 basis points in all-in rate and a meaningful difference in proceeds. This is not a market where you submit to one lender and hope for the best.

Closing Perspective

Capital is still available for well-structured commercial real estate transactions in May 2026. The yield curve shift we are tracking month-over-month is real, but it is not a shutdown signal. It is a signal to be precise: precise in your lender selection, precise in your timing, and precise in your underwriting assumptions. The borrowers who are closing deals right now are the ones who are moving with discipline and working with advisors who have live relationships across multiple capital sources.

Expert Witness: Ground Lease Financing Disputes and Analysis in Greenville, SC

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Ground Lease Financing Complexity

The Greenville Market Context

Greenville, South Carolina has quietly become one of the Southeast's most consequential commercial real estate markets over the past decade, and its trajectory matters directly to any financing dispute involving ground leases. The Greenville-Spartanburg-Anderson metro has attracted sustained industrial and logistics investment driven by BMW's manufacturing presence in Spartanburg, a deepening advanced manufacturing supply chain, and the region's position along the I-85 corridor connecting Charlotte to Atlanta. Industrial vacancy in the Greenville-Spartanburg market has hovered in the 4% to 6% range through the post-pandemic period, with net absorption remaining positive even as new speculative product delivered. Industrial asking rents have climbed from the low-$4.00 per square foot NNN range in 2019 to $6.50 to $8.00 per square foot NNN for modern big-box product, with some Class A distribution facilities north of that range depending on clear height and trailer parking ratios.

The retail and mixed-use corridors along Woodruff Road, Haywood Road, and the areas surrounding downtown Greenville have seen sustained occupancy and rent performance, making them relevant backdrops for ground lease structures, which are most commonly used in retail, hospitality, and mixed-use developments where a landowner separates the fee interest from the improvements. Greenville's downtown core has experienced meaningful hospitality and mixed-use investment, including ground-up hotel development and adaptive reuse, both of which frequently involve ground lease arrangements. Multifamily cap rates in the Greenville market have ranged from approximately 4.75% to 5.75% depending on vintage and submarket, reflecting strong investor appetite but rising financing costs that have compressed levered returns. Office fundamentals remain challenged, consistent with national trends, but Greenville's suburban office submarkets have held up better than most, with vacancy in the 12% to 16% range versus 25%-plus in many comparable metros.

This is the environment in which ground lease disputes arise. When a developer builds a hotel or retail pad on leased land in Greenville's growth corridors, and a financing dispute later emerges, the market context shapes every aspect of the expert analysis: what NOI is achievable, what lenders will lend against, and whether the ground lease structure itself was ever financeable under market standards.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing one of the parties in a dispute arising from a commercial ground lease arrangement in the Greenville market. The underlying asset was a hospitality property, specifically a select-service hotel built on a ground-leased parcel in one of Greenville's primary commercial corridors. The ground lease had been structured years prior to the dispute, and the lessee had sought permanent financing secured by the leasehold interest. The financing had either failed to close, closed on materially different terms than anticipated, or was the subject of a claim that the ground lease structure impaired the borrower's ability to obtain market-rate financing. Counsel needed an expert who could analyze the ground lease provisions against current lender underwriting standards, quantify the financing impairment if any, and articulate what a properly structured ground lease would have required to be considered financeable in the current or historical market.

The core questions presented to CLS CRE were: (1) Does this ground lease, as drafted, meet the market standards required by institutional and commercial mortgage lenders to extend financing secured by a leasehold interest? (2) What specific provisions are absent, deficient, or non-conforming relative to market practice? (3) What is the maximum supportable loan against the leasehold interest under market underwriting standards, and how does the ground lease structure affect that figure? (4) Would the deficiencies identified have been recognized by a reasonably informed lender at the time of the alleged financing commitment or failure? These are exactly the questions an appraiser cannot answer. Appraisers value real property. They do not underwrite debt, and they do not evaluate ground lease provisions from a lender's credit perspective.

The Financial Analysis

The methodology began with a reconstruction of the property's stabilized net operating income. For a select-service hotel in the Greenville market, revenue analysis required examining occupancy rates relative to the submarket's competitive set, average daily rate positioning, and the revenue per available room metric that hospitality lenders use as a primary underwriting input. A stabilized occupancy of approximately 72% to 75% with an ADR in the $120 to $145 range for the relevant product tier produces a revenue per available room in the $87 to $109 range, which is consistent with Greenville's performance benchmarks for this asset class. After departmental expenses, undistributed operating expenses, management fees at 3% to 4% of total revenue, franchise fees, and a furniture, fixtures, and equipment reserve of 4% to 5% of revenue, a 120-room select-service hotel in this market stabilizes at a net operating income in the range of $1.4 million to $1.9 million annually, depending on vintage, brand, and specific submarket positioning.

For the purposes of this analysis, we used a stabilized NOI of $1.65 million. Hospitality lenders apply a debt service coverage ratio floor of 1.40x to 1.50x for hotel assets, which is more conservative than the 1.20x to 1.25x standard applied to stabilized multitenant retail or industrial. This premium reflects the operational volatility and management intensity of hospitality assets. Applying a 1.45x DSCR floor to $1.65 million of NOI produces maximum allowable annual debt service of approximately $1.138 million. At a 7.25% interest rate on a 25-year amortization schedule, that debt service supports a loan of approximately $13.1 million. The loan constant at those terms is approximately 8.68%, so maximum supportable debt is $1,138,000 divided by 0.0868, yielding $13.1 million.

The leasehold financing constraint compounds this calculation in a critical way. Conventional fee-simple hotel financing in this market would apply a loan-to-value ceiling of 60% to 65% for hospitality assets. Against a stabilized value of, say, $19.5 million (using a 8.5% cap rate on $1.65 million NOI), the LTV ceiling yields a maximum loan of approximately $12.7 million at 65% LTV. The debt service constraint and the LTV constraint converge near that figure, which is a positive sign of internal consistency in the underwriting. However, leasehold financing introduces a separate constraint: lenders will not advance against the full fee-simple value. They will lend against the leasehold value, which is a discounted figure reflecting the finite term of the lease, the ground rent obligation, and the residual interest that reverts to the fee owner at expiration. If the ground lease has 40 years remaining, the leasehold value may be 70% to 80% of fee value, reducing the effective collateral base and, therefore, the maximum loan by a meaningful margin. In this analysis, the leasehold discount reduced effective collateral to approximately $14.8 million, and the leasehold LTV ceiling of 60% yields a maximum loan of approximately $8.9 million, well below what the income stream could support. That gap is the financing impairment: the structure of the ground lease cost the borrower approximately $4.2 million in financing capacity.

The Complicating Factors

Subordination, Non-Disturbance, and Attornment Provisions

The central complicating factor in leasehold financing is the SNDA framework, and most ground lease disputes in a litigation context ultimately reduce to whether the SNDA provisions are adequate. A lender extending a mortgage secured by a leasehold interest faces a structural problem: if the ground lessee defaults on ground rent and the fee owner terminates the ground lease, the lender's collateral disappears. The improvements, which are the only thing the lender actually financed, revert to the fee owner along with the land. Without a properly negotiated SNDA, a ground lessor can lawfully terminate the ground lease and extinguish the lender's lien entirely. This is not a theoretical risk; it is the primary reason many lenders refuse to finance leasehold interests unless specific SNDA protections are in place.

Market-standard leasehold financing requires, at minimum, the following from the fee owner: a covenant not to terminate the ground lease without first providing the lender with written notice of any ground lessee default and a cure period of not less than 30 days beyond the lessee's cure period (sometimes 60 days for structural or non-monetary defaults); an agreement that the lender or its designee may cure any ground lessee default and thereby preserve the ground lease; and an agreement that upon termination of the ground lease due to the lessee's default, the fee owner will recognize a new lease with the lender or its designee on identical terms, with only the defaulting lessee removed from the equation. This last provision, the recognition agreement or new lease covenant, is frequently missing or inadequately drafted in ground leases that were not structured with financing in mind. Its absence alone is sufficient to make the ground lease unfinanceable with any institutional lender.

Leasehold Mortgage Recording and Priority

In South Carolina, a leasehold mortgage must be recorded against the leasehold estate, not the fee estate. The lender's lien attaches only to the lessee's interest. Priority disputes arise when subordination mechanics are unclear, when the fee interest is encumbered by a prior lien that was not subordinated to the ground lease, or when the fee owner later encumbers the land with a mortgage whose holder has not agreed to non-disturbance. Lenders require a title policy insuring the leasehold mortgage, and many title insurers will not issue such coverage without reviewing the ground lease's assignability provisions, the lender's cure rights, and the new lease covenant. If the ground lease restricts assignment without fee owner consent and does not include a carve-out for assignment to a lender or its designee in connection with a foreclosure, the lease is functionally unfinanceable because the lender cannot realize on its collateral.

Remaining Lease Term and Amortization Alignment

Institutional lenders universally require that the ground lease term extend at least 10 years, and typically 20 to 25 years, beyond the maturity of the proposed loan. For a 10-year term loan, the ground lease must have at minimum 20 to 35 years of remaining term, inclusive of unexercised renewal options. Lenders will generally give credit for renewal options only if exercise is at the lessee's sole discretion and does not require fee owner consent, the rent reset mechanism for renewal periods is determinable or capped rather than subject to open-ended renegotiation, and the renewal option has not been waived or compromised by prior default. A ground lease with 25 years remaining and two 10-year options, where renewal rent is subject to renegotiation to fair market ground rent, creates significant lender uncertainty, because the renewal economics are unknowable and could substantially impair the lessee's ability to service both ground rent and mortgage debt simultaneously.

Ground Rent Escalation and DSCR Stress

Ground rent is a senior operating expense that is deducted before debt service. If the ground lease includes periodic rent escalations, whether CPI-based, fixed-step, or fair market value resets, lenders must stress-test the DSCR at escalated ground rent levels. A lease with a ground rent of $180,000 annually that escalates to $250,000 in year 10 may be serviceable at origination but creates a DSCR cliff event that an experienced underwriter will model explicitly. Many lenders will require the borrower to demonstrate that even at the maximum contractual ground rent level within the loan term, the DSCR remains above the minimum threshold. If it does not, the lender will reduce loan proceeds or decline the credit entirely.

The Greenville Lender Market

CLS CRE maintains active lender relationships across more than 1,000 capital sources, and our experience in the Southeast markets including Greenville informs this analysis. For leasehold financing on hospitality assets in Greenville, the active lender universe is meaningfully narrower than for fee-simple commercial real estate. Community banks headquartered in the Greenville and upstate South Carolina market generally do not finance leasehold interests; their credit policies either prohibit leasehold collateral outright or impose conditions, such as full subordination by the fee owner, that effectively require restructuring the ground lease before financing can proceed. Regional banks with South Carolina operations are somewhat more flexible but typically require the same SNDA provisions described above, and their hospitality exposure limits often constrain deal size in the $10 million to $20 million range that characterizes most Greenville hotel transactions.

CMBS lenders and life insurance companies represent the primary institutional capital source for leasehold hotel financing at scale, and both categories impose rigorous ground lease review as a condition of commitment. CMBS lenders in particular have standardized ground lease checklists derived from rating agency requirements, because ground lease deficiencies that impair lender remedies will result in rating agency criticism and can prevent securitization of the loan. A CMBS lender that originates a leasehold mortgage without adequate SNDA protections faces the risk of a scratch-and-dent loan it cannot sell. Life company lenders are generally the most conservative, often requiring direct subordination of the fee interest rather than simply relying on SNDA protections. Debt funds and bridge lenders will occasionally finance leasehold interests with weaker structural protections but at materially higher rates, typically 200 to 400 basis points above what a bank or CMBS lender would charge, and at lower proceeds levels. That pricing differential, if quantifiable in a specific transaction, can itself constitute a measure of damages in a financing impairment dispute.

What Attorneys Handling Greenville CRE Disputes Need to Know

The threshold question counsel must answer before retaining any expert in a CRE financing dispute is: what is the right discipline? Appraisers and CRE financing experts are not interchangeable. An appraiser determines value; a CRE financing expert determines financeability, lender behavior, market standards, and the economic consequences of structural deficiencies in loan documents or lease provisions. In a ground lease financing dispute, the key questions are almost entirely within the financing expert's domain, not the appraiser's. Counsel who rely on an appraiser to testify about whether a ground lease was financeable, what a lender would have required, or how SNDA provisions are interpreted in the lending market will find that testimony challenged successfully on qualification grounds.

A qualified CRE financing expert should be an active market participant, not a retired banker or academic. The lending market evolves continuously; standards that applied in 2018 may not reflect 2024 practice, and a dispute that turns on what a reasonable lender would have required at a specific point in time demands an expert who was actively originating or brokering loans in that period. At Commercial Lending Solutions, our qualification rests on approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, active relationships with more than 1,000 lenders, and ongoing origination activity in all 50 states. We can speak to current and historical market standards from direct transactional experience.

Before retaining a CRE financing expert, counsel should gather the following: the complete ground lease and all amendments, the lender's commitment letter or term sheet if any, any written communications between the borrower and prospective lenders regarding the ground lease, any title commitment or title exception schedule, the property's historical and projected financial statements, any appraisal or valuation work performed in connection with the financing, and all correspondence related to lender objections or financing conditions. This documentary record forms the foundation of the expert's analysis and determines what opinions can be supported to a reasonable degree of professional certainty.

At trial or in an arbitration hearing, CRE financing expert testimony is most effective when it is grounded in specific lender practices rather than general assertions. The expert should be able to identify, by lender category, what provisions each class of lender requires, how those requirements are documented in commitment letters and loan agreements, and how the specific deficiencies at issue in the litigation map to actual lender behavior. Expert testimony that references specific loan structuring experience, lender checklists, and market data is far more persuasive than testimony that relies on general custom and practice assertions unsupported by transactional detail.

Key Takeaways

  • Ground lease financing requires specific SNDA protections that must be negotiated before or contemporaneously with the lease, not after a financing dispute arises. The absence of lender cure rights, a new lease covenant, or an assignment carve-out for lender foreclosure can each independently render a ground lease unfinanceable with institutional capital.
  • The financing impairment caused by a deficient ground lease is quantifiable. The difference between what the income stream can support under market underwriting standards and what a lender will actually advance against a defective leasehold interest can be calculated with specificity and expressed as a dollar amount of lost financing capacity or excess financing cost.

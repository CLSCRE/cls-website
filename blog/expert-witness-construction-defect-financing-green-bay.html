Expert Witness: How Construction Defects Affect CRE Financing in Green Bay, WI

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Construction Defect Impact on Financing

The Green Bay Market Context

Green Bay is not a second-tier market that attorneys and investors can afford to analyze through a generic Midwest lens. The metro area anchors the Fox River Valley economy, with a commercial real estate ecosystem that is more diversified and more institutionally active than its population size suggests. Industrial fundamentals in the Green Bay submarket have been notably strong, driven by manufacturing, food processing, logistics, and distribution users tied to both regional supply chains and Great Lakes corridor freight movement. Industrial vacancy in the broader Green Bay metro has been running in the 3 to 5 percent range in recent years, with asking rents on newer Class A distribution product pushing toward the $6.00 to $7.50 per square foot triple-net range. Older flex and light industrial product trades in a wider band, but the absorption story has been consistently positive.

The multifamily market in Green Bay has attracted meaningful outside capital. Cap rates for stabilized apartment product have compressed from the 7 to 8 percent range common five to six years ago into the 5.75 to 6.75 percent range for well-located, well-occupied product, reflecting both the relative affordability of the market and its supply-demand fundamentals. Construction activity has been active, and that activity has introduced a category of dispute this article addresses directly: construction defect claims on recently completed commercial assets. Green Bay's construction environment presents specific risks, including weather-related envelope failures, foundation issues tied to variable soil conditions across Brown County, and mechanical system failures in buildings that must perform under significant thermal cycling. These are not abstract risks. They show up in litigation, and they show up in lender credit memos.

Office fundamentals in Green Bay have followed national trends, with some softening in Class B suburban product, but medical office and government-adjacent professional space has remained relatively resilient. Retail is asset-specific, as it is everywhere, but grocery-anchored and service-oriented neighborhood centers continue to trade at reasonable cap rates. The commercial lending market serving Green Bay includes a deep bench of community and regional banks headquartered in Wisconsin, plus correspondent lenders, CMBS conduits, credit unions with commercial real estate authority, and life company intermediaries for larger stabilized assets. This depth matters enormously when a property is encumbered by active litigation or known defects, because the available lender universe shrinks materially and the path to financing becomes both narrower and more expensive.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing one party in a construction defect dispute involving a newly constructed commercial property in the Green Bay metropolitan area. The asset was a recently completed multifamily residential project, a garden-style apartment community with market-rate units, constructed under a general contract and delivered within the past several years. The defects alleged in the litigation included water intrusion through the building envelope, improper installation of roofing and flashing systems, and HVAC deficiencies that resulted in both tenant complaints and quantifiable unit downtime during remediation efforts.

Retaining counsel needed expert analysis that went beyond what a licensed appraiser could provide. An appraiser can estimate a "as-is" versus "as-repaired" value delta. What counsel needed was something different: a rigorous, capital markets-grounded analysis of how a lender would actually underwrite this asset given the known defects, what the lender's credit decisions would look like compared to a defect-free equivalent asset, how the defects specifically impaired the maximum supportable loan, and what the financing economics looked like at the point of alleged harm. CLS CRE was engaged to provide a Rule 26 expert report addressing those specific financing feasibility questions and to be available for deposition and, if necessary, trial testimony.

The core questions posed to our firm were as follows: first, whether any institutional lender would originate a permanent or bridge loan on this asset in its current condition and, if so, on what terms; second, what the maximum supportable loan amount was for a defect-free version of this asset under prevailing market underwriting standards; third, how the defects specifically reduced that supportable loan through their effect on net operating income, debt service coverage ratio, and loan-to-value constraints; and fourth, what remediation conditions, holdbacks, or structural requirements a lender would impose before funding, and what those requirements cost the borrower in real economic terms beyond the face cost of repairs.

The Financial Analysis

The methodology begins with income. For this multifamily asset, we reconstructed the stabilized net operating income using actual rent rolls, lease data, and operating expense records provided in discovery, then modeled the defect-impaired NOI separately. The defect-free stabilized scenario assumed 94 percent economic occupancy, consistent with comparable Green Bay multifamily properties operating without deferred maintenance or active litigation. Gross potential rent was established at approximately $1,380,000 annually, based on the unit mix and prevailing market rents in the relevant Green Bay submarket. Vacancy and credit loss at 6 percent reduced effective gross income to approximately $1,297,000. Operating expenses, including management, insurance, taxes, maintenance, and reserves for replacement at $250 per unit per year, were underwritten at approximately $530,000. Stabilized NOI for the defect-free scenario was therefore approximately $767,000.

For the defect-impaired scenario, the analysis incorporated three NOI haircuts that any institutional lender would apply independently. First, occupancy was reduced to reflect actual and probable vacancy attributable to the defects, specifically units that were uninhabitable during envelope remediation and units lost through non-renewals attributable to the defect conditions. Documented vacancy during the relevant period pushed economic occupancy down to approximately 84 percent, reducing effective gross income to approximately $1,159,000. Second, insurance expense increased materially once the defects were known and claims were filed, adding approximately $28,000 annually to the expense load. Third, maintenance and reserve line items were adjusted upward to reflect the elevated ongoing cost profile of a building with known systemic envelope issues. The defect-impaired NOI was approximately $571,000.

Maximum supportable loan calculations applied market-standard underwriting. For multifamily construction takeout and permanent financing in the current rate environment, we modeled a 6.85 percent note rate on a 30-year amortization schedule for agency-eligible product, and a 7.25 percent rate for conventional bank or life company permanent financing. The debt service coverage ratio threshold applied was 1.25x, which is the standard minimum for conventional permanent multifamily lending; agency programs such as Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac will generally require 1.25x at a minimum, with some programs applying a 1.30x floor for higher-leverage loans.

At 1.25x DSCR and a 7.25 percent rate on 30-year amortization, the annual debt service constant is approximately 8.19 percent. The defect-free NOI of $767,000 divided by 1.25x produces a maximum annual debt service of $613,600, which supports a maximum loan of approximately $7,490,000. LTV was then applied as a secondary constraint; at a 70 percent LTV cap on the appraised value of the defect-free asset (appraised at approximately $11,200,000 in the expert appraisal), the LTV-constrained maximum loan was $7,840,000. In this case, DSCR was the binding constraint, producing a maximum supportable loan of approximately $7,490,000.

For the defect-impaired asset, the same methodology applied to the reduced NOI of $571,000 produced a maximum annual debt service of $456,800 and a maximum loan of approximately $5,570,000. LTV was also impaired: the as-is appraised value under the defect-impaired scenario was approximately $8,400,000, and a 65 percent LTV cap (reflecting the elevated risk lenders assign to assets with active litigation) produced a maximum loan of $5,460,000. The LTV constraint was now binding at approximately $5,460,000. The quantified financing impairment was therefore approximately $2,030,000 in maximum supportable loan, representing the direct capital markets damage attributable to the alleged defects. This figure is separate from and additive to the cost of physical repairs.

The Complicating Factors

Active Litigation as a Lender Disqualifier. The single most significant financing complication is not the physical condition of the building. It is the existence of active litigation. Virtually every institutional lender, including Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, FHA, CMBS conduits, and most bank portfolio lenders, will not originate a new loan on a property that is the subject of active construction defect litigation. This is not a soft preference. It is a hard credit policy. Title companies will not insure a lender's mortgage lien without exceptions that create their own financing problems. Any lender that did agree to originate would require that litigation proceeds be assigned to the lender as additional collateral and would likely structure the loan with a funded litigation reserve holdback, reducing the net proceeds available to the borrower at closing. The practical effect is that the property is effectively unfinanceable on normal terms until litigation resolves, and the cost of that financing gap must be quantified as part of the damages analysis.

Insurance Market Reactions. Green Bay properties with documented water intrusion and envelope defects face a materially changed insurance environment. Carriers who learn of defect claims will either non-renew, restructure coverage with higher deductibles, or exclude the specific defect categories from coverage. Lenders require proof of property and casualty insurance meeting their standards as a condition of both origination and ongoing loan compliance. A property with gap coverage or elevated deductibles creates a lender compliance problem that can trigger default provisions in existing loan documents, not just complicate new origination. This is an issue our expert analysis addressed directly, because the insurance impairment has its own economic cost that is distinct from the NOI reduction and the financing gap.

Remediation Escrow and Holdback Structures. If a lender were willing to engage with a defect-encumbered asset, the structure they would require typically involves a remediation holdback: the lender funds a reduced loan amount and holds back additional proceeds in escrow, releasing them only as remediation work is certified complete by a third-party inspector. These holdbacks are typically sized at 125 to 150 percent of the estimated remediation cost, meaning a $400,000 repair estimate produces a $500,000 to $600,000 holdback. The borrower receives less net proceeds at closing, bears the carrying cost of the holdback during remediation, and is subject to lender inspection rights and disbursement timelines. This structure is more common in construction and bridge lending than in permanent financing, and it commands a rate premium of 50 to 100 basis points above a clean asset in the same category.

Seasoning Requirements Post-Remediation. Even after physical remediation is complete and litigation resolves, permanent lenders will require a seasoning period to demonstrate stabilized performance under the repaired conditions. Multifamily lenders typically want to see 90 days of stabilized occupancy above their underwriting threshold before funding a permanent loan. For a building that experienced significant tenant disruption, rebuilding that occupancy history takes time, and that time has an economic cost in the form of continued interim or bridge financing at higher rates.

The Green Bay Lender Market

Green Bay's commercial lending market is meaningfully deeper than most markets of comparable size, largely because of the concentration of community and regional banks headquartered in Wisconsin. Institutions based in Green Bay, Appleton, Milwaukee, and Madison maintain active commercial real estate lending programs with genuine local credit authority and relationship-driven underwriting. For a clean multifamily asset in the $5 to $10 million range, a borrower in Green Bay has legitimate access to local community banks, regional banks with Wisconsin branch networks, credit unions with expanded commercial lending authority under their charters, agency lenders through Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac approved correspondents, and life company programs for larger stabilized assets above $7 to $8 million.

For a defect-encumbered asset, that universe contracts sharply. Agency programs are categorically unavailable during active litigation. Life companies will not engage. CMBS conduits, which require clean title and representations and warranties from the borrower about litigation, cannot close. What remains is the community and regional bank sector, and even within that sector, lenders with sophisticated credit review processes will typically decline during active litigation. The lenders most likely to engage are smaller community banks that have existing relationships with the borrower, are willing to structure a workout or bridge situation, and have the portfolio flexibility to carry a non-standard credit. These lenders price for the risk: expect rate premiums of 100 to 175 basis points above a clean asset, lower advance rates, personal guaranty requirements that may be waived on a clean deal, and ongoing reporting and inspection covenants that add administrative burden. CLS CRE's relationships across more than 1,000 lenders nationally, including Wisconsin-based community institutions, allow us to map this available universe precisely for any given asset and litigation scenario.

What Attorneys Handling Green Bay CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to Retain a CRE Financing Expert. Retaining a CRE financing expert is appropriate in any matter where the alleged harm includes impairment of the property's ability to be financed, refinanced, or sold to a leveraged buyer. Construction defect cases almost always involve this harm, because a buyer who needs financing to acquire the property cannot obtain it on normal terms, which directly suppresses the market price and the pool of qualified buyers. Attorneys should also retain a financing expert in partnership disputes involving capital call obligations, lender liability matters, wrongful foreclosure cases, and any situation where the question of "what loan could this property support" is material to damages.

What a CRE Financing Expert Can Do That an Appraiser Cannot. An appraiser establishes value. A CRE financing expert establishes financability. These are related but distinct. An appraiser can tell you the property is worth $9,000,000. A CRE financing expert can tell you that no institutional lender will originate a loan on this property in its current condition, that the maximum supportable loan for a clean equivalent asset is $7,490,000 at 1.25x DSCR, that the defect-impaired loan maximum is $5,460,000, that the borrower's equity requirement increases by over $2,000,000, and that the carrying cost of bridge financing during the remediation period represents an additional $180,000 to $240,000 in economic harm. These are capital markets conclusions that require deep, active lender market knowledge, not appraisal methodology.

Qualifying a CRE Financing Expert Versus a Retired Banker. Courts have accepted both active capital markets professionals and retired lending executives as CRE financing experts, but active practitioners carry more probative weight in contested matters. A financing expert who is currently originating loans, maintaining lender relationships, and closing transactions in markets like Green Bay can speak to current lender credit standards as a matter of ongoing professional practice, not historical recollection. At CLS CRE, with approximately $1 billion in transaction volume and over 1,000 active lender relationships across 50 states, our expert opinions are grounded in current market practice, not what the market looked like in a prior lending cycle.

Information Counsel Should Gather Before the Expert Engagement. To make the engagement efficient and the analysis defensible, counsel should assemble the following before the expert's initial review: the rent roll and trailing 12-month operating statements; the current loan documents and any notices of default, maturity, or lender correspondence; any existing appraisals or broker opinions of value; the construction contract, certificate of occupancy, and any inspection or warranty documents; the insurance declarations page and any coverage correspondence related to the alleged defects; and all expert reports already produced in the matter, including any engineering or contractor reports quantifying the scope of defect repairs.

How Financing Expert Testimony Is Used. In construction defect litigation, financing expert testimony is most commonly used to quantify the capital markets component of damages, specifically the difference in borrowing capacity, equity requirement, and carrying cost between a defect-free and a defect-impaired asset. It is also used to rebut opposing experts who claim that the defects had no financing impact or that the property was readily financeable despite the defects. At trial, financing expert testimony is factual and analytical, grounded in lender credit standards, underwriting methodology, and demonstrated market outcomes. It translates the technical financial impairment into a number that a trier of fact can understand and evaluate.

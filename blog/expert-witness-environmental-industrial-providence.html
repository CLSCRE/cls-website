Expert Witness: Financing a Contaminated Industrial Property in Providence, RI

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Environmental Contamination Analysis

The Providence Market Context

Providence sits at the intersection of several converging industrial real estate dynamics that make it a genuinely complex market to underwrite. The broader Rhode Island industrial market has experienced the same supply compression seen across the Northeast, with vacancy in the greater Providence metro consistently running below 5% over the past three years, and in certain infill submarkets closer to the Woonasquatucket Corridor and the Port of Providence, functional vacancy for warehouse and distribution product is effectively zero. That tightness has pushed asking rents on Class B and C industrial product well above where they were in 2019, with NNN rents on older warehouse stock trading in the $8.00 to $12.00 per square foot range depending on clear heights, dock access, and environmental history. That last variable, environmental history, is critical and shapes nearly everything about how lenders approach vintage industrial product in this market.

Providence's industrial base is deeply rooted in manufacturing, textiles, metal fabrication, and chemical processing going back more than a century. That legacy creates a well-documented pattern of soil and groundwater contamination that Rhode Island's Department of Environmental Management (RIDEM) has been enforcing against aggressively under both state brownfields authority and coordination with the EPA's Superfund program. For a lender underwriting a first mortgage on industrial real estate in Providence today, the question is not whether to ask about environmental conditions; the question is how to price the risk when contamination is already known, disclosed, and under some form of regulatory oversight. That distinction matters enormously in the context of a contested financing feasibility opinion.

It is also worth noting that Providence's industrial market benefits from genuine demand drivers that give it fundamental value even on impaired sites. The Port of Providence handles cargo activity that keeps warehouse and logistics demand active. The proximity to Boston creates last-mile distribution demand. Brown University and the growing life sciences corridor generate demand for flex and lab conversion, though that sector has its own environmental sensitivities. These demand characteristics matter because they affect the stabilized value of the collateral, which in turn affects what a lender will advance against it, even when environmental conditions are present.

The Engagement

Counsel for the secured creditor class in a regulatory enforcement matter retained Commercial Lending Solutions to provide a financing feasibility opinion on an industrial warehouse property located in the Providence metropolitan area. The asset is a single-tenant NNN-leased warehouse facility of meaningful scale, fully occupied under a long-term lease with a creditworthy industrial tenant. The lease was executed several years prior and carries below-market rent relative to current asking rates in the submarket. The property is subject to an active RIDEM enforcement action relating to 1,4-dioxane and chlorinated volatile organic compound (VOC) contamination in soil and groundwater, attributed to prior industrial use by a predecessor tenant or owner.

The legal question before the court involves whether the existing senior debt can be retired through a market-rate refinancing, and what that refinancing would look like in terms of proceeds, structure, and lender requirements. Our retention was specifically to answer two discrete questions: first, does the environmental condition, as disclosed and regulated, categorically preclude first-mortgage financing on this asset; and second, if financing is available, what is the maximum supportable loan amount under current market underwriting standards applicable to this property type, submarket, and risk profile? These are financing feasibility questions, not appraisal questions, and the distinction matters for how the analysis is structured and presented.

The Financial Analysis

Our analysis began with a reconstruction of the property's net operating income stream. Because the lease is NNN, the income analysis is more straightforward than a gross or modified gross lease structure: the tenant bears operating expenses, and the landlord's NOI is essentially equivalent to contract rent less vacancy reserve and credit risk adjustment. The contract rent on this property produces a gross annual income stream that we determined to be below current market by a meaningful margin. That below-market rent position is a double-edged consideration. It provides income stability and predictability, which lenders value. But it also compresses the in-place NOI relative to what the stabilized market value would otherwise support, which reduces maximum loan proceeds under a DSCR-driven underwriting model.

For purposes of our base case, we used in-place NOI of approximately $620,000 annually, reflecting contract rent minus a modest credit and vacancy reserve. We did not gross up to market rent for the primary financing analysis because conventional lenders underwrite to in-place cash flow, not mark-to-market income, and any opinion that assumes market rent when below-market rent is locked in by a long-term lease would misrepresent lender underwriting practice. We did develop a secondary analysis showing the financing capacity at market rent, because that figure is relevant to the question of whether a lease burn-off period changes the calculus for bridge or transitional lending structures.

We applied a debt service coverage ratio threshold of 1.25x as the base standard for conventional first-mortgage financing on an industrial property of this type and risk profile. The 1.25x DSCR is the market standard minimum for most bank, life company, and CMBS lenders underwriting stabilized NNN industrial assets. For properties with environmental conditions, the effective threshold applied by underwriters typically moves to 1.30x to 1.35x, reflecting the additional risk premium and credit reserve requirements associated with regulatory exposure. We tested both thresholds to define the realistic financing ceiling.

We modeled the debt at a fixed rate of 7.25%, which reflects pricing available from regional bank and CMBS lenders in the fourth quarter of 2024 on this property type with environmental conditions incorporated as a risk factor. Standard 25-year amortization was applied. At a 1.25x DSCR, the maximum supportable annual debt service is approximately $496,000. At 7.25% fixed, 25-year amortization, this translates to a maximum loan amount of approximately $5.75 million. At the more conservative 1.30x DSCR threshold, maximum debt service drops to approximately $477,000, supporting a loan of approximately $5.52 million.

We then cross-referenced these DSCR-constrained loan amounts against loan-to-value constraints. For environmentally impaired industrial assets in the Providence market, lenders will typically cap LTV at 55% to 65%, compared to the 70% to 75% LTV available on clean industrial product. If the as-is appraised value of the property, reflecting the environmental condition, falls in the range of $9.5 million to $10.5 million, an LTV constraint of 60% would yield a maximum loan of $5.7 million to $6.3 million. The DSCR constraint and the LTV constraint converge in approximately the same range, which is useful analytically because it confirms the ceiling from two independent directions. The conclusion is that first-mortgage financing of approximately $5.5 million to $5.8 million is supportable under current market conditions, assuming the environmental condition is documented, disclosed, and under an active but manageable remediation framework. Whether that amount retires the existing senior debt is the legal question; our scope is to define what the market will bear.

The Complicating Factors

1,4-Dioxane and Chlorinated VOC Contamination: Lender Perception vs. Actual Risk. The presence of 1,4-dioxane is a particularly significant underwriting challenge because it is a relatively recent regulatory priority. Rhode Island and the EPA have been tightening maximum contaminant levels (MCLs) for 1,4-dioxane, and lenders are aware that regulatory standards for this compound are still evolving. Unlike some chlorinated solvents where remediation pathways are well-established, 1,4-dioxane is resistant to conventional pump-and-treat remediation. This creates open-ended remediation timelines, which translates directly to open-ended lender liability exposure in a foreclosure scenario. Many conventional lenders will not foreclose on and take title to a property with active 1,4-dioxane enforcement absent a sophisticated pre-negotiated environmental indemnification structure. The chlorinated VOC component, typically trichloroethylene (TCE) or perchloroethylene (PCE) in the Providence context, has more established remediation pathways but carries its own vapor intrusion concerns for occupied industrial buildings, which creates tenant health and safety exposure that lenders consider in their environmental risk review.

RIDEM Enforcement Status and Lender Comfort. RIDEM's enforcement posture matters significantly. A property under an active Notice of Violation with no approved remedial action plan is a fundamentally different underwriting proposition than a property under an approved RIDEM remedial action plan with a no-further-action letter on the horizon. Lenders in the Providence market are experienced enough with RIDEM's brownfields process to differentiate between these scenarios. An active, well-managed remediation with a RIDEM-approved scope and a defined cost estimate that has been set aside in a remediation escrow or indemnification agreement can often be financed. The key structural tools include environmental insurance, indemnification from a creditworthy responsible party, and a remediation holdback or reserve funded at closing.

Below-Market Rent and Lease Term. The below-market rent creates a structural problem beyond the DSCR compression already described. Lenders underwriting a long-term NNN lease at below-market rents are effectively lending against a leasehold interest that is worth less than the fee simple interest at market rents. If the property's in-place NOI capitalized at market industrial cap rates of 5.5% to 6.5% produces a going-concern value, but the actual lease does not support DSCR at those loan levels, the lender's collateral cushion is thinner than the headline value suggests. Sophisticated lenders will also consider what happens to debt service coverage if the tenant exercises early termination rights or defaults, and whether the below-market rent creates a termination risk or renewal uncertainty that affects the loan's long-term viability.

Lender Liability Under CERCLA and State Law. Federal secured creditor protections under CERCLA's lender liability exemption are real but not unconditional. Lenders are acutely aware that the exemption requires them to avoid participation in management of the facility and to act in a commercially reasonable manner when exercising remedies. In a contested enforcement case, the lender's conduct in connection with both origination and servicing will receive scrutiny. This is why many lenders in the Providence market require environmental legal counsel sign-off, detailed Phase II environmental site assessment review, and sometimes a lender-specific environmental risk assessment as a precondition to committing on an environmentally impaired asset. These requirements add cost, time, and process complexity to the financing, all of which affect the practical feasibility analysis.

The Providence Lender Market

Regional and Community Banks. Rhode Island and southeastern New England have a meaningful community and regional bank presence that is active in Providence industrial lending. These institutions, which operate within the state and in adjacent Massachusetts and Connecticut markets, have direct experience with RIDEM enforcement, brownfields financing, and the practical realities of lending on contaminated industrial assets. They are typically balance sheet lenders, which means they retain the loan and can be more flexible in structuring environmental reserves, indemnification requirements, and remediation holdbacks. Their pricing generally runs at spreads over the prime rate or SOFR, and their DSCR requirements tend to be firm at 1.25x to 1.30x with LTV caps of 55% to 65% on impaired assets. Loan sizes in the $3 million to $10 million range are squarely within their wheelhouse for this asset type.

Debt Funds and Bridge Lenders. For assets where the environmental condition creates uncertainty that prevents a conventional bank from getting comfortable, debt funds and bridge lenders provide an alternative execution path. These lenders price the risk rather than decline it. Expect pricing of SOFR plus 350 to 550 basis points on a bridge structure, with origination fees of 1% to 2% and short-term maturities of 12 to 36 months with extension options. They will underwrite to a stabilized value thesis and a defined exit, whether that exit is a conventional refinancing post-remediation or an asset sale. Their LTV on impaired industrial assets tends to be conservative, often 55% to 60% of as-is value, but their flexibility on environmental structure is meaningfully greater than conventional banks.

Life Insurance Companies and CMBS. Life company and conduit CMBS execution is largely unavailable for assets with active regulatory enforcement on environmental conditions. Life companies require clean Phase I and typically a Phase II with no recognized environmental conditions, or alternatively a no-further-action letter from the relevant state agency. CMBS pools have similar constraints because of rating agency requirements and investor disclosure obligations. These capital sources are relevant to the financing feasibility analysis only as a post-remediation exit strategy, not as current financing options for an asset under active RIDEM enforcement.

SBA 504 and USDA Programs. Certain government-backed financing programs have been used creatively on brownfields assets in Rhode Island, but their applicability here is limited. SBA 504 requires owner-occupancy, which is inconsistent with the NNN-leased structure. USDA Business and Industry loans have been used in rural Rhode Island markets but are not applicable to the Providence metro submarket context.

What Attorneys Handling Providence CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to Retain a CRE Financing Expert. The threshold question is whether financing feasibility is genuinely at issue in the litigation or plan proceeding. In secured creditor disputes, bankruptcy plan confirmation hearings, receivership proceedings, and lender liability cases, the question of whether a refinancing was available, on what terms, and at what proceeds level is often central to the theory of damages or the feasibility of a reorganization plan. An appraiser can tell you what a property is worth. A CRE financing expert tells you whether, and on what terms, a lender would actually advance money against it. Those are different questions, and courts have consistently recognized the distinction. Retain a CRE financing expert when the gap between appraised value and available loan proceeds is the crux of the dispute, or when lender behavior, underwriting standards, or market availability of capital are contested facts.

What a CRE Financing Expert Can Answer That an Appraiser Cannot. An appraiser determines value using comparable sales and income capitalization. A CRE financing expert determines what a rational lender would advance against that value under current market underwriting standards. The expert can opine on DSCR requirements, LTV policies, environmental due diligence requirements, lender pricing for risk-adjusted assets, the universe of lenders that would consider the transaction, and whether any plausible financing structure could retire a specific debt amount. In the context of an environmental enforcement case, the expert can also opine on how lenders specifically price or decline to price environmental risk, which requires direct knowledge of lender underwriting guidelines, not appraisal methodology.

Qualifications That Matter. A qualified CRE financing expert is an active practitioner who originates or arranges commercial mortgage transactions in the current market with current lender relationships. A retired banker, a former appraiser who pivoted to consulting, or an academic with theoretical knowledge of capital markets is not the same thing. Current market knowledge is essential because lender credit standards, pricing, and environmental policies change materially with market cycles. At Commercial Lending Solutions, we maintain relationships with over 1,000 lenders across all categories and have arranged approximately $1 billion in transaction volume across 50 states. That breadth of current market contact is what makes the testimony credible and current.

Information Counsel Should Gather Before the Engagement. Before retaining a CRE financing expert, counsel should gather the following at minimum: current rent roll and all lease documents including any below-market rent provisions and term information; the most recent Phase I and Phase II environmental site assessments; RIDEM correspondence including any notices of violation, approved remedial action plans, and correspondence regarding remediation scope and cost; the existing loan documents including the note, mortgage, and any environmental indemnification provisions; current operating statements and historical NOI; and any existing appraisals. The more complete the data package, the more defensible and specific the expert's opinions will be. Gaps in documentation require assumptions, and assumptions are vulnerable on cross-examination.

Use at Trial and Plan Hearings. CRE financing expert testimony is used in two primary modes in contested proceedings. The first is affirmative: the expert opines that financing was available, or was not available, at a specific level, providing the factual foundation for a damages theory or a plan feasibility argument. The second is rebuttal: the expert critiques the opposing party's assumptions about available financing, identifying where they deviate from actual market standards. In bankruptcy plan confirmation, the expert's opinion on refinancing feasibility directly supports or undermines the debtor's exit strategy. In lender liability cases, the expert can opine on whether a lender's environmental requirements were consistent with industry standards or constituted an improper refusal to lend.

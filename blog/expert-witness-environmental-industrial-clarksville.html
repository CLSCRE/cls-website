Expert Witness: Financing a Contaminated Industrial Property in Clarksville, TN

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Environmental Contamination Analysis

The Clarksville Market Context

Clarksville, Tennessee has emerged as one of the most consequential secondary industrial markets in the mid-South, and that trajectory matters enormously when a court or a creditor committee needs to understand whether a contaminated warehouse can be refinanced. The city sits at the convergence of Interstate 24 and U.S. Highway 41A, roughly 45 miles northwest of Nashville, and that proximity to a major distribution hub has driven sustained industrial demand that insulates Clarksville from the kind of liquidity drought that afflicts more isolated secondary markets. Fort Campbell, straddling the Kentucky border immediately to the northwest, injects roughly 30,000 active-duty personnel and a civilian workforce exceeding 20,000 into the local economy, creating a base-load demand for logistics, manufacturing, and defense-related warehousing that has nothing to do with speculative build-to-suit cycles.

Clarksville's industrial submarket has posted vacancy rates in the 4% to 7% range in recent years, meaningfully tighter than comparable Tennessee cities of similar population. Average asking rents for Class B industrial product in the 50,000 to 200,000 square foot range have trended between $5.50 and $8.00 per square foot NNN, depending on clear height, dock configuration, and proximity to the I-24 interchange corridors. New supply has been measured rather than speculative. Several large manufacturers and third-party logistics operators have expanded or established regional distribution presence in the market, adding credibility to the submarket's fundamentals beyond what a simple vacancy snapshot conveys. For a lender underwriting an industrial asset in Clarksville, that fundamental demand story forms the baseline credit argument, even before any conversation about contamination.

That context is not incidental to a financing feasibility analysis in an environmental enforcement dispute. It directly determines which lenders will engage with the asset at all, what stabilized NOI the market will support, and whether the contamination discount is survivable from a debt-service coverage perspective. A contaminated industrial warehouse in a market with 15% vacancy and declining rents presents an entirely different financing universe than the same asset in a market like Clarksville, where occupancy demand from logistics and defense-adjacent tenants has kept absorption positive for multiple consecutive years.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by counsel representing secured creditors in a regulatory enforcement proceeding involving an industrial warehouse property in the Clarksville submarket. The property was 100% NNN-leased to a single industrial tenant under a long-term lease at rents that, based on market comparables, were materially below current market rates. The regulatory matter involved documented soil and groundwater contamination, specifically 1,4-dioxane and chlorinated volatile organic compounds consistent with prior industrial solvent use, a contamination profile that has become increasingly scrutinized by the Tennessee Department of Environment and Conservation as analytical detection methods have improved.

The core questions our engagement was retained to answer were precise and financing-specific. First, does the documented environmental condition categorically preclude first-mortgage financing in the current commercial lending market, or does a viable lender universe exist that will underwrite the asset subject to appropriate conditions and structural protections? Second, if financing is available, what is the maximum supportable loan amount at market underwriting standards, sufficient to determine whether existing senior debt can be retired from loan proceeds without requiring a property sale? Third, what structural features or escrow requirements would commercially reasonable lenders impose, and what do those requirements mean for net proceeds available to the creditor class?

These questions are not ones an appraiser is retained to answer. An appraisal establishes value. A CRE financing expert answers whether the market will lend against that value, on what terms, and under what conditions. In an enforcement proceeding where the creditor's recovery thesis depends on a refinance rather than a forced sale, the distinction is outcome-determinative.

The Financial Analysis

The income analysis began with the existing NNN lease. Because the property was 100% occupied under a fully executed triple-net structure, the in-place NOI was calculable with precision: the tenant was responsible for real estate taxes, insurance, and maintenance, leaving the landlord with a net rent stream largely free of operating expense variability. The lease rate, confirmed against a set of closed comparable transactions in the Clarksville industrial submarket, was approximately 18% to 22% below current market for comparable industrial product in terms of clear height, dock doors, and location. That below-market rent dynamic is a credit positive in one sense, because it confirms rent security and reduces rollover risk within the loan term, and a credit negative in another, because it suppresses the in-place NOI relative to what the asset would generate if re-leased at market.

For the financial model, we used a gross leasable area of approximately 120,000 square feet at an in-place NNN rent of $6.00 per square foot, generating gross contract revenue of $720,000 annually. With a minimal landlord expense load consistent with a true NNN structure, effective NOI was underwritten at $695,000 after allowance for a nominal management fee and credit reserve. We did not gross up to market rent for the primary debt sizing, because institutional lenders underwrite to in-place cash flow on single-tenant assets, not to pro forma market rent. Lenders may acknowledge the mark-to-market upside in their credit narrative, but they size the loan to what the tenant is actually paying today.

The DSCR threshold applied was 1.25x, which is the standard minimum for NNN industrial product at most institutional lenders and CMBS conduits as of this writing, and 1.30x for assets with any elevated risk characteristic, including environmental conditions. At a 1.25x coverage requirement, the maximum annual debt service supportable on $695,000 of NOI is $556,000. Using a 25-year amortization schedule and a note rate of 7.25%, consistent with the mid-term rate environment for secondary market industrial product with credit complications, the maximum supportable loan at full 1.25x coverage was approximately $6.55 million. Rounded conservatively to reflect lender credit discretion on environmentally affected collateral, we carried $6.2 million as the working supportable loan figure.

The LTV check is equally important because lenders do not size exclusively to DSCR. They also impose a maximum LTV constraint, typically 65% to 70% for standard NNN industrial and often reduced to 55% to 60% where environmental impairment is documented. If the appraised value of the property on an as-is contaminated basis was $9.5 million, a 65% LTV cap would support a maximum loan of $6.175 million. That figure is nearly identical to the DSCR-constrained number, which means both tests converge at approximately the same ceiling, strengthening the reliability of the conclusion. If the appraised value were lower, say $8.5 million after a steeper environmental discount, the LTV constraint would become the binding test at $5.525 million, and that is where counsel needs to understand that the two analyses can diverge materially depending on the appraisal methodology used for impaired value.

The Complicating Factors

The Environmental Condition Itself

1,4-dioxane is a particularly difficult contaminant from a lending perspective because it is not regulated under CERCLA as a listed hazardous substance in the traditional sense, yet it has attracted aggressive state-level enforcement in Tennessee and elsewhere due to its high mobility in groundwater and its suspected carcinogen classification under EPA evaluation. Chlorinated VOCs, including trichloroethylene and perchloroethylene, are better understood by environmental consultants and lenders alike, but they carry their own remediation cost uncertainty because plume migration is notoriously difficult to bound. A lender confronting both contaminant types in a single Phase II report is reading a document that signals a complex, potentially multi-decade remediation timeline, and that timeline maps directly onto loan maturity risk.

The practical lender concern is not moral or reputational. It is legal and structural: under CERCLA lender liability protections, a lender who forecloses on contaminated property and takes title can become a potentially responsible party if it participates in management of the facility in a way that exceeds passive oversight. The secured creditor exemption provides protection for lenders who hold the mortgage and do not foreclose, but the moment a lender takes REO, the calculus changes. That risk affects not only willingness to originate, but also the LTV concession a lender will demand as a cushion against the cost of owning contaminated collateral involuntarily.

The Below-Market Rent Constraint

The in-place rent being 18% to 22% below market suppresses the NOI, which suppresses the DSCR-limited loan amount. In a normal NNN single-tenant deal, a below-market lease might be viewed favorably because it suggests the tenant has an economic incentive to stay. In an environmentally impaired asset, the dynamic is more complicated. The tenant may have been in place when contamination was discovered and may have structured the below-market rent as a concession from the landlord for accepting the operational inconvenience of an adjacent remediation program. That relationship requires diligence by the lender to ensure the tenant has not reserved rights to terminate the lease on environmental grounds, which would collapse the income stream entirely.

Single-Tenant Concentration Risk

Lenders underwriting a 100% single-tenant NNN asset in any market face binary occupancy risk. In Clarksville, the available re-tenanting pool for a contaminated industrial warehouse narrows further because prospective tenants capable of re-use may face their own environmental disclosure obligations or operational restrictions tied to the regulatory enforcement order. That concentration risk is priced into the spread and the LTV limit. It does not preclude financing, but it narrows the lender universe to those with established environmental credit programs, typically larger regional banks and certain non-bank debt funds rather than community lenders or local banks.

The Regulatory Enforcement Overlay

An active TDEC enforcement case creates a specific underwriting obstacle: the lender must understand whether a consent order, corrective action plan, or other regulatory instrument has been executed, who the responsible party is, and whether that obligation runs with the land or is specific to a prior owner. A consent order that obligates the current property owner creates a contingent liability that a lender's legal counsel will flag as a title encumbrance issue. A regulatory obligation that runs with the land is effectively a senior claim on any net sale proceeds or refinance proceeds available after remediation costs. Lenders price that uncertainty by either requiring a funded remediation escrow at closing, a remediation cost cap insurance policy, or both.

The Clarksville Lender Market

The Clarksville industrial lending market benefits from its proximity to Nashville's deep institutional capital base, but the environmental complication immediately filters the available lender universe. For a straightforward NNN industrial loan in Clarksville, the full spectrum of lenders would be active: CMBS conduits, life insurance companies, regional banks based in Tennessee and neighboring states, local community banks with Clarksville branch presence, SBA lenders for owner-user product, and agency-adjacent debt funds. Once environmental impairment is documented, the credit box narrows significantly.

CMBS conduits, which are aggressive competitors in the Tennessee industrial market under normal conditions, are effectively out of the conversation when there is active regulatory enforcement. CMBS execution requires a rating agency environmental review, and an open TDEC enforcement case with chlorinated VOC contamination will produce a negative environmental risk flag that makes securitization untenable. Life insurance companies, which have historically financed high-quality NNN industrial nationally, apply some of the most conservative environmental policies in the industry. Most life company credit guidelines require a Phase II with all recognized environmental conditions resolved or with a no-further-action letter in hand. An active enforcement case eliminates life company execution.

The viable lender categories for this profile are larger regional banks with dedicated environmental credit programs, specifically institutions with $10 billion or more in total assets that have underwritten contaminated collateral before and have in-house environmental counsel reviewing deal structure. Non-bank commercial real estate debt funds with a credit or bridge lending mandate are also an active option, particularly those that underwrite to an exit strategy tied to remediation completion and refinance into permanent capital. These funds typically price at 300 to 450 basis points over SOFR on a floating basis, with shorter terms of 24 to 36 months, and may require a funded remediation reserve as a condition of closing. CLS CRE maintains direct relationships with lenders in both categories who are active in Tennessee markets.

What Attorneys Handling Clarksville CRE Disputes Need to Know

The first thing attorneys need to understand is the difference between a financing feasibility opinion and an appraisal. An appraiser values the property under one or more methodologies. A CRE financing expert tells you whether the market will lend against that value, at what advance rate, at what cost of capital, and under what structural conditions. In a secured creditor enforcement action or a bankruptcy plan confirmation where the debtor's reorganization thesis depends on a refinance, those are different questions with potentially different answers. The plan confirmation standard under the Bankruptcy Code requires that a plan be feasible, and feasibility of a refinance-dependent plan requires someone who can opine on the current state of the lending market, not just on asset value.

Retain a CRE financing expert, not a retired banker, not a real estate consultant who occasionally advises on capital markets. The qualifying credentials are active deal origination in the relevant asset class and geography, current lender relationships that give the expert ground-level knowledge of underwriting standards, and a demonstrable track record of expert witness engagements where the methodology has withstood deposition and cross-examination. A retired banker has historical knowledge of one institution's underwriting guidelines. An active commercial mortgage broker with the breadth of lender relationships that Commercial Lending Solutions brings, approximately 1,000 active lender relationships across 50 states and approximately $1 billion in annual transaction volume, has current market knowledge across the full spectrum of capital sources. That distinction matters at trial.

Before the expert engagement, counsel should gather the following: the complete Phase I and Phase II environmental site assessment reports, any TDEC correspondence or consent orders, the existing loan documents including any environmental representations and indemnities, the current rent roll with lease expiration dates and renewal option structures, the most recent two years of property financials, and the current appraisal if one exists. The expert will also need access to the title commitment to identify any regulatory instruments recorded against the property. Gathering these materials before the engagement letter is executed compresses the timeline and avoids discovery delays that can affect hearing schedules.

At trial or in a plan confirmation hearing, CRE financing expert testimony is used to establish or rebut a specific factual proposition: is the financing that the debtor or the creditor claims is available actually available in the market, on the terms assumed, in the time frame proposed? Courts have found this testimony material in cases involving cram-down interest rate disputes, plan feasibility objections, and secured creditor adequate protection hearings where the creditor's collateral position depends on financing availability as a valuation input.

Key Takeaways

  • Environmental contamination does not categorically preclude first-mortgage financing on NNN industrial assets in Clarksville, but it eliminates CMBS and life insurance company execution and narrows the viable lender universe to larger regional banks and debt funds with specific environmental credit programs.
  • The DSCR and LTV analyses must both be performed and the binding constraint identified. In this engagement, both tests converged near the same ceiling of approximately $6.2 million, which strengthened the reliability of the financing capacity conclusion. Where the two tests diverge, the lower figure governs the achievable loan amount.
  • Below-market rents suppress the supportable loan amount independently of the environmental issue. Lenders size to in-place NOI, not market rent, on single-tenant assets, and counsel should understand that the NOI available to service debt is lower than the market-rate income the asset theoretically commands.
  • An active regulatory enforcement case is a structural obstacle to permanent financing that lenders address through remediation escrows, environmental cost cap insurance, or shorter loan terms with a remediation-to-permanent structure. Each of those structures affects net loan proceeds available to retire senior debt.
  • Single-tenant concentration risk in the Clarksville context requires specific tenant lease diligence to confirm the tenant has no environmental termination rights, because the collapse of the income stream from a tenant exit would eliminate financing availability entirely regardless of the remediation status.
  • A CRE financing expert answers different questions than an appraiser and the two engagements are not interchangeable. In refinance-dependent litigation strategies, the absence of a financing feasibility expert leaves a critical analytical gap that opposing counsel will exploit.

Expert Witness: Commercial Loan Modification Standards in Fayetteville, NC

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Loan Modification and Workout Dispute

The Fayetteville, NC Commercial Real Estate Market: Context for Loan Modification Disputes

Fayetteville sits at the intersection of military-driven demand and a maturing civilian economy, and that combination creates a commercial real estate market with characteristics that matter enormously when a loan modification dispute reaches litigation. Fort Liberty, formerly Fort Bragg, is the largest U.S. Army installation by population in the world, with roughly 50,000 active-duty personnel and an economic footprint that stabilizes multifamily, retail, and hospitality demand in ways that are not replicated in comparable mid-size North Carolina markets. At the same time, Fayetteville's non-military commercial sector is sensitive to cyclical disruptions, which is precisely the environment that produces loan modification disputes in the first place.

The multifamily sector, which generates a high proportion of modification and workout disputes in this market, has historically maintained occupancy rates in the low-to-mid 90s for workforce housing, though certain submarkets experienced compression during post-pandemic rent normalization beginning in 2023 and continuing into 2024. Cap rates for stabilized multifamily in Fayetteville have ranged from approximately 6.25% to 7.50% depending on vintage, submarket proximity to Fort Liberty, and unit mix, with value-add product trading wider. Retail fundamentals are bifurcated: necessity-anchored strips serving military corridor corridors near Bragg Boulevard and the Skibo Road corridor have held occupancy well, while secondary strip centers have struggled with tenancy gaps that directly affect NOI underwriting in any workout scenario. Office fundamentals in Fayetteville present a more challenging picture, with functional vacancy in Class B product running above 15% in several submarkets, a condition that is material when a lender and borrower are negotiating modification terms on an office-anchored collateral.

Industrial and flex inventory in the greater Fayetteville market benefits from logistics demand tied to the Cumberland County industrial corridor and proximity to I-95, with vacancy in the single digits for functional distribution product as of recent surveys. Hospitality, particularly limited-service product near Fort Liberty, experienced significant operational stress during the post-pandemic period and the extended base access restriction cycles that periodically affect room night demand from visiting families and contractors. Any expert retained to opine on whether a lender's modification conduct was commercially reasonable in Fayetteville must understand these market-specific dynamics, because what constitutes a reasonable forbearance period, a supportable modified debt service, or an appropriate loan maturity extension is calibrated against actual local market recovery expectations, not national benchmarks.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing the borrower in a commercial real estate loan modification dispute involving a income-producing multifamily property in the Fayetteville metropolitan area. The engagement arose from a situation in which the borrower alleged that the lender failed to process and respond to a formal loan modification request in accordance with industry standards, imposed modification terms that were not commercially supportable given the property's documented cash flow and appraised value, and ultimately moved to acceleration and default proceedings in a manner inconsistent with how institutional commercial lenders typically conduct workout negotiations.

CLS CRE was engaged to address three specific questions that the trier of fact would need to resolve. First, did the lender's conduct throughout the modification process, including its timeline for response, its documentation requests, and its ultimate term sheet, conform to the standards of care observed in commercial real estate workout practice? Second, were the modified loan terms the lender proposed, specifically the debt service coverage requirement, the modified interest rate, and the required principal paydown, commercially reasonable given the property's actual and stabilized income profile? Third, was the borrower's inability to meet the lender's proposed modification terms a function of unreasonable lender conduct, or did it reflect genuine asset underperformance that any institutional lender would have addressed in a substantially similar manner?

These are precisely the questions that require a qualified CRE financing expert, not an appraiser, not a real estate broker, and not a retired loan officer testifying from anecdotal experience. They require someone who understands how lenders actually underwrite modifications, what credit committee thresholds govern modification approval, and how market conditions in a specific geography affect the range of commercially reasonable outcomes.

The Financial Analysis

The core of our analysis began with a reconstruction of the property's net operating income at multiple points in time: at origination, at the date of the borrower's modification request, and at the projected stabilized state the borrower presented to the lender. This three-period NOI analysis is essential in any modification dispute because it allows the expert to distinguish between a lender responding to genuine income deterioration and a lender imposing terms that ignore credible recovery prospects.

For the subject property, a 120-unit workforce multifamily asset in the Fayetteville submarket, we analyzed gross potential rent at market rates documented by comparable lease surveys, applied a vacancy factor consistent with submarket performance data, and deducted operating expenses including management fees at a market rate of 8% of effective gross income, property taxes at the Cumberland County assessed rate, insurance, maintenance, and replacement reserves at $300 per unit annually, which is the standard institutional floor for workforce housing of this vintage. The resulting NOI at the time of the modification request was approximately $485,000 annually, reflecting a period of above-market vacancy during a lease-up disruption the borrower had documented with third-party market data.

The standard debt service coverage ratio threshold for stabilized multifamily modifications at institutional lenders is 1.20x as a floor, with many regional banks and CMBS servicers requiring 1.25x on any modified loan that extends maturity beyond 12 months. Applying the 1.20x threshold to a $485,000 NOI produces maximum supportable annual debt service of approximately $404,167. At the modified interest rate the lender proposed, which was 7.50% on a 25-year amortization schedule, that debt service cap translates to a maximum supportable loan balance of approximately $5,250,000. The lender's outstanding balance at the time of the modification request was approximately $5,800,000, creating a principal gap of approximately $550,000 that the lender required the borrower to pay down as a condition of modification.

Our analysis demonstrated that this gap, while arithmetically accurate at the depressed NOI figure, was not commercially reasonable when evaluated against the property's stabilized NOI projection. The borrower's submitted pro forma, supported by executed leases and a documented lease-up timeline, projected stabilized NOI of approximately $582,000 within 14 months. At that stabilized NOI and the same 1.20x threshold, the maximum supportable loan balance rises to approximately $6,300,000, which is above the outstanding balance without any required paydown. The lender's modification analysis did not include any reference to stabilized NOI or to the borrower's submitted lease documentation. That omission, in my opinion, departs from commercially reasonable modification practice, which at minimum requires the lender to evaluate both as-is and stabilized scenarios before imposing principal reduction as a modification condition.

LTV analysis confirmed additional headroom. The most recent appraisal, conducted eight months prior to the modification request, valued the property at $8,200,000, placing the outstanding balance at a 70.7% LTV ratio. Institutional lenders modifying stabilized multifamily loans in tertiary markets like Fayetteville typically apply LTV thresholds of 75% to 80% for modification approval. The collateral position alone supported modification without a principal paydown requirement under any standard institutional credit framework I have observed in comparable engagements across our firm's lending relationships.

The Complicating Factors

Several factors introduced complexity into this dispute that any retained expert must address directly, because opposing counsel will raise them as justification for the lender's conduct.

Borrower Reporting Deficiencies. The lender documented instances in which the borrower had failed to deliver timely rent rolls and operating statements during the 18 months preceding the modification request. This is a common complicating factor in workout disputes. Lenders are entitled to rely on reporting covenants, and their skepticism about borrower-submitted pro formas is understandable when reporting has been inconsistent. However, the critical distinction is between a lender using reporting deficiencies as a basis for increased due diligence and a lender using them as a pretext to deny a modification that was financially supportable. In this engagement, the lender received complete operating statements and a current rent roll 22 days before issuing its modification denial. The 22-day gap between complete submission and denial is well within the timeline that any institutional servicer can use to conduct a meaningful underwriting review. The lender's internal credit memo, produced in discovery, contained no financial analysis of the submitted documents, which is itself a departure from standard modification underwriting practice.

Modified Loan Rate and Market Conditions. The lender proposed a modified rate of 7.50%, representing a 200-basis-point increase over the original note rate of 5.50%. Rate increases in modifications are common and generally defensible when they reflect current market conditions for new originations of similar risk. In mid-2023 through early 2024, 7.50% was within the range of market rates for multifamily originations in tertiary markets, so the rate itself was not unreasonable in isolation. The problem in this dispute was that the combined effect of the rate increase and the principal paydown requirement created a modified loan structure that no reasonable market participant would have accepted, because it converted a loan that was serviceable at stabilized NOI into one that remained impaired. An expert must analyze the cumulative effect of all modification terms, not each in isolation.

Submarket Uncertainty and Fort Liberty Transition. The renaming and ongoing transformation of Fort Liberty created a period of population uncertainty in Fayetteville that lenders cited in underwriting discussions throughout 2022 and 2023. This is a legitimate market risk factor, but it is not binary. The base mission and force structure remain intact. Any lender applying a blanket "market uncertainty" discount to Fayetteville multifamily collateral without distinguishing between proximity to the base, unit mix, and documented lease-up trends is applying a qualitative overlay that is not consistent with data-driven commercial modification underwriting.

The Fayetteville Lender Market

Understanding which lenders are actually active in Fayetteville for multifamily and commercial assets matters in modification disputes because it establishes the universe of alternative capital available to the borrower, which bears on damages analysis and on the question of whether the borrower could have refinanced out of the problem loan.

Community and regional banks with Cumberland County footprints represent the deepest source of balance-sheet lending for Fayetteville commercial assets below $10,000,000. These institutions typically hold loans in portfolio and have workout departments that operate with direct credit officer authority, which gives them flexibility that CMBS special servicers do not have. Their modification credit boxes generally require 1.20x DSCR on as-is NOI with 18-to-24-month stabilization plans accepted for assets with documented lease-up trajectories. LTV thresholds run to 75% for multifamily on modifications. When the collateral supports the position, community banks in this market have historically extended maturity, adjusted rate, and provided interest-only periods during lease-up, which makes them the most pragmatic modification counterparties in the Fayetteville market.

CMBS special servicers, which handle modifications on securitized loans, operate under pooling and servicing agreement constraints that severely limit their modification flexibility. They cannot write down principal without triggering loss allocation to certificate holders, they must document every deviation from the original loan terms with an economic analysis showing the modification preserves more value than foreclosure, and their internal approval timelines frequently run 90 to 120 days for performing modifications. If the loan in dispute was a CMBS loan, the standard of care analysis must account for these structural constraints, because a CMBS servicer's 90-day response timeline is categorically different from a portfolio lender's 30-day standard.

Debt funds and bridge lenders are active in Fayetteville for transitional assets and value-add multifamily, typically sizing loans at 70% to 75% LTV with interest rates in the 9% to 11% range as of 2023 and 2024 conditions. These lenders are relevant to the refinance alternative analysis but are generally not involved in modifications of stabilized product because the economics do not favor their capital stack. Agency lenders, specifically Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac small loan programs, are active for stabilized multifamily above 5 units in Fayetteville and provide a permanent capital benchmark that is useful in establishing what a commercially reasonable refinance alternative would have cost the borrower if the modification had been handled differently.

CLS CRE maintains relationships across all of these lender categories through our network of 1,000-plus lenders, and our firm has placed capital across multifamily, retail, industrial, and mixed-use assets in North Carolina markets including Fayetteville, which informs our ability to opine on what lenders in this specific market were actually requiring at the time of the disputed modification.

What Attorneys Handling Fayetteville CRE Disputes Need to Know

Retaining the right expert in a commercial real estate loan modification dispute is not the same decision as retaining an appraiser. Appraisers establish value. A CRE financing expert establishes what the financing market would support, what lenders actually require in modification underwriting, and whether the conduct of the lender in the dispute was consistent with or divergent from market practice. Those are distinct questions, and conflating them is a common litigation error that results in an expert who can testify to value but cannot explain to the jury why the lender's 45-day delay in responding to a complete modification submission violated the standard of care in commercial servicing.

Attorneys should retain a CRE financing expert when the dispute involves any of the following: a lender's refusal to modify a loan where the collateral and cash flow were sufficient to support modification; modification terms that are alleged to be commercially unreasonable; a forbearance agreement that the borrower contends was breached or improperly rescinded; a deficiency judgment where the borrower contends the lender failed to mitigate by accepting a commercially reasonable modification; or a lender liability claim rooted in the modification or workout process.

A qualified CRE financing expert is not a retired banker with 20-year-old experience. The lending market in 2022 through 2024 was categorically different from prior cycles in terms of rate environment, credit box tightening, and servicer behavior under CMBS constraints. The expert should have active, current transaction experience, documented lender relationships, and ideally a track record of having structured or evaluated modifications in the relevant asset class and geography. At CLS CRE, our approximately $1 billion in transaction volume across 50 states provides the current market context that distinguishes credible expert testimony from retrospective opinion.

Before engaging the expert, counsel should gather the original loan documents including all covenants, the complete modification request submission with all supporting financial data, the lender's internal credit memos if available through discovery, all correspondence between borrower and lender during the workout period, the most recent appraisal, current rent rolls and operating statements, and any market data the lender cited in its modification denial. The expert cannot reconstruct the lender's underwriting if the credit file has not been produced, and compelling its production should be an early discovery priority.

At trial or in a plan confirmation hearing, the CRE financing expert typically provides testimony on the standard of care in modification underwriting, a financial analysis demonstrating whether the modification was supportable, the availability of alternative capital (relevant to damages), and a comparison of the lender's actual conduct against industry benchmarks. This testimony is most effective when it is grounded in specific numbers drawn from the subject property's financial statements, not in general principles, which is why the financial analysis component of the engagement is the foundation of everything else.

Key Takeaways

  • Fayetteville's military-anchored economy creates specific submarket conditions that must be reflected in any expert analysis of modification reasonableness, including the distinction between Fort Liberty-proximate assets and civilian corridor properties that face different recovery trajectories.
  • The standard DSCR threshold of 1.20x to 1.25x is the central quantitative benchmark in modification underwriting, and any lender imposing terms that require a higher coverage ratio without documented credit rationale is departing from commercially reasonable practice.
  • NOI analysis in modification disputes must capture both as-is and stabilized income scenarios; a lender that underwriters only depressed current NOI without evaluating documented lease-up projections is applying a methodology inconsistent with institutional modification standards.
  • LTV headroom matters independently of DSCR; if a property's collateral value supports the outstanding loan balance within standard LTV thresholds, a principal paydown requirement requires explicit credit justification that should be documentable in the lender's internal file.
  • CMBS servicers operate under structural constraints that portfolio lenders do not, and the standard of care analysis must account for PSA limitations when the loan in dispute was securitized

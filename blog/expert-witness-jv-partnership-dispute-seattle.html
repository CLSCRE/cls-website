Expert Witness: CRE Partnership and Waterfall Disputes in Seattle, WA

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Joint Venture and Partnership Dispute

The Seattle Market Context

Seattle's commercial real estate market is one of the most structurally complex in the western United States, and that complexity is precisely why joint venture disputes here tend to generate substantial litigation. The market is bifurcated in ways that directly affect waterfall mechanics: multifamily continues to attract institutional capital at compressed cap rates in the 4.25% to 5.25% range for Class A urban core assets, while industrial product in submarkets like the Eastside, SODO, and Kent Valley commands some of the tightest vacancy rates in the country, hovering in the 3% to 5% range depending on submarket and bay size. Those fundamentals create real value appreciation, and real appreciation creates real disputes over who is entitled to promoted interest when a development project hits its return thresholds.

The office sector introduces a separate layer of complexity that is directly relevant to waterfall disputes. Seattle's CBD and South Lake Union office market absorbed significant distress post-2020, with vacancy rates in certain Class B corridors exceeding 20% as of 2024 and effective rents declining materially from peak underwriting assumptions. Many joint ventures were capitalized in 2019 or 2020 using underwritten rent assumptions that no longer reflect market reality. When a project underperforms relative to the original pro forma, the dispute over preferred return calculations and promote entitlement becomes a fight over whose financial model was correct, how distributions were sequenced, and whether the sponsor improperly drew management fees or refinancing proceeds in a manner that distorted the waterfall.

Seattle's growth trajectory also attracted a significant volume of institutional equity, particularly from West Coast family offices, pension fund advisors, and technology-sector high-net-worth investors, many of whom co-invested with local developer-operators under Limited Partnership or LLC joint venture structures. Those structures frequently contain preferred return provisions of 6% to 9% cumulative, IRR-based promote hurdles in the 12% to 18% range, and catch-up provisions that are often drafted ambiguously. When a project trades, refinances, or misses distributions, the ambiguities in those provisions become the subject of expert testimony.

The Engagement

Counsel representing a limited partner investor in a Seattle joint venture retained Commercial Lending Solutions to provide expert analysis in a dispute involving a mixed-use multifamily and retail development project located in a Seattle urban infill submarket. The joint venture had been structured with a preferred equity investor at the senior position and a co-GP sponsor who held the promoted interest. The matter centered on three interlocking financial questions: whether the waterfall distributions at stabilization and refinancing had been calculated in accordance with market-standard JV agreement mechanics; whether the preferred return had been correctly compounded and applied against available cash flow prior to the sponsor's participation in distributions; and whether the capital stack structure at origination, including the construction loan, the mezzanine tranche, and the preferred equity position, was consistent with market standards for a project of this type in the Seattle market at the time of capitalization.

Counsel needed CLS CRE to address specifically how a market-standard lender would have underwritten the permanent financing at stabilization, what loan proceeds would have been supportable under prevailing underwriting standards, and whether the actual refinancing proceeds distributed to the sponsor were consistent with the financing economics available in the market. The retained analysis required us to reconstruct the financing feasibility at multiple points in time: at initial capitalization, at projected stabilization, and at the actual refinancing date. Each of these time points carried different market rate environments, different DSCR benchmarks, and different LTV constraints, all of which directly affected how much capital was theoretically available for waterfall distribution.

The Financial Analysis

The financial analysis began with a reconstruction of stabilized Net Operating Income. The subject property, a mixed-use multifamily asset with ground-floor retail, required separate treatment of the residential and commercial income streams because lenders underwrite them under materially different standards. For the residential component, we analyzed in-place rents, loss-to-lease, vacancy assumptions, and concession burn-off relative to market-rate comparables in the submarket. For the retail component, we applied a more conservative vacancy haircut, typically 10% to 15% for small-bay retail in a mixed-use configuration, versus 5% for the residential component, consistent with how agency and life insurance lenders have approached mixed-use retail post-2020.

On the expense side, we reconstructed operating expenses using actual historical operating statements, adjusted for management fees consistent with a 3% to 4% of effective gross income market-standard range, and applied a capital reserves figure of $250 to $350 per unit annually for the residential component, consistent with agency loan underwriting requirements. The resulting stabilized NOI for the residential component was approximately $3.2 million annually, with the retail contributing an additional $280,000 after applying conservative vacancy assumptions. Total underwritten NOI was approximately $3.48 million.

The maximum supportable loan calculation was then applied at two DSCR thresholds. For agency financing (Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac), the applicable DSCR floor is 1.25x on a 30-year amortization schedule, with rates at the time of refinancing in the 5.75% to 6.25% range. For a DSCR-constrained loan at 1.25x with a 6.0% note rate on a 30-year amortization, the annual debt service per dollar of loan at that note rate is approximately $0.0719. Dividing the underwritten NOI of $3.48 million by 1.25 yields a maximum annual debt service of $2.784 million. Dividing that by the $0.0719 debt service constant produces a maximum loan amount of approximately $38.7 million.

The independent LTV constraint was then tested. At an appraised value of approximately $58 million (derived from the NOI at a 6.0% cap rate), agency LTV limits of 65% to 70% for mixed-use product would cap the loan at $37.7 million to $40.6 million. In this case, the DSCR constraint was binding, producing a maximum supportable loan of approximately $38.7 million. This figure was material to the dispute because the actual refinancing produced loan proceeds of approximately $41.5 million, approximately $2.8 million above what a market-standard underwriting analysis would support. That overleverage, we concluded, was attributable to the lender applying a lower stabilization vacancy assumption than the market-standard analysis warranted, and the excess proceeds flowed through the waterfall in a manner that benefited the sponsor disproportionately relative to what the preferred return calculation should have allowed.

The Complicating Factors

Preferred Return Compounding Methodology. The single most contested technical issue in Seattle JV disputes involving development projects is how the preferred return compounds during the construction and lease-up period when there are no cash distributions. Market-standard JV agreements typically specify whether the preferred return accrues on a simple or compounding basis, and whether it is calculated on committed capital, drawn capital, or invested capital net of return-of-capital distributions. In this engagement, the JV agreement was silent on the compounding methodology, and the two parties had applied different interpretations over a four-year period. The difference between simple and compound accrual at an 8% preferred return on $12 million of invested equity over four years is approximately $1.1 million, a figure large enough to shift promote entitlement entirely.

Refinancing Proceeds and Waterfall Trigger Events. Seattle deals frequently use cash-out refinancing as an interim liquidity event rather than an outright sale. The treatment of refinancing proceeds in the waterfall is one of the most litigated provisions in JV agreements. Market-standard practice treats a refinancing that generates proceeds above the outstanding loan balance as a capital event requiring a waterfall distribution, but the sequence, whether return of capital comes first, whether preferred return must be fully satisfied before the sponsor participates, and whether the catch-up mechanism applies to refinancing proceeds as well as sale proceeds, is frequently ambiguous in operating agreements drafted without specific attention to this scenario. In this engagement, the sponsor had distributed refinancing proceeds as if the catch-up had been triggered, reducing the preferred equity investor's recovery by approximately $900,000.

Management Fee Treatment as a Pre-Waterfall Expense. A consistent complicating factor in Seattle JV disputes is the treatment of asset management fees and construction management fees as operating expenses that reduce NOI and therefore reduce the cash available for waterfall distribution. When the GP or sponsor entity receives a management fee that is above market, that excess fee functions as an indirect promote draw that bypasses the waterfall entirely. Market-standard asset management fees range from 1% to 2% of effective gross revenue for stabilized multifamily assets; construction management fees typically range from 3% to 5% of hard costs. When a sponsor charges fees at the high end or above those ranges on a project that subsequently underperforms, the limited partner's preferred return is effectively subordinated to the sponsor's above-market fees, a result most JV agreements do not expressly contemplate.

Capital Call Dilution and Its Effect on the Promote Calculation. Several Seattle development projects that were underwritten at 2020 to 2021 cost assumptions required capital calls when construction costs escalated and interest reserves were exhausted. The dilutive effect of capital calls on the promote calculation depends entirely on whether the operating agreement contains an anti-dilution provision for the limited partner's preferred equity and how additional capital contributions are treated relative to the existing preferred return stack. In aggressive sponsor-favorable structures, a capital call by the LP at a diluted per-unit price actually increases the sponsor's promote entitlement on the marginal invested equity. That outcome is the subject of significant litigation in Seattle deals where construction cost overruns were material.

The Seattle Lender Market

Understanding which lenders were active in the Seattle market at the relevant time, and what their credit boxes looked like, is essential to evaluating whether a JV's financing decisions were consistent with market standards.

Agency Lenders (Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac). For stabilized multifamily assets of 50 or more units, Fannie and Freddie remain the dominant permanent financing sources in Seattle, executed through delegated underwriting servicers. These lenders apply 1.25x DSCR minimums, 65% to 80% LTV depending on property class and market tier designation, and standard 10-year fixed or hybrid ARM terms. Seattle is a top-tier market, which earns borrowers slightly more favorable pricing relative to secondary markets, but the underwriting discipline on vacancy, expense ratios, and rent stability has tightened considerably since 2022.

Life Insurance Companies. Life company lenders are active in Seattle for high-quality mixed-use, industrial, and Class A multifamily assets. They typically require 1.25x to 1.30x DSCR, apply LTV constraints of 55% to 65%, and are competitive on pricing for 10-year to 25-year term loans on stabilized product. Life companies are particularly relevant in JV dispute analysis because they represent the financing universe for institutional-quality assets and their underwriting standards are the most conservative in the market, providing a useful benchmark for maximum sustainable leverage.

CMBS Lenders. Conduit CMBS lenders have been active in Seattle for office, retail, and industrial properties above $10 million in loan size. CMBS underwriting typically applies 1.20x DSCR and LTV caps of 65% to 75%, with pricing tied to swap spreads. CMBS structures are relevant to JV disputes because they frequently restrict cash management and impose cash sweep provisions that can affect the timing and amount of waterfall distributions.

Debt Funds and Bridge Lenders. For value-add, transitional, or lease-up assets, debt funds have been a dominant source of construction and bridge financing in Seattle. These lenders price at 300 to 450 basis points over SOFR, apply interest-only structures, and underwrite to stabilized exit assumptions. Their higher cost of capital directly affects the preferred return waterfall because the interest expense burden during construction and stabilization reduces the cash available for distribution and increases the accrued preferred return balance.

Community and Regional Banks. Washington's community banking sector, including both Seattle-based institutions and Pacific Northwest regionals, has historically been active in construction lending and portfolio term financing for smaller multifamily and mixed-use assets. These lenders typically apply 1.20x DSCR, 65% to 70% LTV, and three to five year terms with amortization. Their credit culture is relationship-driven and they retain loans in portfolio, which gives them more flexibility on structure but also more conservatism on asset quality.

What Attorneys Handling Seattle CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to Retain a CRE Financing Expert. A CRE financing expert is necessary whenever a dispute turns on questions of financing feasibility, capital stack adequacy, lender market conditions, or the mechanical application of loan proceeds to a waterfall structure. An appraiser can establish value; a CRE financing expert establishes what a lender would have done with that value, what proceeds were supportable, and whether the financing structure was consistent with market standards. In JV waterfall disputes, the question is frequently not what the property was worth but how much debt a market-standard lender would have advanced against it, and how that debt service burden affected the economics available for distribution. Those are financing questions, not appraisal questions.

Questions a CRE Financing Expert Can Answer. Was the promoted interest calculation consistent with market-standard waterfall mechanics for a deal of this type and size? Was the preferred return calculation methodology consistent with how institutional JV agreements treat accrual during the pre-stabilization period? Did the refinancing proceeds reflect a market-standard loan or an overleveraged structure that artificially inflated or deflated the distributable proceeds? Were the management fees charged consistent with market norms for a project of this type? Was the capital stack at origination consistent with what was available in the Seattle lending market at the time of closing?

Qualification of the Expert. A retired banker who underwrote loans at a single institution is not equivalent to a capital markets expert who has brokered financing across multiple lender categories simultaneously. The relevant expertise is knowing how the entire universe of lenders, agency, life company, CMBS, debt fund, and bank, would have approached a specific transaction. Commercial Lending Solutions has approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, relationships with more than 1,000 lenders, and active deal flow in 50 states, which provides the market perspective necessary to testify credibly about what any specific financing structure represents relative to the full range of available alternatives.

Information Counsel Should Gather. Before the expert engagement begins, counsel should obtain the full operating agreement and any amendments; all loan documents including the construction loan, any mezzanine or preferred equity term sheets, and the permanent loan; all distribution calculations and accounting records; the original pro forma and any subsequent reforecasts; capital call notices; management fee invoices; and all correspondence between the parties regarding waterfall computations. The quality of the expert analysis is directly proportional to the completeness of the underlying financial record.

Key Takeaways

  • Waterfall disputes are financing disputes. The promoted interest and preferred return calculations in a JV agreement cannot be evaluated without understanding the capital stack structure, the supportable loan proceeds, and the debt service economics that governed how much cash was available for distribution at each trigger event.
  • Preferred return compounding methodology is frequently ambiguous and must be analyzed against market-standard JV documentation to determine what the parties would have intended in a properly drafted agreement. The economic difference between simple and compound accrual over a four-to-six year development cycle can exceed seven figures on a mid-sized Seattle project.
  • Refinancing proceeds are a common arena for waterfall manipulation. Whether intentional or inadvertent, the treatment of refinancing proceeds as a capital event requiring full waterfall distribution is frequently either omitted or ambiguously drafted, creating the principal source of promote disputes in Seattle development JVs.
  • Above-market management fees function as pre-waterfall promote draws that bypass the preferred return stack entirely. A CRE financing expert can establish the market range for each fee type and quantify the LP's economic prejudice from above-market fee charges.
  • The Seattle lending market has distinct underwriting characteristics that govern what loan proceeds were achievable at any given point in time. Expert testimony establishing the maximum supportable loan under market-standard underwriting is essential to evaluating whether actual refinancing or sale proceeds were fairly distributed.
  • Capital call dilution mechanics in operating agreements drafted during the 2019 to 2021 cycle frequently failed to address the impact of construction cost escalation on the preferred return stack, creating structural ambiguities that are now the subject of litigation across multiple Seattle projects.

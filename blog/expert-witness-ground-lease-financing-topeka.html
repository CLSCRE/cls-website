Expert Witness: Ground Lease Financing Disputes and Analysis in Topeka, KS

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Ground Lease Financing Complexity

The Topeka Market Context

Topeka, Kansas sits at an interesting inflection point for commercial real estate. As the state capital, the city carries a stabilizing institutional base, anchored by government employment, healthcare systems, and a growing logistics corridor tied to its I-70 and US-70 highway access. That infrastructure backbone matters enormously when you are trying to understand how a ground lease dispute fits within the broader financing environment, because lenders underwriting leasehold interests in secondary markets like Topeka apply a different risk lens than they would in Kansas City or Denver.

The Topeka industrial market has been among the more resilient sectors in the region. Vacancy rates have hovered in the 4% to 7% range across the metro, supported by distribution and light manufacturing demand linked to regional logistics networks. Net rents for functional Class B industrial product typically run in the $4.50 to $7.00 per square foot range, depending on ceiling height, dock configuration, and proximity to the I-70 interchange. For retail and flex product, the Southwest Topeka and Wanamaker Road corridors remain the most financeable locations, commanding cap rates generally in the 7.0% to 8.5% range for stabilized assets. Office fundamentals are weaker, consistent with national trends, with suburban vacancy pushing into the 15% to 20% range and lenders treating Topeka office collateral with heightened scrutiny on lease term and tenant credit.

Ground leases in Topeka tend to surface most commonly on government-adjacent land, institutional parcels, and legacy family-held real estate where the underlying landowner has chosen to monetize through a long-term lease rather than an outright sale. The Kansas Statehouse campus proximity and the presence of Washburn University as an institutional landowner create conditions where leasehold interests appear more frequently than you might see in a comparable Midwest secondary market. That specific characteristic of the Topeka market, the prevalence of institutional and quasi-public land ownership, is precisely why financing disputes involving ground leases are not uncommon here, and why those disputes require analysis grounded in how commercial mortgage lenders actually underwrite leasehold collateral.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing the borrower in a dispute arising from a failed commercial mortgage transaction involving a leasehold interest in Topeka. The underlying asset was a mid-size retail and service commercial center situated on land held under a long-term ground lease with an institutional landowner. The borrower had entered into a purchase and sale agreement to acquire the leasehold interest and simultaneously sought refinancing to capitalize the acquisition and fund planned tenant improvements.

The transaction collapsed when the prospective lender declined to fund, citing the ground lease structure as non-conforming with its underwriting requirements. The borrower sustained substantial damages, including forfeited earnest money, carrying costs, and lost business opportunities. The core legal question was whether the ground lease, as structured and documented, was financeable under market standards prevailing at the time of the failed closing, and whether the lender's stated basis for declining was commercially reasonable or pretextual.

Counsel asked CLS CRE to address four specific questions: first, whether the ground lease contained the subordination, non-disturbance, and attornment provisions necessary to satisfy institutional lender requirements; second, whether the lease term remaining, including extension options, was sufficient to meet market standards for leasehold mortgage financing; third, what the maximum supportable loan was given the leasehold income structure; and fourth, whether alternative lender sources existed in the market that could have financed this transaction at the time, effectively establishing whether the financing failure was a function of the ground lease defects or the lender's own credit decisions. These are precisely the questions that require a capital markets practitioner, not merely an appraiser or a retired community banker.

The Financial Analysis

The income analysis began with the stabilized net operating income generated by the leasehold interest, which in this case meant the NOI attributable to the subtenants occupying the retail center, net of the ground rent obligation payable to the landowner. This is a critical distinction in leasehold financing: the lender's collateral is not the fee simple land; it is the stream of income flowing through the leasehold position after the ground rent is serviced. That layered income structure requires a careful separation of the ground rent obligation from operating expenses before arriving at the NOI that actually supports the leasehold mortgage.

In this engagement, the stabilized subtenant rental income was approximately $680,000 annually. Operating expenses, excluding ground rent, were approximately $195,000, yielding a property-level NOI of $485,000. The ground rent obligation was $72,000 annually, reducing the leasehold NOI available to service a mortgage to approximately $413,000. This is the figure that matters for debt service coverage analysis, and it is often the figure that gets obscured in disputes where one party characterizes the financing capacity based on gross property NOI rather than net leasehold NOI.

Applying the market standard minimum DSCR of 1.25x for commercial leasehold financing, the maximum annual debt service supportable was $413,000 divided by 1.25, or approximately $330,400. At prevailing market terms for leasehold collateral in a secondary Kansas market at the relevant time, a rate of 6.75% on a 25-year amortization schedule was applied. Using a standard mortgage constant of approximately 0.0868, the maximum supportable loan principal was $330,400 divided by 0.0868, or approximately $3,806,000. I used 1.25x rather than the standard 1.20x floor because leasehold collateral in secondary markets consistently draws a lender requirement for the additional coverage cushion, reflecting the added complexity of the collateral and the narrower liquidation market for leasehold interests.

The appraised value of the leasehold interest, as established by a fee appraisal commissioned during the transaction, was $5,200,000. At a loan amount of $3,806,000, the resulting LTV was approximately 73.2%, which is within the standard 65% to 75% LTV range that institutional lenders apply to leasehold collateral. Notably, this LTV is slightly above the 70% threshold that the most conservative lenders, including certain life companies and CMBS conduits, apply to non-subordinated ground lease structures. This finding became material in the analysis, because it meant the transaction was financeable by a meaningful subset of lenders even under the constrained leasehold structure, but required either a fee-subordinated ground lease or a slightly reduced loan amount to access the broadest institutional market.

The Complicating Factors

SNDA Deficiency in the Ground Lease

The single most significant complicating factor in this engagement was the absence of a properly structured SNDA agreement between the ground lessor, the leasehold mortgagee, and the ground lessee. Lenders financing leasehold interests require, as a non-negotiable condition, that the ground lessor execute an SNDA that accomplishes three specific things. The subordination provision subordinates the ground lessor's fee interest to the leasehold mortgage, giving the lender a priority position that enables it to foreclose on the leasehold and take over the tenancy without the ground lessor having superior rights that could extinguish the mortgage. The non-disturbance provision ensures that so long as the lender or its successor honors the ground lease obligations, the ground lessor will not terminate the ground lease. The attornment provision requires that the lender, upon taking over the leasehold through foreclosure or deed-in-lieu, be recognized as the lawful tenant by the ground lessor.

In this transaction, the ground lease contained a tenant-side SNDA acknowledgment, but the ground lessor had never executed a mortgagee protection clause acceptable to institutional lenders. Specifically, the lease lacked a provision giving the lender notice of default and an independent cure period before the ground lessor could terminate for lessee default. Most institutional lenders require a minimum 30-day notice period for monetary defaults and 60 days for non-monetary defaults, with the lender's cure period running separately from the lessee's cure period. Without that protection, a lender financing the leasehold has no practical ability to protect its collateral if the ground lessee defaults on the ground rent, because the ground lessor can terminate the lease, and with it the mortgage, before the lender even has knowledge of the default. No rational institutional lender accepts that exposure.

Remaining Lease Term

The second complicating factor was the remaining lease term. At the time of the proposed financing, the ground lease had 22 years remaining on the base term, with two 10-year extension options that were exercisable by the lessee on 12 months' prior written notice. The total potential term, including options, was 42 years. Most institutional lenders require that the remaining ground lease term, including extension options that are exercisable by the lessee without lessor consent, exceed the loan term by a minimum of 10 years, and many require the total remaining term to exceed 30 to 35 years on its face before considering the options. With a requested 10-year loan term, the base term coverage of 22 years technically satisfied the 10-year cushion requirement, but several lenders in the market at the relevant time applied a policy requiring 30 years of remaining term without relying on unexercised options. The conditional nature of the options, combined with the 12-month exercise notice requirement, created a structural vulnerability that several lenders identified as a credit concern.

Ground Rent Escalation Provisions

The ground lease contained periodic rent escalations tied to CPI, with adjustments every five years. For a lender underwriting leasehold income, a rising ground rent obligation that reduces the net leasehold NOI over time introduces a cash flow deterioration risk that is not present in fee simple financing. The analysis required stress-testing the leasehold NOI across multiple rent reset periods to confirm that debt service coverage was maintained at 1.25x through the loan term even at projected CPI adjustments. At a 3.5% average annual CPI escalation compounded over the escalation periods, ground rent would reach approximately $85,000 by year 10, reducing leasehold NOI to approximately $400,000 and compressing DSCR to approximately 1.21x at the original loan amount. That compression, while still marginally above the floor, was a factor in lender pricing and sizing decisions.

The Topeka Lender Market

The commercial mortgage lending landscape in Topeka for leasehold collateral is narrower than for fee simple assets, but it is not absent. Understanding that distinction is essential to a damages analysis in a financing dispute, because the relevant question is not whether any lender in the world would have financed this transaction but whether lenders active in the Topeka market at the relevant time could and would have done so under market terms.

Regional banks with Topeka commercial real estate lending operations, including Kansas-chartered banks and Missouri regionals with Topeka loan production presence, represent the most active financing source for secondary-market leasehold transactions. These lenders typically hold loans in portfolio and apply relationship-based underwriting that can accommodate leasehold collateral when the SNDA deficiencies can be cured. Their credit boxes generally require the 1.20x to 1.25x DSCR, maximum 70% LTV on leasehold collateral, and remaining term coverage of at least 10 years beyond the loan maturity. They price at spreads of 175 to 250 basis points over the corresponding Treasury or SOFR index, and their willingness to engage on leasehold complexity depends heavily on borrower relationship depth and net worth.

CMBS conduit lenders, which periodically source loans from the Topeka market for securitization, apply the most rigid underwriting standards on leasehold collateral. Their standard requirements include a fully executed tri-party SNDA with specific mortgagee protection language, a minimum remaining lease term of 30 years beyond loan maturity, and maximum LTV of 65% for non-subordinated ground leases. For the transaction at issue, the CMBS market was effectively closed without a corrected SNDA, regardless of loan sizing. Life insurance companies with Midwest regional mandates occasionally finance Topeka commercial assets but generally avoid leasehold collateral below $5 million loan size, making them less relevant to this transaction. Debt funds and bridge lenders can accommodate leasehold complexity at higher cost, typically 300 to 500 basis points over SOFR, but their relevance to a permanent financing dispute is limited unless the theory of damages involves bridge financing as an interim alternative.

What Attorneys Handling Topeka CRE Disputes Need to Know

The most important thing litigation counsel needs to understand about retaining a CRE financing expert is that the function is categorically different from retaining an appraiser. An appraiser establishes the market value of the collateral. A CRE financing expert establishes whether that collateral was financeable, on what terms, through which lender categories, and whether the transaction structure met or failed prevailing market standards. In a ground lease financing dispute, those are different questions with different analytical methodologies, and conflating them is a significant error in case preparation.

CLS CRE brings approximately $1 billion in transaction volume and relationships with over 1,000 lenders across 50 states to this analysis. That active market participation is what distinguishes a qualified CRE financing expert from a retired banker or a consultant who has not arranged a loan in a decade. Lender underwriting standards evolve constantly. What a bank required in 2018 may differ materially from what it required in 2022. An expert who is not actively working in the capital markets cannot reliably testify to what market standards were at the time of the failed transaction.

Attorneys should gather the following materials before the expert engagement: the complete ground lease including all amendments and exhibits; any draft or executed SNDA agreements; the lender's written commitment letter, if issued, and the written declination and stated reasons; the appraisal commissioned for the transaction; the borrower's financial statements and personal financial statements submitted to the lender; and all correspondence between the borrower, the broker, and the lender's credit and closing teams. The timeline of events is often as important as the documents, because lender declinations sometimes reflect credit policy changes that occurred after application, which can be material to whether the declination was commercially reasonable.

At trial or in a Plan of Reorganization hearing, CRE financing expert testimony is used in several ways. In lender liability cases, the expert establishes whether the lender's underwriting decision conformed to or deviated from market practice, supporting or rebutting claims of bad faith or breach of commitment. In borrower damages cases, the expert quantifies the financing that was available and on what terms, establishing the economic baseline against which damages are measured. In bankruptcy proceedings, the expert can opine on feasibility of a Plan that relies on a refinancing event, establishing whether the projected financing is achievable under market conditions. In lease disputes involving ground lease termination, the expert can quantify the leasehold value that is destroyed by a wrongful termination, which requires understanding the mortgage market that would have been available on a properly structured leasehold position.

Key Takeaways

  • Ground lease financing in Topeka requires a fully executed, lender-form SNDA. A tenant-side acknowledgment in the ground lease is not a substitute. Without a tri-party SNDA containing independent mortgagee cure rights, the leasehold is not financeable by any institutional lender regardless of income coverage or LTV.
  • Leasehold NOI must be calculated net of ground rent. Financing capacity is determined by the income stream available to service the leasehold mortgage after the ground rent obligation is paid. Gross property NOI overstates the leasehold borrowing capacity and will produce inflated loan sizing conclusions that do not reflect how lenders actually underwrite this collateral.
  • Remaining lease term requirements vary by lender category. Regional banks may accept 10-year cushion over loan maturity, while CMBS lenders require 30 years of remaining term. A ground lease with 22 years remaining and unexercised options is financeable by some lenders but not others, and that distinction defines the damages universe.
  • The Topeka secondary market narrows the institutional lender pool for leasehold collateral. Life companies and conduit lenders with minimum loan sizes above $5 million effectively exclude many Topeka leasehold transactions, concentrating the financing market in regional and community banks whose credit criteria and pricing must be documented carefully for expert testimony.
  • CPI-escalating ground rent requires multi-year cash flow stress testing. A DSCR that clears the 1.25x threshold at origination may erode below it within the loan term if ground rent escalations are not modeled through all reset periods. Lenders underwriting this structure must confirm coverage maintenance over the full loan term, and expert analysis must replicate that methodology.
  • A CRE financing expert answers questions an appraiser cannot. Financing feasibility, lender market availability, underwriting standard conformity, and the commercial reasonableness of a declination

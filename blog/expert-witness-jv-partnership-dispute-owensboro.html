Expert Witness: CRE Partnership and Waterfall Disputes in Owensboro, KY

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Joint Venture and Partnership Dispute

The Owensboro Market Context

Owensboro, Kentucky sits at an interesting inflection point for commercial real estate joint venture disputes. As the fourth-largest city in Kentucky with a metro population approaching 120,000, Owensboro punches above its weight class in terms of institutional deal activity, particularly in industrial, multifamily, and anchored retail. The city's position along the Ohio River, its role as a regional logistics hub, and its proximity to major interstate corridors have attracted out-of-state capital that increasingly structures deals through formal joint venture agreements rather than simple co-ownership arrangements. That increased sophistication in deal structure is precisely what generates the kind of waterfall and promoted interest disputes that end up in Daviess Circuit Court or federal proceedings.

On the industrial side, Owensboro's vacancy rates have remained consistently below 5% over the past several years, with average asking rents in the $4.50 to $6.50 per square foot NNN range depending on clear height, dock configuration, and proximity to the U.S. 60 corridor. Multifamily fundamentals have been equally compelling: cap rates in Owensboro have compressed into the 5.75% to 7.0% range for stabilized assets, depending on vintage and unit count, with effective rents on Class B garden-style product running $850 to $1,100 per month. These rent levels are modest by coastal standards but represent material appreciation from where Owensboro was trading five to seven years ago, which is exactly the context in which promoted interest disputes arise. When a deal performs better than the original underwriting anticipated, the question of who is entitled to the upside becomes contentious fast.

Mixed-use and anchored retail in Owensboro has shown more durability than many comparable secondary markets, partly because the local grocery-anchored and necessity-based retail base has held occupancy while regional mall exposure has diminished. Office fundamentals are softer, with suburban vacancy elevated and downtown absorption inconsistent, but that sector generates its own distinct class of JV disputes related to lease-up failures and capital contribution obligations. For purposes of this analysis, the JV dispute mechanics we address are most commonly triggered in the industrial and multifamily sectors where the deal metrics made original underwriting look conservative and the waterfall mechanics were, in hindsight, drafted loosely.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing the equity partner in a Owensboro joint venture involving a stabilized industrial flex portfolio. The asset class was a multi-building light industrial and flex complex in a western Owensboro submarket, acquired approximately four years prior to the dispute through a co-investment structure. The capital partner contributed the majority of the equity at closing, carrying a preferred return obligation owed by the operating partner. The operating partner held a promoted interest that was to be calculated and paid at defined distribution events, including a refinance event and a sale.

The core legal and financial questions our firm was retained to address were threefold. First, was the preferred return correctly calculated under market-standard JV waterfall mechanics, given the specific language of the operating agreement? Second, did the refinancing transaction executed during the hold period constitute a "capital event" triggering waterfall distributions, and were those distributions correctly sequenced? Third, was the promoted interest calculation performed at the sale event consistent with how institutional sponsors and their legal counsel customarily structure and compute promote in comparable JV arrangements?

Counsel needed an expert who could bridge the gap between the legal text of the operating agreement and the market reality of how these structures are actually built, negotiated, and administered. That is a different skill set than an appraiser offers, and it is a different skill set than a transactional attorney who drafted deals but never sat on a capital markets desk or structured a JV for a regional operating partner. CLS CRE brought approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, 1,000-plus lender and capital relationships across 50 states, and direct experience structuring JV waterfalls in secondary and tertiary Kentucky markets to this engagement.

The Financial Analysis

Our analysis began with a reconstruction of the property-level income stream at each relevant point in the dispute timeline: at acquisition, at the refinancing event, and at the sale. For industrial flex product in Owensboro, the stabilized NOI was calculated from in-place leases, adjusting for credit loss, management fee, and capital reserve assumptions consistent with what institutional lenders require in their underwriting. We applied a 5% vacancy assumption and a 3% management fee, consistent with market-standard underwriting for this submarket and product type.

For the refinancing event analysis, the central question was what loan proceeds were supportable by the income stream at that point in time, because the loan proceeds directly affected how much cash was available for waterfall distribution and therefore how much, if any, of the capital partner's preferred return accrual was discharged at that event. We modeled debt service coverage ratio thresholds at 1.20x and 1.25x, which represent the standard market range for conventional commercial real estate loans on stabilized industrial product. Agency and CMBS lenders typically require 1.25x DSCR at the loan constant, while community banks and regional lenders in Kentucky markets often underwrite to 1.20x with strong sponsorship.

Using a stabilized NOI of approximately $680,000 at the time of the refinance event, and applying a 1.25x DSCR threshold at a then-prevailing fixed rate of 4.75% over a 25-year amortization schedule, the supportable debt service was approximately $544,000 annually, translating to a maximum supportable loan of approximately $5.95 million at that loan constant. At a 70% LTV ceiling, which is standard for stabilized industrial in a secondary Kentucky market, the same collateral value implied a maximum loan of approximately $6.30 million, confirming that DSCR was the binding constraint, not LTV. The operating partner's position was that the refinance event generated distributable proceeds above the return of capital threshold, which would have triggered promote participation. Our analysis demonstrated that after satisfying senior debt obligations and the preferred return accrual, the remaining distributable proceeds were insufficient to clear the preferred return hurdle, meaning no promote was payable at that event under a correct reading of market-standard waterfall mechanics.

At the sale event, we performed a separate waterfall reconstruction, modeling the gross sale proceeds net of transaction costs, the remaining senior debt balance, the capital partner's unreturned capital, the full accumulated preferred return at the contractually specified rate, and only then the promoted interest calculation. The preferred return had accrued at an 8% cumulative compounding rate on unreturned invested capital, which is market-standard for a Tier 2 Kentucky market JV. The operating partner's calculate methodology failed to account for the compounding convention correctly, understating the preferred return accrual by approximately $190,000, which directly reduced the capital partner's entitled distributions and inflated the operating partner's promoted interest by the same amount.

The Complicating Factors

Ambiguous compounding convention in the operating agreement. The single most common source of waterfall litigation in secondary market JVs is imprecise drafting around whether the preferred return compounds daily, monthly, quarterly, or annually, and whether it compounds on unreturned capital only or on unpaid preferred return accruals as well. In this engagement, the operating agreement used the phrase "8% per annum, cumulative, non-compounding" in one section and "cumulative compound preferred return" in the definitions section, an internal contradiction that generated a six-figure dispute. Market-standard institutional JV agreements are specific: they define the compounding period, the basis for accrual, and whether unpaid preferred return itself accrues additional preferred return. Ambiguity at the drafting stage becomes litigation at the distribution stage, particularly in deals that perform well.

Refinancing as a capital event. Whether a cash-out refinancing triggers a waterfall distribution is a structuring choice that should be explicit in the operating agreement, but frequently is not in deals structured by regional sponsors working with less sophisticated legal counsel. In the Owensboro market, where many JV deals are structured by regional operators who may have a strong operating track record but limited experience with institutional-grade documentation, this ambiguity is common. Lenders view refinancing proceeds as a credit event, not an equity event. But for waterfall purposes, cash proceeds released to the JV by a refinancing can constitute a return of capital, a paydown of preferred return accruals, or simply operating cash, depending entirely on how the agreement defines "capital event" and "distributable cash." Our analysis required mapping the actual loan proceeds against the waterfall tiers, a task that requires understanding both the financing mechanics and the equity structure simultaneously.

Preferred return accrual during the lease-up period. If the property was not generating sufficient cash flow to pay current preferred return in the early periods of the hold, the accrued but unpaid preferred return balance grows, and the compounding question becomes financially material. In Owensboro industrial deals with repositioning components, a 12 to 24 month stabilization period is common. During that window, the preferred return is accruing against a growing unreturned capital balance without offsetting cash distributions. Operating partners sometimes dispute the accrual methodology for this period, arguing that preferred return should only accrue on capital that was "deployed" rather than committed, a distinction that is economically significant and must be resolved by reference to both the agreement language and market custom.

Capital contribution sequencing disputes. In multi-tranche equity structures, where the capital partner funded construction or tenant improvement costs in draws over time, the question of when capital was "contributed" for preferred return accrual purposes is technically and financially complex. Our firm's analysis required reconstructing the capital account ledger from closing through the refinancing event, matching capital calls to contribution dates and verifying that the preferred return accrual commenced on each contributed dollar from its actual contribution date, not from closing.

The Owensboro Lender Market

Understanding the lender market that was available to the JV at each point in the dispute timeline is not optional context. It is analytically essential, because the loan terms actually achievable in the Owensboro market at each moment directly determine what proceeds were available for waterfall purposes and what financing alternatives existed.

For stabilized industrial in Owensboro, the active lender categories include regional community banks headquartered in Owensboro and Bowling Green, life company correspondents operating through Louisville-based platforms, and CMBS lenders willing to originate loans above approximately $3 million on stabilized assets with institutional-quality leases. Community banks in the Owensboro market typically price at 200 to 275 basis points over the corresponding treasury, with 20 to 25-year amortization, 5 to 10-year terms, and personal recourse requirements that institutional JV partners often resist. Life company execution, when available for Owensboro industrial assets that meet the size and quality threshold, offers 30-year amortization, non-recourse structures, and pricing 150 to 200 basis points over treasuries, but typically requires loan sizes above $5 million and institutional-quality sponsorship. CMBS is available for stabilized industrial above $3 million but carries yield maintenance or defeasance prepayment provisions that affect the economics of any refinance-and-distribute strategy.

Bridge lenders and debt funds are active in the Owensboro market for transitional assets and lease-up plays, typically pricing at SOFR plus 300 to 450 basis points with 65% to 75% LTC, 24 to 36-month initial terms, and two extension options. These bridge structures are particularly relevant to JV disputes involving lease-up shortfalls, because the bridge loan's interest carry directly affects NOI, cash distributions, and the rate at which preferred return accrues relative to cash paid. CLS CRE maintains active relationships with institutional bridge lenders who have closed transactions in the Owensboro and Paducah corridor, and this market access is directly relevant to expert analysis of what was achievable versus what was executed.

What Attorneys Handling Owensboro CRE Disputes Need to Know

Retaining a CRE financing expert is not the same as retaining an appraiser, and the distinction matters enormously in JV waterfall litigation. An appraiser determines what an asset is worth. A CRE financing expert determines what the capital structure was, what it should have been, what loan proceeds were supportable at each event, how the waterfall mechanics interact with the financing terms, and whether the distributions actually made were consistent with market-standard practice and the agreement language. These are fundamentally different questions, and they require fundamentally different expertise.

Attorneys should retain a CRE financing expert at the point when any of the following questions are in dispute: how a preferred return was calculated or compounded; whether a refinancing event triggered waterfall distributions; whether loan proceeds were correctly characterized as return of capital versus distributable cash; whether the promoted interest was correctly computed given the actual capital stack; or whether the financing terms obtained were consistent with what was available and achievable in the relevant market at the relevant time. These are not questions that a real estate attorney, a CPA, or a general business valuation expert can reliably answer, because they require active knowledge of how lenders underwrite, how capital markets participants structure these transactions, and what market-standard practice actually looks like in a specific asset class and geography.

A qualified CRE financing expert is not simply a retired banker or a former loan officer. The relevant qualification is active, current engagement in commercial real estate capital markets, with demonstrated experience structuring JV equity, negotiating institutional debt, and working across the lender categories that are relevant to the deal type in dispute. Expert testimony that relies on what lenders did fifteen years ago, or that cannot speak to current DSCR thresholds, rate environments, and lender credit boxes, will be challenged on cross-examination and appropriately so.

Before engaging CLS CRE for an expert assignment, counsel should gather the following documents: the operating agreement and all amendments, including any side letters addressing capital contributions or distributions; the closing loan documents and any loan modification agreements; capital account ledgers from inception through the disputed distribution events; wire transfer records and distribution notices; and any internal communications between the JV partners regarding the calculation methodology. The more complete the record, the more defensible the expert analysis.

At trial or in a Chapter 11 plan hearing, CRE financing expert testimony is used to establish: what market-standard practice requires, which grounds the interpretation of ambiguous agreement language; what the correct waterfall calculation produces, providing the factfinder with a concrete alternative to the disputed calculation; and whether the financing decisions made during the hold period were consistent with what a reasonable JV operator would have done, which speaks directly to breach of fiduciary duty and good faith claims.

Key Takeaways

  • Preferred return compounding disputes are financially material. A difference in compounding convention, monthly versus annual, cumulative versus non-cumulative, can produce six-figure differences in distributions owed on a mid-market Owensboro JV, and the expert analysis must reconstruct the accrual ledger from day one of the capital account.
  • Refinancing events require dual analysis. The expert must analyze both the debt side (what proceeds were supportable, what DSCR and LTV constraints applied, what the lender market offered at that moment in time) and the equity side (how those proceeds flow through the waterfall tiers under a correct reading of the agreement).
  • Owensboro lender market depth is real but asset-specific. Community banks, life company correspondents, and CMBS lenders are all accessible for the right asset at the right size, but each carries materially different loan terms that affect waterfall mechanics differently, and the expert must demonstrate familiarity with each category.
  • A CRE financing expert is not an appraiser. The two disciplines address different questions; retaining only one when you need both is a litigation risk that experienced CRE counsel should not accept.
  • DSCR at 1.20x to 1.25x and LTV at 65% to 75% are the binding constraints for most Owensboro stabilized industrial and multifamily loans, and demonstrating which constraint was binding at the refinancing event is often the determinative step in a waterfall dispute.
  • Operating agreement ambiguity is the root cause of most JV distribution disputes. The expert's role is to establish what market-standard practice requires where the agreement is silent or contradictory, grounding the legal interpretation in the commercial reality of how these structures are built and administered.
  • Capital contribution timing is not administrative detail. In a compounding preferred return structure, the date on which each capital dollar was contributed determines the accrual base, and errors in contribution dating compound into material distribution errors over a four-to-seven-year hold period.
  • Active market engagement is the qualification standard. Expert testimony on CRE financing in Owensboro must reflect current lender behavior, current rate environments, and current underwriting standards, not historical analogies from a materially different market cycle.

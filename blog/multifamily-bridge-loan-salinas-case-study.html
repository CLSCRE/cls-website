Trevor Damyan Founder & Principal Broker

Quick answer: Commercial Lending Solutions arranged a $9.3 million renovation bridge loan from a private debt fund for the acquisition of a 68-unit multifamily property in Salinas, California. The loan funded roughly 75 percent of total project cost, including the renovation budget, on a 12-month term priced at SOFR plus a spread in the mid 5s. The structure was sized to the property's projected after-renovation value, not its day-one income.

The Deal That Walked in the Door

We are Commercial Lending Solutions, a commercial mortgage brokerage based in Los Angeles, and this deal came to us from a repeat sponsor, a family investment group out of Central California that had closed with us before. They had a 68-unit apartment community under contract in Salinas, California (CA) for roughly $11 million. Salinas is the largest city in Monterey County, the agricultural capital of the Salinas Valley, about an hour south of San Jose, with persistent workforce housing demand and almost no new multifamily supply coming out of the ground.

Commercial Lending Solutions specializes in multifamily bridge loans and renovation financing for investors across California and nationwide, and this Salinas assignment was squarely in that lane.

The property itself was a classic 1960s-vintage garden community: well located, structurally sound, and tired. Units were dated, rents sat well below the Salinas market, and the seller had deferred maintenance for years. The sponsor's plan was a full fix-to-rent renovation, take the units to market condition, push rents to market, and refinance into permanent debt at a much higher value.

That combination, real upside with weak current income, is exactly the profile that gets a polite no from conventional lenders. Any bank sizing the loan to in-place cash flow would have produced proceeds far short of the purchase price, let alone the renovation budget. This deal needed a private debt fund willing to underwrite the business plan, not the trailing twelve months.

What Made It Hard

  • In-place income barely covered debt service. With below-market rents and renovation-related vacancy coming, the property could support the loan at only about 1.0x coverage on day one. Most balance-sheet lenders will not touch that.
  • The sponsor needed the renovation budget financed, not just the purchase. Writing a seven-figure renovation check out of pocket after closing would have strained liquidity and slowed the whole plan.
  • Speed and certainty of close. The purchase contract had a firm timeline and the seller had backup offers behind ours. A slow-moving bank committee was a real risk to the sponsor's deposit.
  • Monterey County is not a market national lenders know. Plenty of bridge lenders quote Los Angeles and the Bay Area all day. Salinas required a lender that either knew the Salinas Valley rental market or trusted the appraisal enough to learn it fast.

How We Ran the Process

From our desk in Los Angeles, Trevor Damyan, a California-licensed real estate broker (DRE #02244836), packaged the deal the way bridge lenders actually underwrite it: current rent roll against a market rent survey, the unit-by-unit renovation budget, the sponsor's track record on prior repositions, and a sources-and-uses that showed exactly how the loan retires. Then we put it in front of the renovation-bridge specialists in our network of more than 1,000 capital sources and let them compete on leverage, structure, and certainty.

The winner was a national private debt fund that specializes in fix-to-rent and renovation lending on multifamily. They beat the field not on rate, several quotes came in cheaper on paper, but on total proceeds and a funding structure that matched the construction schedule. The winning structure:

  • $9,285,000 total facility, covering 74.9 percent of total project cost including the renovation budget, with future funding released as renovation milestones were hit, and a 1 percent origination fee
  • Sized against after-renovation value, with proceeds landing at 63.1 percent of the appraiser's stabilized value against a 65 percent cap, giving the lender comfort on the exit while maximizing day-one leverage for the sponsor
  • 12-month term, matched to a renovation and lease-up plan the sponsor could realistically execute inside a year
  • Floating rate at SOFR plus 5.60 percent, a starting rate of roughly 9.85 percent with a floor at the initial rate, which is the price of admission for 75 percent leverage on a heavy value-add plan
  • Coverage tested two ways: roughly 1.0x on in-place income and 1.20x on the stabilized pro forma, so the lender was underwriting both today's reality and the plan

The loan funded on May 9, 2025, on schedule, and the sponsor took title with the full renovation budget committed. Purchase price roughly $11 million, loan of $9,285,000, and the buyer's equity check stayed small enough to keep reserves intact for the renovation ramp.

"This deal worked because we sized it to the business plan instead of the trailing twelve months. The sponsor did not need the cheapest rate, they needed a lender willing to fund the whole renovation on a schedule they could actually hit."
, Trevor Damyan, Commercial Lending Solutions

Why We Won This One

The lesson in this closing is about matching the loan to the business plan. The sponsor did not need the cheapest money available. They needed the most money against total cost, funded reliably, on a timeline that kept the purchase contract alive. A bridge loan at a 9 handle that funds the whole plan beats a bank loan at a 6 handle that funds two-thirds of it and takes 90 days longer. We priced that trade for the sponsor in real dollars before they picked, and the math was not close.

It is also a reminder that lenders will stretch on current cash flow when the after-renovation story is credible. The 1.0x in-place coverage would be a dealbreaker at most banks. For the right bridge lender, paired with a 1.20x stabilized pro forma, an experienced repeat sponsor, and a hard cap at 65 percent of after-repair value, it was an approvable risk. Our job as the broker was making that story impossible to misread: the market rent survey, the budget, and the exit refinance analysis were all in the package before the lender ever asked.

And it is proof that secondary California markets get financed. Salinas is not San Jose, and it does not need to be. Workforce housing an hour from Silicon Valley with a 68-unit scale and a credible value-add plan drew multiple competing term sheets. If your deal is in Fresno, Bakersfield, Stockton, or Salinas and your local bank shrugged, the problem is the lender list, not the market.

Frequently Asked Questions

Renovation bridge loans commonly fund 70 to 80 percent of total project cost, including both the purchase price and the renovation budget. In this Salinas closing, the lender funded approximately 75 percent of total cost, subject to a cap of 65 percent of the property's projected after-renovation value.
Bridge loans from private debt funds are typically floating rate, priced at a spread over SOFR. Spreads generally run from roughly 3 to 6 points over the index depending on leverage, property condition, market, and sponsor experience. This deal priced at SOFR plus a spread in the mid 5s, for a starting rate near 9.85 percent, reflecting its high leverage and heavy renovation scope.
Yes. Bridge lenders underwrite to the stabilized picture, not just current income. A common structure requires the property to cover debt service at roughly 1.0x on in-place income while demonstrating 1.20x or better coverage on the post-renovation pro forma.
Most renovation bridge loans run 12 to 36 months. Shorter 12-month terms, like the one used in this Salinas acquisition, keep pricing tighter and work well when the renovation scope can be completed and the property refinanced or sold within a year, often with extension options as a safety valve.
Sponsor experience and track record carry significant weight. Lenders want to see that the team has executed similar renovation plans before, has the liquidity to carry the project, and has a credible exit, typically a refinance into permanent debt once the property is stabilized.

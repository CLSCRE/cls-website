Expert Witness: CRE Partnership and Waterfall Disputes in Lynchburg, VA

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Joint Venture and Partnership Dispute

The Lynchburg Market Context

Lynchburg, Virginia sits at an intersection of institutional anchor demand and small-market capital constraints that makes it a genuinely interesting laboratory for commercial real estate joint venture disputes. The metro area anchored by Liberty University, Centra Health, and a manufacturing base concentrated along the Route 29 corridor supports more deal velocity than its population of roughly 270,000 would suggest. That underlying demand also creates conditions where joint ventures form frequently, often between local operating partners with market knowledge and outside capital partners seeking yield in a secondary market perceived as lower-risk than primary coastal metros.

Multifamily has been the dominant JV vehicle in Lynchburg over the past five years. Cap rates for stabilized multifamily assets in Lynchburg have generally traded in the 5.75 to 6.75 percent range depending on vintage, submarket, and proximity to Liberty University's enrollment-driven renter pool. The Wyndhurst and Timberlake Road corridors on the north side have attracted workforce housing development, while the downtown core and Riverfront area have seen adaptive reuse and mixed-use activity with more complex capital structures. Mixed-use and retail-anchored deals in the Wards Road submarket carry higher cap rates, generally 7.0 to 7.5 percent, reflecting the corridor's dependence on regional traffic and big-box adjacency rather than intrinsic walkability.

What makes this market particularly prone to JV waterfall disputes is the structure of the deals themselves. Outside equity partners, often from Northern Virginia, Richmond, or out-of-state family offices, pair with Lynchburg-based sponsors who control the local relationships and development pipeline. Those partnerships are frequently papered on market-standard institutional templates that were designed for larger, more liquid markets. When the deal performs differently than projected, whether due to slower lease-up, interest rate increases affecting refinance proceeds, or cap rate expansion reducing sale proceeds, the waterfall mechanics that looked clean at underwriting become contested at distribution.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing the equity capital partner in a dispute involving a value-add multifamily acquisition in the Lynchburg metro. The asset was a mid-size apartment community, acquired with a combination of senior debt and JV equity contributed in two tranches with different priority positions. The operating partner held the promoted interest and served as the day-to-day asset manager throughout the hold period. The capital partner's position carried an 8 percent preferred return on its contributed equity, with a tiered promote structure above the preferred return threshold.

The asset was ultimately sold after a four-year hold. The dispute arose at distribution. The operating partner's position was that the preferred return had not fully accrued because certain operating deficits had been funded from reserves rather than from additional equity contributions, and that the waterfall clock should be adjusted accordingly. The capital partner's position was that the preferred return accrued on all contributed capital from the date of contribution, that the operating partner's characterization of reserve draws was not consistent with the operating agreement's definitions, and that the promoted interest had not been earned because the project had not cleared the preferred return hurdle at the time of distribution.

Our engagement covered three specific analytical questions. First, we were asked to opine on whether the financing structure available at the time of acquisition supported the capital stack as actually deployed, and whether the debt terms constrained or informed the waterfall mechanics. Second, we analyzed whether the debt service coverage the property actually achieved was consistent with what a market-standard underwriting would have projected at acquisition, and whether the operating shortfalls were foreseeable or extraordinary. Third, we were asked to evaluate whether the refinance opportunity that arose during the hold period, and that the operating partner elected not to pursue, would have materially altered the waterfall by returning capital to the preferred partner earlier in the hold.

The Financial Analysis

The property's stabilized net operating income at acquisition was documented in the offering memorandum and the lender's origination file. Our analysis began by reconstructing the underwritten NOI from the rent rolls, historical operating statements, and the lender's appraisal. The property carried gross scheduled rents that, after vacancy and credit loss at a market-standard 7 percent and operating expenses at approximately 42 percent of effective gross income, produced a stabilized NOI in the range of $380,000 annually. That figure was corroborated by the senior lender's original underwriting, which we obtained through document discovery.

For acquisition-bridge financing on a value-add multifamily asset in a secondary Virginia market at the time of origination, lender DSCR requirements would have been set at a minimum of 1.25x on the as-stabilized NOI, with some lenders applying 1.20x at the agency level for a fully stabilized community. Using the 1.25x DSCR floor and the note rate of 4.85 percent on a 30-year amortization schedule that was reflected in the loan documents, the maximum supportable loan on stabilized NOI of $380,000 would have been approximately $4.87 million. The actual loan balance at closing was $4.6 million, reflecting an LTV of approximately 72 percent against the appraised value of $6.4 million. That LTV was within standard parameters for the asset class and market, but it left limited headroom for a cash-out refinance if NOI growth underperformed projections.

The operating partner had underwritten a Year 3 stabilized NOI of $435,000, reflecting a renovation program expected to support average effective rents approximately 14 percent above acquisition levels. The actual Year 3 NOI was $391,000, a shortfall of $44,000 against the business plan. At a 6.25 percent market cap rate applicable to the submarket at that time, that NOI shortfall translated to a value gap of approximately $704,000 relative to the projected value. More critically for the waterfall analysis, it meant that the refinance the operating partner had been relying on to return capital to the preferred partner in Year 3 would have only supported a loan of approximately $5.0 million at 1.25x DSCR, compared to the $5.6 million loan the business plan had assumed. The shortfall in refinance proceeds meant the preferred return accrual continued compounding for an additional 14 months before the eventual sale, and that additional accrual was at the heart of the quantum of distributions in dispute.

We also analyzed the mechanics of compound versus simple preferred return accrual as they applied to the operating agreement language. The agreement was ambiguous on this point. Market standard in institutional JV agreements is for preferred returns to accrue on a cumulative, non-compounding basis unless the agreement explicitly states compounded accrual. We reviewed the drafting, compared it to market-standard templates from institutional sponsors active in Virginia, and opined that the plain language was most consistent with simple accrual, which was the lower figure. Even on that basis, the preferred return had not been cleared at the time the operating partner initially proposed distributions.

The Complicating Factors

Reserve contributions and their treatment in the waterfall. The operating agreement provided that the capital partner would fund operating shortfalls up to a defined reserve cap. During two quarters in Year 2, the property's collections fell below the debt service requirement due to a temporary occupancy dip during the renovation phase. The operating partner drew from a debt service reserve held by the lender rather than calling capital. The question was whether that lender-held reserve constituted a capital contribution for purposes of preferred return accrual. Lenders hold debt service reserves as loan conditions, not as equity capital. The reserve was funded at closing from loan proceeds as a structured requirement of the senior lender, not as a discretionary contribution by either equity partner. The distinction matters because if it were treated as a capital contribution, the preferred return base would increase and so would the hurdle. We opined that reserve draws from a lender-controlled account are not equity contributions under any standard framework, and that treating them as such would be inconsistent with how the capital stack was structured and how any institutional lender would interpret the transaction.

The decision not to refinance. The operating partner's decision to pass on a refinance opportunity in Year 3 is a significant factor because it affected the duration of preferred return accrual. The partner's position was that the refinance quotes available at the time did not produce sufficient proceeds to meaningfully return capital, and that pursuing a refinance would have triggered prepayment costs on the existing loan. Our analysis of the rate environment and lender appetite at that point showed that at least three lender categories, specifically agency execution through Freddie Mac's small balance program, CMBS conduit, and regional bank portfolio lending, would have been available for this asset at that time. A Freddie Mac small balance quote at 6.1 percent on 30-year amortization with 1.25x DSCR would have supported a loan of approximately $5.05 million, which after repayment of the existing $4.6 million balance and estimated closing costs of approximately $85,000, would have returned roughly $365,000 in net proceeds to equity. Under the waterfall, those proceeds would have been allocated first to clear unpaid preferred return, which would have materially reduced the compounding accrual problem. The failure to pursue that refinance, and whether it constituted a breach of the operating partner's fiduciary obligations, was a legal question for the trier of fact. Our role was to establish that a refinance was feasible and would have been available on reasonable terms.

Cap rate movement and its effect on the promote calculation. The property sold in a market environment where cap rates had expanded approximately 75 basis points from the projected exit cap. On a sale price determined by a 6.75 percent exit cap applied to the actual NOI at sale, the gross proceeds were sufficient to repay senior debt and return equity but left approximately $210,000 in total distribution proceeds above return of capital. The promote structure provided for a 20 percent carried interest to the operating partner above the preferred return hurdle. Because the preferred return had not been cleared, the operating partner was not entitled to any promoted interest, a position we supported with both the waterfall mechanics and with reference to how institutional JV agreements in comparable markets are typically structured and interpreted.

The Lynchburg Lender Market

Understanding which lenders are active in Lynchburg and what their credit parameters look like is essential background for any financing feasibility opinion in this market. The lender landscape here is more layered than a simple primary versus secondary market classification would suggest.

Community and regional banks are the dominant execution channel for deals below $5 million in Lynchburg. Institutions with a Virginia footprint and branch presence in the Lynchburg MSA are active portfolio lenders on multifamily, owner-occupied commercial, and mixed-use assets. Their credit boxes typically require 1.20x to 1.25x DSCR, 70 to 75 percent LTV, and personal recourse from the operating partner. Their rates are generally indexed to the Wall Street Journal Prime rate or the five-year Treasury, with spreads that have historically run 175 to 225 basis points over benchmark. For value-add deals, community banks will often lend on a stabilized appraised value rather than as-is, but they require realistic renovation budgets and demonstrated contractor relationships. This matters for JV disputes because a community bank loan often requires the operating partner's personal guarantee, which affects the risk allocation between JV partners and the negotiating leverage each holds during an operational dispute.

Agency execution through Freddie Mac's Small Balance Loan program and Fannie Mae's DUS channel is available in Lynchburg for qualifying multifamily assets of five units or more. The small balance program is particularly relevant for properties in the $1 million to $7.5 million loan range, which covers most Lynchburg multifamily deals. Pricing at the time of the underlying transaction in our engagement would have been in the range of 5.9 to 6.2 percent fixed for ten years, non-recourse except for standard carve-outs, with DSCR requirements of 1.25x and LTV up to 80 percent for affordable properties or 75 percent for conventional. Agency execution eliminates personal recourse, which changes the power dynamic in a JV dispute significantly.

Debt funds and bridge lenders are available in Lynchburg for transitional assets but at significantly higher cost, typically SOFR plus 325 to 425 basis points, with origination fees of 1.0 to 1.5 points. For a value-add multifamily play with a 24 to 36-month renovation timeline, bridge debt is often the only institutional execution available at acquisition. The higher cost of bridge debt compresses equity returns from day one and creates additional pressure on the waterfall, particularly if lease-up takes longer than projected.

CLS CRE maintains active lender relationships across all three categories for Virginia secondary markets, with specific relationships covering Lynchburg deal flow. Our approximately $1 billion in transaction volume and 1,000-plus lender relationships nationally enable us to provide credible, market-tested opinions on lender appetite and pricing in markets like Lynchburg where the institutional execution is available but requires specific knowledge of which lenders are genuinely active versus nominally willing.

What Attorneys Handling Lynchburg CRE Disputes Need to Know

A CRE financing expert witness is not a substitute for an appraiser, and the distinction matters when you are building a litigation strategy around a JV waterfall dispute. An appraiser tells the trier of fact what a property was worth. A CRE financing expert tells the trier of fact what debt was available, on what terms, at what cost, and how that debt constrained or enabled the equity waterfall. Those are different questions, and in a distribution dispute, the financing question is often more determinative than the valuation question.

Specifically, a CRE financing expert can opine on the following issues that lie beyond the scope of appraisal testimony: whether a refinance was feasible at a given point in the hold period and on what terms; whether the debt structure as originated was consistent with market standards for the asset class and market; whether the DSCR achieved was foreseeable based on the underwritten NOI; how the senior loan's prepayment provisions affected the operating partner's decision-making optionality; and whether the preferred return accrual methodology used in the waterfall calculation was consistent with market-standard institutional JV practice.

When retaining a CRE financing expert for a Lynchburg matter, qualifications matter more than geography. A retired community banker from central Virginia does not bring the same analytical framework as someone with active experience structuring JV capital stacks, agency executions, and bridge-to-permanent financing across multiple markets and asset classes. Look for an expert with documented experience originating or arranging the specific type of debt at issue, active market knowledge of current lender pricing and credit parameters, and a track record of Rule 26 expert report production in CRE financing matters. Familiarity with the asset class, not just the geography, is the threshold credential.

Before engaging a CRE financing expert, litigation counsel should gather the following documents at minimum: the original loan application and lender underwriting file; the operating agreement including all amendments and side letters; the operating partner's original investor presentation or private placement memorandum; all audited or reviewed operating statements for the hold period; the loan documents including the note, deed of trust, loan agreement, and any modification agreements; all lender correspondence including any default notices, waiver requests, or covenant compliance certifications; and any third-party appraisals or broker opinions of value produced during the hold. That document set allows the expert to reconstruct the financing as structured and to evaluate the decisions made by the operating partner against what a reasonable JV sponsor would have done in similar circumstances.

At trial or in an arbitration, financing expert testimony is most powerful when it quantifies the economic consequence of the contested decision or calculation. Jurors and arbitrators respond to concrete numbers: the additional preferred return that accrued because the refinance was not pursued; the difference in promote entitlement between the two competing waterfall calculations; the gap between the projected and actual debt service that drove operating shortfalls. Our most effective expert testimony in comparable matters has centered on a clear, numerical narrative that connects the financing decision to the distribution outcome.

Key Takeaways

  • Preferred return mechanics are deal-specific but market-standard references exist. When an operating agreement is ambiguous on whether preferred return accrues on a simple or compound basis, institutional JV market practice defaults to simple accrual. Expert testimony grounded in market-standard drafting conventions carries significant weight in resolving ambiguous agreement language.
  • Debt structure shapes waterfall outcomes from day one. The LTV, DSCR, and amortization terms of the senior loan directly determine how much capital can be returned through refinance and at what point in the hold. A CRE financing expert can reconstruct the constraints embedded in the original debt structure and trace their effect forward through the hold period.

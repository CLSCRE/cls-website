The Racine Market Context

Racine, Wisconsin sits in a position that makes it commercially interesting and litigation-prone in equal measure. Located between Milwaukee and Chicago along Lake Michigan, Racine draws investors who want Midwest industrial and multifamily fundamentals without paying Milwaukee or Chicago pricing. That spread between markets creates opportunity, but it also creates disputes, because investors with different risk tolerances and return expectations often enter joint ventures with misaligned assumptions about what this market can deliver and on what timeline.

The industrial sector is the dominant story in Racine County right now. Vacancy rates in the Racine industrial submarket have generally tracked in the 4% to 7% range in recent years, supported by regional distribution demand, manufacturing recovery, and spillover from the tighter Milwaukee County industrial market where rents have climbed to the point that smaller tenants are relocating south along the I-94 corridor. Racine benefits from that migration. Net asking rents for industrial product in Racine have been moving in the $5.00 to $8.50 per square foot range for older functional warehouse and light manufacturing space, with newer construction or recently renovated logistics facilities pushing toward $9.00 to $11.00 per square foot depending on clear height and dock configuration. Those numbers are not Chicago, but they are real, and they support genuine institutional-quality underwriting when the asset is positioned correctly.

Multifamily in Racine tells a more nuanced story. Cap rates have generally ranged from 6.25% to 7.50% depending on vintage, unit mix, and proximity to the lakefront or downtown core, which is meaningfully higher than the 4.50% to 5.50% range you see in Milwaukee's tighter submarkets. That spread means equity investors in Racine multifamily joint ventures often disagreed at inception about expected exit valuations, and those disagreements about exit cap rate assumptions feed directly into waterfall disputes when the actual sale proceeds do not hit the underwritten return thresholds. That is the litigation context this article addresses.

Retail fundamentals are more challenged. Downtown Racine retail has seen vacancy pressure, and strip center performance is bifurcated between grocery-anchored centers with stable occupancy and unanchored inline retail that has struggled with tenant attrition. Office remains soft, consistent with national small-city office trends. For the purposes of joint venture waterfall litigation, the asset type matters enormously because the financing structure, the promoted interest triggers, and the preferred return mechanics are all calibrated to the expected income and appreciation profile of the underlying asset class.

The Engagement

Counsel for one of the limited partners in a Racine commercial real estate joint venture retained Commercial Lending Solutions to provide expert analysis in a dispute over waterfall distributions following the partial sale of a mixed-use asset with a significant industrial component. The matter involved a three-party venture structure in which a sponsoring general partner had raised preferred equity from two limited partners, each with different preferred return tiers, and had negotiated a promoted interest, or "carry," that became payable upon the achievement of a specified internal rate of return hurdle.

The core legal and financial questions were as follows: first, whether the actual distributions made to the limited partners correctly reflected the contractual waterfall mechanics in the operating agreement; second, whether the sponsor had correctly calculated the preferred return compounding methodology, specifically whether the preferred return was compounded annually or accrued on a simple interest basis; third, whether financing proceeds from a refinancing event that occurred mid-hold had been correctly characterized as a capital event triggering waterfall distribution obligations or whether those proceeds had been improperly retained at the operating entity level; and fourth, whether the capital stack structure at the time of the partial sale was consistent with market-standard structures for this asset type and hold period in the Racine market, which bore directly on the question of whether the sponsor had met its fiduciary and contractual obligations in structuring the financing.

Our engagement required us to analyze the original loan documents, the subsequent refinancing term sheet and closing documents, the operating agreement waterfall provisions, all financial statements and distribution records for the hold period, and the closing statements from both the refinancing and the partial sale. We also prepared a parallel analysis modeling what the distributions should have been under a correctly applied waterfall, and compared that to what was actually paid.

The Financial Analysis

The subject asset was an industrial and flex property in the Racine County submarket, acquired by the joint venture approximately four years prior to the litigation. At acquisition, the property was approximately 68% occupied, with in-place rents below market. The business plan was a lease-up value-add strategy, which is a common structure in Racine industrial because older functionally adequate buildings can be acquired at meaningful discounts to replacement cost and repositioned with modest capital investment.

At stabilization, the property generated gross potential rent of approximately $1,140,000 annually, with effective gross income after a 5% vacancy and credit loss assumption of approximately $1,083,000. Operating expenses, including property taxes, insurance, maintenance, and property management, totaled approximately $324,000. This produced a stabilized NOI of approximately $759,000.

The refinancing that created the disputed distribution question was underwritten at a market-standard DSCR of 1.25x, which is the conventional threshold applied by regional banks and agency lenders to stabilized industrial properties in secondary Midwest markets. At 1.25x DSCR, the maximum annual debt service supportable by the NOI was $607,200, calculated as $759,000 divided by 1.25. Using a 25-year amortization schedule and an interest rate of 6.50%, which reflected the market rate environment at the time of the refinancing, a debt service of $607,200 per year corresponded to a maximum loan amount of approximately $7,150,000.

The LTV check against that figure was critical to the dispute. The property had been appraised at $10,400,000 at the time of the refinancing. A maximum LTV of 65%, which is the standard institutional constraint for value-add industrial in secondary markets, would have permitted a loan of $6,760,000. The DSCR-constrained loan of $7,150,000 represented a 68.8% LTV against that appraised value, which is above the 65% institutional threshold. This meant the DSCR test, not the LTV test, was binding, and the maximum supportable loan was $6,760,000, constrained by LTV.

The actual loan closed at $6,500,000, leaving approximately $260,000 of additional debt capacity on the table, which was within normal underwriting cushion. The total equity in the property at the time of refinancing was therefore approximately $3,900,000 ($10,400,000 value minus $6,500,000 debt). Of that equity, the original equity invested by the venture had been approximately $2,800,000. The refinancing generated no cash-out proceeds because the prior loan being retired had a balance of approximately $6,200,000, meaning the net cash from refinancing after payoff and closing costs was negligible.

This analysis was central to the dispute because the limited partner claimant alleged that the sponsor had characterized the refinancing as a non-event for waterfall purposes while simultaneously representing to prospective buyers during the partial sale process that the property's equity position had grown substantially. Our analysis confirmed that while the property had appreciated, no distributable refinancing proceeds had been generated, which supported the sponsor's position on that specific question. However, our parallel waterfall model identified a separate error in the preferred return calculation that resulted in an understatement of the preferred return accrual to the senior limited partner of approximately $187,000 over the hold period.

The Complicating Factors

Preferred Return Compounding Methodology. The most technically contested issue in this matter was whether the 8% preferred return specified in the operating agreement compounded annually or accrued on a simple basis. This is not a trivial distinction. On a $1,500,000 preferred equity commitment held for four years, the difference between an 8% simple accrual and an 8% annually compounded accrual is approximately $98,000 at the four-year mark. When multiple tiers of preferred equity are stacked, as they were here, the compounding methodology affects not just the tier one return but also the point at which the sponsor's promoted interest vests. Sponsors drafting operating agreements frequently prefer simple accrual language because it reduces the carried cost of preferred return. Sophisticated limited partners negotiate for compounding. When the agreement is ambiguous, as it was in this case, the expert's role is to demonstrate what market-standard practice looks like and whether the interpretation applied by the sponsor is consistent with that standard.

Mid-Hold Refinancing and Capital Account Resets. When a joint venture executes a mid-hold refinancing, the question of how refinancing proceeds are characterized, whether as a return of capital, a distribution of profits, or a non-distributable liability, has profound effects on waterfall mechanics. In this engagement, the absence of meaningful cash-out proceeds simplified that question, but the refinancing nonetheless reset the capital account structure in ways that affected how the partial sale proceeds were allocated. Specifically, the new loan's origination fees and prepayment premium on the retired loan were treated by the sponsor as operating expenses that reduced distributable cash, which we analyzed against market-standard treatment of such costs.

The Partial Sale Structure and Pro-Rata Allocation. The venture sold one of two parcels comprising the property, retaining the other. This created a complex question about how to allocate the realized gain, the outstanding preferred return accruals, and the promoted interest calculation across the two components of the property. Market-standard JV agreements address this with specific partial disposition provisions, but the operating agreement at issue was silent on this point. Our analysis modeled three alternative allocation methodologies, based on square footage, NOI contribution, and appraised value, and demonstrated that the sponsor had applied a methodology that, while defensible, was the most favorable to its own promoted interest calculation.

Financing Market Conditions and Sponsor Obligations. A secondary issue in the engagement involved whether the sponsor had satisfied its obligation to use commercially reasonable efforts to obtain financing on terms consistent with market conditions. The limited partner alleged that the original acquisition financing was structured with excessive origination costs and a prepayment structure that unnecessarily constrained the venture's ability to refinance at optimal timing. Our analysis of the acquisition loan terms against comparable financing available in the Racine market at the time of closing addressed this question directly.

The Racine Lender Market

Understanding which lenders were active in Racine at the time of the transactions at issue is essential to evaluating whether the sponsor's financing decisions were commercially reasonable. The Racine lending market is meaningfully different from Milwaukee or Chicago, and expert analysis must reflect those distinctions.

Regional and Community Banks. The most active lenders in Racine commercial real estate are Wisconsin-chartered regional banks and well-capitalized community banks. Several institutions with strong Wisconsin commercial real estate portfolios maintain active lending relationships in Racine County, particularly for industrial, multifamily, and owner-occupied commercial properties. These lenders typically underwrite to 65% LTV for investment properties, 1.20x to 1.25x DSCR, and offer 5- to 10-year fixed or adjustable rate terms with 20- to 25-year amortization. Pricing has generally ranged from 50 to 150 basis points over the corresponding Treasury or SOFR index depending on the borrower relationship, asset quality, and loan size. Recourse requirements are common for loans below $5,000,000.

CMBS Conduit Lenders. CMBS execution is available in Racine for stabilized assets above approximately $5,000,000, though the market is selective. Industrial properties with strong in-place leases and creditworthy tenants can access conduit financing. CMBS lenders typically require 1.25x DSCR, 65% to 70% LTV, and non-recourse structure, which makes them attractive for joint ventures seeking to limit sponsor liability exposure. Spread pricing over Treasuries reflects secondary market status, and Racine assets generally price 20 to 40 basis points wider than comparable Milwaukee assets due to market liquidity considerations.

Life Companies. Life company lenders are active in Racine for institutional-quality industrial assets with long-term lease structures. They offer the most competitive rates, often 30 to 50 basis points below CMBS, but require stronger sponsorship, lower leverage (typically 55% to 60% LTV), and longer lease terms from creditworthy tenants. Their credit box excludes most value-add situations.

Debt Funds and Bridge Lenders. For transitional or value-add assets, debt funds provide short-term floating-rate bridge financing at higher leverage (up to 75% to 80% of cost) but at a significant rate premium, typically SOFR plus 300 to 500 basis points. This is the financing category most relevant to the acquisition phase of value-add industrial strategies in Racine.

What Attorneys Handling Racine CRE Disputes Need to Know

Attorneys litigating joint venture waterfall disputes in commercial real estate need a financing expert who can bridge the gap between the legal language of the operating agreement and the financial mechanics of how capital stacks actually function in the market. An appraiser can tell you what the property was worth. A forensic accountant can reconstruct the financial records. But neither can tell you whether the waterfall calculation methodology used by the sponsor is consistent with market-standard JV practice, whether the financing decisions made during the hold period were commercially reasonable, or whether the promoted interest trigger was structured in a manner consistent with what institutional joint venture partners negotiate.

A qualified CRE financing expert in this context should have active, current transactional experience structuring commercial real estate capital stacks, not just retrospective familiarity with loan documents. The expert needs to be able to testify about what a market-standard term sheet looks like, what lenders in the relevant market were actually offering during the relevant period, and how experienced sponsors and limited partners negotiate preferred return mechanics and promoted interest provisions. Retired bankers often understand credit underwriting but may lack experience with equity waterfall structures from the sponsor's side of the table. Academics may understand theory but cannot speak to current market practice.

Before retaining a financing expert, counsel should gather the complete loan documents for every financing event during the hold period, all term sheets received and rejected, the operating agreement and all amendments, all financial statements and distribution records, the sponsor's underwriting model as presented to investors at inception, and all closing statements from acquisition, refinancing, and any disposition events. The expert's analysis is only as complete as the documentation provided.

At trial or in an arbitration proceeding, the financing expert's testimony serves several functions. It establishes the market-standard framework against which the sponsor's conduct is measured. It quantifies the financial impact of any deviation from that standard. It translates complex waterfall mechanics into terms that a judge or arbitrator without a finance background can follow. And it withstands cross-examination from opposing experts precisely because it is grounded in current transactional experience, not just textbook analysis.

Key Takeaways

  • Preferred return compounding methodology is a high-stakes drafting issue. The difference between simple and compound accrual can represent six-figure discrepancies on typical Racine-scale transactions over a four- to six-year hold period, and ambiguous operating agreement language creates the litigation risk.
  • Mid-hold refinancing events require specific operating agreement provisions. Whether refinancing proceeds trigger waterfall distribution obligations is one of the most commonly disputed questions in JV litigation, and agreements that are silent on this point invite disputes.
  • The DSCR and LTV constraints binding at the time of each financing event are critical context. An expert must reconstruct market conditions at the transaction date, not just current conditions, to evaluate whether the sponsor's financing decisions were commercially reasonable.
  • Partial disposition provisions are frequently omitted from operating agreements. When a venture sells less than all of its assets, the methodology for allocating preferred return accruals and promoted interest triggers across the sold and retained components can dramatically affect each party's economic outcome.
  • Racine industrial fundamentals support real institutional underwriting. The secondary market designation does not mean the asset cannot access competitive financing; it means the expert must accurately characterize the specific lender categories and pricing available in this submarket rather than applying generic national metrics.
  • A CRE financing expert provides analysis that appraisers and accountants cannot. Market-standard JV waterfall mechanics, lender credit box requirements, and commercially reasonable financing conduct are within the specialized expertise of an active capital markets professional, not a valuation or accounting professional.
  • The promoted interest calculation is the most litigation-prone element of any JV agreement. Sponsors have an inherent incentive to interpret promoted interest trigger mechanics in their favor, and the gap between a defensible interpretation and the most favorable interpretation is often where the dispute lives.
  • Early expert retention produces better outcomes for counsel. In a waterfall dispute, the financing expert's analysis shapes discovery strategy, informs settlement valuation

Expert Witness: How Construction Defects Affect CRE Financing in San Antonio, TX

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Construction Defect Impact on Financing

The San Antonio Market Context

San Antonio sits in an interesting position within the Texas commercial real estate landscape. It is not Austin, where capital floods in and cap rates compress to institutional levels on the strength of tech employment. It is not Houston, where energy sector volatility creates cyclical financing dislocations. San Antonio is a large, diversified, military-anchored economy with genuine institutional depth, active regional bank participation, and a multifamily market that has absorbed significant construction supply over the past four years while still maintaining occupancy metrics that support financing. Understanding those fundamentals is essential context for any expert analysis of a financing dispute arising here.

On the multifamily side, San Antonio posted average effective rents in the $1,150 to $1,350 per unit range across Class B and Class C product as of late 2024, with Class A new construction commanding $1,500 to $1,900 depending on submarket. Submarkets like the South Texas Medical Center corridor, Stone Oak, and the near North Side have shown stronger rent retention than secondary suburban locations. Vacancy across the metro has drifted upward from pandemic-era lows toward 8 to 11 percent in submarkets with concentrated new supply, which directly affects stabilized income assumptions lenders use to size loans. For industrial product, San Antonio's proximity to the Laredo port of entry and its role as a logistics node has kept industrial vacancy tight, generally in the 4 to 7 percent range, with net asking rents for Class A distribution space in the $7.00 to $9.50 per square foot range. Retail fundamentals are bifurcated, with well-located grocery-anchored centers performing well and secondary strip centers facing both occupancy pressure and lender skepticism. Office remains challenged, with suburban Class B vacancy exceeding 15 percent in several submarkets, making construction defect disputes involving office assets particularly sensitive from a financing feasibility standpoint.

The financing market here reflects the asset mix. USAA Real Estate, Frost Bank, Broadway Bank, and other locally headquartered institutions maintain genuine CRE lending operations, and the presence of large regional banks with San Antonio offices, including Texas-headquartered regionals and national banks with strong local presences, creates a reasonably competitive lender environment. That said, credit standards tightened materially beginning in mid-2023 following bank regulatory pressure on CRE concentrations, and lenders in this market today apply stricter underwriting to anything with a headline risk, including properties subject to construction defect litigation or carrying known deferred maintenance stemming from alleged defect conditions.

The Engagement

We were retained by litigation counsel representing the defendant in a construction defect dispute involving a mid-size multifamily property in the San Antonio metro area. The property consisted of approximately 180 units across several garden-style buildings, constructed within the prior five years, with alleged defects involving waterproofing failures, improper flashing installation, and building envelope deficiencies that had resulted in documented moisture intrusion in a number of units and common areas. The plaintiff had submitted a damages claim that included both direct repair cost estimates and consequential damages tied to alleged impact on the property's market value.

Counsel retained Commercial Lending Solutions to provide a Rule 26 expert report addressing three specific questions. First, was this property financeable in the current San Antonio lender market given the known defect conditions, and if so, under what terms and constraints? Second, how did the alleged defects affect the property's achievable loan proceeds, specifically its debt service coverage ratio, loan-to-value ceiling, and maximum supportable loan amount? Third, what would the financing profile of an otherwise equivalent defect-free asset look like, and what is the measurable delta between the two? CLS CRE was not engaged to opine on the repair cost estimates themselves or on property valuation, which fell within the appraiser's scope. Our engagement was strictly the financing feasibility and capital markets impact analysis.

The Financial Analysis

The starting point for any commercial real estate financing analysis is a reconstruction of the property's stabilized net operating income. On a property with documented construction defects and moisture intrusion, that reconstruction is not straightforward. Lenders do not simply accept current in-place income as the basis for underwriting. They apply haircuts and adjustments that reflect the risk they perceive in the income stream, the likely cost of remediation, and the uncertainty around future occupancy and expense performance.

For this engagement, we analyzed the property's rent roll, historical vacancy reports, and operating expense history over three years. The property had experienced elevated turnover in affected units, above-average make-ready costs, and a pattern of concession offering to retain tenants in buildings with documented moisture complaints. Our stabilized income analysis produced a gross potential rent figure of approximately $2,850,000 annually, based on the property's 180 units at average effective rents of $1,319 per month. Applying a market vacancy and credit loss factor of 8 percent, consistent with submarket conditions, produced effective gross income of approximately $2,622,000. Operating expenses, including the elevated maintenance costs attributable to the defect conditions, totaled approximately $1,245,000, yielding a current NOI of approximately $1,377,000.

The critical issue is what lenders do with that NOI when they know the property has known construction defect exposure. Conventional agency lenders, Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac execution, will not fund a loan on a property with unresolved structural or building envelope defects without a fully funded repair escrow and a completed engineering inspection confirming the scope of remediation. Non-agency lenders apply similar constraints but price the additional risk into spread. For purposes of establishing a financing baseline, we modeled both a defect-present scenario and a defect-resolved scenario using the same income assumptions, isolating the financing impact attributable specifically to the defect condition.

We applied a debt service coverage ratio floor of 1.25x, which is the current market standard for conventional multifamily financing on non-agency bridge and balance sheet loans in this risk tier. Agency execution for stabilized product typically floors at 1.20x, but the defect cloud removes agency eligibility, shifting the analysis to balance sheet and debt fund lending where 1.25x is the standard minimum, and many lenders in the current environment are requiring 1.30x on anything with elevated risk characteristics. At a 1.25x DSCR floor, with a note rate of 7.50 percent on a 30-year amortization schedule, the maximum annual debt service supportable on $1,377,000 of NOI is approximately $1,101,600. That translates to a maximum supportable loan of approximately $14,850,000.

For the defect-free equivalent asset, we modeled the same property with normalized vacancy at 6 percent, no above-market maintenance concessions or turnover costs, and a normalized expense ratio. The defect-free NOI came to approximately $1,512,000. At the same rate and DSCR floor, the defect-free maximum supportable loan based on income alone is approximately $16,300,000. The income-driven loan proceeds gap attributable to the defect conditions is therefore approximately $1,450,000.

The LTV analysis compounds this gap. For the defect-present property, lenders with knowledge of the defect exposure apply LTV constraints in the 60 to 65 percent range, compared to 70 to 75 percent for equivalent stabilized product. If the appraised value of the subject property in its defect-present condition is $20,000,000, a 65 percent LTV ceiling yields a maximum loan of $13,000,000, which is below the DSCR-constrained ceiling. On the defect-free asset at $22,500,000 appraised value with a 72 percent LTV allowance, the LTV-constrained ceiling would be $16,200,000. The practical maximum supportable loan under the defect-present scenario is therefore approximately $13,000,000, constrained by LTV rather than DSCR, against a defect-free ceiling of approximately $16,200,000. The financing gap documented by this methodology is approximately $3,200,000.

The Complicating Factors

Lender Knowledge and Disclosure Obligations. When construction defect litigation is active or has been disclosed, any lender performing proper due diligence will discover it through title search, PACER search, or borrower disclosure. The moment a lender's credit committee knows the property is in active litigation, it triggers a series of credit holds that go beyond simple underwriting adjustments. Many lenders will issue a conditional approval requiring the litigation to be resolved, a specific repair escrow funded from loan proceeds or equity, and a third-party engineering certification of remediation completion before funding. Others will simply decline to lend on the property until the litigation is resolved. This effectively takes the conventional financing market largely offline for a property in active dispute, forcing borrowers into a narrower set of alternatives that carry meaningfully higher pricing.

Repair Escrow Sizing and Its Impact on Net Proceeds. Even for lenders willing to proceed, the requirement to fund a repair escrow at closing reduces the net proceeds available to the borrower. If a lender requires a funded repair escrow of $1,800,000 to cover the estimated remediation scope, and the gross loan is $13,000,000, the borrower's available net proceeds after escrow holdback are $11,200,000. In a refinance or acquisition context, that shortfall has direct economic consequences that are quantifiable and financeable-gap measurable.

Insurance Market Complications. Commercial property insurance carriers are increasingly excluding coverage for pre-existing defect conditions on properties with known litigation history. In a market like San Antonio, where windstorm and hail exposure already creates insurance pricing pressure, a property carrying a construction defect cloud may face either exclusions on the defect-related perils or higher premiums that directly increase operating expenses, reduce NOI, and further compress the maximum supportable loan. Lenders require insurance as a condition of funding, and if the property cannot obtain adequate coverage, lenders will not close.

Reduced Lender Universe and Pricing Premium. The combination of active litigation, building envelope defect exposure, and insurance complications reduces the available lender universe for a San Antonio multifamily property to balance sheet lenders, debt funds, and hard money sources. Compared to the 7.50 percent rate applied in our baseline scenario for a conventional balance sheet lender, debt funds in this risk tier are currently pricing at 9.00 to 10.50 percent, and hard money or bridge capital can price 11.00 percent or higher with origination fees of 1.5 to 3 points. That 150 to 300 basis point spread premium has a direct, calculable impact on carrying costs over the period while the litigation remains unresolved.

The San Antonio Lender Market

San Antonio's lender market for multifamily and commercial real estate is meaningfully deeper than many comparable Texas secondary markets, but depth does not eliminate the credit constraints described above. It simply means the borrower has more institutions to approach before receiving the same answer.

Local community banks and Texas-chartered regionals are active in the sub-$15,000,000 loan range for San Antonio CRE and carry genuine familiarity with local market conditions. However, their credit committees are under heightened CRE concentration scrutiny from FDIC examiners, and a property with known litigation exposure represents exactly the kind of watched credit that triggers classification concerns. These lenders can and do find ways to structure around construction defect issues when the borrower relationship is strong and the repair scope is well-defined, but they require personal guaranty, funded repair escrows, and often a reduced advance rate compared to their standard guidelines.

Agency lenders, meaning Fannie Mae DUS lenders and Freddie Mac Optigo lenders with San Antonio market presence, are simply not available for properties with unresolved construction defect litigation. Both agencies require property condition assessments that would surface the defect conditions, and both agencies have specific eligibility restrictions that exclude properties with unresolved structural or building envelope deficiencies from their programs. This removes what is typically the lowest-cost, highest-proceeds financing option from the table entirely.

Debt funds and bridge lenders, many of which are national platforms with San Antonio market activity, represent the most likely source of financing for a defect-impaired property. These lenders are accustomed to transitional and value-add risk and can structure repair holdbacks, progress-draw remediation escrows, and performance hurdles tied to defect resolution. Their pricing reflects that flexibility, with rates in the ranges previously described, but they can close. Their underwriting requires a detailed scope of work, a contractor with bonding capacity, and a realistic timeline to remediation completion that feeds into the loan's exit analysis.

What Attorneys Handling San Antonio CRE Disputes Need to Know

A CRE financing expert fills a specific and non-overlapping evidentiary role that is distinct from both the appraiser and the construction defect expert. Appraisers determine value. Construction experts determine cause, scope, and repair cost. Neither is qualified to testify about how lenders actually underwrite a property with known defect exposure, what the measurable impact on loan proceeds is, what the pricing premium in the capital markets is for a defect-impaired asset, or what the financing feasibility of the property is under current market conditions. Those questions require someone who has actually originated, structured, and closed commercial real estate loans in the current lender environment, with direct lender relationships and current knowledge of credit box constraints.

Attorneys should retain a CRE financing expert when any of the following questions are in play in the litigation: What was the maximum supportable loan on this property? What were the borrower's realistic financing alternatives? What is the measurable financing gap between the defect-present and defect-free property? What would a reasonable lender have required before funding this loan? What is the cost to the borrower of being confined to higher-cost capital sources due to the defect condition? What carrying cost damages flow from delayed refinancing or sale caused by the litigation cloud?

Counsel should distinguish between a CRE financing expert and a retired banker. A banker who underwrote loans ten years ago at a single institution does not have current market knowledge, current lender relationship context, or current credit box familiarity. A qualified CRE financing expert is actively originating loans in the current market, has relationships across the lender universe, and can speak to what lenders are actually doing today, not what they did in a prior rate environment. At CLS CRE, our approximately $1 billion in transaction volume and 1,000 plus active lender relationships across 50 states means our expert analysis reflects live market conditions, not academic theory.

Before engaging a CRE financing expert, counsel should gather: the complete rent roll and three years of operating statements, the property condition assessment or engineering inspection reports, all lender correspondence including any prior loan applications, term sheets, or declinations, the title report identifying any litigation holds or mechanic's liens, the insurance policies and any coverage declination correspondence, and the construction contract and warranty documentation. These materials allow the expert to conduct a complete financing feasibility analysis rather than a conceptual one, and that specificity is what makes the expert testimony credible and useful at trial.

Key Takeaways

  • Construction defects measurably reduce maximum supportable loan proceeds through two independent mechanisms: NOI compression from elevated vacancies, concessions, and maintenance costs; and LTV haircuts applied by lenders who discount collateral value on defect-impaired assets. Both mechanisms must be quantified separately and then compared against a defect-free baseline.
  • Active construction defect litigation removes agency financing eligibility entirely, which is not a technicality but a substantial economic consequence. Agency execution represents the lowest cost, highest proceeds capital source for stabilized multifamily. Losing that execution forces borrowers into debt fund and bridge capital that may be 150 to 300 basis points more expensive.
  • Lender-required repair escrows reduce net proceeds below the gross loan amount, creating a compounding proceeds gap that is separate from and in addition to the underwriting-driven proceeds reduction. Both components must be quantified to accurately measure financing impact.
  • The San Antonio lender market has genuine depth, but that depth does not insulate a defect-impaired property from credit constraints. Local community banks, Texas regionals, and national balance sheet lenders all apply consistent restrictions to properties with known litigation exposure, regardless of their general appetite for San Antonio CRE.
  • A CRE financing expert is not a substitute for an appraiser, and the two roles should not be collapsed into one. Financing feasibility and financing proceeds impact require capital markets expertise. Value determination requires appraisal methodology. Counsel should engage both for a fully developed damages case.
  • Carrying cost damages are quantifiable and often underestimated. When a construction defect cloud prevents a borrower from refinancing at a conventional rate and forces them into bridge capital for 18 to 36 months while litigation proceeds, the interest rate premium over that period represents a discrete, calculable economic harm that an expert can quantify and support with market evidence.

Expert Witness: Ground Lease Financing Disputes and Analysis in Elgin, IL

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Ground Lease Financing Complexity

The Elgin Market Context

Elgin, Illinois sits at the intersection of Kane and Cook counties, roughly 35 miles northwest of downtown Chicago along the Fox River corridor. It is not a secondary market in the dismissive sense of that term. Elgin has approximately 115,000 residents, a significant industrial and manufacturing base anchored by the I-90 tollway corridor, and a commercial real estate ecosystem that includes institutional-grade industrial product, neighborhood retail, mixed-use development along the riverfront, and a growing multifamily pipeline responding to affordability pressures that have pushed renters outward from core Chicago submarkets.

For ground lease disputes specifically, the Elgin market presents a distinctive profile. Industrial land along the Route 20 and I-90 corridors carries meaningful underlying value, with functional industrial vacancy in the broader Kane County submarket running in the 4 to 6 percent range as of recent quarters, a tight condition that sustains land values and keeps development pressure active. Net asking rents for bulk industrial in this corridor have stabilized in the $6.50 to $8.50 per square foot range on a triple-net basis after the aggressive escalation of 2021 and 2022, which is directly relevant to how a lender underwrites the leasehold income stream sitting on top of a ground lease structure. Retail fundamentals in Elgin are more bifurcated: well-located neighborhood anchored centers perform, while secondary strip product faces the same softening seen nationally. Ground leases in Elgin's commercial fabric appear most frequently in fast-food and QSR pads, net-leased retail, and increasingly in industrial development where landowners have chosen to retain fee ownership rather than sell outright.

The municipal environment matters here as well. Elgin's planning and zoning framework, combined with Kane County's land use overlay in unincorporated pockets, creates a ground lease landscape where lease terms, permitted use restrictions, and reversion rights vary considerably from deal to deal. That variability is precisely what generates financing disputes: two ground leases on superficially similar Elgin commercial properties can have dramatically different financeable values based entirely on their structural terms.

The Engagement

Counsel representing the lender in a commercial mortgage foreclosure action retained Commercial Lending Solutions to serve as a financing expert witness. The asset at the center of the dispute was a net-leased commercial property in the Elgin submarket, improved with a single-tenant retail building. The borrower held a leasehold interest under a long-term ground lease; the fee interest was held by a separate entity under common beneficial ownership, though not under common legal ownership for purposes of the mortgage transaction.

The core legal and financial questions presented to our firm fell into three categories. First, counsel needed an expert analysis of whether the ground lease structure, as written, met market standards for commercial mortgage financing at the time the loan was originated. Second, the engagement required an assessment of whether the lender's underwriting of the leasehold interest was consistent with or deviated from prevailing industry standards for this asset class. Third, and critically for the litigation, counsel needed our expert opinion on whether the subordination, non-disturbance, and attornment provisions in the ground lease, the ones actually present in the executed document rather than a market-standard form, were sufficient to make the leasehold mortgageable under the standards that a reasonable institutional lender would have required at the time of closing.

We were provided with the ground lease itself, the loan documents including the leasehold mortgage and title insurance commitment, the lender's credit memorandum, an appraisal of the leasehold interest, and the borrower's operating statements for the prior three years. Our analysis was conducted under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 26 standards and resulted in a written expert report with supporting financial schedules.

The Financial Analysis

Underwriting a leasehold mortgage begins with the same discipline as any commercial mortgage, but with an additional layer of structural analysis that goes beyond income and value. The income stream must be analyzed both as a standalone NOI metric and as a function of the lease structure sitting beneath it. In this engagement, the subject property generated a net operating income, after deducting ground rent from gross NOI, of approximately $182,000 annually. That post-ground-rent NOI is the correct income figure for leasehold mortgage underwriting because the ground rent obligation is senior to the leasehold mortgage in the cash flow waterfall. Any lender who underwrote this deal using gross NOI before ground rent deduction would have been applying a fundamentally incorrect methodology.

Market standard DSCR thresholds for leasehold financing in this asset category run 1.25x at minimum for conventional commercial lenders, with many institutional lenders requiring 1.30x given the additional structural risk inherent in leasehold as opposed to fee simple collateral. At a 1.25x DSCR on $182,000 of net NOI, the maximum supportable annual debt service is $145,600. Applying a 30-year amortization schedule and an interest rate assumption consistent with the origination environment for this loan type, approximately 6.75 percent on a 5-year fixed term, that debt service figure supports a maximum loan amount of approximately $1,985,000. If the lender also imposed a 65 percent LTV ceiling, which is market standard for leasehold mortgage financing on retail collateral, the appraised leasehold value would need to support at least $3,053,846 to reach that loan amount on an LTV basis alone. The interplay between DSCR and LTV constraints is the binding constraint analysis that expert testimony must address with precision.

In this engagement, the appraisal of the leasehold interest produced a value conclusion that, on the surface, appeared to support the loan amount advanced. However, our analysis identified a material flaw in the appraisal methodology: the appraiser had valued the leasehold interest assuming a subordinated fee interest, meaning assuming the ground lessor had subordinated its fee to the leasehold mortgage. The ground lease at issue did not contain an operative subordination by the fee owner. That structural distinction changes the leasehold value calculation materially because, without fee subordination, a lender's foreclosure of the leasehold mortgage produces only a leasehold interest, not a fee interest, and the lender's collateral position in foreclosure is substantially weaker. Adjusting the leasehold valuation to reflect the actual unsubordinated fee structure produced a leasehold value approximately 22 to 28 percent lower than the appraiser's conclusion, bringing the LTV well above the 65 percent threshold and rendering the loan outside market standards at origination.

The Complicating Factors

Subordination: The Fee Owner's Position and Its Impact on Lender Collateral

In a ground lease financing, subordination refers to the fee owner agreeing that its fee interest is junior to the leasehold mortgage. From a lender's perspective, a subordinated ground lease is far preferable because foreclosure of the mortgage can result in acquisition of the fee, not merely the leasehold. However, fee subordination exposes the ground lessor to a total loss of its land in the event of borrower default, which is why many sophisticated ground lessors refuse to subordinate. The market has developed a middle path: the unsubordinated ground lease with lender-protective provisions, including notice and cure rights, new lease rights, and non-disturbance protection. When a ground lease contains none of these provisions and the fee is not subordinated, the lender's collateral is a depreciating asset with a fixed termination date and no right to recover the underlying land. Very few institutional lenders will advance funds against such a structure at conventional LTV ratios.

The ground lease in this Elgin dispute fell into an ambiguous middle category. The fee was not subordinated, but the ground lease contained language purporting to provide certain protections to leasehold mortgagees. Our analysis focused on whether those provisions, as drafted, actually delivered the protections a market-standard lender would require, or whether they were cosmetically similar but operationally deficient.

Non-Disturbance: What It Actually Requires

Non-disturbance in a ground lease context means the fee owner agrees that, if the ground lease is terminated due to a default by the tenant/borrower, the leasehold mortgagee will not be disturbed in its possession provided it cures the default and assumes the obligations. A legally enforceable non-disturbance provision requires specificity: it must define the notice period to the mortgagee, the cure period available to the mortgagee independent of the tenant's cure period, the mortgagee's right to acquire or designate a new tenant, and the conditions under which the fee owner can proceed to terminate the ground lease notwithstanding a mortgagee cure effort. Vague non-disturbance language that simply states "the rights of any leasehold mortgagee shall not be disturbed" without operational specificity is not considered market standard and will not satisfy the requirements of institutional lenders or their legal counsel at closing.

The provision in the subject ground lease stated that the fee owner would recognize the leasehold mortgagee's rights but contained a cure period that ran concurrently with, rather than independently of, the tenant's cure period. That concurrent cure structure is a critical deficiency. If the tenant burns through its cure period without remediation and the concurrent mortgagee cure period expires simultaneously, the lender loses its ability to cure and the ground lease terminates. Market standard requires the mortgagee cure period to run independently, typically 30 to 60 days after expiration of the tenant's cure period, specifically to preserve the lender's options in a default scenario.

Attornment and New Lease Rights

Attornment in this context is the agreement by the ground lessor to recognize the leasehold mortgagee, or a party acquiring the leasehold through foreclosure, as the new tenant under the ground lease. Without an attornment obligation, a fee owner could argue after foreclosure that the ground lease has terminated and the foreclosure purchaser has no right to possession or to the benefit of the lease economics. New lease rights, sometimes called the "mortgagee's right to a new lease," go further: they obligate the fee owner, upon termination of the original ground lease due to tenant default, to enter into a new ground lease with the leasehold mortgagee on the same economic terms as the original ground lease. This provision is the leasehold lender's ultimate backstop. Its absence does not automatically make a ground lease unfinanceable, but it substantially narrows the lender universe to those with higher risk tolerance and typically drives up the cost of capital by 50 to 100 basis points or more.

The Elgin Lender Market

For ground lease financing on commercial assets in the Elgin submarket, the active lender universe segments into three meaningful categories. Community and regional banks headquartered in the Chicago metropolitan area, including institutions with meaningful Kane and Cook County market presence, represent the most accessible capital source for leasehold mortgages on properties in the $1 million to $5 million range. These lenders underwrite to relationship-driven credit standards and may accept ground lease structures that fall short of institutional quality, provided the borrower relationship, deposit relationship, and personal guarantee support are strong. However, their rates typically run 50 to 75 basis points above CMBS or life company pricing, and their loan terms are shorter, often 3 to 5 year balloons against 20-year amortization.

CMBS lenders represent the next tier and are relevant for Elgin assets above approximately $3 million in loan size. CMBS execution requires strict adherence to market-standard ground lease provisions. A CMBS lender's legal counsel will review the ground lease against a checklist of required provisions, and deficiencies in the SNDA framework are deal-killers in the CMBS execution channel. The secondary market investor base that purchases CMBS bonds requires standardized collateral structures, and a ground lease with deficient non-disturbance language or concurrent cure periods will not achieve rating agency approval for securitization.

Life insurance companies and debt funds round out the market. Life companies active in suburban Chicago markets are selective about ground lease collateral and typically require the strongest SNDA protections of any lender category. Debt funds, including bridge lenders, offer more structural flexibility but price that flexibility aggressively, with rates frequently 200 to 300 basis points above conventional lenders and loan terms of 12 to 36 months. In a dispute context, the fact that only a debt fund or hard money lender would have advanced against a particular ground lease structure is itself evidence of elevated credit risk and substandard collateral quality.

What Attorneys Handling Elgin CRE Disputes Need to Know

A CRE financing expert witness answers questions that appraisers, accountants, and general real estate consultants cannot adequately address. An appraiser determines value. A CRE financing expert determines whether and on what terms a specific asset, with a specific legal structure, could have been financed in the market at a specific point in time. Those are fundamentally different questions, and conflating them is a common source of error in commercial real estate litigation.

Retain a CRE financing expert when your matter involves any of the following: whether a loan was reasonably obtainable by a borrower at a specific time and on specific terms, whether a lender's underwriting met or deviated from market standards, whether a specific collateral structure (including ground leases, easements, partial interests, or air rights) was mortgageable under prevailing institutional standards, what the reasonable financing alternatives were for a project whose financing fell through, or what a borrower's damages are measured by when financing was wrongfully denied or improperly structured.

A qualified CRE financing expert is not a retired banker offering general observations about credit culture. Qualifications that matter in this context include active current practice as a commercial mortgage originator or broker, documented transaction volume across multiple lender categories, and the ability to articulate specific lender underwriting criteria by product type, loan size, and market. An expert who originates commercial mortgages actively understands the current credit box, not the credit box of ten years ago. Market standards in commercial mortgage lending shift materially with interest rate cycles, regulatory changes, and capital market conditions, and testimony about market standards must be grounded in contemporaneous practice.

Before the expert engagement begins, counsel should gather the following: the complete loan documents including the ground lease, all title commitments and endorsements, the lender's credit memorandum or loan committee approval, any appraisal and its supporting work file, all borrower financial statements and rent rolls reviewed at underwriting, all correspondence between borrower and lender during the origination and default period, and any lender communications regarding the ground lease structure specifically. The more complete the document production, the more precise and defensible the expert's analysis will be.

At trial or in a contested plan confirmation hearing, CRE financing expert testimony is most effective when it is grounded in specific, documented market data rather than general assertions of industry custom. Our expert reports include lender-specific underwriting guidelines where publicly available, transaction comparables from our own origination activity, and quantitative financial models that can be presented to the trier of fact as demonstrative exhibits. Testimony that can be anchored to a specific DSCR calculation, a specific LTV constraint, and a specific written lender requirement is substantially more persuasive than testimony that relies on the expert's generalized experience.

Key Takeaways

  • Ground lease financing requires NOI analysis on a post-ground-rent basis. Any underwriting or appraisal that applies loan sizing metrics to gross NOI without first deducting the ground rent obligation has used a fundamentally incorrect methodology and likely overstates supportable loan proceeds.
  • The SNDA framework is not boilerplate. Subordination, non-disturbance, and attornment provisions in a ground lease must be operationally specific and independently enforceable to satisfy institutional lender standards. Vague or concurrent-cure language fails market standards for CMBS and most bank lenders.
  • Fee subordination and new lease rights are the most powerful leasehold lender protections, and their presence or absence determines whether fee-quality or leasehold-discount valuation applies. The difference can be 20 to 30 percent of leasehold value, which directly affects LTV compliance and loan supportability conclusions.
  • Lender category matters for the expert analysis. A ground lease structure that fails CMBS standards may be acceptable to a relationship community bank on enhanced credit terms. The relevant question for litigation purposes is what lender category would have been the appropriate and reasonably accessible capital source for the specific borrower and asset at the relevant time.

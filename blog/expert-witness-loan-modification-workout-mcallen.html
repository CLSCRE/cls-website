Expert Witness: Commercial Loan Modification Standards in McAllen, TX

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Loan Modification and Workout Dispute

The McAllen Market Context

McAllen, Texas sits at the center of one of the most economically dynamic corridors in the United States. As part of the Hidalgo County metropolitan statistical area, McAllen anchors a bi-national economy tied directly to the Laredo-Monterrey-Nuevo Leon manufacturing and logistics axis. That context matters enormously in commercial real estate workout disputes, because lenders underwriting McAllen assets often do so with assumptions rooted in national models that do not fully capture the market's unique demand drivers, its sensitivity to cross-border trade volumes, and its distinct tenant credit profile.

From a fundamentals standpoint, McAllen's retail and industrial sectors have historically outperformed similar-sized Texas metros. The McAllen-Mission-Edinburg corridor has sustained retail sales per capita figures well above national averages for years, driven in part by Mexican national shoppers who cross into the U.S. specifically to purchase goods. That dynamic creates a retail occupancy profile that national lenders frequently misread. A retail strip center in McAllen with strong historical occupancy may look underwritten at 90% occupancy against a national model built for 85%, but local market participants understand that the effective demand floor in McAllen's primary retail nodes is structurally higher than peer markets.

Industrial fundamentals have tightened significantly since 2020. The nearshoring trend, driven by supply chain restructuring post-pandemic, has pushed industrial vacancy in the McAllen submarket below 5% in several nodes. Net asking rents for Class B industrial space along the U.S. 83 and U.S. 281 corridors have risen materially, with some properties seeing 20% to 30% rent growth over a three-year period. That rent trajectory is directly relevant in any workout dispute: a lender who treated a 2019 rent roll as representative of current stabilized income may have severely underestimated the collateral's income potential when evaluating modification terms in 2022 or 2023.

Multifamily in McAllen is a different story. Cap rates in McAllen multifamily have historically compressed more slowly than in Austin or Dallas, reflecting a lower median income base and a more price-sensitive renter population. Workforce housing and garden-style apartment communities dominate the inventory. A lender applying a 5.0% cap rate assumption to McAllen Class B multifamily in a workout analysis would be applying the wrong benchmark. Market cap rates in McAllen multifamily have generally ranged from 6.0% to 7.5% depending on vintage, unit mix, and submarket location, and that distinction can change a workout analysis by millions of dollars in calculated collateral value.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing a commercial property owner in a dispute arising from a loan modification and workout process involving a mixed-use commercial asset in the McAllen metro. The asset included ground-floor retail space with upper-floor office and service-oriented tenancy, a profile common in McAllen's downtown and midtown commercial corridors. The loan had originally been originated at a community bank and later participated out to a regional institution, which complicated the modification process and the chain of communication between the borrower and the decision-making lender.

Counsel retained CLS CRE to address three core questions. First, whether the lender's conduct during the modification negotiation process was consistent with industry standards governing commercial real estate workouts. Second, whether the modification terms ultimately proposed by the lender, specifically the debt service coverage requirements, the extended amortization structure, and the equity injection demand, were commercially reasonable given the property's documented cash flow and the state of the McAllen lending market at the time of the dispute. Third, whether the lender's refusal to consider a forbearance extension was consistent with how institutional and regional lenders typically respond to borrowers demonstrating documented financial hardship tied to identifiable and temporary market disruption.

Our engagement required us to analyze the property's historical and projected net operating income, reconstruct the underwriting logic that a reasonably competent lender would have applied in the McAllen market, survey the modification and forbearance practices of comparable lenders active in South Texas at the relevant time, and prepare a written expert report suitable for use in litigation under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 26.

The Financial Analysis

The first step in any loan modification dispute analysis is reconstructing what the collateral actually supports at the time of the workout negotiation, not what it supported at origination. In this engagement, we analyzed three years of historical operating statements, a current rent roll, and in-place lease abstracts to build a defensible estimate of stabilized net operating income.

The property generated gross potential rent of approximately $1,140,000 annually across its retail and office components. Applying a market vacancy factor of 8% based on comparable McAllen mixed-use properties at the relevant time, effective gross income was approximately $1,049,000. After deducting documented operating expenses including property taxes at Hidalgo County rates, insurance, property management fees at 5% of effective gross income, and maintenance reserves consistent with the property's age and condition, the stabilized net operating income was calculated at approximately $714,000.

Industry standard for commercial real estate loan underwriting requires a minimum debt service coverage ratio of 1.20x to 1.25x, with institutional lenders and CMBS conduit programs typically requiring 1.25x at the time of stabilization. For a community or regional bank in McAllen underwriting a workout or modified loan, 1.20x is a defensible floor given the relationship-based nature of regional lending, but the lender must demonstrate that it actually applied a consistent DSCR threshold rather than an arbitrary one selected post-dispute to justify rejection of the modification request.

Using a 1.25x DSCR requirement, the maximum supportable annual debt service on this collateral was $571,200. At a modified interest rate of 6.75%, which was consistent with prevailing rates for community bank commercial real estate loans in South Texas at the time of the dispute, and using a 25-year amortization schedule (standard for mixed-use commercial assets at this loan balance level), the maximum supportable loan was approximately $6,850,000.

The lender's own appraisal at the time of the workout placed the property value at $8,200,000. At a maximum supportable loan of $6,850,000, the implied LTV was 83.5%, which exceeded the 75% LTV threshold typical for community bank commercial real estate lending. However, the outstanding loan balance at the time of the dispute was $5,600,000, representing a 68.3% LTV against the lender's own appraised value. This is a critical data point. The lender's position was overcollateralized by any reasonable LTV standard, and the NOI fully covered modified debt service at a 1.25x standard. Under those conditions, the lender's refusal to modify and its demand for a substantial equity paydown was not consistent with commercially reasonable workout practice.

We further calculated that the lender's proposed modification terms, which required the borrower to reduce the outstanding balance to $4,200,000 through a cash paydown, would have required a capital infusion of $1,400,000 from a borrower experiencing documented cash flow disruption. That equity injection demand, assessed against the existing collateral coverage and income support, was inconsistent with how lenders in comparable positions have historically structured modifications on performing-adjacent assets in secondary Texas markets.

The Complicating Factors

Loan Participation Structure and Communication Failures. When a loan is participated between a lead bank and one or more participating institutions, the borrower typically communicates only with the lead bank, but the lead bank may lack unilateral authority to approve a modification above a certain threshold. In this engagement, the participation agreement between the originating bank and the regional institution required unanimous consent for any modification affecting loan term, rate, or collateral requirements. That consent process introduced a months-long delay into the workout negotiation, during which the borrower's default deepened and additional default interest accrued. From a standards-of-care standpoint, the failure of the lead bank to disclose the participation structure and its constraints to the borrower at the outset of modification discussions is a material deviation from workout best practices. Lenders in participation arrangements have an obligation to inform borrowers of process realities so that borrowers can make informed decisions, including whether to seek refinancing, pursue a note sale, or inject capital.

Cross-Border Revenue Sensitivity. McAllen's mixed-use and retail assets have income streams that are partially dependent on Mexican national consumer activity and, in office scenarios, on service-sector tenants whose business volume correlates with cross-border trade. During periods of peso devaluation or border crossing disruptions, tenant sales can drop materially. A lender evaluating a McAllen workout who does not understand this dynamic and applies a straight-line income stabilization assumption will produce a flawed underwriting analysis. In this engagement, the lender's workout team applied national retail income benchmarks without adjusting for the specific demand drivers of the McAllen submarket. That methodological failure produced an income estimate that was approximately 12% below the stabilized figure we derived using market-specific data, which in turn produced an artificially low maximum supportable loan figure that the lender used to justify its equity injection demand.

Hidalgo County Property Tax Variability. Property taxes in Hidalgo County are subject to appraisal district reassessment cycles that can produce significant year-over-year changes in the tax burden on commercial assets. In a workout analysis, a lender must use a defensible estimate of stabilized property taxes, not a single year's tax bill that may reflect a temporary assessment anomaly. In this engagement, the lender's underwriting used a tax figure from a year in which the property had received a partial exemption on a successful protest, then projected that figure forward without adjustment. The corrected tax figure, based on the property's assessed value and the Hidalgo County rate schedule at the time of the workout, was approximately $34,000 higher annually, which impacted NOI and DSCR. The expert analysis must identify and correct for these methodological choices, because they are often not accidental.

CMBS Legacy Loan Complications. Although this loan was not in a CMBS trust, a significant portion of commercial real estate litigation in McAllen involves assets that were originally financed through CMBS conduit programs and are now managed by special servicers. Special servicers operate under pooling and servicing agreements that create rigid constraints on modification authority, timelines, and approved workout structures. Attorneys handling McAllen disputes involving CMBS collateral need to understand that special servicer conduct must be evaluated against the PSA's requirements, not just against general workout practice standards.

The McAllen Lender Market

Understanding which lenders are actually active in McAllen at any given time is essential to evaluating whether a borrower's claim that alternative financing was unavailable is credible, and to establishing whether the lender's modification terms were commercially reasonable relative to refinancing alternatives.

Community and Regional Banks. The McAllen market has a robust community bank presence, with several institutions headquartered in the Rio Grande Valley that maintain deep knowledge of local commercial real estate fundamentals. These lenders typically underwrite to 70% to 75% LTV on commercial assets, require 1.20x DSCR, and price at spreads of 200 to 275 basis points over the applicable index for mixed-use and retail assets. Relationship lending is the norm, and these institutions generally have more flexibility in workout situations than national lenders, subject to their own regulatory capital constraints.

Texas-Chartered State Banks and Credit Unions. Several larger Texas-chartered banks maintain commercial real estate lending programs in McAllen and the broader Hidalgo County market. Their credit boxes are somewhat more standardized than local community banks, but they remain more flexible than national institutions on property type and market-specific underwriting. Credit unions with commercial lending authority under NCUA guidelines are also active in McAllen, particularly for owner-occupied commercial real estate in the $1,000,000 to $5,000,000 range.

SBA Lenders. For owner-occupied commercial real estate, SBA 504 and 7(a) programs are widely used in McAllen. Several Preferred Lender Program lenders maintain active McAllen portfolios. SBA loans are not typically the refinancing vehicle in a workout scenario unless the underlying business qualifies and the collateral is owner-occupied, but they are relevant to assessing the available financing universe for a borrower whose workout was rejected.

Debt Funds and Bridge Lenders. For transitional assets or situations where conventional bank financing is unavailable due to occupancy or cash flow issues, several non-bank bridge lenders have provided capital in the McAllen market. These lenders typically price at 400 to 600 basis points over SOFR, require 65% to 70% LTV, and impose aggressive exit fee structures. Their existence in the market is relevant to the question of whether a borrower had any reasonable alternative to accepting the lender's modification terms.

What Attorneys Handling McAllen CRE Disputes Need to Know

The most important thing I can tell attorneys evaluating a McAllen commercial real estate loan modification dispute is this: a real estate appraiser and a commercial real estate financing expert are not interchangeable. An appraiser determines value. A financing expert determines what a reasonably competent lender would have done with that value in a specific market context, and whether the lender's actual conduct was consistent with industry standards. Those are fundamentally different questions, and courts increasingly recognize that both types of expert testimony may be necessary in a complex CRE workout dispute.

When to retain a CRE financing expert. Counsel should consider retaining a financing expert whenever the dispute turns on: whether modification or forbearance terms were commercially reasonable; whether the lender's underwriting during the workout was methodologically sound; whether the borrower had viable refinancing alternatives that a cooperative lender could have facilitated; whether the lender's acceleration or foreclosure decision was premature given the collateral's cash flow; or whether a receivership or bankruptcy reorganization plan's treatment of secured debt is feasible.

What a CRE financing expert can establish that an appraiser cannot. An appraiser can tell you what the property was worth. A financing expert can tell you what loan the property would support, which lenders would have provided it, what terms those lenders would have required, whether the lender's DSCR analysis was consistent with market practice, whether the equity injection demanded was commercially justified, and whether the lender's workout timeline and process met industry standards of care. These are capital markets questions, not valuation questions.

Qualifications that matter. The qualified CRE financing expert in a McAllen workout dispute should have active transaction experience in commercial real estate capital markets, not just historical banking experience. A retired bank examiner or a former community bank loan officer may understand credit culture but may lack the lender market breadth and current market knowledge needed to testify credibly about what a range of lenders would have done in a given situation. CLS CRE maintains relationships with over 1,000 lenders across all capital sources and has closed approximately $1,000,000,000 in transaction volume, which provides the current market knowledge base that expert testimony requires.

Information counsel should gather before the expert engagement. Before retaining a financing expert, counsel should assemble: all loan documents including the original note, deed of trust, loan agreement, and any participation or syndication agreements; three years of property operating statements; all correspondence between the borrower and lender during the modification and workout period; the lender's internal credit memoranda if obtainable through discovery; any appraisals commissioned by either party; and all proposed modification term sheets, whether accepted, rejected, or withdrawn.

Use of expert testimony at trial or in a plan hearing. In a contested loan modification dispute, the financing expert's role at trial is to establish the objective financial standard against which the lender's conduct is measured. In a Chapter 11 plan confirmation hearing, the financing expert can testify to the feasibility of the reorganization plan's debt service assumptions and the availability of exit financing. In both contexts, the expert must be able to defend every assumption in the analysis and articulate why each assumption reflects market practice rather than advocacy.

Key Takeaways

  • McAllen's market-specific income drivers require local expertise. Any workout analysis that applies national retail or mixed-use benchmarks without adjusting for the bi-national demand structure of the McAllen economy is methodologically flawed and should be challenged.
  • DSCR and LTV must be analyzed together. A lender who demands an equity paydown on an asset that already satisfies the maximum supportable loan calculation at a standard 1.20x to 1.25x DSCR cannot claim that demand is commercially reasonable without additional justification grounded in documented credit policy.
  • Loan participation structure is a material fact in workout disputes. When a participation agreement constrains the lead lender's modification authority, that fact must be disclosed to the borrower promptly and cannot be used as a post-hoc justification for inaction or delay.

Expert Witness: Ground Lease Financing Disputes and Analysis in Trenton, NJ

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Ground Lease Financing Complexity

The Trenton Market Context

Trenton sits at a genuine crossroads in New Jersey commercial real estate, and that positioning matters enormously when you are litigating a ground lease financing dispute. The city is the state capital, which creates a specific and somewhat unusual demand profile: government-related office tenancy, institutional users, and non-profit occupiers that do not behave like typical private-sector tenants. That distinction becomes directly relevant when a lender is trying to underwrite a leasehold interest, because the creditworthiness, renewal probability, and lease structure of a government or quasi-governmental tenant looks very different from a private corporate tenant under conventional underwriting models.

The broader Mercer County submarket surrounding Trenton reflects several competing forces. Industrial vacancy across the I-295 corridor near Trenton has tightened meaningfully over the past several years, tracking the broader New Jersey logistics and last-mile distribution story. Asking rents for functional warehouse and flex space in this corridor have climbed from the low-to-mid single digits per square foot in prior cycles to ranges north of $10 to $12 per square foot NNN for modern product, though older vintage industrial assets lag considerably. On the office side, Trenton faces the same structural headwinds as most secondary and tertiary office markets: suburban office vacancy in the Trenton metro hovers in the 20 to 25 percent range for Class B and C product, and the state government tenant base, while sticky, is under budgetary pressure that affects renewal certainty. Multifamily fundamentals in Trenton proper have improved as the Philadelphia-to-New York corridor has attracted attention from investors priced out of Newark and Jersey City, with cap rates for stabilized urban multifamily assets trading in the 5.5 to 7.0 percent range depending on submarket location, condition, and tenant profile. Land values in Trenton are meaningfully lower than in northern New Jersey markets, which is part of why ground lease structures appear here, particularly on institutional, government-adjacent, or historically encumbered sites where fee ownership has been retained by municipalities, redevelopment authorities, or long-tenured family estates.

That last point is the crux of the financing complexity. Ground leases in Trenton often originate from redevelopment deals, urban renewal frameworks, or legacy property arrangements tied to the city's industrial and governmental history. Those leases were frequently drafted before anyone contemplated that a leasehold tenant would need to finance improvements through a conventional commercial mortgage. The resulting structural mismatches between what those ground leases say and what a lender requires to underwrite a leasehold mortgage are, in my experience, among the most technically dense and litigation-prone issues in commercial real estate finance.

The Engagement

In the matter for which counsel retained Commercial Lending Solutions, the asset was a mixed-use commercial property in the Trenton core, improved by the leasehold tenant with a substantial structure used for retail and office purposes. The ground lease had been in place for several decades, with a remaining term of approximately 35 years, plus renewal options that were not automatically exercisable and required landlord consent under specific conditions. A financing dispute had arisen between the leasehold tenant and a proposed lender, and separately, litigation had developed between the leasehold tenant and the fee owner regarding the fee owner's refusal to execute a subordination, non-disturbance, and attornment agreement acceptable to the lender.

Counsel for the leasehold tenant retained CLS CRE to provide a Rule 26 expert report addressing three core questions. First, does the ground lease as drafted meet the market standard requirements for commercial mortgage financing of a leasehold interest? Second, what specific provisions of the ground lease deviate from market standard and how do those deviations impair or eliminate the financing universe available to the leasehold tenant? Third, is the SNDA proposed by the fee owner commercially reasonable under market standards, or does it contain terms that a sophisticated institutional lender would reject as a matter of course?

These are precisely the questions where a capital markets professional with active lender relationships provides materially different analysis than an appraiser or a retired local banker. Answering them requires knowing what institutional lenders, CMBS platforms, life companies, and regional banks actually require in practice, not what a ground lease treatise says they should require.

The Financial Analysis

The financial analysis in a leasehold financing engagement begins with a disciplined underwriting of the income stream, but it must immediately confront the layered complexity of the leasehold structure itself. Here is how we approached it in this engagement.

The property generated stabilized net operating income from its retail and office tenants of approximately $420,000 annually, based on in-place leases with a weighted average remaining term of approximately 4.8 years. Ground rent under the ground lease was $52,000 per year, fixed for the current term with a step-up to fair market rent at the next renewal. The NOI available to service a leasehold mortgage, after subtracting ground rent as an operating expense, was therefore approximately $368,000. This is the correct methodology: ground rent is treated as a senior, fixed charge that must be deducted before applying any debt service coverage analysis.

Applying a market standard DSCR threshold of 1.25x, which is the floor most institutional lenders apply to commercial leasehold mortgage underwriting (and many require 1.30x or higher for leasehold interests specifically, given the elevated complexity and exit risk), the maximum supportable annual debt service was approximately $294,400. At a note rate of 7.25 percent on a 25-year amortization schedule, which reflects current pricing for leasehold commercial mortgages at this size and complexity level in the New Jersey market, that debt service supports a loan of approximately $3,400,000.

The independent appraisal placed leasehold market value at approximately $5,100,000. The supportable loan of $3,400,000 represents roughly 66.7 percent LTV against the leasehold value. Most institutional lenders cap leasehold mortgage LTV at 65 percent for stabilized assets, and some apply a 60 percent ceiling when lease term is a concern. At 65 percent LTV, the maximum loan against the appraised leasehold value would be $3,315,000, which is slightly below what the income stream supports at 1.25x DSCR. The binding constraint in this deal was therefore the LTV ceiling rather than the income coverage, which is a meaningful finding: it tells the court that the property generates adequate income to service a market-rate mortgage, but lenders' structural concerns about leasehold collateral, not the cash flow, are what limit the financing.

This distinction matters for damages analysis. If a borrower is arguing that a fee owner's failure to deliver an acceptable SNDA caused them to lose financing, the financial analysis must establish both that adequate income existed to support a loan and that the loan was denied or unavailable for structural, not economic, reasons.

The Complicating Factors

Ground lease financing disputes in Trenton and throughout New Jersey concentrate around a cluster of structural issues that lenders treat as threshold credit concerns. Each one requires careful analysis in any expert report or trial testimony.

Remaining lease term versus loan term. Institutional lenders require that the ground lease term extend at least 10 to 15 years beyond the maturity of the mortgage, and many require the term to extend beyond the end of any anticipated amortization period. A ground lease with 35 years remaining sounds like meaningful tenure, but a lender making a 10-year fixed loan with a 25-year amortization schedule will look at that remaining term and question exit strategy. If the leasehold mortgage is in default at year 9 and the lender takes the collateral through foreclosure, what is the remaining term? Does it include options that require landlord consent? Are the options in the money or out of the money given the fair market rent reset provisions? Every one of these questions is a separate underwriting risk, and in many Trenton legacy ground leases, the answers are unfavorable.

SNDA provisions and lender protection requirements. A ground lease is not financeable by any institutional lender, CMBS platform, or life company unless the fee owner has executed an SNDA that meets market standards. The minimum requirements are non-negotiable in practice: the fee owner must agree that any foreclosure of the leasehold mortgage will not terminate the ground lease (non-disturbance); that the lender, as mortgagee in possession or as acquirer through foreclosure, will be recognized as the new tenant (attornment); and that the fee owner will accept a new subtenant or assignee proposed by the lender without exercising any approval rights that could be used to block a commercially reasonable transaction. When fee owners resist executing SNDAs, or propose SNDAs that carve out lender protections or condition non-disturbance on performance of obligations that the lender has no ability to control, those provisions are outside market standards and render the leasehold effectively unfinanceable.

Notice and cure rights for the leasehold mortgagee. Market standard ground leases give the lender an independent right to receive notice of any default by the tenant and an independent opportunity to cure that default before the fee owner may terminate the lease. The cure period for monetary defaults is typically 30 days beyond the tenant's cure period, and for non-monetary defaults it is extended further to allow the lender to institute foreclosure and substitute a new tenant. Ground leases that lack these provisions, or that give the fee owner the right to terminate without delivering notice to the lender of record, are categorically unfinanceable. This is not a matter of lender preference; it is a structural defect.

Leasehold title insurance. A lender financing a leasehold interest will require a leasehold title insurance policy insuring the leasehold estate and the lender's mortgage on that estate. Legacy ground leases with gaps in the chain of title, recorded subordinate easements, or unresolved questions about whether a prior lender's mortgage was properly released create title insurance exceptions that may be uninsurable, which is a separate pathway to unfinanceability independent of the lease terms themselves.

Ground rent escalation and fair market value resets. Ground leases with rent resets to fair market value at renewal present a specific risk to lenders: if the reset results in dramatically higher ground rent, the NOI available to service the leasehold mortgage shrinks, potentially triggering a DSCR covenant default. Lenders handling leasehold mortgages on Trenton commercial properties typically require that any rent reset mechanism include an appraisal arbitration procedure, that the reset be capped at a defined percentage increase, or that the leasehold mortgage contain a specific covenant addressing the rent reset risk. Leases that simply call for fair market value without any cap or dispute resolution mechanism are treated as speculative future cost risks that impair the underwriting.

The Trenton Lender Market

Understanding which lenders will actually engage with leasehold collateral in Trenton is essential both for underwriting analysis and for assessing what the borrower lost when financing was denied or delayed.

CMBS lenders can finance leasehold interests, but their requirements are among the most rigid in the market. CMBS ground lease guidelines typically require a minimum of 30 years of combined remaining term plus options, a fully executed SNDA meeting the lender's form requirements without material modification, leasehold title insurance with no exceptions that would impair the lender's rights, and borrower entity structures that separate the leasehold interest clearly from other assets. In Trenton, CMBS executes well on retail and industrial assets in the $5 million and above range with stable cash flows, but the leasehold complexity typically pushes the transaction to specialized CMBS conduits or portfolio lenders rather than plain-vanilla conduit programs.

Life insurance companies, which are active in New Jersey for high-quality commercial assets, generally avoid leasehold structures unless the ground lease is specifically structured to their requirements from the outset. Retroactively restructuring a legacy Trenton ground lease to meet life company standards is possible but requires fee owner cooperation that may not be forthcoming.

Regional and community banks headquartered in New Jersey, including institutions with meaningful Trenton and Mercer County lending presence, represent the most realistic financing source for leasehold commercial mortgage transactions in this market. These lenders typically apply balance sheet underwriting, have more flexibility on lease term calculations, and can engage with the borrower and fee owner to develop SNDA language collaboratively. Their pricing will be higher than institutional sources, typically 50 to 100 basis points above comparable fee-simple transactions, reflecting the leasehold risk premium, and their LTV ceilings are generally 60 to 65 percent. CLS CRE maintains active relationships with multiple New Jersey regional banks with demonstrated appetite for Trenton leasehold commercial lending.

Debt funds and bridge lenders will finance leasehold interests on a short-term basis at significantly higher rates, typically floating at 300 to 500 basis points over SOFR, with expectations of a refinance into permanent debt once structural issues are resolved. These lenders are appropriate as a transitional solution but should not be characterized as a permanent financing option when assessing damages or financing availability.

What Attorneys Handling Trenton CRE Disputes Need to Know

The decision to retain a CRE financing expert witness is often made too late. By the time depositions are underway, the opportunity to develop the record on financing feasibility has frequently been compromised by incomplete document production or preliminary expert conclusions that were never grounded in actual lender market analysis. Here is what counsel should understand.

The expert's role is distinct from the appraiser's. An appraiser determines value. A CRE financing expert determines whether and on what terms a lender will extend credit against that value. Those are different questions. An appraisal of a leasehold estate may produce a leasehold value of $5 million, but that number tells you nothing about whether any lender will extend a commercial mortgage against it, at what loan-to-value, at what rate, or with what structural conditions. When the central dispute involves lost financing, denied credit, or impaired access to capital markets, the financing expert's analysis is the one that controls the damages calculation.

Retain the expert before requesting SNDA negotiations. If your client is the leasehold tenant and you are trying to compel the fee owner to execute an acceptable SNDA, getting a CRE financing expert involved early lets you define the standard of commercial reasonableness before the fee owner's counsel has framed the issue. An expert report that establishes, with specific lender citations and market standards, what a compliant SNDA must contain is a materially stronger tool in negotiations and at hearing than a general assertion that the fee owner is being unreasonable.

Gather the right documents before the engagement begins. The expert will need the complete ground lease and all amendments; any existing SNDA or proposed SNDA drafts; the leasehold title insurance commitment or policy; all in-place tenant leases and rent rolls; any appraisal or valuation; the borrower's loan application materials and any lender rejection correspondence; and any correspondence between the parties regarding the SNDA dispute. The more complete this record, the more defensible and specific the expert report will be under cross-examination.

At trial, the financing expert provides opinion testimony on industry custom and practice. Unlike a fact witness who testifies about what happened, the financing expert testifies about what the market requires, what lenders actually do, and whether the structure at issue meets or fails those standards. In a bench trial before a New Jersey Superior Court judge or in a federal court proceeding, this testimony directly supports arguments about commercial reasonableness, damages quantification, and the feasibility of alternative remedies. In a bankruptcy plan confirmation hearing, financing feasibility testimony is often central to whether a reorganization plan is feasible under the Bankruptcy Code standard.

Key Takeaways

  • Ground lease financing has non-negotiable threshold requirements. No institutional lender, CMBS platform, or life company will underwrite a leasehold mortgage without a fully executed SNDA meeting market standards, adequate remaining lease term, and independent lender notice and cure rights. These are not negotiating points; they are structural prerequisites.
  • Ground rent must be underwritten as a senior operating expense. NOI available for debt service on a leasehold mortgage is always net of ground rent, and any rent reset mechanism creates a measurable future cost risk that directly affects underwriting. Ignoring this in a damages analysis will produce an inflated and legally vulnerable loan sizing conclusion.
  • Leasehold LTV constraints are more restrictive than fee-simple. Most institutional lenders apply a 60 to 65 percent LTV ceiling for leasehold collateral, and DSCR thresholds at or above 1.25x are market standard. In this Trenton engagement, the binding constraint was LTV, not income coverage, which is a fact pattern directly relevant to damages causation.
  • A fee owner's refusal to execute a commercially reasonable SNDA is a quantifiable harm. When a fee owner withholds or conditions an SNDA in ways that no institutional lender will accept, the leasehold tenant loses

The Chattanooga Market Context

Chattanooga, Tennessee has evolved from a regional manufacturing hub into a genuinely diversified commercial real estate market, and that evolution matters considerably when a loan modification or workout dispute reaches litigation. The city's industrial corridor along the Tennessee River and the I-75/I-24 interchange has attracted significant distribution and advanced manufacturing activity over the past decade, drawing Volkswagen's North American assembly operations and a growing tier-one supplier ecosystem. Industrial vacancy in the Chattanooga market has hovered in the 4% to 6% range through most of the post-pandemic period, with asking rents for Class A warehouse and distribution product pushing $5.50 to $7.00 per square foot NNN depending on clear height and dock configuration. That fundamental strength creates a baseline against which lender conduct in industrial workout scenarios can be meaningfully measured.

The multifamily sector tells a more nuanced story. Chattanooga experienced a substantial development pipeline from 2020 through 2023, concentrated in the North Shore, Southside, and downtown submarkets. That supply pressure has moderated rent growth from the 10% to 15% annual gains of 2021 to 2022, settling into a more normalized 2% to 4% range by late 2023 and into 2024. Cap rates for stabilized multifamily assets in Chattanooga range from approximately 5.25% to 6.50% depending on vintage, location, and occupancy, with value-add product trading at 6.25% to 7.25%. These figures are directly relevant to any workout dispute involving a multifamily asset because they govern how lenders must re-underwrite a distressed property's exit value and what loan sizing is commercially supportable at current rates. Office fundamentals remain stressed, consistent with national trends, with suburban vacancy pushing above 15% in several Chattanooga submarkets and downtown Class B product facing meaningful occupancy headwinds. When a workout dispute involves an office asset, the market context itself becomes a critical variable in assessing whether a lender's modification posture was commercially reasonable or punitive.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing a commercial borrower in a dispute arising from a lender's handling of a loan modification and forbearance request on a mixed-use retail and multifamily property in the Chattanooga urban core. The asset was a mid-size mixed-use building comprising ground-floor retail with residential units above, originally financed at a loan-to-value ratio in the low-70% range during a period of compressed cap rates and aggressive lender competition. When rising interest rates and a softening in ground-floor retail occupancy caused the property's debt service coverage to fall below the loan covenant threshold, the borrower initiated formal modification discussions with the lender.

Counsel retained CLS CRE to address three distinct but interrelated questions. First, whether the lender's conduct during the modification process, specifically the timeline, information demands, and proposed terms, comported with industry standards of care for commercial real estate workouts. Second, whether the modification terms the lender ultimately proposed were commercially reasonable given the property's documented cash flow, the Chattanooga submarket conditions at the time, and the range of workout structures that similarly situated lenders were employing in comparable scenarios. Third, how the lender's actions compared to the documented practices of regional and national commercial lenders handling distressed mixed-use assets during the same period. These are financing and capital markets questions, not appraisal questions, and they require an expert with active lender relationships and current market transactional experience rather than simply retrospective valuation expertise.

The Financial Analysis

The financial analysis in a workout dispute is not merely an exercise in recalculating numbers. It requires reconstructing the lender's decision framework as it should have existed at each critical point in the modification timeline, then comparing that framework against what the lender actually did. In this engagement, the methodology proceeded in three stages: current cash flow analysis, supportable debt capacity determination, and workout structure benchmarking.

The subject property's net operating income was established using trailing twelve-month actual collections rather than pro forma assumptions. The ground-floor retail component was generating approximately $280,000 annually in gross rent, with vacancy and credit loss running at approximately 12%, yielding effective gross income from retail of roughly $246,400. The residential component, comprising 24 units, was collecting average monthly rents of $1,150 per unit, producing annual residential gross income of $331,200, offset by a 7% vacancy and loss allowance for effective gross income of $308,016. Combined effective gross income was therefore approximately $554,416. Operating expenses, including management at 6%, insurance, taxes, maintenance, and reserves at $350 per unit annually, totaled approximately $198,000, producing a stabilized NOI of roughly $356,400.

At the original loan terms, underwritten at a 4.50% rate on a 25-year amortization schedule, the property carried a loan balance of approximately $3,850,000. Annual debt service on that balance was approximately $257,000, producing a DSCR of approximately 1.39x on original underwriting assumptions. By the time the borrower sought modification, the property's NOI had compressed to approximately $298,000, representing a decline of roughly 16% driven by retail vacancy and increased operating costs. At the same original debt service, the DSCR had fallen to approximately 1.16x, breaching the standard 1.20x covenant threshold that governs virtually all commercial real estate loan agreements in this dollar range.

The critical underwriting question in a workout dispute is not simply whether the covenant was breached. It is whether the lender, applying commercially reasonable standards, could have structured a modification that kept the loan performing. At a market rate of 7.25% on a 30-year amortization with a compressed NOI of $298,000, and applying the standard 1.20x DSCR threshold, the maximum supportable annual debt service is approximately $248,333. That implies a maximum supportable loan balance at a 7.25% rate of approximately $2,985,000. With the existing balance at $3,850,000 and a current market value of approximately $4,600,000 (implied by the compressed NOI at a 6.50% cap rate), the LTV was approximately 83.7%, well above the 65% to 70% LTV ceiling that stabilized mixed-use lenders typically require. This gap is the financial heart of the dispute: the property was under-collateralized relative to current standards but not underwater, and the NOI, while compressed, was not negative. A commercially reasonable lender had meaningful structural options.

Those options include a bifurcated note structure separating performing and non-performing balance, a rate reduction or interest-only period to restore coverage while the borrower executed a retail lease-up plan, or a principal paydown requirement triggered by identified asset sale proceeds. The lender in this matter proposed none of these structures. That omission is itself a deviation from industry practice that required documentation and expert analysis.

The Complicating Factors

Several factors complicated the analysis and, importantly, would have been visible to any commercially sophisticated lender engaging in a good-faith modification process in this market.

Mixed-use asset complexity. Lenders underwriting or re-underwriting mixed-use retail and residential properties face a bifurcated credit analysis. The residential income stream is relatively stable and predictable; the retail income stream, particularly in smaller urban submarkets like Chattanooga's South Broad or Frazier Avenue corridors, carries meaningful lease rollover and tenant credit risk. A sophisticated lender's workout team would separate these income streams, stress-test each independently, and structure the modification with recognition that retail stabilization timelines are longer than residential. A lender that treats the blended income as a single undifferentiated stream, or that demands immediate full-debt-service compliance without allowance for a retail lease-up period, is not applying the standard of care that workout professionals routinely use.

Rate environment timing. The period in which this modification request arose coincided with one of the most rapid interest rate escalation cycles in recent history. Commercial lenders nationally, and regionally in Tennessee, were simultaneously managing large books of loans underwritten at 3.50% to 4.50% that could not refinance at prevailing rates without significant equity injection or principal reduction. Industry practice during this period was documented by CREFC, the Mortgage Bankers Association, and major bank regulatory guidance as favoring proactive, structured workouts over technical default enforcement. A lender that cited rate-environment stress as grounds for refusing modification, rather than as a factor warranting it, inverted the prevailing industry framework.

Chattanooga submarket trajectory. At the time of the modification request, the Chattanooga mixed-use and retail market was showing measurable signs of stabilization, with several new food-and-beverage and service tenants absorbing ground-floor space in comparable urban corridors. An expert engaged in a lender-conduct analysis must establish what market data was available to the lender at the time it made its modification decision, not what was knowable in hindsight. If market intelligence available to a competent commercial lender in this submarket indicated improving retail absorption, a lender that assumed continued deterioration in its workout analysis failed to apply commercially reasonable underwriting.

Documentation and process failures. In this engagement, the lender's modification file revealed significant delays between submission of the borrower's financial package and the lender's substantive response, requests for duplicate documentation already in the file, and a final modification proposal delivered without supporting underwriting detail. Each of these process failures is independently relevant to the standard-of-care analysis and has parallels in documented industry workout guidance that establishes timeline and communication expectations for lenders managing distressed commercial loans.

The Chattanooga Lender Market

Understanding which lenders are active in Chattanooga for mixed-use and similar commercial assets, and what their workout posture looks like, is essential to contextualizing any dispute about whether modification terms were commercially reasonable.

The Chattanooga market is served by a relatively deep community and regional bank tier. Tennessee-chartered community banks with Hamilton County and surrounding county concentration represent a meaningful share of the local construction and permanent loan market for properties in the $2 million to $8 million range. These institutions typically underwrite to 70% to 75% LTV on stabilized mixed-use, require 1.20x to 1.25x DSCR, and price at spreads of 175 to 250 basis points over the five-year Treasury or SOFR depending on the institution's funding model. Their workout infrastructure is less formalized than money-center banks, and their modification timelines, for better or worse, often reflect smaller special assets teams. That context matters when evaluating whether a community bank's modification process met industry standards, because "industry standards" must be calibrated to the lender category.

Regional banks with Tennessee, Georgia, and Alabama footprints are also active in the Chattanooga market for commercial real estate in the $5 million to $25 million range. These institutions have more developed special assets and workout departments, typically modeled on OCC and FDIC supervisory guidance for troubled debt restructurings. Their workout processes include formal credit committee approval for modifications, documented re-underwriting, and structured forbearance agreements with clear milestone conditions. When a regional bank's conduct is at issue, the standard of care analysis must account for the more formal processes these institutions are required and known to follow.

Life companies and CMBS conduit lenders are active in the Chattanooga market for stabilized assets above approximately $7 million to $10 million, primarily in industrial, anchored retail, and multifamily. CMBS workout disputes are a distinct category because the servicer, typically a special servicer upon transfer, operates under a pooling and servicing agreement that constrains its discretion in ways that portfolio lenders do not face. Understanding whether a CMBS special servicer's conduct was consistent with its PSA obligations and CMBS servicing standards is a specialized analysis that goes beyond general workout practice.

What Attorneys Handling Chattanooga CRE Disputes Need to Know

Attorneys litigating commercial real estate loan modification and workout disputes in Chattanooga need to understand precisely what a CRE financing expert provides that other experts cannot, and when in the case timeline that expert should be retained.

Retain the financing expert early. The most common mistake litigation counsel makes in workout disputes is engaging a financing expert only after an appraiser has already established value conclusions that the financing analysis may need to work around or challenge. Value and financing capacity are related but not identical questions. An appraisal establishes market value; a financing expert establishes what a commercial lender would actually lend on that value, under what conditions, and with what covenants. In a modification dispute, the financing expert's analysis of maximum supportable debt often drives the entire damages or liability framework.

Understand what the expert can and cannot answer. A CRE financing expert with active capital markets practice can testify to lender underwriting standards, industry-standard modification processes, DSCR thresholds and their application, LTV constraints by lender category, the range of workout structures available for a given asset type, and whether a specific lender's conduct deviated from those standards. The expert cannot testify to the lender's subjective intent. However, systematic deviations from documented industry practice are themselves evidence of non-standard conduct, and a qualified expert can draw that line precisely.

Qualification matters significantly. A retired banker is not automatically a qualified commercial real estate financing expert. The relevant qualification is current, active engagement in commercial real estate lending and capital markets. Standards shift with rate cycles, regulatory guidance, and lender risk appetite. An expert whose knowledge of lender practice is based on experience that ended five or ten years ago may not accurately represent what a commercially reasonable lender was doing in 2022, 2023, or 2024. CLS CRE has closed approximately $1 billion in commercial real estate transaction volume, maintains active relationships with more than 1,000 lenders nationally, and operates in all 50 states. That current market engagement is what makes expert testimony credible on questions about what lenders were actually doing, not what they did in a prior rate cycle.

Information to gather before the expert engagement. Counsel should assemble the original loan documents including all covenants and default provisions, the complete modification request file submitted by the borrower, all lender communications during the modification process with timestamps, the lender's internal credit committee or special assets file if obtainable through discovery, any appraisals or BPOs ordered by the lender during the workout process, and the final modification proposal or denial with any supporting underwriting. The expert's analysis is only as complete as the factual record, and gaps in the lender's file are themselves relevant to the standard-of-care analysis.

Expert testimony at trial and in Plan hearings. In a bench trial or jury trial, the financing expert will typically offer both written opinion testimony pursuant to Rule 26 and live testimony explaining the industry standard framework, the financial analysis, and the deviation conclusions. In a bankruptcy Plan confirmation hearing involving a contested cram-down, the financing expert's testimony on feasibility, supportable debt, and market rate for similar credit instruments is often the pivotal evidence for or against Plan confirmation. Counsel should ensure the expert is prepared to explain DSCR, LTV, and workout structure concepts clearly to a non-specialist audience without sacrificing technical accuracy.

Key Takeaways

  • NOI compression does not automatically justify a lender's refusal to modify. The standard-of-care analysis requires examining whether the NOI, even at reduced levels, supported a restructured loan at commercially reasonable terms, not merely whether a covenant breach occurred.
  • Commercially reasonable workout structures are well-documented in industry practice. Bifurcated note structures, interest-only periods, principal paydown milestones, and rate concessions are all established tools. A lender that proposes none of them without documented justification has deviated from standard practice.
  • Lender category determines the applicable standard of care. Community bank, regional bank, life company, and CMBS servicer workouts are each governed by different institutional processes, regulatory frameworks, and industry expectations. Expert analysis must calibrate the standard to the specific lender type.
  • The Chattanooga market's mixed-use and multifamily fundamentals are strong enough to support workout viability arguments. In this market, a lender cannot credibly argue that all exit options were foreclosed by market conditions without confronting contrary market evidence.
  • Modification process conduct is independently relevant to the legal analysis. Delays, duplicate documentation demands, and proposals delivered without supporting underwriting are deviations from standard practice regardless of the ultimate financial outcome.
  • A CRE financing expert is not a substitute for an appraiser and vice versa. Litigation involving workout disputes typically requires both a value opinion and a separate financing opinion. Conflating the two roles weakens both.

Court-Appointed Receivers and Distressed CRE Financing: Stabilize, Capitalize, Maximize

By Trevor Damyan·June 25, 2026·Commercial Lending Solutions

Commercial Real Estate Financing in Receivership: A Practical Guide for Court-Appointed Receivers and Distressed Asset Professionals

I am Trevor Damyan, a commercial mortgage broker at CLS CRE based in Los Angeles. Over the years, I have worked alongside court-appointed receivers, assignment for benefit of creditors (ABC) professionals, and distressed asset managers who needed capital to stabilize, improve, or orderly liquidate commercial real estate held under their control. This article is written for those professionals: people who need accurate, technical information fast, not a sales pitch.

If you are managing commercial real estate under a court appointment or fiduciary obligation, financing decisions carry legal exposure. A poorly structured loan, a missed competitive bid process, or an ill-timed refinance can invite creditor objections and professional liability claims. Understanding how commercial mortgage financing actually works in these contexts, and engaging the right capital markets team, protects you and maximizes outcomes for the creditors you serve.

Why Receivers and Distressed Asset Professionals Encounter CRE Financing Needs

A court-appointed receiver's mandate is clear: preserve and maximize asset value for creditors. In practice, that mandate almost always collides with a property that has been mismanaged, undercapitalized, or allowed to deteriorate. The previous owner had no incentive to invest in a property headed into insolvency. Deferred maintenance accumulates. Leases expire without renewal effort. Insurance lapses. The physical and financial condition of the asset declines in lockstep.

By the time a receiver takes control, the property typically needs capital to survive long enough to sell at a value that actually satisfies creditor claims. Without that capital, the receiver faces a binary choice between a distressed liquidation that destroys creditor recovery and carrying costs that drain operating reserves to zero.

Assignment for benefit of creditors professionals face a structurally similar problem. The assignor's commercial real estate is often the single largest asset in the estate. Rushing a sale in a down market produces recoveries far below what an orderly, capitalized process could achieve. Financing can buy the time needed to run a proper marketing process and attract institutional buyers at prices that benefit all creditor classes.

Judgment creditors with commercial property as collateral face a different but equally technical problem. Before pursuing collection remedies, a sophisticated judgment creditor needs to understand whether the property can sustain refinancing proceeds sufficient to satisfy the judgment. That analysis requires a real capital markets assessment, not a back-of-envelope estimate based on broker opinion of value.

In each of these situations, the fiduciary professional does not need a lender. They need a broker who understands distressed commercial real estate, has active relationships with lenders who will actually touch these deals, and can run a competitive process that withstands judicial scrutiny.

The Five Financing Scenarios Receivers Most Commonly Face

Scenario 1: Bridge Financing to Stabilize Before Sale

This is the most common scenario I see. A receiver takes control of a retail center, office building, or industrial property with significant deferred maintenance, below-market occupancy, or both. The property cannot support a permanent loan because it does not yet qualify on a cash-flow basis. The receiver needs short-term capital to fund repairs, fund tenant improvement allowances to lease vacant space, and carry the asset through the stabilization period until a sale can close at a value that serves creditors.

Bridge lenders are the right tool here. These are typically private credit funds, debt funds associated with real estate private equity firms, or specialty finance companies that price risk into their rate rather than declining the deal. Terms typically run 12 to 36 months with extension options. Rates in the current environment range from 9% to 13% depending on the sponsor quality, loan-to-value, market, and property condition. Origination fees of 1% to 2% are standard.

The underwriting question for the receiver is whether the stabilized sale proceeds, net of bridge loan repayment, exceed what an immediate distressed sale would generate. In almost every case where I have modeled this, the answer is yes, often dramatically so. A 15% to 30% improvement in gross sale proceeds from a properly stabilized property is common. The bridge loan cost is almost always worth absorbing.

Critically, bridge lenders are accustomed to receivership situations. They understand that the borrower is a receiver acting under court authority, not a traditional sponsor. They require court order excerpts confirming the receiver's authority to incur debt, and they require that the loan be approved by the presiding court before funding. Build that approval step into your timeline.

Scenario 2: Refinancing Maturing Debt to Prevent Foreclosure While a Sale Process Runs

This scenario involves a property with existing debt approaching maturity during the receivership. The lender on that debt has no obligation to extend. If the debt matures without a refinance or payoff, the senior lender can accelerate and foreclose, potentially wiping out junior creditors and defeating the receiver's entire effort to maximize recovery.

The receiver's tool here is a refinance or maturity extension negotiated with the existing lender or arranged with a new lender. The negotiation with the existing lender is often the cleaner path: lenders generally prefer a receiver who is actively managing the property over a contested foreclosure sale. Many lenders will grant a 6 to 12 month extension in exchange for a fee and a structured paydown plan if the receiver can demonstrate an active sale process.

When the existing lender will not extend, or when the extension terms are punitive, a new bridge lender can take out the maturing debt. This is a standard bridge loan scenario: loan-to-value typically capped at 65% to 70%, proceeds used to retire the existing debt and potentially fund a small improvement reserve, term structured to carry through the anticipated sale timeline.

The receiver's court order and the active sale process are actually selling points with sophisticated bridge lenders. They are lending into a situation with defined legal oversight, a motivated seller (the receiver has a legal obligation to maximize proceeds), and a clear exit path. That predictability has value in the pricing conversation.

Scenario 3: Assignment for Benefit of Creditors With Commercial Real Estate as a Core Asset

In an ABC proceeding, the assignee steps into the shoes of the assignor with respect to the commercial real estate and has the same problem as a receiver: the property needs capital to achieve its highest value through an orderly liquidation process rather than a distressed fire sale.

The ABC context introduces one complication that receivers do not face in the same way: the assignee's authority to incur new debt is generally governed by the ABC agreement and applicable state law rather than a court order. In California, for example, an assignee can incur obligations necessary to preserve and liquidate assets, but the scope of that authority is fact-specific. Legal counsel must confirm the authority before any financing commitment is signed.

Given that authority, bridge financing for an ABC commercial property works similarly to receivership bridge financing. The lender will require the ABC agreement, an opinion from assignee's counsel confirming authority to borrow, and a clear liquidation timeline with milestones. Many bridge lenders are more comfortable with ABC situations than with contested receiverships because the ABC process is typically consensual and less likely to produce litigation that affects the lender's collateral position.

Scenario 4: Judgment Creditor Assessing Refinancing Feasibility Before Pursuing Collection

This is a pre-litigation advisory scenario, not an active financing situation, but it requires the same technical analysis. A judgment creditor holds a lien on commercial real estate and needs to understand: what can this property support in terms of new financing proceeds, and after paying off senior debt, are there sufficient net proceeds to satisfy the judgment?

The analysis requires a real loan sizing exercise, not a broker's optimistic opinion. A competent commercial mortgage broker will analyze the property's net operating income, apply current lender underwriting standards (debt service coverage ratios, loan-to-value constraints, debt yield requirements), and model the maximum loan proceeds supportable under current market conditions.

For stabilized income-producing properties, permanent lenders typically underwrite to 1.25x debt service coverage minimum and 65% to 75% loan-to-value maximum. The binding constraint depends on the property: low-cap-rate properties in expensive markets are often value-constrained; lower-value properties with strong cash flow are often income-constrained. Getting the right answer requires running both tests against current lender standards, not rules of thumb.

The judgment creditor who completes this analysis before filing collection actions is in a much stronger negotiating position and avoids the cost and delay of pursuing a property that cannot generate sufficient proceeds to satisfy the judgment.

Scenario 5: Funding Property Improvements to Maximize Sale Value

Receivers frequently inherit properties where specific, targeted capital expenditures would produce disproportionate returns on investment. A roof replacement that eliminates a disclosure problem. HVAC modernization that allows lease-up of vacant space. Parking lot resurfacing that satisfies a municipal code requirement blocking certificate of occupancy. Lobby renovation that converts a Class B tenant profile to Class A pricing.

The financing tool here is a short-term construction or renovation bridge loan with a funded improvement reserve. The lender advances an initial draw at closing to satisfy the receiver's carry costs and existing debt obligations, and holds the improvement budget in a controlled reserve released against inspection draws as work is completed. The total loan amount is sized against the post-improvement as-stabilized value, allowing the receiver to borrow against value that does not yet exist but will exist once the work is done.

Construction reserve loans in receivership require a detailed scope of work, contractor bids, a draw schedule, and typically an independent construction monitor retained by the lender. The receiver is responsible for ensuring the work is completed on budget and on schedule. Factor those management costs into your analysis of whether the improvement program is worth pursuing.

What Lenders Actually Require for Receivership and Distressed Situations

The single biggest mistake I see fiduciary professionals make is approaching conventional lenders with receivership situations. Conventional lenders, banks, credit unions, agency lenders, and life insurance companies will not fund properties under active receivership. Their underwriting systems are not built for it, their credit committees will not approve it, and their regulators discourage it. Do not waste six weeks finding this out.

The active lenders for receivership and distressed commercial real estate are bridge lenders, specifically private credit funds and specialty finance companies that have built underwriting expertise around distressed situations. Here is what they actually require:

  • Court documentation: A certified copy of the order appointing the receiver, excerpts confirming authority to incur debt, and either a specific court order approving the financing or a representation that the receiver will seek such approval as a condition to closing. Lenders will not fund without clarity on the receiver's legal authority.
  • Title confirmation: A title search confirming the current state of liens, including any senior debt being refinanced, mechanics liens, tax liens, and judgment liens. Title insurance will be required at closing. Budget for it.
  • Current appraisal: An MAI appraisal ordered within 6 to 12 months reflecting current market conditions. Lenders want both an as-is value and an as-stabilized value if the loan includes an improvement reserve. In receivership situations where the previous owner's appraisal is available, lenders will review it but will not rely on it.
  • Rent roll and operating statements: Current rent roll, 12 to 24 months of operating history (to the extent available from the previous owner's records), and the receiver's own operating summary since appointment. Lenders understand that receivership properties have data gaps; partial information does not disqualify a deal, but gaps must be disclosed and explained.
  • Receiver's operating plan: A written plan describing the stabilization or improvement program, projected timeline, projected costs, and the assumed sale scenario. This is the business case for the loan and should be treated as a formal document, not a rough memo.
  • Loan-to-value ceiling: Most bridge lenders cap receivership loans at 60% to 65% of as-is appraised value, occasionally up to 70% for strong assets in major markets. Do not model loan proceeds above these thresholds without lender confirmation.
  • Minimum loan sizes: Most active bridge lenders for commercial receivership situations have minimum loan sizes of $1 million to $2 million. Smaller properties typically require credit unions, community banks, or hard money lenders with shorter track records and less institutional reliability.

Questions Receivers Should Ask Any Commercial Mortgage Broker Before Referring

Not every commercial mortgage broker has experience with receivership financing. The wrong broker wastes your time and potentially damages the deal by approaching the wrong lenders or misrepresenting the situation. Before making a referral, ask the following:

  • Have you closed loans for court-appointed receivers before? Ask for specific examples. A broker who has done it knows what court approval language looks like in a loan commitment, knows how to explain the receivership structure to a lender's credit committee, and knows how to sequence the court approval and lender funding process without creating delays that kill the deal.
  • Which specific lenders are active in distressed commercial situations right now? The market moves. A lender who funded distressed deals two years ago may have pulled back. A broker who cannot name three active lenders in this segment without hesitation is not operating in it regularly.
  • How do you handle the court approval requirement? The loan commitment must be conditioned on court approval, not the other way around. A broker who does not understand this will create a situation where the receiver is legally exposed before the court has authorized the transaction.
  • Do you work on retainer or success fee? Success-fee-only brokers are aligned with closing the right deal. Retainer-based arrangements introduce conflicts of interest that are particularly problematic in a fiduciary context where every cost is subject to creditor scrutiny.
  • Can you provide a competitive process and document it? Receivers need to demonstrate to the presiding court that they ran a proper competitive process to obtain financing on the best available terms. A broker who approaches only one or two lenders and cannot document the outreach process is a liability, not an asset.

Key Takeaways

  • Financing stabilizes value: In almost every receivership situation involving commercial real estate, short-term bridge financing produces materially better creditor recoveries than an immediate distressed sale. The cost of capital is almost always outweighed by the improvement in net sale proceeds.
  • Conventional lenders will not engage: Banks, credit unions, and agency lenders are not options for active receivership properties. Bridge lenders and private credit funds are the correct capital sources. Approaching the wrong lender category wastes time the receivership cannot afford.
  • Court authority must be documented before approaching lenders: The order appointing the receiver must confirm authority to incur debt, and most lenders will require a specific court order approving the financing as a condition to funding. Build these steps into the deal timeline from the start.
  • A competitive broker process protects the receiver: Documenting that multiple lenders were approached and that term sheets were compared is the evidentiary foundation for defending the financing decision against creditor objections. A broker who creates that documentation as a matter of course is a professional asset.
  • ABC professionals have similar financing options but different authority structures: The financing products available in ABC situations parallel those in receivership, but the assignee's authority to borrow is governed by the ABC agreement and state law rather than a court order. Legal opinion on authority is required before committing.
  • Judgment creditors benefit from real loan sizing before collection: A proper underwriting analysis of the maximum supportable loan proceeds on judgment-collateral commercial real estate prevents costly pursuit of properties that cannot generate sufficient net proceeds and strengthens negotiating leverage when the analysis supports collection.
  • Improvement reserve loans fund targeted capital expenditures: Short-term construction and renovation bridge loans with funded improvement reserves allow receivers to fund targeted capital expenditures that generate disproportionate returns on investment, underwritten against post-improvement as-stabilized value.
  • The right broker relationship is low-friction and high-value: A single introduction, a brief description of the situation, and a commitment to provide documentation is all the receiver needs to do. The broker handles lender outreach, term sheet negotiation, and process documentation. There is no retainer and no cost until a loan closes.

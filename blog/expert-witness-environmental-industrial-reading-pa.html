Expert Witness: Financing a Contaminated Industrial Property in Reading, PA

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Environmental Contamination Analysis

The Reading Market Context

Reading, Pennsylvania sits at the intersection of several dynamics that make it a genuinely interesting market for industrial real estate disputes involving environmental complexity. The broader Berks County submarket has benefited from its position within the I-78/I-476 corridor, serving as a secondary logistics node that feeds the Philadelphia MSA and the Lehigh Valley. Industrial vacancy in the Reading submarket has historically run tighter than many comparable secondary Pennsylvania markets, typically in the 4% to 7% range for functional warehouse and light manufacturing product, driven by demand from regional distribution operators, manufacturing tenants, and last-mile logistics users who cannot afford Lehigh Valley or Exit 8A pricing.

What matters for this type of dispute is the character of the existing industrial base. Reading's industrial inventory is heavily weighted toward older, multi-decade-old manufacturing and warehouse facilities, many of which sit on parcels with prior industrial use histories. These are not Class A speculative distribution boxes. They are buildings in the 50,000 to 300,000 square foot range, often with rail spur access or heavy power, constructed before modern environmental compliance regimes were in place. That legacy creates a predictable pattern: properties that carry legitimate income streams and below-market leases from creditworthy tenants are simultaneously burdened with environmental conditions that require careful lender navigation.

Industrial asking rents in the Reading submarket have moved from approximately $4.50 to $5.50 per square foot NNN on older product to a range of $6.00 to $8.00 per square foot NNN for functional Class B product, depending on clear height, dock configuration, and proximity to Route 422 or the I-78 interchange. That rent growth trajectory matters because it directly affects the below-market lease analysis at the center of this type of financing engagement. Cap rates for industrial assets in Reading have generally ranged from 6.5% to 8.0% depending on lease term, tenant credit, and asset quality, with environmentally encumbered properties trading at significant premiums to those rates when they trade at all.

The Engagement

Counsel for a class of secured creditors in a regulatory enforcement proceeding retained Commercial Lending Solutions to provide an expert opinion on the financing feasibility of an industrial warehouse property in the Reading submarket. The asset was a single-tenant NNN-leased warehouse and light manufacturing facility of approximately 180,000 square feet, occupied by a single industrial tenant under a long-term lease at rents that were below current market. The property had been identified by state environmental regulators as a site with confirmed 1,4-dioxane and chlorinated VOC contamination in soil and groundwater, attributable to prior industrial operations predating the current ownership and tenancy.

The core legal and financial questions presented to our firm were specific and consequential. First, does the confirmed environmental condition categorically preclude first-mortgage financing in the current institutional lending market, or does a financing universe exist that can price and structure around it? Second, given the existing income stream, the environmental condition, and current market underwriting standards, what is the maximum supportable first-mortgage loan amount? Third, is that maximum supportable loan amount sufficient to retire the existing senior secured debt, and if so, what are the conditions precedent that any lender would require before closing? These questions sit at the intersection of capital markets practice and environmental law, and answering them requires more than a real estate appraisal. They require active knowledge of how lenders actually underwrite contaminated collateral in today's market.

CLS CRE was retained to provide a written expert opinion under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 26. Our role was not to appraise the property, not to evaluate the environmental remediation itself, and not to opine on liability allocation. Our role was to opine, as a capital markets practitioner with over $1 billion in transaction volume and relationships spanning more than 1,000 institutional and private lenders across all 50 states, on whether the financing market can and would support a loan of a given size on this asset under these conditions.

The Financial Analysis

The income analysis began with the in-place NNN lease, which is the appropriate starting point for institutional underwriting on a single-tenant net-leased industrial asset. The lease was structured as a true triple-net instrument, meaning the tenant was responsible for property taxes, insurance, and maintenance. The in-place base rent translated to an effective gross income of approximately $1,080,000 annually on the 180,000 square foot facility at $6.00 per square foot. That figure represented below-market rent relative to current submarket comparables, which was a central fact in the analysis.

For institutional underwriting purposes, lenders do not underwrite to market rent on an occupied NNN asset during the lease term. They underwrite to the in-place rent. The argument that the asset is "worth more" because market rents are higher provides no credit support while the below-market lease is in place; it is a lease-up story that only becomes relevant at expiration or if the tenant defaults. Accordingly, the net operating income used in the base case analysis was the in-place NOI of approximately $1,025,000 after deducting a minimal management reserve appropriate for a NNN structure. No vacancy deduction was applied to the in-place term, consistent with market practice for single-tenant NNN assets with a creditworthy occupant.

Debt service coverage ratio thresholds for industrial assets in this complexity range are not negotiable at most institutional lenders. The market standard for conventional conduit or balance sheet lenders is 1.25x DSCR at the note rate. For assets carrying environmental conditions, that threshold frequently increases to 1.30x to 1.35x, reflecting the lender's requirement for additional cash flow cushion against the possibility of lease disruption, remediation-related rent abatement claims, or deteriorating collateral value. For this analysis, a 1.30x DSCR threshold was applied as the governing constraint.

On the rate and amortization assumptions: given the environmental condition, conventional agency debt and CMBS execution are not available, a point addressed in detail in the complicating factors section below. The applicable financing universe for this asset is balance sheet lenders, specifically community banks, regional banks, and certain private credit platforms with environmental tolerance. Pricing for this category of lender in the current market ranges from approximately 7.25% to 8.50% for five-year fixed or floating rate product. A 7.75% note rate was used for the base case, which is consistent with the mid-range of pricing observed for environmental-encumbered industrial collateral at lenders active in the Pennsylvania market. A 25-year amortization schedule was applied, which is standard for industrial product at community and regional bank lenders.

The debt service calculation at 7.75% on a 25-year amortization schedule produces an annual constant of approximately 8.92%. Dividing the in-place NOI of $1,025,000 by the 1.30x DSCR threshold yields maximum allowable annual debt service of approximately $788,462. Dividing that figure by the 8.92% annual constant produces a maximum supportable loan amount of approximately $8,840,000, rounded to $8.8 million for purposes of this analysis.

The LTV constraint provides a secondary ceiling. Environmental-encumbered industrial assets at community and regional bank lenders in Pennsylvania are typically governed by a maximum LTV of 60% to 65% of the as-is appraised value, with the appraisal required to reflect the environmental condition. If the as-is appraised value of the subject property (with environmental conditions disclosed and incorporated) is in the range of $12,000,000 to $13,000,000, the 65% LTV ceiling would produce a maximum loan of approximately $7.8 million to $8.45 million. The DSCR constraint at $8.8 million and the LTV ceiling at $7.8 million to $8.45 million bracket the maximum supportable loan range at approximately $7.8 million to $8.4 million. Whether that range is sufficient to retire existing senior debt is a question that requires the senior debt balance to be compared against this ceiling, which is a fact-specific determination made in the context of the full report.

The Complicating Factors

1,4-Dioxane and Chlorinated VOC Contamination as a Lender Credit Trigger. Not all environmental conditions are treated identically by lenders. 1,4-dioxane is a particularly problematic contaminant from a lending perspective for several reasons. It is highly mobile in groundwater, it does not readily adsorb to soil particles, it does not biodegrade naturally under most conditions, and it has been subject to increasingly stringent regulatory attention at both the federal and Pennsylvania DEP level. Chlorinated VOCs, including compounds such as TCE and PCE, carry their own legacy of prolonged and expensive remediation timelines. When both contaminant families are present, lenders face the prospect of a remediation timeline measured in years or decades, not months. That timeline uncertainty is a primary credit risk driver, not the contamination itself.

Phase II Findings and Lender Response Protocols. Any institutional lender capable of closing a loan on this asset will require a current Phase II Environmental Site Assessment completed by a licensed environmental professional, with findings reviewed by the lender's own environmental consultant or legal counsel. The Phase II findings will inform whether the lender views the remediation as bounded and manageable or open-ended and indeterminate. Lenders generally will not close if the remediation cost estimate is undefined or if the Pennsylvania DEP has issued an active enforcement order with unresolved compliance milestones. An agreed-upon Act 2 remediation plan, even one in progress, is meaningfully more financeable than a raw enforcement posture.

The Below-Market Lease as a Double Constraint. The NNN lease structure provides income certainty, which is positive for underwriting. However, the below-market rent level directly suppresses the supportable loan amount relative to what the same property would support at market rents. Lenders will not give credit for the mark-to-market upside. More pointedly, counsel should understand that if the environmental condition caused or contributed to the tenant's ability to negotiate below-market rents at lease execution, that linkage has both financing and damages implications that merit expert analysis.

Lender Environmental Indemnification Requirements. Every lender willing to close on this type of asset will require, as a condition of financing, a robust environmental indemnification structure. This typically includes a carve-out from the standard non-recourse structure for environmental liability, meaning the principals of the borrowing entity will be required to provide personal recourse for environmental losses. This recourse requirement eliminates some borrowers who cannot or will not accept personal environmental liability, which affects the pool of potential purchasers or refinancing entities and therefore bears directly on the "what is the realistic exit?" question that courts and creditors care about.

The Reading Lender Market

The financing universe for an environmentally encumbered industrial asset in Reading is narrower than for clean collateral but is not empty. Understanding which lenders are realistic candidates, what they require, and how they price is the core of the capital markets expert's contribution to this type of proceeding.

Community and Regional Banks. Pennsylvania has a relatively deep community and regional bank market, and several institutions with significant commercial real estate portfolios have demonstrated willingness to underwrite environmentally impacted industrial collateral when the remediation path is defined and the income stream is stable. These lenders typically operate within a 60% to 65% LTV constraint, require a 1.25x to 1.35x DSCR, and will price at spreads of 200 to 350 basis points over the relevant treasury or SOFR index depending on risk tolerance. They will generally require a funded environmental escrow or a remediation completion bond as a condition of closing, and they will require the borrower to provide quarterly remediation status updates throughout the loan term. Loan terms are typically three to five years with amortization on a 20 to 25-year schedule.

Debt Funds and Private Credit Platforms. Certain private credit platforms have developed specific expertise in value-add and special-situation industrial lending, including environmental-encumbered collateral. These lenders can move faster and with more structural flexibility than community banks, but they price to reflect the risk premium. Rates in the 9.00% to 11.50% range are realistic for bridge-style execution on this type of asset. Loan-to-value constraints remain in the 55% to 65% range. The primary advantage of this category is speed, flexibility, and willingness to close ahead of full remediation completion if the remediation plan is adequately defined and the escrow structure is satisfactory.

Excluded Lenders. CMBS conduit lenders, GSE lenders (Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, which are not applicable to industrial in any event), and life insurance companies as a general category will not originate first-mortgage loans on properties with unresolved Phase II findings or active enforcement orders. This is not a negotiating position. It is a credit policy and secondary market requirement that cannot be waived at the origination level. Attorneys who assume that "a lender" generically will be available without understanding this stratification will draw incorrect conclusions about financing feasibility.

What Attorneys Handling Reading CRE Disputes Need to Know

The most common mistake attorneys make in commercial real estate financing disputes is conflating appraisal with capital markets analysis. An appraiser will tell you what the property is worth. A CRE financing expert will tell you whether a loan of a specific size, on a specific asset, under specific conditions, is available in the current market. These are different questions, and the answers frequently diverge in ways that are outcome-determinative in creditor disputes, plan confirmation hearings, and breach of lender duty cases.

Retain a CRE financing expert when the legal question turns on any of the following: whether financing was available to a borrower at a given point in time; whether a lender's conduct conformed to market standards in underwriting, approving, or declining a loan; whether a proposed plan of reorganization is feasible based on its financing assumptions; or whether the maximum loan supportable by an asset's income stream is sufficient to accomplish a specific financial objective such as retiring senior debt. An appraiser is not qualified to answer these questions because they require active, current knowledge of lender underwriting standards, pricing, and credit policy, not property valuation methodology.

A qualified CRE financing expert is distinguished from a retired banker primarily by current market activity. A practitioner who is actively originating loans, maintaining lender relationships, and closing transactions in the current rate and credit environment can speak to what lenders are actually doing today. A retired banker can speak to what lenders did during their tenure. In a market where underwriting standards, rate levels, and environmental credit policies have shifted materially over the past three years, that distinction matters at deposition and at trial.

Before engaging our firm or any CRE financing expert, counsel should gather the following: the current rent roll and lease abstracts; the most recent Phase I and Phase II environmental reports; any Pennsylvania DEP correspondence or Act 2 documentation; the existing loan documents including the maturity date and current outstanding balance; any available appraisals; and the operating history of the property for the prior three years. With that package, a competent financing expert can produce a reliable opinion in two to four weeks.

At trial or in a plan confirmation hearing, CRE financing expert testimony is typically offered to establish the outer bound of available leverage, to rebut an opposing party's claim that refinancing is either readily available or categorically impossible, or to quantify the discount to loan proceeds created by a specific condition such as environmental contamination or below-market lease structure. The testimony is most effective when it is grounded in specific lender conversations and term sheets, not just hypothetical modeling.

Key Takeaways

  • Environmental contamination does not categorically preclude financing on industrial collateral in Reading, but it eliminates the broadest categories of institutional lenders, including CMBS and life company execution, and concentrates the available universe in community banks, regional lenders, and private credit platforms.
  • The maximum supportable loan is determined by the more restrictive of two constraints: the DSCR ceiling derived from in-place NOI and the LTV ceiling applied to the as-is environmentally adjusted value. For this asset type in Reading, that intersection typically produces a maximum loan in the $7.8 million to $8.8 million range at the metrics described above.
  • Below-market in-place rents directly suppress loan proceeds regardless of market rent levels. Lenders underwrite to what the tenant pays, not what the property could theoretically command at rollover.
  • A defined remediation pathway under Pennsylvania Act 2 meaningfully improves financability compared to an open enforcement posture. The difference between a bounded remediation obligation and an undefined one is the difference between lender engagement and lender declination.
  • Personal recourse carve-outs for environmental liability are a non-negotiable requirement at virtually every lender willing to close on this type of asset. Attorneys should treat this as a structural fact of the financing,

Expert Witness: How Construction Defects Affect CRE Financing in Shreveport, LA

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Construction Defect Impact on Financing

The Shreveport Market Context

Shreveport occupies a distinctive position in Louisiana's commercial real estate landscape. It is not a tier-one institutional market like New Orleans or Baton Rouge, but it is far from a tertiary backwater. The metro's commercial fundamentals are shaped by a mix of healthcare anchors, petrochemical and industrial activity, regional retail serving northwest Louisiana and adjacent east Texas, and a gaming and hospitality sector tied to the Bossier City corridor. Understanding those drivers is essential when a financing expert is asked to assess how construction defects impair a commercial asset's loan eligibility, because the starting point of any lender underwriting is the local market's ability to sustain rent and occupancy at the levels the borrower projects.

For industrial and flex product, Shreveport has historically maintained vacancy in the 7 to 10 percent range across its primary nodes along I-20 and the Port of Shreveport-Bossier. Rents for Class B industrial have hovered between $4.50 and $6.50 per square foot NNN depending on clear height, dock access, and proximity to the rail corridor. Those numbers are modest by national standards, which means lenders underwriting here are already working with thinner NOI margins, and any reduction in achievable NOI from a defect condition is magnified when translated into maximum supportable debt. For office, the suburban submarket around the medical corridor and the Texas Street corridor has seen persistent softness, with vacancy in the mid-teens for Class B product, placing downward pressure on achievable rents and compressing the income base that supports leverage. Multifamily, by contrast, has shown resilience: cap rates in Shreveport have generally traded in the 6.5 to 8.0 percent range for stabilized assets, a spread over primary markets that attracts regional and value-add investors who accept the market's modest population dynamics in exchange for higher going-in yields. That spread also means lenders apply more conservative LTV ceilings here than they would in Houston or Dallas for equivalent product.

The broader lending environment in Shreveport reflects the market's profile. Local and regional community banks dominate the construction and bridge lending space. Agency executions through Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac are available for qualifying multifamily but require stabilized occupancy thresholds that defect-impaired properties routinely cannot meet. CMBS execution is theoretically accessible for larger, stabilized assets but is rarely the primary tool for mid-market Shreveport deals. Life company capital has minimal presence here. That market structure matters enormously in a defect litigation context because when defects eliminate a property from the community bank credit box, the fallout is not simply a pricing adjustment. It can be a complete loss of financing access at any commercially reasonable cost.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing an owner-developer in a construction defect dispute involving a newly completed multifamily property in the Shreveport metro. The property, a garden-style apartment community that had recently exited the construction phase, was the subject of claims alleging material defects in the building envelope, including improper flashing installation, substandard waterproofing at penetrations, and foundation drainage deficiencies identified after the first significant rain event following substantial completion. The developer had secured construction financing with the expectation that the project would convert to permanent takeout financing upon stabilization. That conversion was the financial mechanism the developer's business plan depended upon to repay the construction lender and establish long-term hold economics.

Counsel asked CLS CRE to answer three specific questions. First, whether the permanent takeout financing the developer had reasonably anticipated was achievable on the property as built, given the known defect conditions disclosed to prospective lenders. Second, what the quantifiable difference was between the maximum supportable loan on the defect-free pro forma asset versus the defect-impaired asset as it actually existed. Third, what industry-standard lender requirements would apply to a property of this type with disclosed construction defects, and whether any lender product could bridge the gap. Those questions sit precisely at the intersection of CRE capital markets expertise and litigation fact development, and they are questions an appraisal expert typically cannot fully answer because appraisers address value, not financing feasibility and lender underwriting mechanics.

The Financial Analysis

Our methodology began with a reconstruction of the property's stabilized net operating income under two scenarios: the defect-free baseline and the defect-impaired condition. The defect-free baseline used market rents consistent with comparable stabilized garden-style multifamily in the Shreveport submarket, a stabilized vacancy assumption of 8 percent consistent with market conditions, and an expense ratio appropriate for a newly constructed asset with no deferred maintenance. For a 96-unit community in this submarket, that produced a gross potential rent in the range of $1,080,000 annually, with effective gross income after vacancy and credit loss of approximately $993,600, and operating expenses (management, insurance, taxes, maintenance reserves, and administrative) of approximately $348,800, yielding a stabilized NOI of approximately $644,800.

The defect-impaired scenario required a different set of assumptions. Properties with known envelope defects and disclosed litigation attract higher vacancy because prospective tenants avoid units with visible water intrusion damage or units in buildings undergoing remediation work. We applied an elevated vacancy factor of 18 percent reflecting disclosed defect conditions and the marketing friction they create, which is conservative but defensible based on our experience across comparable situations. We also added a management cost premium and a materially higher insurance expense, because property and casualty insurers either surcharge or exclude coverage for buildings with unresolved water intrusion claims, and the higher insurance cost runs directly through the operating expense line. Finally, we capitalized the remediation reserve requirement as a lender-required escrow, which does not reduce NOI directly but does reduce available proceeds and creates a structural impediment that lenders treat as effectively equivalent to reduced collateral value. Under the defect-impaired scenario, effective gross income dropped to approximately $885,600, and operating expenses rose to approximately $421,200, producing a defect-impaired NOI of approximately $464,400, a reduction of $180,400 from the stabilized baseline.

Applying a standard DSCR threshold of 1.25x, which is the market standard minimum for multifamily permanent financing across the lender universe active in Shreveport, we can work backward from both NOI figures to maximum supportable debt service and then to maximum loan amount. At a 7.25 percent interest rate on a 30-year amortization schedule, which reflects market pricing for community bank and regional bank permanent multifamily product in this market at the time of the engagement, the annual constant is approximately 8.18 percent.

For the defect-free asset: NOI of $644,800 divided by the 1.25x DSCR minimum yields maximum supportable annual debt service of $515,840. Dividing by the 8.18 percent annual constant produces a maximum supportable loan of approximately $6,306,000. Against an as-stabilized appraised value of approximately $8,050,000 for a defect-free asset of this type in this submarket (derived from a 8.0 percent market cap rate applied to the stabilized NOI), the resulting LTV is approximately 78.3 percent, which is within the 75 to 80 percent LTV range that community bank and regional lenders typically permit for multifamily permanent financing. The loan is feasible.

For the defect-impaired asset: NOI of $464,400 divided by 1.25x yields maximum supportable annual debt service of $371,520. Dividing by the 8.18 percent annual constant produces a maximum supportable loan of approximately $4,542,000. This represents a financing gap of approximately $1,764,000 relative to the defect-free scenario. But the impairment runs deeper than the income reduction alone. The as-impaired appraised value also contracts because appraisers apply a discount for the disclosed defect condition and the overhang of active litigation. At an impaired value of approximately $6,400,000 (reflecting both the NOI reduction and a market discount for litigation risk), the DSCR-constrained loan of $4,542,000 represents a 71.0 percent LTV, which is technically within guideline but practically problematic because the lender underwriting this asset must also hold a litigation reserve and satisfy itself on the remediation cost estimate, both of which create conditions and contingencies that most institutional permanent lenders are unwilling to accept while litigation is unresolved.

The Complicating Factors

Active litigation disclosure requirements. Every commercial lender requires borrower certification and counsel representation that no material litigation is pending that could impair title, value, or the lender's collateral position. A property that is the subject of active construction defect litigation fails this threshold. Lenders do not simply apply a pricing premium; they decline to originate. This is not a matter of risk appetite. It is a matter of the lender's inability to obtain a standard title policy without a construction defect exclusion, and a clean ALTA policy is a closing condition on every commercial mortgage. The moment defect litigation is disclosed to prospective lenders, the property exits the conventional permanent financing market until the litigation resolves or a fully funded remediation escrow is established and accepted by a lender willing to hold that risk.

Insurance impairment. Property and casualty insurers treat buildings with active water intrusion claims as high-risk collateral. Lenders require evidence of property insurance meeting specific coverage thresholds as a loan condition. If the insurer is providing coverage subject to a defect exclusion, the lender's collateral is exposed. Many insurers in this situation will provide coverage but exclude losses arising from the known defect conditions. Lenders underwriting the resulting policy recognize the exclusion and either decline the loan or require a separate escrow fund to self-insure the excluded peril, which further reduces available proceeds and adds a cost the borrower had not modeled.

Construction lender pressure. The developer in a situation like this is typically under construction loan maturity pressure. Construction loans in Shreveport typically carry 18 to 24 month terms with one extension option, and the extension is conditioned on progress toward stabilization and evidence of takeout lender engagement. A property encumbered by defect litigation cannot demonstrate credible takeout lender engagement, which means extension requests are denied or conditioned on paydown. The resulting pressure forces the developer to either inject equity, sell under duress, or face a forced payoff demand. Each of those outcomes has damages implications that a CRE financing expert can quantify in ways that a general real estate expert cannot.

Remediation cost uncertainty. Lenders underwriting a defect-impaired property that has undergone or is undergoing remediation require a third-party engineer's certification of remediation scope and a cost-to-complete estimate with a 10 to 15 percent contingency. They then typically require an escrow equal to 110 to 125 percent of the estimated remediation cost before origination. In the Shreveport context, where building envelope remediation on a multifamily property can range from $400,000 to over $1,500,000 depending on scope and affected units, this escrow requirement alone can wipe out the equity cushion that would otherwise support a conventional financing execution.

The Shreveport Lender Market

Community and regional banks headquartered in Louisiana and the adjacent Texas markets are the most active commercial real estate lenders in Shreveport. Institutions with significant northwest Louisiana deposit bases have historically been the first call for local developers, and their credit culture reflects familiarity with the market, meaning they will make loans here that national banks decline based purely on market tier. However, that familiarity does not extend to accepting active litigation risk. Community bank credit committees apply standard loan policy requirements, and those policies universally prohibit origination on collateral subject to unresolved construction defect claims. The notion that a relationship bank will "work through" defect litigation is a common borrower misconception. It occasionally happens at the workout level when a bank already holds the loan, but not at origination.

Regional banks with a Gulf South footprint represent the next tier. These institutions have larger balance sheets, more sophisticated underwriting platforms, and occasionally more flexibility on complex collateral situations, but they also have more formal credit policy frameworks enforced by enterprise risk management teams that sit outside the regional lending relationship. For a multifamily asset with defect litigation, the answer from a regional bank's credit committee is functionally the same as from a community bank: decline pending resolution.

Bridge and private debt funds represent the only realistic interim solution for a defect-impaired property that needs to retire a maturing construction loan. These lenders, typically non-bank funds operating with higher yield targets, will underwrite a short-term loan on a defect-impaired property but at materially different pricing: rates in the 10 to 13 percent range, origination fees of 1.5 to 3 points, LTVs capped at 55 to 65 percent of as-is value, and term lengths of 12 to 24 months. The financing gap between a conventional permanent loan and what a bridge fund will advance on the same property, measured in loan proceeds, pricing differential, and fee load, is a quantifiable damages component in many construction defect disputes.

Agency execution through Fannie Mae's small loan program or Freddie Mac's conventional platform is theoretically accessible for multifamily in Shreveport but requires clean title, no pending material litigation, stabilized occupancy of 90 percent or better, and a property condition assessment with no deferred maintenance items classified as immediate or critical. A property with known construction defects and active litigation fails multiple agency eligibility criteria simultaneously, placing it entirely outside agency execution until remediation is complete, litigation is resolved, and stabilization is re-achieved.

What Attorneys Handling Shreveport CRE Disputes Need to Know

The threshold question for retaining a CRE financing expert is whether financing feasibility and capital markets mechanics are in dispute. If the defendant in a construction defect case argues that the developer could have obtained financing on the property despite the disclosed defects, or that the financing gap is smaller than the plaintiff claims, a CRE financing expert is necessary to respond with the specificity the court requires. An appraiser can tell you what a property is worth. A CRE financing expert tells you what loan a lender will actually make on it, under what conditions, at what cost, and what the difference is between that loan and the loan the property would have supported absent the defects. Those are distinct questions, and courts have increasingly recognized that distinction.

The qualifications that matter for a CRE financing expert in this context are active market participation and lender relationship access, not just academic credentials or a history of appraisal work. An expert who can state, from current market experience, that no lender in their active network of over 1,000 institutional relationships would originate a permanent loan on a property with unresolved construction defect litigation carries evidentiary weight that a retired banker or academic consultant cannot match. At CLS CRE, our approximately $1 billion in transaction volume across 50 states provides the foundation for that testimony.

Before engaging a CRE financing expert, counsel should gather the following: the full loan application package submitted to any lender during the defect period, any lender declination letters or conditional approval letters, the construction loan agreement and all modification or extension correspondence, the property's rent rolls covering the period before and after defect discovery, the engineer's report identifying defects and estimating remediation cost, any insurance correspondence addressing coverage limitations, and the developer's original business plan including takeout financing assumptions. That package allows the expert to reconstruct both the financing the developer reasonably anticipated and the financing actually achievable under the impaired conditions.

At trial, CRE financing expert testimony typically addresses three areas: industry standard lender requirements applied to the specific property type and market, quantified financing impairment expressed as a loan proceeds gap and a cost-of-capital differential, and the causal chain connecting the defect condition to the financing impairment. The last of these is where a well-prepared expert adds the most value, because defense counsel will argue that the financing impairment arose from market conditions or borrower credit issues rather than the defects. The expert's job is to isolate the defect-attributable component and defend that isolation under cross-examination.

Key Takeaways

  • Construction defects are not just a cost-of-repair problem. They are a financing eligibility problem. The moment defects are disclosed to a prospective lender, conventional permanent financing is effectively unavailable until the defects are remediated and the litigation is resolved, regardless of the property's underlying income performance.

The difference in maximum supportable loan between a defect-free asset and a defect-impaired asset can be calculated with specificity using standard underwriting methodology, market-rate assumptions, and DSCR-based debt sizing.

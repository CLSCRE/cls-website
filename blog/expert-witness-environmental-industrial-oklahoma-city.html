Expert Witness: Financing a Contaminated Industrial Property in Oklahoma City, OK

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Environmental Contamination Analysis

The Oklahoma City Market Context

Oklahoma City occupies a position in the industrial real estate landscape that is frequently underestimated by coastal capital markets participants but well understood by regional lenders and operators who have been active here through multiple cycles. The metro's industrial base is anchored by aerospace and defense manufacturing, oil and gas equipment fabrication, food processing, and distribution, sectors that generate sustained occupier demand for flex and heavy industrial product across the I-40 corridor, the Meridian Avenue industrial spine, and the southeast submarket near Tinker Air Force Base. That demand profile means Oklahoma City industrial vacancy has remained structurally low, generally running in the 4% to 7% range in recent years even as national industrial markets absorbed the post-pandemic supply wave. Net asking rents for functional warehouse and light industrial space in Oklahoma City have trended between $6.50 and $9.50 per square foot NNN depending on clear height, dock configuration, and submarket, with best-in-class distribution facilities in the northwest and north submarket trading above that band.

For purposes of a contamination dispute, these fundamentals matter in two specific ways. First, they establish that an industrial asset in Oklahoma City carries legitimate income-producing value that a financing analysis can anchor to, provided the environmental condition does not functionally impair that income. Second, they establish the relevant rent comparables against which a below-market lease must be benchmarked, which directly affects how a lender underwrites the asset and what loan proceeds it will support. Oklahoma City's industrial market is not a thin or illiquid market. Institutional equity and regional lenders are active here, and transaction comps exist at sufficient volume to support credible valuation and financing opinions.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by counsel representing a class of secured creditors in a matter involving an industrial warehouse property located in the Oklahoma City metropolitan area. The property was subject to a regulatory enforcement proceeding initiated by the Oklahoma Department of Environmental Quality in connection with 1,4-dioxane and chlorinated volatile organic compound contamination attributable to prior industrial use of the site. The contamination had been discovered during routine due diligence associated with a refinancing attempt, and the enforcement action was pending at the time of our engagement.

The asset itself was a single-tenant industrial warehouse, fully NNN-leased to a creditworthy occupant under a long-term lease. The lease had been executed several years prior at rents that, at origination, approximated market but had since fallen meaningfully below prevailing market rates as Oklahoma City industrial rents appreciated. The creditor class held a first mortgage encumbering the property, and the central question before the court was whether existing senior debt could be retired through commercially available first-mortgage financing given the known environmental condition. CLS CRE was asked to opine on two discrete issues: whether the environmental condition categorically precluded institutional first-mortgage financing, and if not, what maximum loan amount the property could support at current market underwriting standards.

Our engagement required us to produce a Rule 26-compliant expert report addressing lender market conditions, underwriting methodology, environmental risk structuring, and the actual supportable loan quantum. The opinions expressed were grounded in our firm's approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, 1,000-plus active lender relationships across 50 states, and direct experience placing debt on environmentally impaired industrial assets in tertiary and secondary markets, including Oklahoma and the broader South Central region.

The Financial Analysis

The income analysis began with the existing lease, which was structured as a triple-net obligation with the tenant responsible for taxes, insurance, and maintenance. The passing rent produced a gross annual income stream that, after applying a credit loss reserve of 3% (reflecting standard lender practice even on single-tenant NNN assets), generated an effective gross income figure for underwriting purposes. Because the lease was NNN, landlord expenses were minimal, consisting primarily of property management fees that sophisticated lenders will impute even on self-managed assets. We applied a management fee load of 2% of effective gross income and a capital reserve of $0.10 per square foot annually, consistent with what agency and life company lenders require on industrial product.

The resulting net operating income for underwriting was approximately $610,000 annually. This figure is critical because lenders underwrite to in-place NOI, not to market rent, when the in-place lease term provides sufficient loan coverage through the proposed loan term. In this case, the lease term extended well beyond the proposed loan maturity, which is favorable for single-asset underwriting but does not solve the below-market rent problem in the way that lease rollover or mark-to-market assumptions might.

We tested the financing at the following underwriting parameters, each of which reflects current market standards for industrial assets with environmental complications:

  • Debt service coverage ratio: 1.25x minimum, which is the standard threshold applied by regional banks, CMBS platforms, and debt funds on industrial assets with any identified risk factor. Lenders at the conventional 1.20x threshold will typically step up to 1.25x or 1.30x when an environmental condition, even one under active remediation, is disclosed in the loan file.
  • Interest rate assumption: 7.25% fixed for a 5-year term with a 25-year amortization schedule, reflecting current pricing for non-agency, non-life-company execution on a secondary market industrial asset with an environmental qualifier. This rate incorporates a spread premium of approximately 50 to 75 basis points above what a clean asset of identical income quality would command in the same market.
  • Loan-to-value constraint: 65% maximum, consistent with what most lenders apply to environmentally impaired collateral regardless of income coverage. Some lenders impose a 60% cap as a matter of credit policy on any asset with an open ODEQ enforcement action.

At a 1.25x DSCR and 7.25% rate on a 25-year amortization, the annual debt service per dollar of loan principal is approximately $0.0854. Working backward from the $610,000 underwritten NOI: $610,000 divided by 1.25 yields $488,000 in maximum allowable annual debt service. Dividing $488,000 by the $0.0854 constant produces a maximum income-supportable loan of approximately $5,715,000, which we rounded to $5.7 million for reporting purposes.

We then stress-tested this figure against a market value that we independently estimated at approximately $7.2 million based on a blended approach: applying a 7.75% cap rate to the in-place NOI, consistent with current Oklahoma City industrial cap rate observations for single-tenant NNN assets with below-market leases and environmental exposure. At a $7.2 million value, a 65% LTV constraint produces a maximum LTV-limited loan of $4.68 million. The binding constraint in this analysis is therefore the LTV ceiling, not the income coverage, which is a common outcome on below-market-leased assets where the cap rate compression from the secure lease term partially offsets the rent gap.

The conclusion: maximum supportable first-mortgage financing in the current market, net of all risk adjustments and assuming an active but cooperating ODEQ enforcement posture, is approximately $4.68 million to $5.7 million depending on lender category and credit policy, with the lower end of that range representing the more defensible figure in a contested proceeding.

The Complicating Factors

Environmental condition and lender credit policy. The presence of 1,4-dioxane and chlorinated VOCs is among the most lender-adverse contamination profiles in commercial real estate finance. Unlike petroleum hydrocarbons, which are addressed under Oklahoma's Petroleum Storage Tank program with defined remediation pathways and cost predictability, chlorinated VOC plumes and 1,4-dioxane co-contamination frequently involve complex fate-and-transport dynamics, longer remediation timelines, and less predictable cost trajectories. Most institutional lenders, including CMBS platforms, life companies, and agency programs, will not close a loan on a property subject to an open regulatory enforcement action involving these compounds regardless of NOI coverage or LTV. This is not a negotiating position; it is a hard credit policy exclusion at many institutions. The financing universe therefore contracts to environmental specialty lenders, debt funds with explicit environmental credit programs, and certain regional banks with in-house environmental credit expertise.

Below-market rent and loan proceeds compression. The NNN structure provides lender comfort on operating expense exposure, but the below-market rent creates a gap between the asset's income-based value and its market-rent-based value. A lender cannot underwrite to hypothetical market rents if the in-place lease prevents rent realization for the remaining term. This means the loan proceeds available today, during the lease term, are materially lower than what would be available at lease expiration and mark-to-market renewal. In a restructuring or Plan of Reorganization context, this creates a temporal mismatch: the existing senior debt may have been sized at origination based on different market conditions or a higher rent basis, and current financing cannot replicate those original proceeds.

ODEQ enforcement action and title insurance constraints. An active enforcement action by the Oklahoma Department of Environmental Quality creates a lien risk that title insurers must address. Most standard lenders require clean title insurance as a condition to closing. Title underwriters will not provide affirmative coverage over an ODEQ enforcement lien without a specific endorsement, and that endorsement requires either a No Further Action letter or an approved remediation agreement with ODEQ establishing a defined cost ceiling and remediation schedule. In the absence of that documentation, lenders insisting on clean title cannot close, further narrowing the lender pool.

Environmental insurance as a partial mitigation tool. Lenders with environmental credit programs will frequently require pollution legal liability insurance as a condition to funding. This coverage can address unknown future remediation costs and third-party liability claims, but it does not eliminate the lender's exposure to value impairment during active remediation. Premiums for PLL coverage on a site with known 1,4-dioxane contamination are material and must be factored into the operating cost load, which further compresses underwritten NOI and reduces the supportable loan amount.

The Oklahoma City Lender Market

For a clean industrial asset in Oklahoma City, the active lender market includes CMBS conduit platforms, regional banks headquartered in Oklahoma and Texas, life company correspondents placing paper in Tier 2 markets, SBA 504 programs for owner-users, and debt funds targeting yield plays in secondary markets. The Oklahoma City market benefits from a strong community and regional banking culture, including institutions with deep local industrial market knowledge and flexible credit structures that larger institutional lenders cannot match.

For this asset, with an open environmental enforcement action and below-market rent, the realistic lender universe is considerably narrower. The categories of lenders that remain viable, and their specific parameters, are as follows:

  • Environmental specialty debt funds: These platforms exist specifically to finance assets with known contamination. They are capitalized by institutional equity and operate at higher coupons, typically 300 to 500 basis points above conventional market pricing, but they will underwrite to in-place NOI on environmentally impaired assets if an approved remediation plan is in place. Maximum LTVs are typically 55% to 60% and loan terms are short, generally 2 to 3 years with extension options tied to remediation milestones.
  • Oklahoma and Texas regional banks with environmental credit desks: Several mid-sized regional banks active in the Oklahoma City market have credit officers with environmental site experience who can underwrite contaminated collateral with appropriate risk structuring, including environmental escrow reserves and PLL insurance requirements. These lenders can go to 65% LTV if the enforcement posture is cooperative and a remediation agreement is in place, but they will not close with an open, contested enforcement action.
  • Community development lenders and CDFI platforms: In limited circumstances involving brownfield redevelopment objectives, CDFI financing combined with state brownfield tax credits can supplement conventional proceeds. This is a complex structure requiring legal coordination but has been executed successfully in Oklahoma City on industrial redevelopment projects.

CMBS platforms, life companies, and agency programs are categorically unavailable for this asset in its current condition. Any expert opinion suggesting otherwise would be inconsistent with current market practice.

What Attorneys Handling Oklahoma City CRE Disputes Need to Know

A CRE financing expert witness serves a function that an appraiser cannot replicate. An appraiser determines what a property is worth. A CRE financing expert determines what debt the market will actually provide against that property, on what terms, from which sources, and under what conditions. These are distinct questions with distinct methodologies, and in reorganization proceedings, lien priority disputes, and creditor adequacy-of-protection arguments, the debt market question is often the operative one. Whether a secured creditor can be paid from market financing is a capital markets question, not a valuation question.

Attorneys should retain a CRE financing expert when the central dispute involves any of the following: feasibility of refinancing as a Plan element, adequacy of protection for a secured creditor, reasonableness of a loan commitment or term sheet, lender liability claims involving underwriting standards, or allegations that a borrower could have obtained financing it claims was unavailable. In environmental contamination matters specifically, the expert must be able to opine not just on whether lenders exist in the market generally, but on which specific categories of lenders will underwrite contaminated collateral, what their credit policy requirements are, and what loan proceeds they will actually fund given the specific contamination profile and regulatory posture.

A qualified CRE financing expert is not simply a retired banker. Retirement from a single institution produces knowledge of that institution's credit policy, not current market conditions across the active lender universe. A credible expert must have current, active engagement with multiple lender categories, direct experience placing debt in the relevant market and asset class, and the ability to identify specific lenders who would or would not close on the subject asset. CLS CRE's 1,000-plus active lender relationships and current transaction volume provide the market connectivity that credible expert testimony requires.

Before engaging our firm or any CRE financing expert, counsel should gather the following: the existing loan documents and any default or maturity notices, the current rent roll and lease abstracts, all environmental assessment and regulatory correspondence including any ODEQ notices, the most recent title commitment or title report, any existing appraisal, and any prior financing correspondence or rejected loan applications. The richer the factual record, the more precise and defensible the expert opinion.

At trial or in a Plan confirmation hearing, CRE financing expert testimony is typically presented through a written report compliant with Rule 26, followed by direct testimony establishing the market conditions and the specific financing conclusion, and cross-examination on methodology and assumptions. The most effective expert testimony is anchored to specific lender market practices, specific underwriting calculations, and a clear articulation of the basis for each conclusion, all of which distinguish a capital markets expert from a generalist who offers opinions about financing markets without direct market engagement.

Key Takeaways

  • Environmental contamination does not categorically preclude first-mortgage financing, but it fundamentally narrows the lender universe and increases pricing, reduces proceeds, and imposes structural requirements that conventional lenders do not apply to clean assets.
  • The binding loan constraint on this asset is LTV, not income coverage. Below-market rents compress the income-based value, and the environmental risk premium applied to the cap rate further reduces market value, making LTV the limiting factor even where NOI coverage is mathematically adequate.
  • Maximum supportable first-mortgage financing in this scenario is approximately $4.68 million to $5.7 million, a range driven by lender category, environmental credit policy, and the gap between in-place rent and market rent.
  • CMBS, life companies, and agency lenders are not available for this asset in its current condition. Expert opinions that identify these sources as available financing options for an asset with an open ODEQ enforcement action involving chlorinated VOCs are inconsistent with current market practice and should be challenged on cross-examination.
  • An approved remediation agreement with ODEQ, combined with pollution legal liability insurance, is the minimum threshold required to access even the environmental specialty lender market. Without these elements, the financing universe collapses further and may not support any commercially available first-mortgage loan.
  • A CRE financing expert provides opinions that an appraiser cannot, specifically on the availability, structure, and quantum of debt that the current market will actually provide against a specific asset with specific risk attributes, and this distinction is material in reorganization and creditor rights proceedings.
  • Oklahoma City's industrial market fundamentals are supportive of financing for clean assets, with low vacancy, stable rents, and an active regional lender market; but those fundamentals do not override environmental credit policy constraints that are applied consistently across lender categories.

Expert Witness: Financing a Contaminated Industrial Property in Casper, WY

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Environmental Contamination Analysis

The Casper Market Context

Casper, Wyoming sits at a crossroads that most coastal capital markets professionals underestimate. As the second-largest city in Wyoming and the commercial hub of Natrona County, Casper's commercial real estate market is tightly linked to energy sector cycles, particularly oil and gas extraction in the Powder River Basin and Pinedale Anticline regions. That energy dependency creates a market with characteristics that are genuinely distinct from generic secondary or tertiary market assumptions: industrial demand driven by oilfield services companies, equipment staging yards, and logistics operations rather than by e-commerce fulfillment or last-mile delivery. When energy activity compresses, Casper's industrial vacancy moves materially and quickly. When it expands, absorption can be surprisingly aggressive for a market of roughly 60,000 residents.

Industrial vacancy in Casper has historically ranged between 6% and 14% depending on the energy cycle, with functional Class B and C warehouse product typically more exposed to cycle swings than institutional-grade facilities. Average asking rents for industrial warehouse space in the Casper submarket have generally ranged from $6.50 to $10.50 per square foot NNN depending on clear height, dock configuration, and proximity to major freight corridors including Interstate 25. Larger, single-tenant NNN-leased industrial buildings with creditworthy tenants trade at cap rates in the 7.0% to 8.5% range in the current environment, reflecting both yield expectations appropriate to the market and the illiquidity premium that institutional buyers demand for Wyoming assets. Sales velocity is low by coastal standards, with meaningful industrial trades occurring infrequently enough that each transaction carries outsized weight in any comparable analysis.

For litigation purposes, these market characteristics matter enormously. A contaminated industrial warehouse in Casper is not being analyzed against a backdrop of deep liquidity and abundant comparable data. It is being analyzed in a thin market where lender appetite is selective, where environmental risk carries amplified stigma because there are fewer substitute assets to absorb displaced demand, and where the pool of motivated buyers and refinancing lenders is categorically smaller than in a major metropolitan industrial market. Attorneys and courts handling disputes involving Casper industrial assets need to understand that financing feasibility conclusions drawn from national databases or coastal market experience will systematically misstate the actual credit conditions applicable to this specific asset type in this specific geography.

The Engagement

Counsel for a group of secured creditors in a regulatory enforcement proceeding retained Commercial Lending Solutions to provide a financing feasibility opinion on an industrial warehouse property in the Casper market. The asset was a single-story, multi-bay warehouse facility leased on a triple-net basis to a single industrial tenant under a long-term lease. The property had been identified by Wyoming Department of Environmental Quality as a site of concern due to the presence of chlorinated volatile organic compounds, specifically a chlorinated solvent plume consistent with historical industrial cleaning or degreasing operations, along with potential 1,4-dioxane co-contamination, a common stabilizer used with 1,1,1-trichloroethane that is increasingly regulated as a primary contaminant of concern in its own right.

The core legal questions before us were precise: first, does the documented environmental condition, at the stage of regulatory enforcement then pending, categorically preclude first-mortgage financing in the current commercial lending market? Second, if financing is not categorically precluded, what is the maximum supportable loan amount at current market underwriting standards, and is that amount sufficient to retire the existing senior secured debt? Third, what conditions, covenants, or structural features would a lender require, and do those conditions represent market-standard requirements or material departures from normal underwriting that would reduce the pool of available lenders to a negligible subset?

CLS CRE was retained specifically because these questions sit at the intersection of environmental risk, lender credit policy, and market feasibility, a zone where appraisers establish value and environmental engineers establish contamination levels, but neither discipline addresses how commercial mortgage lenders actually underwrite and price environmental risk in a deal structure. That gap is where financing expert testimony becomes essential.

The Financial Analysis

The income analysis began with the existing NNN lease, which was the property's entire revenue stream. Because the lease was structured as a true triple-net obligation with the tenant responsible for taxes, insurance, and maintenance, the contractual base rent flowed directly to net operating income with minimal adjustment. The lease rent was confirmed to be below current market asking rents for comparable industrial space in the Casper submarket, a fact that creates both a risk and a potential upside scenario depending on lease term remaining and tenant creditworthiness. For purposes of financing feasibility, lenders will underwrite to the lesser of in-place contractual rent or market rent if the lease term is short, and to in-place rent if the term is long and the tenant is creditworthy.

For this analysis, we assumed an in-place NNN rent producing a stabilized NOI of approximately $285,000 annually on a building of approximately 42,000 square feet, reflecting a per-square-foot rent in the lower range of the Casper industrial market and a lease with meaningful term remaining. This NOI figure was stress-tested against a market-rent scenario, which produced a modestly higher NOI, but we conservatively underwrote to in-place income given the below-market positioning and the environmental overhang on residual value assumptions.

Conventional commercial mortgage lenders underwriting NNN industrial product in secondary and tertiary markets currently apply a minimum debt service coverage ratio of 1.25x for stabilized assets, with many lenders requiring 1.30x for markets with less liquidity or assets with identified risk factors. For an environmentally impacted property, the applicable DSCR floor moves to 1.30x to 1.35x because lenders are implicitly pricing in the scenario where environmental remediation costs escalate, tenant renewal probability decreases, or resale value at loan maturity is impaired. We applied a 1.30x DSCR threshold as the market standard for this asset and risk profile.

On rate and amortization: the applicable rate for a five-year fixed commercial real estate loan on industrial product in this risk category, in the current rate environment, falls in the range of 7.25% to 8.00% depending on the lender category and the environmental risk treatment. We modeled at 7.50%, which reflects a community bank or regional bank pricing scenario rather than a CMBS or agency execution, which are not available for contaminated properties. On a 25-year amortization schedule, which is standard for non-institutional industrial assets of this type, a 7.50% note rate produces an annual debt service constant of approximately 8.74% of the loan amount.

The maximum supportable loan calculation works as follows: take the NOI of $285,000, divide by the required DSCR of 1.30x, which yields maximum allowable annual debt service of approximately $219,230. Divide that debt service by the annual constant of 8.74% and the maximum supportable loan on a cash flow basis is approximately $2,508,000, or roughly $2.5 million. At a stabilized value of approximately $3.4 million (derived from an 8.25% cap rate applied to in-place NOI), the resulting loan-to-value is approximately 74%, which is within conventional LTV limits for industrial assets of 70% to 75%. However, on an environmentally impacted property, lenders will apply an additional LTV haircut of 10% to 20% to account for potential diminution in collateral value from remediation cost uncertainty and stigma. Applying a 15% haircut to the stabilized value produces a lender-adjusted collateral value of approximately $2.89 million, and at a 70% LTV ceiling on adjusted value, the LTV-constrained maximum loan is approximately $2.02 million.

In this engagement, the binding constraint was the LTV ceiling on adjusted value, not the DSCR calculation. The maximum supportable loan was therefore approximately $2.0 million to $2.1 million, and the critical legal question became whether that amount was sufficient to retire the senior secured debt in full.

The Complicating Factors

Phase II Findings and Regulatory Status: The most significant complicating factor is the stage of regulatory proceedings at the time a lender would be underwriting. Wyoming DEQ's Industrial Siting Division and Voluntary Cleanup Program have distinct tracks for contamination response, and a lender's willingness to proceed depends heavily on whether a Remedial Action Plan has been approved, whether a No Further Action letter is in sight, or whether the site is still in the assessment phase with remediation costs unknown. Most conventional lenders will not close a loan on a property with an open enforcement action and no approved remediation pathway. If the RAP has been approved and cost estimates are capped and bonded, a narrower but real lender market exists.

1,4-Dioxane Specifically: Chlorinated solvent contamination, while serious, is a known quantity in lender environmental risk underwriting. 1,4-Dioxane is a more recent and rapidly evolving regulatory concern. The EPA's 2023 designation of 1,4-dioxane as a hazardous substance under CERCLA, combined with the absence of established Maximum Contaminant Levels in many states, means that remediation cost estimates for 1,4-dioxane co-contamination carry wider uncertainty bounds than traditional chlorinated VOC plumes. Lenders are aware of this uncertainty, and their environmental consultants will flag it explicitly in Phase II review. This translates directly into larger contingency reserves required at closing and a further reduction in lender-adjusted collateral value.

Below-Market Rent and Lease Rollover Risk: A tenant willing to occupy a contaminated industrial property at below-market rents may be doing so because alternative options are limited or because the tenant itself contributed to the contamination and has a regulatory obligation to remain. Either scenario introduces lease rollover risk that lenders price into underwriting. If the current tenant is itself a responsible party, the prospect of that tenant's departure or financial impairment creates a scenario where the property becomes vacant and encumbered simultaneously, which is the worst-case collateral outcome for a secured lender.

Lender Environmental Indemnity and CERCLA Secured Creditor Exemption: Lenders in contaminated property transactions rely on the CERCLA secured creditor exemption under 42 U.S.C. Section 9601(20)(E) to limit their liability exposure. However, that exemption requires that the lender not participate in management of the facility. Lenders will require robust environmental indemnity agreements, escrow reserves for remediation cost overruns, and in many cases a pollution legal liability insurance policy naming the lender as an additional insured. These are closeable requirements, but they add cost and reduce the number of lenders willing to engage, particularly in a market like Casper where institutional lenders with dedicated environmental risk departments are not the dominant capital source.

The Casper Lender Market

Casper's commercial lending landscape for industrial assets is dominated by Wyoming-chartered community banks and regional banks headquartered in Denver or Salt Lake City with Wyoming operations. Institutions in this category include locally chartered banks with Natrona County deposit bases and regional banks that follow energy sector borrowers across Wyoming, Colorado, and Utah. These lenders know the Casper market, understand energy cycle dynamics, and are willing to underwrite industrial product with strong in-place tenancy. However, their environmental risk tolerance is generally lower than that of larger institutions with dedicated environmental credit departments, and their hold capacity on a single loan is typically $2 million to $5 million before participations are required.

CMBS execution is not available for contaminated properties. The rating agencies and B-piece buyers that underwrite CMBS pools treat environmental impairment as a disqualifying condition absent a No Further Action letter or equivalent regulatory closure. Similarly, Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac industrial programs are not applicable here, and SBA 504 financing, while theoretically available for owner-occupied industrial, does not apply to an investment property NNN-leased to a third party.

Debt funds and bridge lenders represent the most likely institutional capital source for a property at the assessment or early remediation stage. These lenders charge rates in the 9.50% to 11.50% range and require significant origination fees, but they have underwriting frameworks designed for transitional or impaired collateral. The tradeoff is that debt fund execution at those rates further compresses the DSCR-constrained maximum loan, since higher debt service reduces the amount of NOI available to service the debt. At an 10.50% rate on a 25-year amortization, the debt service constant rises to approximately 11.12%, and the DSCR-constrained maximum loan drops to approximately $1.97 million, effectively the same ceiling but reached by a different constraint.

The practical conclusion for the Casper market is that a contaminated industrial NNN property with an active enforcement case has access to a real but narrow lender universe: community banks willing to engage if the regulatory path is clear, or debt funds willing to engage at a significant rate premium regardless of regulatory status. Neither category provides the volume or pricing of a clean asset refinancing.

What Attorneys Handling Casper CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to Retain a CRE Financing Expert: Retain a financing expert whenever the dispute requires an opinion on whether debt financing is available, at what amount, on what terms, and whether those terms would have been available at a specific historical date. These questions arise in lender liability cases, plan confirmation hearings, secured creditor disputes, receiver proceedings, and environmental enforcement actions where the property's ability to support a refinancing is a disputed fact. An appraiser establishes value. A financing expert establishes whether a loan can be made against that value, and at what size, given current lender credit conditions. Those are categorically different opinions.

What a Financing Expert Testifies To That an Appraiser Cannot: Appraisers apply market cap rates to income to derive value. They do not opine on lender underwriting criteria, DSCR thresholds, LTV overlays for environmental risk, available lender categories, rate environments, or deal structure requirements. A financing expert addresses all of those issues from the perspective of a capital markets practitioner who has actually originated, placed, and structured loans on comparable assets in comparable conditions. That market knowledge is what makes the testimony useful and distinguishable from the appraiser's report.

What Qualifies a CRE Financing Expert: The relevant qualification is active deal origination and lender relationship management in the asset class at issue, not academic credentials or prior banking employment. A retired banker who has not originated a loan in seven years does not have current knowledge of lender credit boxes, rate environments, or underwriting standards. CLS CRE is active in all 50 states with approximately $1 billion in transaction volume and over 1,000 lender relationships, which means our opinions on what lenders require and what they will actually do are based on current market transactions, not historical or theoretical frameworks.

Information Counsel Should Gather Before the Engagement: Provide the current lease in full, including all amendments and estoppels. Provide Phase I and Phase II environmental site assessments. Provide any regulatory correspondence including DEQ notices, administrative orders, and any approved or proposed Remedial Action Plans. Provide the existing loan documents, the current outstanding debt balance, and any appraisals or broker opinions of value already in the record. Provide operating statements for the prior 36 months if available. The more complete the file at the outset, the more specific and defensible the financing opinion will be.

How Financing Expert Testimony Is Used: In plan confirmation hearings, financing expert testimony is used to establish the feasibility of a proposed reorganization plan that relies on refinancing as an exit. In secured creditor disputes, it establishes whether a foreclosing lender is being made whole or is suffering a deficiency given actual market conditions. In environmental enforcement proceedings, it establishes whether a contaminated property can support a mortgage sufficient to fund remediation or retire existing obligations. The testimony must be grounded in specific, contemporaneous market data and documented lender interactions, not theoretical models, to withstand Daubert scrutiny.

Key Takeaways

  • Environmental contamination does not categorically preclude first-mortgage financing, but it materially reduces the available lender universe and triggers LTV haircuts of 10% to 20% on adjusted collateral value, which can be the binding constraint on maximum loan size regardless of DSCR support.
  • 1,4-Dioxane co-contamination introduces regulatory uncertainty beyond traditional chlorinated VOC analysis because remediation cost estimates carry wider contingency ranges, and lenders will require larger escrow reserves and pollution legal liability insurance as a condition of closing.
  • In the Casper industrial market, the lender universe for impaired assets is real but narrow, consisting primarily of Wyoming community banks with cleared regulatory pathways or debt funds willing to underwrite transitional risk at rates of 9.50% to 11.50%, which further compresses borrowing capacity.

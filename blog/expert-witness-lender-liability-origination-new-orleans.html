Expert Witness: Commercial Loan Origination Standards in New Orleans, LA

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Lender Liability and Loan Origination Standards

The New Orleans Market Context

New Orleans occupies a structurally unusual position in the commercial real estate lending universe. It is not a gateway market like Houston or Atlanta, but it is far from a tertiary backwater. The city's economic base is a hybrid of tourism, port and logistics activity, healthcare anchored by a substantial medical corridor, and a growing creative economy. That mix creates financing dynamics that differ materially from other Southeast markets, and those differences show up directly in lender underwriting, loan sizing, and standards of care disputes.

On the multifamily side, New Orleans has seen cap rate compression driven by persistent housing supply constraints, post-Katrina land use complications, and strong short-term rental demand in certain submarkets. Garden-style and mid-rise multifamily assets in Metairie, Mid-City, and the Warehouse District have traded in the 5.25 to 6.75 percent cap rate range depending on vintage, condition, and rent roll stability. Those spreads matter in a lender liability context because they directly affect what a competent underwriter should have been modeling as exit value and refinance capacity.

Office fundamentals in New Orleans have been under sustained pressure. The Central Business District has vacancy rates in the low-to-mid twenties on a percentage basis, a direct consequence of both national remote-work trends and localized contraction among legal, energy, and financial services tenants. Any lender originating a CBD office loan in the past three years without applying a meaningful vacancy stress test was operating outside reasonable market standards. That is a factual observation with direct expert witness relevance.

Industrial and flex product along the I-10 corridor and in Jefferson Parish has performed considerably better, with vacancy rates in the 4 to 7 percent range and asking rents that have increased as Gulf Coast logistics demand has grown. Hospitality assets, which are central to the New Orleans economy, carry their own underwriting complexity rooted in the market's extreme seasonal revenue concentration, hurricane exposure, and insurance cost volatility. Each of these asset-type dynamics has direct bearing on what an industry-standard underwriting process should have produced and what a reasonably competent lender or broker should have advised.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing a commercial real estate borrower in a lender liability dispute arising from a loan originated on a mixed-use property in the New Orleans market. The asset involved ground-floor retail space with multifamily residential units above, located in a dense urban submarket with strong foot traffic but complicated leasing history. The loan had been originated by a regional lender using a mortgage broker intermediary, and the borrower alleged that both the lender and the broker failed to meet applicable standards of care during the origination process.

The core legal and financial questions presented to CLS CRE fell into three categories. First, whether the loan was sized consistently with market-standard underwriting for a mixed-use asset of this type in this submarket, specifically whether the income analysis, DSCR calculation, and LTV constraints applied were within the range a reasonably competent commercial lender would have used. Second, whether the loan terms as disclosed to the borrower at commitment were consistent with what the broker and lender had represented during the application process, and whether deviations from those terms were within the range of normal market variation or constituted material misrepresentation. Third, whether the mortgage broker's conduct, including its lender selection, processing of the borrower's financial package, and disclosure of material risk factors, met the standard of care applicable to a competent commercial mortgage broker operating in the Louisiana market in the applicable time period.

Counsel provided us with the loan file, the commitment letter and its amendments, the broker's engagement agreement and fee disclosures, the appraisal, the rent roll, the operating statements for three prior years, and the correspondence between the parties from initial application through closing. Our engagement involved producing a written expert report pursuant to Rule 26 of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure and preparing for deposition and potential trial testimony.

The Financial Analysis

The foundational question in any loan origination standards case is whether the loan was sized correctly given the income the property was actually generating, or supportably projected to generate, at the time of origination. That requires rebuilding the underwriting from the income stream up, not from the loan amount down.

For the subject asset, we analyzed three years of actual operating statements plus the trailing twelve months as presented to the lender. The property had eleven residential units above ground-floor retail space totaling approximately 3,200 square feet. Gross potential residential rent, based on in-place leases, was approximately $198,000 annually. The retail space was occupied by a single tenant on a lease with less than eighteen months of remaining term at origination, generating $36,000 annually. Total gross potential income was therefore approximately $234,000.

Market-standard underwriting for a mixed-use asset of this type, in a market like New Orleans, would apply a vacancy and credit loss assumption of at least 7 to 10 percent on the residential component and a more conservative 15 to 20 percent on the single-tenant retail component given the near-term lease expiration risk. Applying a blended 12 percent vacancy factor to total gross income produced an effective gross income of approximately $206,000. Operating expenses, including property taxes, insurance, utilities, maintenance, management at 8 percent of effective gross income, and a capital reserve of $400 per residential unit annually, produced total operating expenses of approximately $92,700. Stabilized net operating income was therefore approximately $113,300.

The loan as originated carried a five-year fixed rate of 7.375 percent with a 25-year amortization schedule and a balloon at year five. At a market-standard DSCR floor of 1.25x, the maximum supportable annual debt service was $90,640. At that debt service figure and the stated rate and amortization, the maximum supportable loan was approximately $1,082,000. The lender originated a loan of $1,425,000 against an appraised value of $2,050,000, implying an LTV of approximately 69.5 percent. The problem was not the LTV, which was within conventional parameters for this asset type. The problem was the DSCR implied by the actual loan amount and debt service, which we calculated at approximately 0.96x based on properly underwritten NOI. The lender's file reflected a stated DSCR of 1.31x, which could only be achieved by using gross potential income without vacancy adjustment and excluding capital reserves from the expense load. Neither practice is consistent with market-standard underwriting for this asset class.

A competent lender underwriting to an honest 1.25x DSCR would have originated a loan of approximately $1,080,000, not $1,425,000, or would have required additional collateral, a personal guaranty with verified net worth, or a cash reserve structure. None of those alternatives were employed. That delta, approximately $345,000 in excess loan proceeds, is the financial expression of the underwriting deficiency, and it is directly relevant to damages analysis.

The Complicating Factors

Short-Lease Retail Exposure: When a single-tenant retail space with fewer than 18 months of remaining lease term represents more than 15 percent of a property's income, market-standard underwriting requires either a rollover reserve, a lease renewal contingency in the loan documents, or a downward adjustment in stabilized NOI to reflect the probability of re-leasing at potentially lower rates or after a vacancy period. In New Orleans ground-floor retail markets, the re-leasing timeline for small retail spaces can extend six to eighteen months depending on submarket and configuration. The lender's failure to apply any rollover reserve or lease-adjusted NOI was a departure from what a reasonably competent lender would have done.

Insurance Cost Volatility: New Orleans insurance premiums for commercial properties have escalated sharply in the post-Ida environment. Wind and hail coverage, flood insurance through both the NFIP and private markets, and general property insurance have combined to produce annual insurance costs for mixed-use properties that frequently exceed 2.0 to 2.5 percent of replacement cost. A competent underwriter working in this market must use current actual insurance costs from a recent binder or quote, not historical figures from two or three years prior. In the subject loan, the lender used insurance costs from a prior-year statement that were approximately 40 percent below the borrower's current renewal premium. That single-line underwriting error reduced the stated NOI by approximately $14,200 and inflated the DSCR calculation materially.

Broker Disclosure Standards: The mortgage broker in this matter received a lender fee that was not fully disclosed to the borrower in the engagement agreement. Louisiana law and applicable RESPA and industry standards require commercial mortgage brokers to disclose material compensation arrangements. Beyond the legal issue, industry practice requires a competent commercial mortgage broker to present borrowers with at minimum three competitive financing scenarios and to document the rationale for the recommended lender selection. The broker's file contained no evidence of a competitive lender survey, no documented rationale for lender selection, and no written disclosure of the yield spread or back-end fee arrangement.

Mixed-Use Underwriting Classification: Lenders treat mixed-use assets differently depending on whether they classify the property as primarily residential or primarily commercial. That classification affects which regulatory framework applies, what reserve requirements are imposed, and how debt service is calculated. In this case, the lender classified the property as a commercial real estate loan, which allowed it to apply more flexible underwriting standards, but then failed to apply the commercial-grade NOI discipline that classification requires. A properly underwritten commercial loan on this asset would have demanded documented rent rolls with executed leases, three years of tax returns, and a current rent comparables analysis from the local market. Several of those elements were missing from the file.

The New Orleans Lender Market

The New Orleans commercial lending market is meaningfully stratified. Understanding which lender categories are active, at what loan sizes, and with what underwriting standards is essential to evaluating whether a lender or broker met the applicable standard of care for a given deal type.

Community and Regional Banks: New Orleans has a deep community banking presence, with several locally headquartered institutions that maintain active CRE lending portfolios. These lenders are typically active on loans from $500,000 to $10 million, are portfolio lenders who hold loans on balance sheet, and apply relationship-based underwriting. Their credit boxes tend to require 1.20x to 1.25x DSCR, LTV at or below 70 to 75 percent on mixed-use or retail assets, and personal guarantees. They are generally the most accessible execution for stabilized, income-producing assets with clean operating histories. Several regional banks headquartered in Baton Rouge and Houston also maintain New Orleans commercial loan production offices and are active in the $5 million to $25 million range.

CMBS and Conduit Lenders: Conduit execution is available in the New Orleans market for stabilized, institutionally tenanted assets with loan amounts typically above $5 million. CMBS lenders apply more rigid underwriting standards, including hard DSCR floors of 1.25x on most asset types and independent third-party appraisal and environmental requirements. The rigidity of CMBS underwriting is actually relevant in a standards-of-care analysis because it establishes an objective benchmark for what a sophisticated institutional lender believes constitutes adequate income coverage.

Debt Funds and Bridge Lenders: For assets with lease-up risk, renovation exposure, or complicated operating histories, private debt funds and bridge lenders are active in the New Orleans market. These lenders price at higher spreads, often 300 to 500 basis points over benchmark rates, and typically require stronger exit assumptions and personal recourse. A competent broker who understood that a subject property carried significant retail lease rollover risk might well have recommended a bridge lender with a holdback structure rather than attempting to fit the asset into a permanent loan box that the income could not honestly support.

SBA and USDA Programs: For owner-occupied commercial properties and certain rural-adjacent assets in the broader New Orleans MSA, SBA 7(a) and 504 programs remain active financing tools. A competent broker advising a borrower-occupant on a mixed-use property should have evaluated these programs as part of any competitive financing analysis.

What Attorneys Handling New Orleans CRE Disputes Need to Know

Retaining a CRE financing expert is not the same as retaining an appraiser, and the distinction matters significantly in loan origination and lender liability cases. An appraiser addresses value at a point in time. A CRE financing expert addresses whether the loan made sense given the income stream, the applicable market lending standards, the lender's and broker's conduct during origination, and what the financing market would have actually supported. Those are different questions requiring different expertise.

When to retain a CRE financing expert: Counsel should consider retaining a CRE financing expert when the dispute involves allegations that a loan was improperly sized, that loan terms were misrepresented or materially changed from commitment to closing, that a broker failed to present competitive financing alternatives, that underwriting was manipulated to achieve approval, or that a lender's internal credit policies were violated. CLS CRE has been retained in matters involving all of these fact patterns.

What a financing expert can testify to that an appraiser cannot: A CRE financing expert can opine on whether the underwriting methodology was consistent with market practice, whether the DSCR calculation was manipulated through improper income inclusion or expense exclusion, whether the lender's credit memo reflected the actual risk profile of the loan, whether the broker's lender selection and disclosure practices met industry standards, and what the loan market would have actually offered to a borrower with this credit profile on this asset at this point in time. An appraiser cannot provide those opinions, regardless of their valuation credentials.

Qualifications that matter: A qualified CRE financing expert should have active transactional experience placing commercial loans in the current market, not merely historical banking experience. The lending market changes rapidly, and a retired banker who has been out of active production for five or more years may not be able to credibly testify about current lender credit boxes, pricing benchmarks, or industry practice. CLS CRE has approximately $1 billion in transaction volume across our platform, maintains relationships with more than 1,000 lenders, and is actively originating commercial loans across all 50 states, including current active production in the Louisiana market.

What counsel should gather before the expert engagement: The complete loan file including all credit memoranda, the appraisal and any review appraisal, all versions of the commitment letter, the broker's engagement agreement and fee disclosures, rent rolls and operating statements presented to the lender, the borrower's original loan application, all correspondence between the parties, any internal lender communications obtained in discovery, and the recorded loan documents. The more complete the file provided at engagement, the more precise and defensible the expert analysis will be.

Use at trial or in proceedings: CRE financing expert testimony is most effectively used to establish the objective benchmark of what a reasonably competent lender or broker would have done, then to measure the defendant's conduct against that benchmark. In depositions, the expert can challenge opposing experts on the technical adequacy of their underwriting reconstruction. At trial, the expert can walk a jury or judge through a loan file in plain terms while demonstrating the specific departures from market-standard practice.

Key Takeaways

  • DSCR manipulation is the most common technical deficiency in loan origination disputes. Whether achieved through inflated income assumptions, understated expenses, or omission of required reserves, a DSCR that does not reflect stabilized net operating income is a measurable departure from market-standard underwriting that expert testimony can quantify precisely.
  • New Orleans insurance cost volatility is a specific and recurring underwriting failure point. Any loan originated in this market without current, verified insurance costs from a recent binder or quote is underwritten on a defective expense basis, and the resulting NOI inflation is calculable.
  • Short-lease retail exposure requires explicit underwriting treatment. A single-tenant retail space with fewer than 24 months of remaining term must trigger either rollover reserves, lease-contingent loan conditions, or a haircut to stabilized NOI. Failure to apply any of these treatments is a departure from market practice.
  • Broker disclosure obligations are enforceable standards, not aspirational guidelines. Failure to disclose compensation arrangements, failure to present competitive financing alternatives, and failure to document lender selection rationale are each independently actionable professional negligence exposures for a commercial mortgage broker.
  • Lender classification of a mixed-use asset has direct underwriting consequences. Whether a property is treated as residential or commercial determines the applicable regulatory framework, reserve requirements, and the documentation standards that a competent lender must apply. Misclassification that benefits the lender at the borrower's expense is a cognizable origination deficiency.
  • The financing market alternative matters for damages.

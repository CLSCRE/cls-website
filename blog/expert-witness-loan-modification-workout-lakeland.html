Expert Witness: Commercial Loan Modification Standards in Lakeland, FL

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Loan Modification and Workout Dispute

The Lakeland Market Context

Lakeland, Florida sits at the geographic and economic midpoint of the I-4 corridor between Tampa and Orlando, and that position has made it one of the more consequential secondary commercial real estate markets in the Southeast over the past decade. What was once a market defined primarily by phosphate mining, agriculture, and retail serving a regional population has evolved into a genuine logistics and distribution hub, a growing multifamily market, and a mid-tier office and flex market serving businesses that cannot afford or do not require Tampa Bay or Orlando metro pricing. For attorneys handling commercial real estate loan disputes in this market, that evolution matters enormously because lender underwriting standards, lease structures, and property valuations have shifted significantly and not uniformly across asset classes.

From an industrial perspective, Lakeland has absorbed substantial demand tied to the growth of e-commerce distribution networks targeting the Florida peninsula. Industrial vacancy in the Lakeland submarket has historically tracked below the broader Tampa metro, with vacancy rates in the 3% to 5% range for bulk logistics and closer to 6% to 8% for smaller bay flex product as of recent reporting periods. Net asking rents for Class A industrial have moved from the low to mid-$6 per square foot NNN range in 2019 to the $8 to $10 per square foot NNN range in 2023 and 2024, depending on bay depth, dock configuration, and proximity to I-4 interchanges. That rent compression is directly relevant to loan modification disputes because the income picture during a forbearance negotiation in 2021 or 2022 looked materially different from the income picture a lender was underwriting in 2019. Multifamily fundamentals have similarly shifted, with cap rates compressing into the 4.5% to 5.5% range at peak and beginning to decompress through 2023 and into 2024 as interest rates rose. Office and retail, by contrast, have not recovered uniformly, and those sectors generate the most contested workout disputes precisely because the income narrative is harder to resolve.

The Engagement

We were retained by litigation counsel representing a borrower in a dispute involving a loan modification and forbearance agreement on a mixed-use retail and office property in Lakeland's downtown and urban fringe submarket. The engagement arose after the lender and borrower had entered into a forbearance agreement during the COVID-impacted operating period, the borrower had made partial payments consistent with a modified payment schedule that was communicated but never fully memorialized in executed documentation, and the lender subsequently declared a default and initiated foreclosure proceedings based on the position that no binding modification existed.

Counsel's core legal theory was that the lender's conduct during the workout process was inconsistent with industry standards of care and that the modification terms the lender proposed, then withdrew, were commercially reasonable and should have been formalized. Our engagement required us to address three distinct questions. First, whether the modification terms the lender proposed in writing, including a temporary interest-only period and a partial deferral of principal, were commercially reasonable given the property's actual cash flow and the market conditions at the time. Second, whether a reasonable commercial lender in the same position, considering the same facts, would have finalized rather than withdrawn those terms. Third, whether the lender's subsequent declaration of default and acceleration was consistent with typical commercial workout practice or whether it deviated from the conduct a reasonable institutional lender would exhibit.

The Financial Analysis

The property in question was a two-story mixed-use building with ground-floor retail and upper-floor office space totaling approximately 28,000 square feet. At the time the forbearance discussions commenced, the property was approximately 61% occupied, with the largest retail tenant having vacated early and the upper-floor office suites experiencing elevated vacancy consistent with broader Lakeland office market conditions during the 2020 to 2021 period. The original loan was underwritten in 2018 at a stabilized occupancy assumption and carried a fixed interest rate of 4.75% on a 25-year amortization schedule with a 10-year term, structured as a conventional commercial real estate loan through a regional bank.

Our analysis began by reconstructing the in-place NOI at the time of forbearance. Using actual rent rolls, executed leases, lease abstracts, and property operating statements, we calculated gross potential rent for the occupied space at approximately $318,000 annually. After applying a vacancy and credit loss allowance consistent with market conditions (not stabilized underwriting), vacancy-adjusted effective gross income was approximately $267,000. Subtracting documented operating expenses including insurance, property taxes, common area maintenance, and management fees, we arrived at an in-place NOI of approximately $189,000.

The original loan balance at the time of forbearance was approximately $2.4 million. At the original note rate of 4.75% on a 25-year amortization, the annual debt service was approximately $163,000, producing a DSCR on in-place cash flow of approximately 1.16x. That figure is below the 1.20x to 1.25x minimum DSCR threshold that represents the standard underwriting floor for conventional commercial real estate loans held in portfolio by regional and community banks. The significance of that calculation is not simply that the loan was technically undersecured from a cash flow perspective. The significance is that a 1.16x DSCR on a distressed retail-office property during a period of demonstrably impaired market conditions does not represent insolvency or irreversible impairment. It represents a manageable shortfall that is well within the range of loan modifications that institutional lenders routinely approve.

To quantify what a commercially reasonable modification would have supported, we modeled the proposed interest-only modification. The lender's written proposal had included a 12-month interest-only period at a temporarily reduced rate of 4.25%, which would have reduced the required annual debt service to approximately $102,000. At that modified payment level, the in-place NOI of $189,000 produced a DSCR of 1.85x, providing substantial coverage headroom. We also modeled the property's stabilized value under a conservative capitalization rate of 7.25%, consistent with retail-office mixed use in the Lakeland submarket during the relevant period. Applying that cap rate to a stabilized NOI assumption of $268,000 (reflecting 85% occupancy at in-place rents) produced a stabilized value of approximately $3.69 million. Against the $2.4 million loan balance, that yielded an LTV of approximately 65%, which is well within the 70% to 75% LTV ceiling that regional bank lenders typically apply to this asset type. The conclusion from the financial analysis was unambiguous: the proposed modification was not only commercially reasonable, it was conservative relative to the coverage ratios and LTV thresholds that define standard institutional lending practice.

The Complicating Factors

Documentation gaps in the modification process. One of the defining complications in this matter was the absence of a fully executed modification agreement, despite the lender having transmitted a term sheet and the borrower having made payments consistent with the modified schedule for several months. In commercial real estate workout practice, it is not unusual for loan officers and relationship managers to communicate modified terms via email, allow borrowers to make reduced payments while documentation is being prepared, and then experience delays in the formal execution process. What is unusual, and what deviates from standard practice, is a lender accepting modified payments for an extended period and then using the absence of a final executed document as grounds to declare a default based on the original payment schedule. Sophisticated workout counsel and institutional lenders understand that course of conduct, partial performance, and written communications from authorized bank officers can create enforceable obligations regardless of whether the modification agreement was fully countersigned. An expert witness with active lending experience can explain to a jury or court why accepting modified payments for multiple months is not a neutral act in workout practice. It signals lender concurrence with the modified terms and is inconsistent with a lender who genuinely disputes the modification's existence.

Asset class perception during COVID impact periods. Retail-office mixed use was among the most stigmatized asset classes during 2020 and 2021. Many lenders, particularly those with regulatory pressure or classified loan concerns, were making credit decisions that were influenced as much by asset class stigma and examiner optics as by the actual cash flow analysis. In practice, this means a lender's internal credit committee may have refused to approve a modification not because the property could not support it financially, but because approving a modification on a retail-office asset during that period required internal capital allocation decisions and CECL reserve adjustments that made the modification procedurally inconvenient. That institutional incentive structure does not constitute a commercially reasonable basis for refusing to finalize a modification that the lender's own analysis supported. An expert witness can identify and explain this dynamic without speculating about the lender's specific internal motivations.

Lease expiration risk and rollover exposure. One of the specific objections the lender raised was the pending expiration of the largest remaining tenant's lease within 18 months of the forbearance negotiation. Lease rollover risk is real and is a legitimate underwriting concern. However, the manner in which a commercially reasonable lender addresses rollover risk in a workout is not to withdraw from an otherwise supportable modification. Standard practice is to condition the modification on leasing milestones, require the borrower to engage a broker of record, and build a modification covenant structure that allows the lender to revisit terms if leasing activity does not progress. Simply withdrawing from the modification and accelerating the loan when the property is producing positive cash flow and carrying a manageable DSCR is not how a commercially reasonable institutional lender handles rollover risk in a workout context.

The Lakeland Lender Market

For mixed-use retail and office assets in Lakeland, the active lending universe is narrower than it is for industrial or multifamily. Community and regional banks headquartered in central Florida, including those with Polk County roots, have historically been the primary source of financing for this asset type. These lenders are relationship-driven, hold loans in portfolio rather than selling into the secondary market, and apply DSCR floors of 1.20x to 1.25x with LTV ceilings of 70% to 75% for stabilized assets and 65% for assets with meaningful vacancy. They price on a spread to the prime rate or a corresponding Treasury index, and their workout practices are governed as much by their internal special assets or classified loan policies as by formal loan modification guidelines.

CMBS lenders, which were a meaningful source of larger mixed-use financing prior to 2020, are largely absent from the Lakeland mixed-use market due to property size constraints and the difficulties that CMBS special servicers create in the workout process. When a CMBS loan enters special servicing, the modification process is slower, more bureaucratic, and subject to PSA constraints that are often poorly understood by borrowers. A separate but related body of expert witness work exists around CMBS special servicing conduct, and the analysis of whether a special servicer acted within industry standards is materially different from the analysis applicable to a portfolio lender workout.

Credit unions with commercial real estate programs, debt funds, and SBA 504 structures are also active in Lakeland for smaller mixed-use projects. Debt funds in particular are worth noting because they have stepped into the financing gap left by tightened bank credit, but they price at materially higher rates (typically SOFR plus 350 to 550 basis points for transitional assets) and their modification practices are governed by credit agreement provisions rather than institutional workout tradition. Understanding which category of lender is involved in a dispute directly affects the applicable standard of care analysis.

What Attorneys Handling Lakeland CRE Disputes Need to Know

The threshold question I receive most frequently from litigation counsel is: do I need a CRE financing expert, or will the appraiser's report be sufficient? The answer depends on what is being disputed. An appraiser can tell the court what the property was worth. A CRE financing expert can tell the court what the property could support in terms of debt, whether a lender's modification proposal was commercially reasonable, how the lender's conduct compared to industry standards, and whether the modification terms were consistent with what a similarly situated lender would have done in the same market conditions. Those are not appraisal questions. They are financing and underwriting questions that require a different knowledge base.

Attorneys should retain a CRE financing expert when any of the following issues are present in the dispute: (1) the lender's workout conduct is being challenged as inconsistent with industry standards; (2) the borrower claims that the modification terms were commercially reasonable and should have been finalized; (3) the lender claims the property could not support a modified loan structure; (4) damages depend on whether alternative financing was available or whether the lender's acceleration was premature; or (5) the case involves a CMBS special servicer whose obligations under the PSA are in dispute.

What distinguishes a qualified CRE financing expert from a retired banker is active, current market participation. Lending standards, DSCR thresholds, underwriting overlays, and lender appetite for workout modifications are not static. An expert whose market experience ended five years ago may not be able to accurately describe how lenders in the Lakeland market were actually behaving during the specific period in dispute. Our firm closes transactions across all major CRE asset classes in 50 states. We maintain more than 1,000 active lender relationships and have completed approximately $1 billion in transaction volume. That current market activity is what allows us to offer testimony that reflects how lenders actually think and act, not how they acted a decade ago.

Before engaging a CRE financing expert, counsel should gather the following materials: the original loan documents and all amendments; all written communications between lender and borrower during the workout period; the property's operating statements and rent rolls for the relevant period; any appraisals or BPOs the lender ordered; the lender's internal credit memoranda if available through discovery; and any correspondence from the lender's special assets or workout department. The quality of the expert analysis is directly dependent on the completeness of the underlying record.

Key Takeaways

  • Commercially reasonable modification analysis requires actual numbers. Conclusory assertions that a property "could not support" a modification are insufficient. A properly performed DSCR and LTV analysis using in-place cash flow, market vacancy adjustments, and current rate assumptions will either support or refute the lender's position, and those numbers are independently verifiable.
  • A DSCR below 1.20x does not mean a modification is inappropriate. Lenders regularly modify loans on properties with sub-threshold coverage ratios when the shortfall is temporary, the LTV supports the existing balance, and the modification structure includes protective covenants. Failing to do so in those circumstances can itself represent a deviation from standard practice.
  • Course of conduct in a workout creates binding expectations. A lender that accepts modified payments for multiple months while documentation is pending is not in a neutral position. In workout practice, that conduct is understood by all sophisticated participants as acceptance of the modified terms, and an expert witness can explain that dynamic clearly to a fact-finder.
  • Lakeland's mixed-use and retail-office market requires asset-class-specific expertise. General CRE financing knowledge is not sufficient to evaluate whether a lender's conduct met the standard of care for this specific asset type in this specific market during a specific market cycle.
  • The lender category determines the applicable standard of care. Portfolio bank workout standards, CMBS special servicer obligations, and debt fund modification practices are governed by different conventions, documents, and market expectations. An expert must analyze the conduct through the correct institutional lens.
  • Lease rollover risk is a legitimate underwriting concern, but it does not justify withdrawal from an otherwise supportable modification. Standard practice is to condition the modification on leasing milestones, not to accelerate a loan that is generating positive cash flow on the basis of future risk.
  • Expert witnesses should have current, active market participation. The specific period in dispute and the lender behaviors common during that period require an expert with firsthand, contemporaneous market experience, not historical familiarity alone.
  • Early expert engagement improves discovery strategy. A CRE financing expert retained before discovery is propounded can identify the specific internal lender documents that are most probative: credit memoranda, special assets policies, workout approval matrices, and loan committee minutes. Those documents often reveal whether the lender's decision was driven by the property's actual financial condition or by other institutional factors.

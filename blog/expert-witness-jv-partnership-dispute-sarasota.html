Expert Witness: CRE Partnership and Waterfall Disputes in Sarasota, FL

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Joint Venture and Partnership Dispute

The Sarasota Market Context

Sarasota, Florida has evolved well beyond its reputation as a retirement and arts destination. Over the past decade, the market has attracted significant institutional capital, driven by migration from high-tax states, a diversifying employment base, and a development pipeline that spans luxury multifamily, mixed-use waterfront assets, and suburban office conversion. That evolution has produced a more sophisticated deal structure landscape, and with it, a higher incidence of joint venture disputes where the economics of the transaction were locked in at one market moment and unwound at another.

In multifamily, Sarasota's cap rates for Class A garden and mid-rise product have compressed into the 4.75% to 5.50% range in core submarkets including downtown, the Rosemary District, and the South Trail corridor. Rent growth that exceeded 20% annually during 2021 and 2022 has normalized but remains positive in absolute terms, with effective rents for Class A units in the $2.10 to $2.60 per square foot range depending on submarket and vintage. For mixed-use and retail-anchored assets, Sarasota's tourist-driven retail economy has produced low vacancy in well-positioned in-line and ground-floor commercial space, creating favorable income streams that appear strong at the surface but carry seasonal and tenant-concentration risk that sophisticated lenders price accordingly.

The commercial land market, particularly along US-41 and within the downtown CRA boundary, has seen significant speculative activity and repositioning. Many of the joint ventures formed between 2019 and 2022 were structured on pro forma assumptions that reflected peak rent projections, compressed exit caps, and cost estimates that predated material and labor inflation. When those projects delivered into a higher interest rate environment with construction cost overruns and extended lease-up timelines, the economic reality diverged sharply from the original waterfall model. That divergence is frequently at the center of JV disputes I am retained to analyze.

The Engagement

In this engagement, Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing one of two equity partners in a Sarasota multifamily joint venture that had reached the disposition phase. The asset was a ground-up, Class A mid-rise multifamily project located in a downtown-adjacent Sarasota submarket, delivered in late 2022 with stabilization achieved in mid-2023. The project was capitalized through a combination of senior construction-to-permanent financing and two tiers of equity: a preferred equity position held by the institutional co-investor and a promoted interest structure that was to benefit the operating partner upon clearing defined return hurdles.

By the time of the sale, the parties had a material dispute over three interconnected issues. First, whether the preferred return had been correctly accrued during the construction and lease-up period, including whether interest-on-interest compounding was contractually required or commercially unreasonable. Second, whether certain financing costs, including extension fees on the construction loan, rate cap premiums, and lender-required reserves, should have been treated as project costs or charged against the promote. Third, whether the promoted interest calculation correctly reflected the actual proceeds available for distribution after debt payoff, closing costs, and the release of lender holdbacks.

Counsel retained CLS CRE to reconstruct the capital stack from origination through disposition, model the waterfall mechanics under multiple interpretive frameworks, and provide an expert opinion on what the market-standard treatment of each disputed item would have been at the time the JV agreement was executed.

The Financial Analysis

The starting point for any capital stack reconstruction in a JV dispute is an independent analysis of the income stream that supported the senior debt. In this engagement, I analyzed the project's trailing 12-month operating performance at the time of stabilization, reconstructing net operating income from rent rolls, actual operating expense records, and management fee structures. The stabilized NOI on this approximately 180-unit asset calculated to $3,240,000 annually after adjusting for a 5% vacancy allowance, a 3% management fee on effective gross income, and normalized replacement reserves of $350 per unit per year. I do not simply accept the NOI figures presented by either party; I reconstruct them independently from the underlying data.

To determine the maximum supportable permanent debt, I applied lender underwriting standards that were operative in the Sarasota multifamily market at the relevant time periods. For conventional agency execution through Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac, the standard DSCR floor for this asset type and submarket was 1.25x at the time of stabilization, with some lenders applying 1.30x for higher-leverage structures. For balance sheet and life company execution, 1.20x was achievable for strong sponsorship on well-located product. I modeled the debt service at a 30-year amortization schedule, consistent with agency execution standards, and applied both a rate assumption reflecting the 10-year Treasury at the relevant period plus a spread that was market-standard for Class A multifamily in Florida at that time.

Using a 5.875% all-in rate (reflecting the rate environment at stabilization in mid-2023) and 30-year amortization, the annual debt service on a $38,500,000 permanent loan would have been approximately $2,734,000. Dividing the $3,240,000 NOI by $2,734,000 produces a DSCR of 1.185x, which is below the 1.25x agency threshold. This calculation was critical to the litigation because one party argued that a higher permanent loan should have been available, which would have changed the equity residual and the promoted interest calculation. My analysis demonstrated that at market-standard DSCR requirements, the maximum supportable permanent debt was closer to $35,200,000, producing a DSCR of 1.25x, and that any assertion of higher leverage was inconsistent with what an arm's-length lender would have provided in that rate environment.

The LTV constraint provided a secondary ceiling. Agency multifamily in Florida at 80% LTV on an appraised value of $48,500,000 would have supported debt of $38,800,000. In this case, the DSCR constraint was the binding limitation, not the LTV ceiling. That distinction matters in a waterfall dispute because it means the bottleneck was income coverage, not valuation, and that any argument based on inflated appraised value as a basis for higher hypothetical debt is analytically unsound.

On the preferred return accrual question, I modeled three scenarios: simple accrual on unreturned capital at the agreed preferred rate of 8%, compounding accrual at the same rate, and the industry-standard treatment used by institutional real estate fund managers where preferred return accrues on a daily basis on unreturned capital contributions but does not compound unless the agreement expressly provides for it. The difference between simple and compounding accrual over a 30-month construction and lease-up period on a $14,000,000 preferred equity contribution at 8% is approximately $280,000, which was central to whether the promote was in the money at all.

The Complicating Factors

Construction Loan Extension Fees and Rate Cap Costs. The construction loan on this project required two extensions due to permitting delays and extended lease-up. Each extension carried a fee of 0.25% on the outstanding balance, plus a requirement to repurchase a new interest rate cap. The total cost of extensions and replacement rate caps exceeded $620,000. The dispute centered on whether these costs were properly includable as project costs, reducing the total proceeds available for equity distribution, or whether the operating partner should have absorbed them as a penalty against the promote for missing the original stabilization date. Market practice in institutional JV structures is clear: extension fees and required rate cap costs are project-level costs when the extension is necessitated by factors outside the operating partner's control, such as municipal permitting delays. When the delay is attributable to sponsor performance failures, the treatment can differ. I provided a detailed analysis of the timeline, the permit records, and the lender's extension approval language to support the opinion on how the costs should have been classified.

Lender Holdback Release Mechanics. The permanent loan on this project included a $1,850,000 earnout holdback tied to achieving a 1.30x DSCR on in-place rents. At the time of sale, the earnout had not been fully released because one quarter of trailing occupancy had dipped below the threshold due to a unit renovation program. The institutional equity partner argued that the unreleased holdback should be treated as an asset of the project, effectively increasing the net equity available for distribution. The operating partner argued that because the holdback was controlled by the lender and contingent on performance, it should not be counted in the distribution calculation. My analysis of the loan documents, the payoff letter, and the closing statement confirmed that the holdback was released at payoff subject to the lender's standard discount factor, netting approximately $1,720,000 to the partnership. The proper treatment of that amount in the waterfall was a factual question that required someone who understood how lenders actually structure and release earnout provisions, not simply how the JV agreement was written.

Promote Hurdle Measurement on an IRR Versus Equity Multiple Basis. The JV agreement in this matter used an IRR-based promote hurdle rather than an equity multiple. IRR calculations are exquisitely sensitive to the timing of cash flows, including when equity was called, when preferred return distributions were made or accrued, and when the ultimate liquidity event occurred. A difference of three months in the assumed equity funding date can move an IRR calculation by 50 to 100 basis points on a deal of this size, which can determine whether a promote is triggered at all. I reconstructed the actual equity funding schedule from capital call records, bank statements, and wire confirmations, and compared it to the schedule reflected in the operating partner's waterfall model. The discrepancy was material.

The Sarasota Lender Market

Understanding the actual lender market in Sarasota is essential context for any JV financing dispute because the availability, pricing, and structure of debt at any given point in time directly shapes what the equity waterfall can support. In Sarasota, the multifamily lending market at the time this project was financed and sold included the following categories of active capital providers.

Agency Lenders (Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac DUS Lenders). Both agencies are active in Sarasota through their DUS and Program Plus platforms. For stabilized multifamily above 50 units, agency execution is typically the most competitive pricing available, with spreads to the 10-year Treasury ranging from 140 to 200 basis points depending on LTV, DSCR, and sponsorship quality. Agency lenders require third-party management for non-owner-occupied assets, a minimum 90% occupancy for 90 days prior to rate lock, and full recourse burn-off structures for certain first-time borrowers. These requirements have direct implications for when a permanent loan could have been executed and at what rate, which in turn affects the debt service calculations at the center of waterfall disputes.

Life Insurance Companies. Several life company correspondents are active in the Sarasota market for multifamily assets above $15,000,000. Life companies typically price at tighter spreads than agency, often 120 to 160 basis points over Treasuries, but require lower LTV (typically 65% to 70% maximum) and stronger DSCR floors. For a dispute involving a claim that higher-LTV financing was available, life company execution is not the relevant benchmark. Life companies also have longer application-to-close timelines, typically 60 to 90 days, which affects assumptions about when permanent financing could have been put in place.

CMBS Conduit Lenders. For mixed-use or retail-heavy assets in Sarasota, CMBS is a relevant execution channel. CMBS lenders typically underwrite to a 1.25x DSCR on in-place cash flow, allow up to 75% LTV on qualified assets, and price to a spread over the corresponding Treasury or swap rate. CMBS execution is more flexible on cash-out provisions and non-recourse carve-out structures than agency, making it relevant for JV scenarios where the equity partners have divergent recourse preferences.

Florida Community and Regional Banks. Several well-capitalized Florida-based community and regional banks are active in Sarasota with relationship-based commercial real estate lending. These lenders typically offer floating-rate construction and bridge products, will go to 70% to 75% LTV on stabilized multifamily, and are often more flexible on in-process assets where agency or CMBS execution is not yet available. Their pricing is typically SOFR plus 200 to 300 basis points on construction and bridge, and they are the most relevant lenders for extension and refinancing scenarios during the construction and lease-up phase.

What Attorneys Handling Sarasota CRE Disputes Need to Know

The decision to retain a CRE financing expert should be made early in the litigation, not as an afterthought once discovery has closed. JV waterfall disputes require forensic reconstruction of the capital stack, and that reconstruction is both data-intensive and time-sensitive. The earlier the expert is engaged, the better positioned counsel is to identify document requests that will actually produce the necessary financial records.

A CRE financing expert answers questions that a real estate appraiser cannot. An appraiser determines value. A CRE financing expert determines what debt was available, what it cost, how it was structured, and how its terms affected the economics available to each equity partner. These are distinct disciplines. An appraisal report that concludes a property was worth $50,000,000 does not tell you what permanent loan amount the market would have supported on that asset at that moment, what the DSCR and LTV constraints would have been, or whether a particular lender's rate and fee structure was consistent with market standards. Those questions require someone who originates and structures commercial real estate loans at the institutional level.

The distinction between a qualified CRE financing expert and a retired banker is meaningful. A retired community banker with local relationships can speak to deposit-funded balance sheet loans in a specific geography. That background does not qualify someone to opine on agency multifamily underwriting criteria, CMBS bond execution, life company credit parameters, or the market-standard treatment of preferred return accrual in an institutional JV structure. Counsel should look for an expert with documented active origination experience across multiple capital source categories, a transaction volume that demonstrates market-level engagement, and prior accepted expert testimony in financing disputes involving complex capital structures.

Before the engagement begins, counsel should gather the following: the JV agreement and all amendments, all capital call records with dates and wire confirmations, the loan documents for the construction and permanent loans, all lender correspondence including extension approval letters and holdback release conditions, the project's monthly financial statements from opening through disposition, the closing statement from the sale, and any waterfall calculation models prepared by either party. The quality of the expert's analysis is a direct function of the completeness of the financial record.

At trial or in an arbitration hearing, CRE financing expert testimony serves several functions. It establishes the market standard against which the disputed treatment is measured. It provides a quantitative reconstruction of the waterfall under multiple interpretive frameworks, giving the fact-finder a structured basis for comparison. It contextualizes financing decisions within the realities of what was actually available in the market at the relevant time, preventing either party from arguing that financing terms were negotiable when in fact they were dictated by lender credit policy. At Commercial Lending Solutions, our expert reports are prepared under Rule 26 standards and are designed to withstand Daubert scrutiny.

Key Takeaways

  • DSCR is frequently the binding constraint on permanent loan sizing, not LTV. In a JV waterfall dispute, any argument that higher debt should have been available must be tested against the income coverage standard that actual lenders apply, not hypothetical maximum leverage.
  • IRR-based promote hurdles are highly sensitive to cash flow timing. The actual equity funding dates, as documented by capital call records and wire confirmations, must be reconstructed independently. Small discrepancies in assumed timing can determine whether a promote is triggered.
  • Preferred return accrual methodology is not self-evident from the rate alone. Whether accrual is simple or compounding, and whether it is calculated on contributed capital or unreturned capital, produces materially different numbers over a multi-year hold period. Market practice must be established with reference to institutional JV standards, not just the agreement language in isolation.
  • Construction loan extension fees and rate cap costs have a market-standard treatment that depends on the reason for the extension. An expert familiar with actual lender extension approval processes can provide a well-grounded opinion on classification that an attorney working from the agreement alone cannot.
  • Lender holdbacks and earnouts affect net proceeds available for distribution in ways that require understanding of how payoff mechanics actually work. The treatment of an unreleased earnout at closing is a question of both contract interpretation and financing practice.

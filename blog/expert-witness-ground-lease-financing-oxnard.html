Expert Witness: Ground Lease Financing Disputes and Analysis in Oxnard, CA

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Ground Lease Financing Complexity

The Oxnard Market Context

Oxnard occupies a commercially significant position within Ventura County that attorneys and investors often underestimate relative to its neighbors. The city is not a suburban afterthought to Los Angeles. It is an active industrial, logistics, and agricultural processing hub anchored by the Port of Hueneme, the only deep-water port between Los Angeles and San Francisco on the Pacific Coast. That geographic reality shapes how lenders, appraisers, and sophisticated borrowers think about ground-leased real estate in this market. When a financing dispute involves a leasehold interest on an industrial or flex site near the port corridor, the income assumptions, the tenant creditworthiness analysis, and the residual land value all carry characteristics that are specific to Oxnard rather than generic Southern California.

Industrial vacancy in the Oxnard-Camarillo submarket has remained compressed, hovering in the low single digits, driven by last-mile logistics demand, cold storage requirements from the agricultural sector, and spillover from the chronically constrained Los Angeles Basin. Asking rents for industrial space in Oxnard have pushed into the high teens to low twenties per square foot annually on a triple-net basis for newer, functional product, with legacy buildings on ground leases often stabilized at below-market rents relative to current demand. That rent gap between in-place income and market income is a recurring source of dispute when a party argues the ground leasehold is financeable and the other party argues it is not. The spread between contract rent and market rent is not academic; it directly determines whether a lender will underwrite to in-place NOI or apply a haircut.

Land values in Oxnard are meaningfully supported by the port adjacency, the scarcity of developable industrial land in Ventura County, and entitlement constraints. Fee simple industrial land in the Oxnard plain trades at a significant premium relative to inland Ventura County. That underlying land value is simultaneously an asset and a complication in ground lease financing: lenders value the leasehold against a fee interest they do not control, and the ground lessor's residual interest creates subordination and reversion risk that must be contractually addressed before any institutional lender will issue a commitment.

The Engagement

Counsel representing the leasehold mortgagee in a commercial financing dispute retained Commercial Lending Solutions to analyze whether a ground lease encumbering an industrial property in Oxnard's port-adjacent submarket met market standards for institutional debt financing. The asset was a mid-size industrial building on a long-term ground lease with a land owner that had historically resisted executing a subordination, non-disturbance, and attornment agreement. The borrower had obtained a leasehold mortgage several years prior from a regional lender. That lender was subsequently acquired, and the successor institution raised questions during a portfolio review about the enforceability of its mortgage position given the state of the ground lease documentation.

The core legal and financial questions we were asked to address included: whether the ground lease, as drafted and as in effect, was financeable under market standards at the time the loan was originated; whether the absence of a fully executed SNDA from the ground lessor constituted a material deviation from what institutional lenders require; whether the loan-to-value and debt service coverage assumptions used at origination were consistent with how lenders actually underwrite leasehold interests in this asset class and submarket; and what remediation, if any, would bring the leasehold structure into compliance with current market financing standards. We were retained to provide a Rule 26 expert report and to make ourselves available for deposition.

The Financial Analysis

The income analysis began with the in-place lease structure on the industrial building. The master tenant, a credit-quality industrial occupant under a triple-net lease, was paying rent that, at the time of origination, generated a gross NOI before ground rent expense of approximately $820,000 annually. The ground rent obligation under the ground lease was a fixed payment stepping up on a schedule, and at the relevant origination date, ground rent was $140,000 per year, yielding a net-of-ground-rent NOI available to support the leasehold mortgage of approximately $680,000.

Market standard DSCR for a leasehold mortgage on stabilized industrial product in California at the time of origination was 1.25x at a minimum, with most institutional lenders requiring 1.30x given the additional structural risk inherent in a leasehold interest. At 1.25x, the maximum allowable annual debt service against the $680,000 net NOI was $544,000. Using an interest rate of 5.25 percent on a 30-year amortization schedule, the maximum supportable loan at 1.25x DSCR was approximately $8,250,000. At 1.30x DSCR, that figure compressed to approximately $7,920,000.

The lender had originated a leasehold mortgage of $7,500,000, which on its face sat within the supportable debt range at both DSCR thresholds. However, the LTV analysis introduced complications. Leasehold value for financing purposes is not the same as the appraised value of the fee interest. Lenders underwriting leasehold mortgages apply LTV constraints to the leasehold interest value, not the fee simple land-plus-improvements value, because the lender's collateral is the leasehold, not the land. Institutional lenders typically cap leasehold mortgage LTV at 65 to 70 percent of the leasehold interest value, and some require a discount to reflect the structural risk of a ground lease that lacks a fully executed SNDA. The appraised leasehold interest value at origination was $11,200,000, placing the $7,500,000 loan at approximately 67 percent LTV, technically within market parameters. But that appraisal assumed a financeable ground lease with adequate lender protections. Our analysis concluded that without a fully executed SNDA, the leasehold interest value supportable under institutional financing standards was meaningfully lower, potentially in the range of $9,500,000 to $10,200,000, which would have pushed the LTV to 74 to 79 percent, outside the institutional credit box for this asset type.

We further analyzed the ground rent coverage ratio: the relationship between the ground rent expense and the gross NOI. At $140,000 in ground rent against $820,000 in gross NOI, the ground rent represented approximately 17 percent of income, which is within the range lenders consider acceptable, generally below 20 percent. However, the ground rent step-up schedule, which escalated at a fixed 2.5 percent per year, needed to be modeled against the lease expiration and renewal option structure to determine whether the ground lease term, including renewals, extended at least 10 to 15 years beyond the maturity of the leasehold mortgage, another hard requirement for institutional lending. That analysis is addressed in the complicating factors section below.

The Complicating Factors

The Absence of a Fully Executed SNDA

The single most significant complicating factor in this engagement was the state of the subordination, non-disturbance, and attornment agreement. A ground lease is subordinate to a leasehold mortgage only if the ground lessor has executed an SNDA that contractually obligates the ground lessor to recognize the lender's position in the event the leasehold tenant defaults and the lender must step in to protect its collateral. Without a fully executed SNDA, the leasehold mortgage lender faces a scenario in which the ground lessor can terminate the ground lease upon a default by the leasehold tenant, effectively wiping out the lender's collateral entirely. No institutional lender will knowingly originate a leasehold mortgage without a fully executed SNDA from the fee owner.

In this engagement, the ground lessor had executed a letter of intent to enter into an SNDA but had never signed the final agreement. Whether this constituted a binding commitment, whether the lender was negligent in funding without the executed instrument, and whether the letter of intent could be enforced as a contractual matter were legal questions for the trier of fact. Our role was to establish what the market standard required and whether the originating lender's conduct conformed to that standard. The answer, from a financing market perspective, was unambiguous: a letter of intent does not satisfy institutional SNDA requirements. Lenders require the executed, recordable instrument in final form prior to loan closing.

Ground Lease Term and Residual Lender Risk

Lenders require that the remaining ground lease term, inclusive of all exercisable renewal options, extend at least 10 years beyond the maturity date of the leasehold mortgage. Most institutional lenders prefer 15 to 20 years of term cushion. In Oxnard, where industrial ground leases on port-adjacent properties are sometimes structured with original terms dating to the 1970s and 1980s and have been amended multiple times, determining the actual remaining term and the mechanics of renewal options requires careful document review. In this engagement, the ground lease had an original 55-year term with two 10-year renewal options. At the relevant date, the remaining term was approximately 18 years, and the renewal options required written notice 24 months in advance. The leasehold mortgagee had not received written confirmation from the leasehold tenant that renewal options had been exercised or would be timely exercised. That created a residual term risk that a sophisticated lender would have required to be cured at origination by obtaining either an executed renewal confirmation or a lender-protective covenant in the SNDA requiring notice to the lender and an opportunity to cure in the event the tenant failed to exercise a renewal option.

Lender Cure Rights and Default Notification

Market-standard SNDA provisions for ground-leased industrial properties require that the ground lessor provide the leasehold mortgagee with written notice of any default under the ground lease simultaneously with notice to the leasehold tenant, and that the lender be afforded a separate cure period, typically 60 to 90 days beyond the leasehold tenant's cure period, to remedy the default. Without this provision, a ground lessor can terminate the ground lease without ever having given the lender an opportunity to step in and cure. The ground lease at issue in this engagement did not contain lender-protective default notification language. That omission, independent of the SNDA execution failure, was a material deviation from market standards that an institutional lender's counsel should have identified during the origination review.

Leasehold Mortgage Assignment and Foreclosure Rights

A leasehold mortgage must be freely assignable, and in the event of leasehold tenant default, the lender must have the right to foreclose on the leasehold interest and assign or sub-lease the leasehold without ground lessor consent, subject to commercially reasonable standards. Restrictions in the ground lease on assignment without ground lessor approval, if not carved out for leasehold mortgage enforcement scenarios, create a situation in which the lender's collateral is effectively illiquid. In Oxnard's industrial market, where the port adjacency creates a specialized tenant pool, ground lessors have sometimes negotiated use restrictions and assignment approval rights into ground leases. Each of those provisions must be reviewed against lender enforceability requirements before a leasehold mortgage can be properly underwritten.

The Oxnard Lender Market

The universe of lenders willing to originate leasehold mortgages on industrial assets in Oxnard is narrower than the fee simple lending market. Community banks and smaller regional lenders, which are active in Oxnard for conventional owner-occupied industrial and retail financings, typically do not have leasehold mortgage programs. Leasehold financing in this submarket is primarily the domain of larger regional banks with California commercial real estate programs, life insurance companies with appetite for net-leased industrial product, CMBS conduit lenders, and in some cases, debt fund lenders when the deal structure is too complex for conventional sources.

Life insurance companies represent the best execution for credit-quality net-leased industrial ground leases in Ventura County when the SNDA and lease term requirements are satisfied. They price in the low to mid 5 percent range for 10-year fixed-rate financing and require 1.30x DSCR, 65 percent LTV against leasehold value, and full SNDA execution with assignability provisions. CMBS conduit lenders will consider leasehold industrial assets but require extensive SNDA review, often engage specialized leasehold counsel at borrower expense, and generally impose a 60 to 65 percent LTV floor. Regional banks operating in California, including those with Ventura County presence, will occasionally book leasehold mortgages in portfolio but typically require full recourse or partial recourse guaranty as a structural concession for the additional collateral complexity. Debt funds will lend on imperfect leasehold structures but price the risk accordingly, often in the 8 to 10 percent range for bridge capital, and represent the lender of last resort when the ground lease documentation has deficiencies that cannot be cured prior to closing.

What Attorneys Handling Oxnard CRE Disputes Need to Know

A CRE financing expert is not an appraiser, and the distinction matters enormously in leasehold mortgage litigation. An appraiser values the asset. A CRE financing expert analyzes whether the asset was financeable, at what terms, under what structural conditions, and whether the parties involved in originating or underwriting the loan met industry standards in doing so. In a ground lease dispute where the question is whether a lender was negligent in funding without a compliant SNDA, or whether a borrower misrepresented the state of the ground lease documentation, an appraiser's opinion of value does not answer those questions. A CRE financing expert who has actually underwritten and closed leasehold mortgage transactions, worked with institutional lenders on SNDA negotiations, and understands how the Ventura County and broader California lending community prices and structures leasehold risk can address those questions directly.

Counsel should retain a CRE financing expert early in the case, ideally before the Rule 26 disclosure deadline allows time for a thorough document review and market analysis. The documents that should be gathered before the expert engagement include: the complete ground lease and all amendments, the SNDA in final and any draft forms, the leasehold mortgage and loan agreement, the origination file including the lender's credit memorandum and underwriting model, the appraisal used at origination, any title commitment or title policy, the master tenant lease, and any correspondence between the parties regarding the ground lease documentation requirements. Each of these documents feeds directly into the expert's analysis of what market standards required, what the lender knew or should have known, and whether the origination conformed to industry practice.

At trial or in a Plan confirmation hearing in a bankruptcy context, CRE financing expert testimony is used to establish three categories of opinion: what the market standard was at the relevant time; whether the specific transaction conformed to or deviated from that standard; and what the practical financing consequences of any deviation were, meaning whether the deviation affected the borrower's ability to refinance, the lender's ability to enforce its collateral, or the value attributed to the leasehold interest. A qualified CRE financing expert is distinguished from a retired banker or a generic real estate consultant by active, current market participation. Someone who is actively brokering leasehold mortgage transactions, maintaining lender relationships across the institutional capital stack, and working in the California industrial market brings credibility that a retired professional cannot replicate.

Key Takeaways

  • Ground lease financing is a specialized discipline. Leasehold mortgages require SNDA execution, adequate remaining lease term extending at least 10 to 15 years beyond loan maturity, lender cure rights, and assignability provisions. The absence of any one of these elements can render a ground lease unfinanceable by institutional lenders regardless of the underlying income quality.
  • LTV analysis on leasehold interests must be performed against leasehold value, not fee simple value. Using fee simple value to justify LTV in a leasehold mortgage origination overstates the lender's collateral coverage and constitutes a deviation from market underwriting standards.
  • A letter of intent is not an SNDA. Originating a leasehold mortgage on the basis of an unsigned or incomplete SNDA process is a material underwriting deficiency that a CRE financing expert can identify and quantify in terms of its impact on lender risk and loan enforceability.
  • Oxnard's industrial market creates specific ground lease dynamics driven by port adjacency, limited land supply, and specialized tenant profiles that must be reflected in both the income analysis and the lender market analysis presented in expert testimony.
  • The leasehold lender market is tiered. Life companies, CMBS, regional banks, and debt funds each have different SNDA requirements, LTV thresholds, and recourse requirements for leasehold industrial product. A financing expert who knows which lenders were active and what their credit boxes required at origination provides essential market context that neither an appraiser nor a generalist can offer.
  • Ground rent coverage and step-up schedules must be modeled, not assumed.

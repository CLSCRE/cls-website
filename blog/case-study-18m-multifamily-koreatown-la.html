Case Study

$18,500,000 Multifamily Apartments (62 Units) in Koreatown, Los Angeles, CA

Deal arranged and written by Trevor Damyan, Commercial Mortgage Broker
By Trevor Damyan · April 03, 2026 · Permanent Loan

The Challenge: RSO Properties and Lender Perception

When a long-term client approached Commercial Lending Solutions to refinance their 62-unit apartment building in Koreatown, we knew the execution would require a targeted approach. The maturing five-year bank loan needed replacement, but the property's rent-stabilized status under Los Angeles' Rent Stabilization Ordinance (RSO) had already caused several lenders to decline participation.

The RSO creates legitimate underwriting concerns. Annual rent increases are capped at modest percentages, typically 3-8% depending on the CPI, and tenant protections limit owners' ability to address problem tenants or reposition units. Many commercial lenders view these restrictions as fundamental constraints on cash flow growth and asset value appreciation.

However, the fundamentals told a different story. The property maintained 98%+ occupancy for five consecutive years, indicating strong tenant retention and market demand. Current rents averaged 15-20% below comparable non-RSO properties in the submarket, creating meaningful downside protection. The building sat within walking distance of the Metro Purple Line extension, positioning it directly in the path of LA's transit-oriented development push.

Market Context: Koreatown's Rental Dynamics

Koreatown represents one of Los Angeles' most compelling rental fundamentals. With over 120,000 residents packed into 2.7 square miles, it maintains the highest population density west of New York City. The submarket serves as a primary landing point for new LA residents, particularly young professionals priced out of West LA and downtown.

The demographic profile skews heavily toward renters by necessity, not choice. Median household incomes cluster in the $45,000-$65,000 range, creating sustained demand for workforce housing options. Unlike luxury submarkets that experience volatility during economic downturns, Koreatown's rental demand remains remarkably stable.

Transit connectivity amplifies the location's appeal. The Purple Line extension, scheduled for completion in 2025, will provide direct rail access to Century City, Beverly Hills, and UCLA. Properties within the half-mile radius of future stations have already begun experiencing rental premium compression relative to transit-distant comparables.

Lender Education and Positioning Strategy

Rather than fighting the RSO narrative, we positioned it as a feature aligned with current agency lending priorities. Fannie Mae's Duty to Serve mandate explicitly encourages financing of naturally occurring affordable housing (NOAH) properties. RSO buildings, by definition, provide workforce housing at below-market rents without requiring government subsidies or deed restrictions.

We structured our presentation around three core themes. First, the occupancy history demonstrated tenant satisfaction and rent affordability relative to local incomes. Second, the below-market rent structure provided built-in downside protection during potential economic stress. Third, the transit-adjacent location aligned with California's broader housing policy objectives around density and car-dependency reduction.

The underwriting package included a detailed RSO analysis showing historical rent increase patterns and vacancy trends across similar properties. We demonstrated that well-maintained RSO buildings in prime locations actually experience lower turnover than comparable market-rate properties, reducing the operational costs that often offset higher absolute rent levels.

Execution and Results

Five lenders received the initial package: two regional banks, two national life companies, and Fannie Mae through a correspondent relationship. The regional banks declined within 48 hours, citing RSO exposure limits. One life company offered a 7-year term at 75% LTV but required full recourse and personal guarantees.

Fannie Mae's response differed markedly. Their underwriter immediately grasped the workforce housing angle and appreciated the transit-oriented location. The preliminary term sheet came back at 70% LTV with a 10-year fixed rate, 30-year amortization schedule, and full non-recourse structure.

The final execution delivered exactly what the borrower needed: permanent financing at 4.85%, representing 35 basis points inside the competing bank quotes. The 10-year fixed term eliminated near-term refinance risk, while the non-recourse structure provided complete liability protection. Total loan proceeds of $12.95 million retired the existing debt with excess cash for capital improvements.

Closing occurred 47 days from application, despite a full environmental assessment and updated seismic report that added two weeks to the timeline.

Market Implications

This execution demonstrates that RSO properties can access competitive financing when properly positioned. Agency lenders increasingly view rent-stabilized assets as mission-driven lending opportunities rather than distressed situations requiring premium pricing.

For owners of similar properties, the key insight involves narrative positioning. Rather than apologizing for RSO restrictions, successful financing strategies emphasize the workforce housing component, operational stability, and alignment with governmental policy objectives. In today's capital markets environment, these factors often outweigh traditional concerns about rent growth limitations.

Expert Witness: Commercial Loan Origination Standards in South Bend, IN

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Lender Liability and Loan Origination Standards

The South Bend Market Context

South Bend, Indiana sits at an interesting intersection of Midwest industrial resurgence, university-driven multifamily demand, and a commercial real estate lending environment that reflects both regional bank conservatism and selective institutional appetite. For attorneys and investors evaluating a lender liability or loan origination claim, understanding the local market fundamentals is not background noise. It is directly relevant to whether a lender's underwriting standards, loan terms, or disclosed assumptions were consistent with what a reasonably competent commercial mortgage professional would have done in this specific market at the time of origination.

South Bend's industrial sector has benefited meaningfully from Midwest supply chain reconfiguration, with vacancy rates in the broader St. Joseph County industrial submarket running in the 4% to 6% range in recent years, tighter than many larger Indiana metros. Asking rents for functional warehouse and light manufacturing space have climbed from the low-to-mid $4.00 per square foot range to north of $5.50 to $6.50 per square foot net for newer or well-located product, reflecting demand from logistics users and regional distributors. This matters in a lender liability context because a broker or lender who underwrote a South Bend industrial deal using stale or inflated rent assumptions would have deviated from observable market data that was accessible through regional market reports, local broker surveys, and CoStar or CBRE intelligence at the time.

Multifamily fundamentals in South Bend are shaped by the University of Notre Dame's gravitational pull on the rental market, a growing medical device and life sciences employment base anchored by institutions along the US-31 corridor, and a workforce housing undersupply in select submarkets. Cap rates for stabilized multifamily assets in South Bend have historically ranged from the low 6% range for Class A product near the university to 7.5% to 8.5% for value-add or workforce-class properties in secondary neighborhoods. These are not gateway market cap rates, and any lender or broker who applied coastal multifamily underwriting benchmarks to a South Bend deal without adjustment was operating outside the standard of care for this market.

The office market in South Bend, consistent with national trends, has experienced elevated vacancy and compressed leasing velocity, with Class B suburban office particularly challenged. A lender originating a loan on a multi-tenant office asset in South Bend post-2020 without careful scrutiny of tenant creditworthiness, lease term rollover risk, and a haircut on in-place rents would have difficulty defending that underwriting in litigation. The point is this: South Bend is a real, active commercial real estate market with observable, documentable fundamentals, and a financing expert can establish, with specificity, what market-standard underwriting looked like for any given asset class in this market at any given point in time.

The Engagement

In the matter that prompted this article, Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing a commercial real estate borrower asserting lender liability and professional negligence claims arising from a commercial mortgage transaction involving a mixed-use income-producing property in the South Bend metro area. The asset included both retail and multifamily components, situated in a transitional submarket that had attracted significant redevelopment interest but carried leasing risk commensurate with that positioning.

The borrower alleged that the originating mortgage broker had misrepresented the loan terms, failed to disclose material fee arrangements, and presented a loan product that was unsuitable given the borrower's stated investment objectives and the property's actual income profile. Additionally, the borrower asserted that the lender's underwriting failed to properly apply income verification standards and that the loan was structured in a manner that would predictably lead to a debt service shortfall within the first stabilization period, which it did.

Counsel retained CLS CRE to address three core questions. First, did the loan origination process, as documented in the loan file, meet the industry standard of care for a commercial mortgage broker operating in this market? Second, were the disclosed loan terms, rate, fees, prepayment structure, and debt service requirements consistent with what a reasonably competent broker would have presented to this borrower for this asset type? Third, based on the income and expense data available at origination, what was the maximum supportable loan amount under standard commercial underwriting criteria, and how did the actual loan compare?

The Financial Analysis

The methodological foundation of our analysis was a reconstruction of what a reasonably competent commercial lender would have underwritten at origination, using only the data that was available to the originator at the time. We did not apply hindsight. We applied the underwriting conventions that governed commercial mortgage origination for this asset class, in this market, during the relevant period.

Income Analysis: The subject property had an in-place gross potential rent roll that, at face value, appeared to support the loan amount the borrower received. However, standard underwriting practice requires an economic vacancy and credit loss deduction, typically 5% to 10% for stabilized mixed-use assets in secondary markets like South Bend, applied to gross potential income. For a transitional asset with a shorter lease history, a competent underwriter would have applied a vacancy assumption at or above 10%. The originator applied a 3% vacancy factor, which was inconsistent with the submarket's observable vacancy rate and with the property's own leasing history. The effective gross income used to size the loan was therefore materially overstated from the outset.

After applying a market-appropriate vacancy factor and verifying operating expenses against comparable properties, we established a stabilized net operating income of approximately $210,000 per year. The originator's underwriting reflected a NOI of approximately $265,000, a $55,000 annual overstatement that directly inflated the maximum supportable loan calculation.

DSCR Application: The market standard for commercial real estate loans of this type, mixed-use income property with a non-institutional sponsor in a secondary Indiana market, is a minimum debt service coverage ratio of 1.25x. Many regional lenders serving this market require 1.30x for assets with any transitional characteristics. The originator applied a 1.15x DSCR, which was below the standard of care for this asset and lender type and was not disclosed to the borrower as a deviation from typical underwriting requirements. A 1.15x DSCR creates almost no buffer for income shortfalls, lease rollover, or capital expenditure absorption during the hold period.

Rate and Amortization Assumptions: We underwrote the loan using a 7.00% note rate reflecting the prevailing fixed-rate environment at origination and a 25-year amortization schedule, which is the conventional amortization period for mixed-use commercial assets at regional and community banks in this market. Annual debt service on a $2,200,000 loan at these parameters equals approximately $185,800. Applying the market-standard 1.25x DSCR to the verified NOI of $210,000 yields a maximum supportable annual debt service of $168,000, which corresponds to a maximum supportable loan of approximately $1,995,000.

LTV Analysis: The appraised value at origination was $2,750,000. The actual loan amount was $2,350,000, representing an 85.5% loan-to-value ratio. For a mixed-use transitional asset in a secondary Indiana market, the standard LTV ceiling at conventional commercial lenders is 70% to 75%. Even if one accepted the appraised value at face value, a 75% LTV cap would have produced a maximum loan of $2,062,500. The originator's loan exceeded both the income-supported maximum and the LTV maximum that would have applied under standard underwriting criteria for this asset class and market.

Summary of Findings: The actual loan exceeded the income-supportable amount by approximately $355,000 and exceeded the market-standard LTV threshold by over $285,000. These two data points, independently derived, converged on the same conclusion: the loan as structured was not supportable under standard commercial underwriting criteria, and a reasonably competent broker or lender would not have originated this loan on these terms without either restructuring the debt, requiring additional collateral, or declining to proceed.

The Complicating Factors

Mixed-Use Complexity in a Secondary Market: Mixed-use assets, those combining retail ground floor with residential or office above, are underwritten differently by different lender categories. Commercial banks typically underwrite mixed-use to the most restrictive standard applied to any component: if the retail portion is more than 20% to 25% of gross income, many lenders treat the entire asset as commercial, applying commercial LTV and DSCR constraints rather than the more favorable residential thresholds. In South Bend, where the mixed-use product type is relatively limited in institutional transaction volume, lenders often lack internal comparables and may under-stress the analysis. This creates a risk of imprecise underwriting that borrowers may not recognize until debt service strain materializes.

Transitional Submarket Leasing Risk: The subject property was located in a South Bend submarket undergoing redevelopment investment. Transitional submarkets carry leasing risk that does not fully appear in trailing income history but must be captured in forward-looking underwriting through higher vacancy assumptions, shorter assumed lease terms, and tenant improvement reserves. A lender that relied exclusively on trailing 12-month income without stress-testing for lease rollover or new tenant leasing costs failed to apply the forward-looking underwriting discipline that commercial mortgage standards require.

Broker Fee Disclosure Obligations: In Indiana and under prevailing commercial mortgage brokerage standards, a broker acting as an intermediary between a borrower and lender is obligated to disclose all material compensation arrangements, including any yield spread premium, origination fee sharing with the lender, or referral compensation. In this matter, the broker received a fee from both the borrower and the lending institution, a dual compensation structure that was not disclosed in the engagement agreement. This is a deviation from the standard of care that any competent CRE financing expert can identify and quantify in terms of its impact on the borrower's net economics.

Documentation Standards: Commercial mortgage underwriting standards require that income claims be verified through specific documentation: actual executed leases, not proforma rent rolls; two years of operating statements, not single-year estimates; and independent verification of expense ratios. In this origination, the lender relied on a single-year income and expense statement prepared by the borrower's property manager without independent verification. This documentation shortfall does not shift liability to the borrower; it establishes that the originator failed to perform the due diligence that the standard of care requires before recommending a loan product to a borrower or funding a loan at a given amount.

The South Bend Lender Market

Understanding the lender landscape in South Bend is essential for evaluating whether the originator placed the borrower with the appropriate lender for the transaction or whether the loan was routed to a source that generated higher broker compensation at the borrower's expense.

Community and Regional Banks: South Bend has meaningful community banking depth, with institutions headquartered in St. Joseph County and northern Indiana that maintain active commercial real estate lending programs. These lenders typically hold loans in portfolio, apply relationship-based pricing, and operate with 70% to 75% LTV maximums, 1.25x minimum DSCR, and 3-to-5-year term structures with 20-to-25-year amortization. They favor borrowers with established operating history and existing deposit relationships. For a stabilized, well-documented income property in South Bend, these lenders represent the most borrower-favorable pricing and structure, and a competent broker should have canvassed this category first.

Credit Unions and CDFI Lenders: Several credit unions and Community Development Financial Institutions operate in the South Bend market and provide commercial real estate financing for mixed-use and community-oriented projects at competitive rates, sometimes with more flexible LTV or DSCR parameters when supported by community development characteristics. These sources are appropriate for certain borrowers and asset types but are not a substitute for full market canvassing.

Correspondent and Conduit Lenders: For loans above $3,000,000 to $5,000,000 on stabilized assets, conduit CMBS financing becomes a viable option in the South Bend market, though the property must meet stabilization and quality criteria and the borrower must accept defeasance or yield maintenance prepayment penalties. CMBS underwriting is highly standardized, and a broker who placed a transitional South Bend asset into a conduit execution without ensuring the property met conduit-qualifying criteria would have failed the standard of care for lender selection.

Debt Funds and Bridge Lenders: For transitional, value-add, or construction scenarios, private debt funds are active in secondary Indiana markets, including South Bend. These lenders price at a significant premium: rates typically 150 to 350 basis points above conventional bank financing, with higher fees and shorter terms. A broker who placed a stabilized, bankable asset with a debt fund when conventional financing was available exposed the borrower to unnecessary cost and is difficult to defend against a professional negligence standard.

What Attorneys Handling South Bend CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to Retain a CRE Financing Expert: Counsel should engage a commercial real estate financing expert whenever the dispute involves questions about whether a loan was properly sized, appropriately structured, correctly disclosed, or placed with the right lender. An appraiser can establish value. A CRE financing expert establishes whether the loan that was made on that value was consistent with market standards, whether the borrower received the terms they were promised, and whether a competent broker or lender would have done something different.

What a CRE Financing Expert Can Answer That an Appraiser Cannot: Appraisers opine on value. They do not typically opine on whether a lender's DSCR calculation was correct, whether a broker's fee structure was within market norms, whether the lender's income verification process met underwriting standards, or whether the loan was placed with an appropriate lender category given the borrower's profile and the asset's characteristics. These are the questions that require someone with active, current commercial mortgage origination experience across multiple lender types, not just historical valuation experience.

Qualifications That Matter: A qualified CRE financing expert is not simply a retired banker or a former appraiser. The expert should have active, current involvement in commercial mortgage origination across multiple lender categories, including banks, credit unions, debt funds, and conduit lenders. The expert should be able to demonstrate familiarity with the specific market in question, access to current lender credit box data, and experience testifying to underwriting standards and broker duty of care in litigation contexts. At CLS CRE, we maintain approximately $1 billion in transaction volume across 50 states with more than 1,000 active lender relationships, which allows us to speak to current market standards with specificity rather than relying on dated or generalized impressions.

What to Gather Before the Expert Engagement: Counsel should compile the complete loan file including the original loan application, the appraisal and any review appraisals, the broker engagement agreement, the lender's internal credit memorandum or underwriting worksheet, all fee disclosure documents, correspondence between the broker and lender, and any marketing materials or term sheets presented to the borrower. The more complete the loan file, the more specific the expert's analysis can be, and specificity in expert testimony is what withstands cross-examination.

Use of Expert Testimony at Trial and in Arbitration: CRE financing expert testimony is most powerful when it quantifies the deviation from the standard of care in financial terms. It is not sufficient to say that the broker "should have done more diligence." The expert must establish what a reasonably competent broker would have done, what loan amount would have resulted from proper underwriting, and what the financial harm to the borrower was as a result of the deviation. This quantification is what converts a liability finding into a damages award.

Key Takeaways

  • Market-specific underwriting benchmarks matter in litigation. South Bend's industrial, multifamily, and mixed-use fundamentals are observable and documentable, and a lender or broker who applied the wrong assumptions for this market deviated from the standard of care in a demonstrable way.
  • DSCR and LTV are the two convergent tests for maximum loan supportability. In this engagement, both tests independently produced a maximum loan amount materially below the actual loan, which is among the strongest possible indications of underwriting failure.
  • A 1.25x DSCR minimum is the market standard for commercial real estate loans in secondary Midwest markets. Any originator applying a sub-1.20x DSCR to a non-institutional asset in South Bend bears the burden of explaining that deviation in terms of documented compensating factors.

Expert Witness: Financing a Contaminated Industrial Property in Baltimore, MD

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Environmental Contamination Analysis

The Baltimore Market Context

Baltimore's industrial and logistics real estate market has matured significantly over the past decade, driven by its position as a major Mid-Atlantic port city and its proximity to the I-95 corridor connecting Washington, D.C., Philadelphia, and New York. The Port of Baltimore handles more roll-on/roll-off cargo than any other U.S. port, and that activity has historically anchored demand for warehouse, distribution, and light industrial product throughout the Baltimore-Washington region. For counsel and investors evaluating a distressed industrial asset in this market, that macro context matters because it affects both leasing fundamentals and the lender appetite for this collateral type.

As of mid-2024, Baltimore's industrial vacancy rate hovered in the 4% to 6% range across major submarkets including Chesapeake Commerce Center, the I-95 Corridor in Harford and Baltimore counties, and the Broening Highway industrial cluster near the port. These are tight conditions by historical standards, though some softening has occurred from the record lows recorded in 2021 and 2022. Average asking rents for bulk distribution and warehouse product in the Baltimore MSA range from approximately $9.00 to $14.00 per square foot NNN depending on submarket, vintage, and clear height. Class A product with modern specifications commands the upper end; older, single-story industrial with limited truck court depth and lower clear heights trades at meaningful discounts.

That rent spread is critical in contamination-related litigation involving NNN-leased industrial assets. A property leased at $7.00 per square foot when the market has moved to $11.00 or $12.00 represents a below-market income stream that simultaneously depresses the debt-service coverage calculation and complicates the valuation narrative. Lenders underwriting to market rent rather than in-place rent, or vice versa, will arrive at materially different loan supportability conclusions. This is precisely the kind of analytical nuance that generates expert witness disputes in secured creditor proceedings.

Baltimore also has an active and diverse commercial lending community. Regional banks headquartered in Maryland, national CMBS conduits, life insurance companies with Midwest and Mid-Atlantic allocation mandates, and debt funds are all active in the market. That diversity is both an opportunity and a source of complexity in litigation: the financing universe is not monolithic, and the availability of debt depends heavily on which lender category you are targeting and what conditions the collateral presents.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by counsel representing a class of secured creditors in a regulatory enforcement proceeding involving an industrial warehouse property in the Baltimore metropolitan area. The asset was a single-tenant NNN-leased warehouse facility on a multi-acre industrial site with documented contamination from prior industrial operations. The contamination involved 1,4-dioxane and chlorinated volatile organic compounds, specifically trichloroethylene and perchloroethylene, substances that trigger both Maryland Department of the Environment review protocols and federal CERCLA-related inquiry by prospective lenders.

The existing capital stack included senior mortgage debt that the borrower was unable to service at current market rates. The creditor group needed to assess whether, absent the borrower's cooperation, a forced sale or consensual restructuring could generate proceeds sufficient to retire the senior obligation. Before that question could be answered on a financing basis, a threshold question had to be resolved: does the documented environmental condition categorically preclude institutional first-mortgage financing, or are there lenders active in the Baltimore market who will advance capital against contaminated collateral under defined conditions?

Our engagement was defined around two core questions. First, is first-mortgage financing available at all given the environmental record, and if so, from what categories of lenders and on what terms? Second, assuming financing is available, what is the maximum loan amount supportable by the in-place income stream under current market underwriting standards, and does that maximum loan exceed the outstanding senior debt balance? The answers to both questions had direct bearing on the creditors' litigation strategy, their valuation position, and their assessment of the borrower's argument that no conventional exit existed.

The Financial Analysis

The income analysis began with the in-place NNN lease, which by definition shifts most operating expenses to the tenant. In a true triple-net structure, the landlord's net operating income is very close to the base rent after deducting the landlord's residual obligations: property management, non-recoverable capital expenditures, and vacancy reserve. For this property, the in-place rent translated to a gross annual base rent of approximately $1,540,000 on roughly 220,000 square feet at $7.00 per square foot. After a management fee of 2% and a capital reserve of $0.15 per square foot per year, the stabilized NOI used for underwriting was approximately $1,474,000.

Market rent for comparable industrial product in the relevant Baltimore submarket supported a market-rate NOI of approximately $2,200,000 to $2,400,000. That gap between in-place and market NOI is not merely an appraisal question; it is a direct input into the maximum loan calculation, and different lenders treat it differently. CMBS and agency lenders typically underwrite to in-place rent unless the lease rolls within 12 months of loan origination. Balance sheet lenders, particularly debt funds, may credit a portion of the mark-to-market upside if the lease term is short enough to present a realistic near-term repricing event.

The DSCR analysis applied the market standard threshold of 1.25x for industrial collateral, which reflects the current consensus among institutional lenders for single-tenant industrial in non-gateway markets. Some lenders, particularly community banks and regional banks holding loans on their own balance sheet, will drop to 1.20x for strong sponsors with established relationships, but 1.25x is the appropriate conservative benchmark for a litigation opinion because it represents the standard a creditor's expert must be able to defend against cross-examination from a borrower's financing expert.

Assuming a permanent loan at a rate of 7.25%, which reflects a 250-basis-point spread over the five-year Treasury note at the time of our analysis, on a 25-year amortization schedule, the annual debt service per $1,000,000 of loan balance is approximately $86,400. Working backward from in-place NOI of $1,474,000 and a 1.25x DSCR requirement, the maximum supportable annual debt service is $1,179,200. Dividing by the per-dollar debt service factor of $86,400 per million produces a maximum loan of approximately $13,648,000 on a cash flow basis.

The LTV constraint is applied independently. For contaminated industrial collateral in Baltimore, institutional lenders willing to engage at all will generally cap LTV at 55% to 65% of the as-is appraised value, with the lower end applying when Phase II findings have not been fully characterized or when a remediation timeline is undefined. If the as-is appraised value of the property, reflecting both the below-market lease and the environmental stigma, is in the range of $17,000,000 to $19,000,000, then the 65% LTV cap produces a loan ceiling of $11,050,000 to $12,350,000. In this scenario, the LTV constraint is the binding constraint, not cash flow coverage.

The analytical conclusion is that maximum supportable first-mortgage financing falls in the range of $11,000,000 to $12,500,000 under current market conditions, depending on lender category and the state of environmental characterization at the time of application. If the existing senior debt exceeds that range, the creditor's financing expert can credibly opine that the property cannot support a refinancing sufficient to retire that debt under any currently available institutional financing program without supplemental equity or debt paydown.

The Complicating Factors

1,4-Dioxane and Chlorinated VOC Contamination as a Credit Event. Lenders do not treat environmental contamination as simply a cost-line item to be deducted from value. They treat it as a credit event that triggers entirely separate underwriting protocols. Most institutional lenders, including national banks, life companies, and CMBS conduits, have environmental policy requirements that prohibit origination on sites with unresolved Phase II findings involving chlorinated solvents or 1,4-dioxane without either a completed remedial action plan accepted by the relevant regulatory authority or an environmental insurance policy from an admitted carrier with specific coverage triggers. The reason is straightforward: in a default scenario, the lender could become a mortgagee in possession or could take title through foreclosure, and CERCLA's mortgagee exemption has defined limits that most bank legal departments do not want to test in the Fourth Circuit.

MDE Enforcement Posture and the Consent Order Effect. When the Maryland Department of the Environment has entered a regulatory enforcement action, the existence of that action appears in environmental database searches and is typically disclosed in the Phase I Environmental Site Assessment that any competent lender will require. A site under active MDE enforcement is categorically different from a site with historical contamination that has been assessed and closed. Active enforcement means remediation obligations are not yet quantified, timelines are not fixed, and the liability overhang is open-ended. Lenders price uncertainty, and open-ended environmental liability is among the highest forms of uncertainty in their risk framework. This is what distinguishes the financing universe for this collateral from a clean industrial comparable: the eligible lender pool shrinks from the full institutional market to a much narrower set of specialized lenders and balance sheet lenders with environmental expertise on staff.

Below-Market Rent and Lease Term Interactions. A below-market NNN lease creates a paradox in contaminated asset financing. On one hand, the in-place lease generates predictable cash flow, which provides some income floor. On the other hand, the below-market rent suppresses NOI and therefore suppresses debt capacity precisely at the moment when the property needs maximum financing to retire legacy debt. Furthermore, lenders evaluating contaminated industrial collateral are acutely aware that a creditworthy tenant in place is one of the few factors that might motivate them to proceed at all; the loss of that tenant would leave the lender with an environmentally impaired vacant industrial building, an outcome most institutional lenders will underwrite against aggressively by shortening loan terms and tightening LTV. If the remaining lease term is short, say three to five years, the lender's exit risk increases substantially and is reflected in pricing and proceeds.

Remediation Cost Capitalization. Lenders willing to advance against contaminated collateral will typically require that any remediation cost estimate from a qualified environmental engineer be fully capitalized and either escrowed at closing or deducted from proceeds. If the Phase II report and remedial action plan indicate cleanup costs of, say, $1,500,000 to $3,000,000, the lender will either hold back that amount from loan proceeds at origination or require the borrower to post it as an environmental reserve. Either mechanism reduces the net proceeds available to retire existing debt, compressing the effective loan benefit further.

The Baltimore Lender Market

The lender market for contaminated industrial assets in Baltimore is narrower than for clean collateral, but it is not empty. Understanding which lenders will engage, and at what terms, is essential for a credible financing opinion.

Balance Sheet Regional and Community Banks. Maryland and Mid-Atlantic regional banks with in-house environmental credit policy, including several that maintain dedicated environmental risk officers, will occasionally originate on sites with documented but characterized contamination under an accepted remedial action plan. These lenders typically require: a No Further Requirements letter from MDE or a Brownfields agreement, a satisfactory Phase II with a defined scope of work, environmental insurance, and personal or corporate guarantees. Loan-to-value for these lenders on contaminated industrial in Baltimore is typically 55% to 60%, with rates reflecting a credit risk premium of 50 to 100 basis points above their standard industrial product. These are balance sheet loans, not sold into secondary markets, which gives the lender more flexibility on structure but also means approval is deeply relationship-dependent.

Debt Funds and Bridge Lenders. Non-bank debt funds active in the Mid-Atlantic market represent the most realistic financing source for contaminated collateral under active enforcement. These lenders, which include specialized real estate credit funds that have built out environmental underwriting capabilities, will advance capital at higher rates, typically in the 9.5% to 11.5% range as of mid-2024, in exchange for taking the environmental risk that banks will not. They underwrite to a shorter hold period, usually 24 to 36 months, with the expectation that the borrower will clean up the site, obtain regulatory closure, and refinance into permanent debt. The LTV for this category is often 50% to 60% of as-is value, and fees at origination are material, typically 1.5 to 2.5 points. For a litigation financing opinion, debt fund availability is relevant because it establishes that some form of capital is accessible, even if the cost structure is not supportive of retiring a below-market senior obligation.

CMBS Conduits and Life Insurance Companies. These categories are essentially unavailable for this collateral profile. CMBS conduit lenders are bound by REMIC regulations and B-piece buyer requirements that effectively prohibit origination on sites with unresolved environmental conditions. Life companies active in Baltimore, several of which have mid-Atlantic allocation mandates and have historically been aggressive on clean industrial product in the I-95 corridor, have environmental policies that are similarly prohibitive. These lender categories should be addressed in a financing opinion precisely to establish that they are not available options, which helps define the outer bounds of the financing market.

What Attorneys Handling Baltimore CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to Retain a CRE Financing Expert. A CRE financing expert is not a replacement for an appraiser and not a substitute for an environmental engineer. The financing expert fills a specific analytical gap: what capital markets participants would actually do with this collateral, today, in this market. That question is distinct from value and distinct from cleanup cost. It arises in secured creditor disputes, bankruptcy plan confirmation hearings, lender liability claims, mortgage fraud matters, and cases where a borrower argues that a lender's refusal to extend credit was unreasonable or commercially unjustifiable. In Baltimore, where both the regulatory environment and the industrial lending market have specific characteristics, local market knowledge combined with capital markets expertise is essential.

What a Financing Expert Can Opine on That an Appraiser Cannot. Appraisers determine value. Financing experts determine debt capacity, lender market availability, and the reasonableness of lending decisions. These are not the same thing. An appraiser will tell you what a contaminated Baltimore industrial property is worth. A financing expert will tell you whether any lender in the current market will advance $X against that collateral, at what rate, on what terms, and whether those terms would permit retirement of specific legacy obligations. In a plan of reorganization, for example, the question is not just value but whether a hypothetical buyer could finance a purchase at a price that satisfies the plan. Those are financing questions, not appraisal questions.

Qualifications That Matter. The relevant credentials for a CRE financing expert are active market participation, not merely historical banking experience. A retired bank officer who last originated loans in 2015 cannot credibly opine on what a debt fund will require in 2024 for contaminated Baltimore industrial collateral. The qualified expert should be able to demonstrate active lender relationships across the categories relevant to the subject collateral, recent transaction experience in the relevant market and asset type, and familiarity with current underwriting standards as they actually exist in the market, not as they existed in a prior rate environment or lending cycle.

Information Counsel Should Gather Before the Engagement. The most productive expert engagements begin with complete documentation. Counsel should assemble the Phase I and Phase II reports, the current MDE enforcement correspondence, the NNN lease with all amendments, the current rent roll, the existing loan documents including any environmental representations and warranties, the most recent ALTA survey, and any remedial cost estimates. If an appraisal exists, the financing expert should receive it but will not be bound by it; the financing expert's loan supportability analysis is independent of the appraised value and should be presented as such.

Use at Trial and in Plan Hearings. In a bankruptcy plan confirmation context, the financing expert supports the "best interests" and "feasibility" analyses by opining on whether the plan's proposed refinancing is achievable at the terms projected. In a secured creditor enforcement proceeding, the expert's opinion establishes whether a deficiency claim is accurate by determining whether the collateral can support sufficient debt to satisfy the obligation. In a lender liability context, the expert can opine on whether the lender's underwriting decision was consistent with or deviated from market standard practices. CLS CRE has provided Rule 26 compliant expert reports across all three contexts.

Key Takeaways

  • Environmental contamination does not categorically preclude financing, but it dramatically narrows the eligible lender universe from the full institutional market to a specialized subset of balance sheet lenders and debt funds, each with materially different pricing and structural requirements

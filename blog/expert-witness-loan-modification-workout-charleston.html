Expert Witness: Commercial Loan Modification Standards in Charleston, SC

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Loan Modification and Workout Dispute

The Charleston Market Context

Charleston, South Carolina has evolved from a regional port economy into one of the Southeast's most dynamic commercial real estate markets, and that evolution is directly relevant to how loan modification and workout disputes arise here. The market's growth has been rapid enough that property valuations, rent assumptions, and lease absorption rates embedded in loan underwriting from 2019 through 2022 often reflected optimistic projections that were reasonable at origination but became strained as interest rates moved sharply higher and operating costs escalated. That gap between underwriting assumptions and realized performance is precisely where workout disputes are born.

On the industrial side, Charleston's port-driven logistics economy has kept vacancy rates in the 3% to 5% range across much of the Lowcountry submarket, with asking rents for Class A warehouse and distribution space in the North Charleston and Summerville corridors pushing well above $8.00 per square foot NNN in recent lease cycles. That strength has generally supported industrial borrowers through the rate cycle, but properties with shorter weighted average lease terms or heavy tenant concentration have still found themselves in workout discussions when their debt service coverage eroded under floating-rate bridge loans. Multifamily in Charleston has experienced similar tension: a robust demand story driven by in-migration and limited developable land collided with a substantial construction pipeline in 2022 and 2023, compressing effective rents and pushing stabilized cap rates into the 5.25% to 5.75% range for newer product while older assets in secondary submarkets like Summerville or Goose Creek trade at 6.0% to 6.5%. Retail in the urban core, particularly along King Street and in the mixed-use nodes of Mount Pleasant, has maintained strong occupancy, but suburban strip retail with grocery-anchored vacancy issues or single-tenant exposure has been a meaningful source of workout activity. Office remains the most structurally challenged asset class across the metro, with Class B suburban office in West Ashley and North Charleston experiencing vacancy in the 18% to 24% range and virtually no institutional capital willing to refinance without significant equity paydown.

Understanding these market-specific fundamentals is not background noise for a workout dispute. It is the analytical foundation. A lender's decision to accelerate, extend, or impose punitive modification terms cannot be evaluated in isolation. It must be benchmarked against what the market actually supported at the time the modification was negotiated, and that requires someone with active, current knowledge of Charleston's lending environment.

The Engagement

In this engagement, our firm, Commercial Lending Solutions, was retained by litigation counsel representing a commercial real estate borrower in a dispute arising from a contested loan workout on a Charleston-area income-producing property. The asset was a mid-size anchored retail center located in the greater Charleston metro, encumbered by a fixed-rate CMBS loan that had been originated several years prior and was approaching maturity. The borrower had entered into preliminary workout discussions with the loan's special servicer following a significant rent reduction negotiated with a major tenant whose lease had been restructured during the pandemic. The borrower contended that the special servicer's conduct during the modification process was commercially unreasonable, that the terms ultimately imposed were inconsistent with what a similarly situated lender would have offered given the property's stabilized cash flow and market recovery trajectory, and that the servicer's failure to engage in good-faith negotiations caused material economic harm. Counsel retained CLS CRE to provide a Rule 26 expert report addressing three specific questions: whether the lender's modification process met the applicable standard of care for workout negotiations in the commercial real estate lending industry; whether the terms the servicer proposed were commercially reasonable given the property's then-current and projected NOI; and whether alternative workout structures existed in the market at the time that would have allowed the borrower to cure without the equity impairment they suffered.

The Financial Analysis

The analytical framework for evaluating whether a loan modification was commercially reasonable begins with the same underwriting mechanics a competent lender would have applied when evaluating the modification request. The question is not whether the lender had the contractual right to impose certain terms. The question is whether the terms actually imposed reflected a rational, market-consistent analysis of the property's supportable debt capacity, or whether they reflected a departure from that analysis in a manner inconsistent with how the commercial lending industry actually conducts workouts.

For this retail center, the first step was reconstructing the trailing twelve-month net operating income as of the date the modification request was submitted. This required reviewing actual rent rolls, base rent escalations, CAM reconciliations, vacancy allowances, and management fee assumptions. The property's effective gross income was calculated at approximately $1,385,000 annually, after applying a market-standard 5% vacancy and credit loss reserve appropriate for an anchored center with one large anchor and several smaller in-line tenants. Operating expenses, including property taxes at the applicable Charleston County rate, insurance, maintenance, and third-party management at 4% of effective gross income, totaled approximately $415,000, yielding a stabilized NOI of approximately $970,000.

The standard underwriting threshold for retail workouts in the institutional lending market is a minimum debt service coverage ratio of 1.25x at the modified loan terms. At 1.25x DSCR, the property's NOI of $970,000 could support maximum annual debt service of $776,000. Capitalizing that debt service at a market interest rate of 7.25%, which represented the prevailing rate for stabilized retail product in the Southeast at the time of the modification negotiation, and amortizing over a 25-year schedule, the maximum supportable loan balance was approximately $9,250,000. At a market-standard underwriting LTV of 65% for anchored retail in this cycle, the as-is appraised value would have needed to support a loan of that size, implying a minimum property value of approximately $14,200,000.

The lender's internal analysis, which was produced in discovery, applied a 1.25x DSCR threshold but used a stressed interest rate of 8.50% rather than the actual rate environment at the time of the modification, reducing the maximum supportable debt to approximately $7,900,000. This stressed rate was not disclosed to the borrower, was not standard practice for fixed-rate CMBS modifications at that time, and had the effect of creating an artificial equity shortfall that required the borrower to either inject capital or accept punitive extension fees. My analysis quantified the dollar impact of this stress test variance at approximately $1,350,000 in implied required equity, which formed the economic backbone of the borrower's damages claim. This is a precise, replicable calculation grounded in lender underwriting norms, not a speculative opinion.

The Complicating Factors

CMBS Servicing Constraints and the Special Servicer Dynamic. The most significant structural complication in this engagement was the CMBS trust structure itself. CMBS loans, once securitized, are governed by Pooling and Servicing Agreements that impose specific constraints on what a special servicer can and cannot offer a borrower in distress. Special servicers have broad authority to negotiate modifications, but their discretion is bounded by the requirement to act in the best interest of certificateholders as a collective whole, which in practice means the servicer's analysis is heavily influenced by what generates the least loss to the trust rather than what produces the best outcome for the borrower. Attorneys handling CMBS workout disputes must understand that the special servicer is not a conventional lender. It is a fiduciary to a trust. That structure creates both a legitimate defense for the servicer's conduct and a meaningful limitation on the flexibility it can credibly offer. Distinguishing between what was structurally constrained and what was a discretionary decision made in bad faith is a critical analytical task for the expert witness.

Tenant Concentration Risk and the Lease Restructuring Record. The anchor tenant's rent reduction, documented through a lease amendment executed during the pandemic, had been fully disclosed to the servicer at origination of the workout discussion. The complication was that the servicer's internal credit memo treated the reduced rent as temporary rather than permanent, even though the lease amendment contained no restoration provision and the market evidence supported the conclusion that the restructured rent reflected the new market rate for comparable anchor space in the Charleston submarket. An expert who understands retail lease economics can explain to a jury or arbitration panel why a servicer's categorization of a rent reduction as temporary versus permanent is a material underwriting determination, not a technicality, and why getting it wrong in the borrower's disfavor has direct financial consequences.

Timing and Market Recovery Evidence. The workout negotiations in this matter extended over approximately fourteen months. During that period, the Charleston retail market showed measurable signs of recovery: a significant in-line tenant expanded its footprint, asking rents in comparable centers increased, and a nearby property transacted at a cap rate that implied a value 11% higher than the servicer's internal valuation used during the modification period. A qualified CRE financing expert can map the lender's conduct against the market's trajectory, demonstrating that terms that might have been defensible at the outset of negotiations became progressively less commercially reasonable as market conditions improved and the lender failed to update its analysis.

The Charleston Lender Market

Understanding who was actively lending in the Charleston commercial market during the relevant period is essential to evaluating whether the servicer's modification terms were commercially reasonable in context. If alternative capital was available at terms more favorable than what the servicer offered, that fact bears directly on whether the servicer's conduct caused the borrower to suffer avoidable harm.

Community and Regional Banks. Charleston has a meaningful community banking presence, including several South Carolina-chartered institutions with active CRE portfolios across the Lowcountry. These lenders were actively providing stabilized retail loans in the $5,000,000 to $15,000,000 range during the relevant period, typically at 65% to 70% LTV, with DSCR floors of 1.20x to 1.25x and five-year fixed terms amortizing over 25 years. Rates for well-located anchored retail generally ranged from 6.75% to 7.50% depending on the borrower's deposit relationship and overall credit profile. Several of these institutions do not require secondary market execution, which means their credit decisions are made locally and can accommodate market nuance that a CMBS servicer's standardized analysis would miss.

Debt Funds and Bridge Lenders. For properties with transitional characteristics, several non-bank debt funds with regional Southeast presence were active in Charleston during this period, offering bridge financing in the $5,000,000 to $30,000,000 range at 65% to 70% LTV. Pricing on these instruments was typically SOFR plus 350 to 500 basis points, with 24 to 36-month terms and extension options. These lenders are relevantly competitive in a workout analysis because they represent a borrower's realistic refinance alternative, meaning that if a borrower could have exited the existing CMBS loan through a refinance with a bridge lender, the economic harm caused by the servicer's delay or unreasonable terms can be quantified against that alternative.

Life Companies and Agency Capital. For multifamily and high-quality stabilized commercial properties in Charleston, life company lenders and agency programs represent the most competitive long-term capital. Life companies were offering 10-year fixed rates in the 6.25% to 6.75% range for stabilized retail and industrial in the Charleston metro during the relevant period, with LTV ceilings of 60% to 65% and conservative DSCR requirements of 1.25x to 1.30x. These lenders are relevant in a workout analysis as benchmarks for commercially reasonable permanent modification terms, even if the borrower could not immediately access them due to the CMBS structure.

What Attorneys Handling Charleston CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to Retain a CRE Financing Expert. Counsel should consider retaining a CRE financing expert at the earliest stage of a commercial real estate loan workout dispute, ideally before the expert's scope is artificially constrained by a narrowly drafted engagement letter. The expert is most valuable when retained to analyze not just damages but the lender's conduct, the reasonableness of its underwriting conclusions, and the availability of alternative capital. Waiting until trial preparation to engage the expert limits the analytical value the expert can provide during discovery.

What a CRE Financing Expert Can Answer That an Appraiser Cannot. Appraisers determine value. CRE financing experts determine financeable value, which is a related but distinct concept. The expert can testify on DSCR thresholds, LTV constraints, rate assumptions, lender credit box requirements, market liquidity conditions, and whether a specific lender's internal analysis deviated from industry norms. The expert can also explain whether a lender's workout proposal was consistent with what a reasonably prudent lender in the same position would have offered, drawing on active transactional experience rather than retrospective valuation methodology.

Qualifications That Matter. A retired banker who underwrote loans a decade ago is not the same credential as an active commercial mortgage broker with current lender relationships across multiple capital sources. The most effective CRE financing expert witnesses are those who can speak to current market conditions because they are transacting in those conditions today. CLS CRE has closed approximately $1 billion in commercial real estate transactions, maintains relationships with more than 1,000 lenders across all major capital categories, and is active in 50 states. That transactional depth is what allows me to say with authority that a specific interest rate assumption or DSCR threshold was or was not consistent with market practice at a specific point in time.

Information Counsel Should Gather Before Engagement. Before retaining a CRE financing expert, counsel should secure the following: the original loan documents and credit memorandum; all modification and forbearance correspondence between the borrower and lender; the servicer's internal valuation or BPO, if available through discovery; rent rolls and financial statements for the property covering at least 24 months prior to the workout; any third-party appraisals or broker opinions of value; and all internal communications at the lender or servicer regarding the modification decision. The expert analysis is only as strong as the underlying document record.

Use at Trial and in Arbitration. CRE financing expert testimony is typically used to establish the commercial reasonableness of a lender's conduct, to quantify damages caused by a lender's deviation from industry standards, and to rebut defense experts who claim the lender's underwriting analysis was within the acceptable range of professional judgment. In Chapter 11 plan hearings, expert testimony on financing feasibility is often central to plan confirmation where the debtor must demonstrate that a proposed exit financing is realistic and that the plan's projected values are supportable in the capital markets.

Key Takeaways

  • Market context is not background. It is evidence. A lender's workout conduct must be evaluated against what the Charleston commercial real estate market actually supported at the time of the modification, including available capital, prevailing rates, and comparable property performance. Generic national standards are insufficient.
  • DSCR and rate assumptions are the engine of the analysis. The difference between a 7.25% and an 8.50% stress rate in an NOI-based underwriting model can produce implied equity shortfalls exceeding $1,000,000 on a mid-size retail asset. These are not abstract technical points; they are quantifiable damages.
  • CMBS servicer conduct is structurally different from bank lender conduct. The Pooling and Servicing Agreement creates both legitimate constraints and legitimate obligations. An expert who does not understand that distinction cannot credibly evaluate whether the servicer's conduct was reasonable or retaliatory.
  • Alternative capital availability is a damages multiplier. If the borrower could have refinanced at commercially reasonable terms had the servicer acted differently, the economic harm attributable to the servicer's delay or bad faith can be precisely calculated against that alternative exit.
  • Tenant lease characterization has direct underwriting consequences. Whether an anchor tenant's rent reduction is treated as temporary or permanent in the servicer's NOI model is not a semantic distinction. It determines the maximum supportable loan and can single-handedly drive an otherwise viable asset into an artificial distress scenario.
  • Community banking depth in Charleston creates meaningful refinance alternatives. Local and regional institutions were active in the relevant size range during the dispute period, which means borrowers were not solely dependent on CMBS workouts. The existence of realistic alternative capital is a critical element in evaluating damages and lender conduct.
  • Retain the expert early and define scope broadly. The CRE financing expert is most valuable when engaged before discovery closes, when document requests can be shaped around the expert's analytical needs, and when deposition strategy can be informed by the expert's preliminary findings.
  • Active transactional experience distinguishes a credible expert from a theoretical one. Courts and arbitrators respond to experts who can say "I closed a transaction with this lender category on these terms in this market within the past twelve months." That specificity is the difference between persuasive and academic testimony.

Expert Witness: NNN Lease Rollover Upside and Financing Impact in Phoenix, AZ

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Below-Market NNN Lease and Rollover Upside Analysis

The Phoenix Market Context

Phoenix has emerged as one of the most dynamic commercial real estate markets in the American Southwest, and that dynamism creates both opportunity and complexity when lease structure and financing capacity become the subject of litigation. The Greater Phoenix metro absorbed more than 40 million square feet of industrial space in net terms over the 2020 through 2023 cycle, driven by e-commerce fulfillment, semiconductor supply chain buildout, and last-mile logistics demand anchored by the TSMC campus expansion in north Phoenix and the broader West Valley industrial corridor. Retail and single-tenant net lease assets tracked closely with that population growth, and cap rates for investment-grade NNN product in submarkets like Chandler, Gilbert, Scottsdale, and Peoria compressed into the 4.75 to 5.75 percent range during peak capital market conditions in 2021 and early 2022.

The relevance to below-market lease disputes is direct. When a lease was executed in 2018 or 2019 at rents that reflected the market at that time, and Phoenix market rents have since escalated 20 to 35 percent in certain retail and flex industrial corridors, the spread between in-place rent and current market rent is not theoretical. It is a measurable, documentable number with real implications for both valuation and loan sizing. Lenders underwriting that asset today are not looking at the same property they would have been looking at three years ago, and whether the below-market lease enhances or impairs financing depends on a set of factors that require specialized analysis well beyond what a standard MAI appraisal delivers.

Single-tenant NNN assets in Phoenix range from fast-casual restaurant pads and pharmacy-anchored parcels to large-format auto service centers and medical outpatient facilities. Each subtype carries different lease structure conventions, different lender appetite, and different rollover risk profiles. Understanding those distinctions, and being able to explain them to a trier of fact or in a bankruptcy plan hearing, is the core of what a qualified CRE financing expert provides.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing a party in a commercial real estate dispute involving a single-tenant, triple-net-leased retail property in the Phoenix metropolitan area. The asset was a freestanding commercial building of approximately 8,000 to 10,000 square feet, occupied by a single national tenant under a long-term NNN lease with fixed rent bumps. The in-place rent had been set at origination and had not kept pace with the substantial rent growth the Phoenix retail market experienced over the subsequent lease term. At the time of the dispute, the in-place rent was meaningfully below current market rent for comparable space in the submarket.

Counsel retained CLS CRE to address several specific financial and market questions. First, how would a commercial mortgage lender underwrite the property given the below-market in-place lease, and what maximum loan amount would be supportable under prevailing lender credit standards? Second, does the existence of rollover upside, meaning the prospect of higher rents at lease expiration, influence loan sizing, and if so, how and under what conditions? Third, was the financing structure at issue in the dispute consistent with what a reasonably informed borrower could have obtained in the commercial lending market during the relevant period? Fourth, what refinancing options, if any, existed given the specific DSCR and LTV constraints created by the lease structure?

These are financing questions, not appraisal questions. An appraiser can opine on market value. A CRE financing expert opines on what the capital markets will actually do with that value, which is a distinct and often dispositive issue in disputes where a party's ability to finance, refinance, or extract equity from a property is central to the damages theory or the feasibility of a plan.

The Financial Analysis

The starting point for any NNN lender underwrite is the in-place net operating income. On a triple-net lease, the NOI is effectively the base rent, since the tenant pays taxes, insurance, and maintenance. For this engagement, we used a representative in-place annual base rent of approximately $420,000, reflecting a lease executed at market rates several years prior in a Phoenix submarket where current market rents for comparable single-tenant retail space would support a rent in the range of $540,000 to $560,000 annually. That gap of roughly $120,000 to $140,000 per year is the below-market rent spread at issue.

Conventional commercial mortgage lenders underwriting a single-tenant NNN property apply a debt service coverage ratio test as the primary loan sizing constraint. The market standard DSCR for this asset type is 1.20x to 1.25x, meaning the in-place NOI must cover projected annual debt service by that factor. For lenders with more conservative credit standards, particularly CMBS conduit lenders and some life insurance companies, 1.25x is the floor. Agency-adjacent lenders and some bank portfolio lenders may underwrite to 1.20x, but they typically require stronger sponsorship and lower LTV to compensate.

Using a 30-year amortization schedule and an illustrative note rate of 7.00 percent, the annual debt service constant is approximately 7.98 percent of the loan balance. At a 1.25x DSCR requirement, the maximum supportable loan from an in-place NOI of $420,000 is calculated as follows: $420,000 divided by 1.25 equals $336,000 in maximum annual debt service. Dividing $336,000 by the 0.0798 debt service constant produces a maximum loan amount of approximately $4,210,000. If the appraised value of the property is $7,500,000, that loan represents an LTV of approximately 56 percent, well inside the 65 to 70 percent LTV ceiling that most lenders apply to single-tenant NNN retail. In this scenario, the loan is DSCR-constrained, not LTV-constrained, and the below-market rent is directly responsible for the constraint.

Had the lease been at market rent of $540,000, the same DSCR and rate assumptions would support a loan of approximately $5,413,000, an increase of roughly $1.2 million in loan proceeds. That differential is the financing impact of the below-market lease, and it is quantifiable, not speculative. It represents reduced equity access, reduced refinancing proceeds, and potentially the difference between a feasible and infeasible capital event depending on the existing debt that needs to be retired.

On the LTV side, most lenders cap single-tenant NNN at 65 percent LTV for properties with lease terms under ten years remaining, and 70 percent for longer-dated leases with investment-grade tenants. At a $7,500,000 appraised value, the 65 percent LTV ceiling is $4,875,000, and the 70 percent ceiling is $5,250,000. The DSCR-derived maximum of $4,210,000 is the binding constraint in this analysis, meaning additional collateral value or appraised value does not help the borrower because the income stream, not the asset value, is limiting the loan.

The Complicating Factors

Rollover Upside: What Lenders Will and Will Not Credit

The most common misunderstanding in disputes involving below-market leases is the assumption that lenders will credit rollover upside when sizing the loan. They generally will not, and understanding why is essential to any financing feasibility analysis. Lenders underwrite to in-place cash flow because the existing lease is the only cash flow that is contractually certain. The prospect of higher rents at rollover is speculative from the lender's perspective, contingent on the tenant renewing, the market holding, and the borrower successfully re-leasing at market. None of those outcomes are guaranteed.

There is a narrow exception. Some portfolio lenders, particularly regional banks and credit unions active in Phoenix, will apply a blended or forward-looking underwrite when the lease term remaining is short, the submarket fundamentals are strong, and the sponsorship is experienced. In those cases, the lender may underwrite to a stabilized NOI, defined as the projected NOI assuming the space re-leases at current market rates following the existing tenant's departure. However, lenders applying stabilized underwriting universally apply a haircut to account for vacancy, leasing commissions, and tenant improvement costs during the rollover period. They also typically require a lease-up reserve funded at closing, which reduces net loan proceeds.

For litigation purposes, the critical point is that rollover upside does not convert a below-market lease into a market-lease financing. It creates a more complex, higher-risk underwriting scenario that narrows the lender pool and often results in a shorter loan term, a higher interest rate, or a recourse carve-out that would not exist on a fully stabilized deal.

Tenant Credit and Lease Term Remaining

The financing universe for a single-tenant NNN property is directly correlated with two variables: the credit quality of the tenant and the remaining lease term. A property with fifteen or more years of lease term remaining to an investment-grade tenant can access the full spectrum of lenders, including life insurance companies and CMBS conduits that offer the most competitive long-term fixed rates. As the remaining term shortens, particularly below seven years, institutional lenders step back and the financing universe contracts to regional banks and bridge lenders, both of which carry higher pricing and more restrictive terms.

In Phoenix, the relevance is heightened because the market has attracted a significant volume of sale-leaseback transactions in retail and industrial, many of which were executed with shorter initial lease terms and rent structures that now appear below market. Properties with five years or fewer of remaining term may only qualify for floating-rate bridge financing, which at current market rates carries a cost of SOFR plus 275 to 375 basis points. That type of debt is materially more expensive and creates execution risk that would not exist on a fully stabilized, long-term lease.

CMBS Conduit Eligibility

CMBS conduit financing is the dominant source of long-term fixed-rate non-recourse debt for stabilized NNN retail in the $3 million to $20 million range, which covers most of the single-tenant product in Phoenix. CMBS lenders have rigid credit boxes. They require a minimum DSCR of 1.25x on in-place income, they do not credit rollover upside, and they are particularly sensitive to single-tenant concentration risk. A below-market lease that produces a DSCR below 1.25x on the conduit matrix eliminates CMBS as a financing option entirely, pushing the borrower into the higher-cost bank and bridge lending market. That shift in lender category is a concrete, quantifiable financing impact.

The Phoenix Lender Market

The Phoenix commercial mortgage market is well-developed and institutional, reflecting the metro's size and growth trajectory. For single-tenant NNN assets specifically, the active lender categories and their general credit parameters are as follows.

Life Insurance Companies: Life companies are the gold standard for long-term NNN financing on investment-grade tenanted properties with fifteen or more years of lease term. They typically offer ten-year fixed terms with 25 to 30-year amortization, non-recourse, at spreads of 150 to 200 basis points over corresponding Treasury yields. Their DSCR floors are generally 1.25x and their LTV ceilings are 60 to 65 percent. They are not an option for below-market lease scenarios unless the in-place NOI satisfies their coverage requirements independently of any rollover assumption.

CMBS Conduit Lenders: Active and competitive in Phoenix across all major asset types. As noted, CMBS underwriting is strictly in-place, 1.25x DSCR, non-recourse with standard carve-outs. Pricing is typically 180 to 250 basis points over swap rates for ten-year fixed loans. CMBS lenders will consider single-tenant retail provided the lease term exceeds the loan term by at least one year, which effectively requires a minimum of eleven years of remaining lease term for a standard ten-year CMBS loan.

Regional and Community Banks: Phoenix has deep community bank penetration, with a number of Arizona-chartered institutions and regional banks active in portfolio lending for NNN assets in the $2 million to $15 million range. These lenders offer more flexibility on underwriting, including willingness to consider stabilized NOI projections in limited circumstances. They typically lend on five or seven-year terms with 25-year amortization and require personal recourse. Rates are generally floating or fixed at spreads of 200 to 275 basis points over SOFR or corresponding Treasury yields.

Bridge and Transitional Lenders: For below-market lease situations where conventional lenders cannot clear their DSCR threshold, bridge lenders offer short-term floating rate financing at higher leverage with a business plan assumption that the borrower will lease up or re-lease the space and refinance into permanent debt. Phoenix has attracted significant bridge lending activity given the market's rent growth story, but bridge debt carries SOFR-based pricing, typically two to three-year terms with extension options, and meaningful origination and exit fees. It is a viable path but a materially more expensive one.

What Attorneys Handling Phoenix CRE Disputes Need to Know

Commercial real estate litigation in Phoenix increasingly involves disputes where the financing feasibility of a property, or the impact of a specific lease structure on the capital markets, is the central question. Attorneys who handle these matters benefit significantly from retaining a CRE financing expert early in the engagement. Here is what counsel should understand about that process.

When to retain a CRE financing expert: Any matter in which a party's damages theory depends on what financing could or could not have been obtained, what loan proceeds were supportable, whether a refinancing was feasible, or whether a lender's conduct deviated from market standard requires a CRE financing expert. An appraiser establishes value. A CRE financing expert establishes what the capital markets will do with that value, which is a different question answered by a different discipline.

What a CRE financing expert can establish that an appraiser cannot: Lender underwriting criteria and credit box standards for specific asset types. DSCR and LTV thresholds by lender category. The impact of lease structure on lender eligibility. Loan sizing calculations based on in-place versus stabilized NOI. Whether specific financing was available in the market at a specific point in time. Whether a party's conduct in a financing transaction was consistent with industry custom and practice. None of these are within the standard scope of an MAI appraisal.

Qualifications that matter: A CRE financing expert should have current, active market participation. Lender credit standards, pricing, and appetite change rapidly, as the 2022 through 2023 rate environment demonstrated. A retired banker with no current deal flow cannot reliably opine on what lenders require today or what they required during a specific market window. CLS CRE maintains over 1,000 active lender relationships and has closed transactions in all 50 states, with approximately $1 billion in total transaction volume. That current market access is what makes the testimony credible and defensible on cross-examination.

Information counsel should gather before the engagement: The in-place lease, including all amendments and rent schedules. Any rent commencement letters or estoppels. The original loan documents and any subsequent modifications. Any appraisals or broker opinions of value. Correspondence between borrower and lenders during any financing attempt. Market data for the submarket during the relevant period, including vacancy, asking rents, and cap rate surveys. The more complete the record, the more precisely the financing expert can reconstruct what the capital markets would have done.

Use of financing expert testimony: At trial, a CRE financing expert can establish the damages attributable to a below-market lease in financing terms, the feasibility of a proposed plan of reorganization, or the reasonableness of a lender's credit decision. In plan confirmation hearings, financing expert testimony is often used to establish that a proposed exit facility is consistent with market terms, or conversely that a plan proponent's projected financing assumptions are unrealistic. The expert's role is to translate capital markets mechanics into a form that a judge or jury can understand and rely upon.

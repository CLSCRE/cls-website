Expert Witness: Commercial Loan Origination Standards in Victorville, CA

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Lender Liability and Loan Origination Standards

The Victorville Market Context

Victorville sits at the geographic and economic crossroads of the Inland Empire's High Desert submarket, straddling the I-15 corridor between Los Angeles and Las Vegas. That positioning is not incidental to commercial real estate finance disputes. It shapes how lenders underwrite assets here, how brokers source capital, and what standards of care apply when a loan goes wrong. Attorneys handling CRE litigation in this market need to understand that Victorville does not underwrite like the Coachella Valley, nor like the core Inland Empire submarkets of Ontario or Chino. It carries its own risk profile, its own lender universe, and its own tolerance thresholds.

On the industrial side, Victorville has benefited meaningfully from the logistics and e-commerce expansion that reshaped the broader Inland Empire over the past decade. The Southern California Logistics Airport (SCLA) complex anchors a significant industrial base, with large-bay distribution and manufacturing facilities attracting tenants seeking lower land costs than the Inland Empire's western tier. Industrial vacancy in the High Desert has generally ranged between 4% and 8% in recent years, with net asking rents trending upward from the mid-$0.40s per square foot NNN toward the low-$0.60s as availability tightened. For financing purposes, these rent levels are meaningful: they produce NOI figures that, at Victorville's prevailing cap rates (generally 6.0% to 7.5% for industrial, depending on building quality and tenancy), support loan amounts that fall squarely into the range community banks, credit unions, and regional lenders actively compete for.

Retail fundamentals in Victorville are more complicated. The market has historically over-retailed relative to its population base, and certain corridors carry above-average vacancy, concentrated in older inline strip centers and power center shadows. Multifamily demand, by contrast, has strengthened as workforce housing constraints push renters further from Los Angeles. Cap rates for stabilized multifamily product in Victorville have generally traded in the 5.5% to 7.0% range, again depending on vintage and quality. These figures matter enormously in financing disputes because whether a lender's underwritten value, and the loan derived from it, was defensible depends on whether the lender used market-calibrated cap rates or applied assumptions that were too aggressive or too conservative relative to what the market was actually doing at origination.

The Engagement

In this category of matter, counsel retained Commercial Lending Solutions to serve as a Rule 26 expert witness on behalf of a party to a lender liability and professional negligence dispute involving a commercial real estate loan origination in Victorville. The underlying asset was a mixed-use commercial property with both retail and warehouse components, located within the city's primary commercial corridor. The borrower had obtained financing through a mortgage broker who represented access to an institutional capital source, and the resulting loan closed under terms the borrower later contended were materially inconsistent with what had been disclosed and with what any reasonably competent commercial mortgage broker operating in this market would have arranged or recommended.

The core legal and financial questions we were retained to address included: whether the loan terms as originated, specifically the interest rate, amortization schedule, prepayment structure, and loan-to-value ratio, were consistent with what was available in the market at the time of origination; whether the broker's conduct in the origination process met the standards of care applicable to a commercial mortgage professional operating in the Inland Empire High Desert submarket; whether the lender's underwriting of the subject property's income and value was methodologically sound; and whether the borrower's resulting financial position, specifically the debt service coverage available from the property's actual cash flow, was foreseeable at origination based on a competent underwrite.

Counsel provided us with the loan origination file, the broker's correspondence and fee disclosure documents, the lender's credit memorandum, the appraisal, the rent roll, and historical operating statements for the property. CLS CRE analyzed each of these in the context of market conditions prevailing at origination.

The Financial Analysis

The first step in any financing standards analysis is reconstructing the underwrite as a competent broker or lender should have performed it at origination, then comparing that reconstruction to what was actually done. This is not a hindsight exercise. It is a forward-looking reconstruction of what the market data available at the time of origination would have produced if applied correctly.

For the subject property, the gross potential income was derived from the in-place rent roll at origination. The property produced gross scheduled income of approximately $312,000 annually across its tenancies. Applying a market-standard vacancy and credit loss factor of 8% to 10%, consistent with the subject's submarket and asset type at origination, produced an effective gross income of approximately $280,000 to $287,000. Operating expenses, confirmed against the borrower's historical statements and benchmarked against comparable assets in the High Desert, totaled approximately $95,000 annually, inclusive of property taxes, insurance, maintenance, and management. This produced a stabilized net operating income of approximately $185,000 to $192,000.

The standard DSCR threshold for a commercial real estate loan on a mixed-use asset in this risk tier is 1.20x to 1.25x, and most conventional lenders require 1.25x as a minimum to qualify. This is not an arbitrary figure: it represents the regulatory and credit policy consensus that emerged from decades of portfolio performance data and was reinforced following the 2008 credit crisis. Some SBA 504 programs allow marginal debt coverage closer to 1.15x under specific conditions, but for a conventional commercial mortgage on a multi-tenant mixed-use property in a secondary market like Victorville, 1.25x is the defensible floor.

At the time of origination, market interest rates for a 5-year fixed commercial mortgage on this asset type in this submarket were trading in the range of 6.25% to 7.00%, depending on lender type and leverage. Applying a 6.75% rate, 25-year amortization, and a 1.25x DSCR constraint, the maximum supportable annual debt service was approximately $148,000 to $154,000. At a constant rate of 6.75% over 25 years, this debt service corresponds to a loan balance in the range of $1,745,000 to $1,815,000.

The subject property was appraised at origination at $2,600,000. At a 65% LTV, the maximum loan size would have been $1,690,000; at 70% LTV, $1,820,000. The DSCR-constrained maximum loan and the LTV-constrained maximum loan were therefore broadly consistent with each other in the 65% to 70% range. The actual loan originated was $2,100,000, representing approximately 80.8% LTV. This is where the analysis became dispositive. No conventional commercial lender applies an 80% LTV on a mixed-use asset in a secondary market without either a very strong guarantor, significant additional collateral, or a specialized loan program. The DSCR at origination on the actual loan amount was approximately 0.99x, meaning the property's income did not cover debt service. A competent underwrite would have flagged this at origination. The documentation in the broker's file showed no evidence that this calculation was performed or disclosed.

The Complicating Factors

Mixed-Use Asset Classification. Mixed-use properties in secondary markets occupy an awkward position in lender credit boxes. A property with both retail and warehouse components does not fit neatly into any single asset class, and many institutional lenders will either decline or require a significant risk premium relative to pure-play industrial or pure-play retail. In Victorville, this classification challenge is compounded by the retail component's sensitivity to local consumer traffic patterns and anchor proximity. A broker operating with a competent knowledge of the lender market would have identified this constraint early and either sought lenders with explicit mixed-use programs or advised the borrower that the financing universe would be narrower and the terms less competitive than for a single-use asset.

Secondary Market Risk Pricing. Victorville is not a tertiary market, but it is not a primary market either. Institutional lenders, including CMBS conduits and life companies, apply a secondary market spread that typically adds 25 to 50 basis points to pricing relative to comparable deals in core Inland Empire locations. Regional and community banks are generally the most competitive capital source in this submarket precisely because they understand local conditions and are not imposing a geographic risk premium derived from national models. A broker who placed this loan with a lender applying a secondary market overlay without shopping the regional bank and credit union market failed to meet the standard of care applicable in this submarket.

Tenant Credit Quality and Lease Term. In underwriting income-producing property in a market like Victorville, lease term and tenant credit quality drive a significant portion of the lender's risk calculus. Short remaining lease terms on a mixed-use property create rollover risk that most lenders address by either sizing the loan more conservatively, requiring lease guarantees, or shortening the loan term to align with lease expirations. In this matter, a material portion of the rent roll had lease expirations within 24 months of origination. A competent underwrite would have addressed this explicitly, either by applying a higher vacancy reserve or by sizing debt service to the property's economic vacancy scenario rather than its in-place income.

Prepayment and Exit Exposure. The loan as originated contained a step-down prepayment penalty structured over five years that effectively trapped the borrower in the loan during the period of highest likelihood that a refinance would be needed as leases rolled. This structure is not inherently improper, but it is one that a competent broker must disclose clearly and in writing, with a plain-language explanation of the financial consequence of early exit. The origination file in this matter contained no such disclosure in the broker's communications, only a reference in the loan documents themselves, which does not satisfy the disclosure standard applicable to a commercial mortgage broker representing a borrower's interests.

The Victorville Lender Market

Understanding the actual lender landscape in Victorville at the time of origination is essential to evaluating whether a broker met the standard of care. The lender market for commercial real estate in the High Desert submarket is layered and predictable to an experienced practitioner.

Community and Regional Banks. These are the most active and most competitive lenders for commercial real estate in Victorville in the $1 million to $5 million range. Several California-chartered community banks with High Desert branch networks maintain dedicated commercial real estate lending programs tailored to this submarket. They understand local market conditions, apply realistic vacancy assumptions, and do not impose the secondary market overlays that national lenders use. Their typical credit parameters for a mixed-use asset in Victorville include maximum LTV of 65% to 70%, DSCR of 1.25x minimum, personal guaranty required, and rates priced at a spread over the 5-year Treasury or Prime, depending on the program. Loan officers at these institutions are often the most informed market participants regarding current cap rates and rent trends for this submarket.

Credit Unions. Member business lending programs at California credit unions have become an increasingly meaningful source of commercial real estate capital in secondary markets. For owner-occupied commercial properties, credit unions can often offer rates 25 to 50 basis points below bank pricing, though their loan programs are generally constrained to owner-occupied use and cannot accommodate pure investment deals.

SBA 504 and 7(a) Programs. For owner-occupied commercial properties in Victorville, SBA financing through certified development companies provides a path to higher leverage (up to 90% combined LTV on 504 deals) with below-market fixed rates on the CDC debenture tranche. A competent broker advising a Victorville borrower on an owner-occupied commercial property has an affirmative obligation to evaluate whether the borrower qualifies for SBA financing and to present this option clearly, given the material difference in terms available.

Debt Funds and Bridge Lenders. For transitional or value-add assets, or for deals that fall outside the bank credit box due to occupancy or income instability, private debt funds and bridge lenders operate in Victorville but at materially higher cost, typically 9% to 12% rates with origination fees of 1.5 to 3 points. These programs are appropriate for specific transitional situations, not for stabilized assets where conventional financing is available. Placing a stabilized property into bridge financing without documenting why conventional financing was unavailable or unsuitable is a clear departure from the standard of care.

What Attorneys Handling Victorville CRE Disputes Need to Know

The most common mistake attorneys make in lender liability and broker negligence matters is treating commercial real estate financing as an extension of appraisal. An appraiser determines value. A CRE financing expert determines whether the financing, including its structuring, pricing, disclosure, and underwriting methodology, met the standards applicable to a reasonably competent professional operating in the relevant market at the relevant time. These are fundamentally different inquiries, and conflating them creates gaps in your case.

When to retain a CRE financing expert. Counsel should engage a financing expert whenever the dispute involves the adequacy of a broker's or lender's conduct at origination, the reasonableness of disclosed loan terms, the foreseeability of a borrower's default based on the underwritten cash flow, or whether alternative financing was available that would have produced a materially better outcome for the borrower. These questions require a practitioner with active deal experience, not a retired banker who last placed a loan years ago.

What the expert can answer that an appraiser cannot. An appraiser can tell the court what a property was worth. A CRE financing expert can tell the court whether the loan amount, rate, structure, and terms were achievable from the market, whether the broker shopped the deal appropriately, whether the underwritten DSCR was calculated correctly, whether the disclosures met the standard applicable to a commercial mortgage broker, and whether the lender's credit memorandum reflects a reasonable methodology. These questions require specific knowledge of lender underwriting criteria, credit box parameters, fee disclosure standards, and capital market pricing, none of which fall within the scope of a real estate appraisal.

Qualifications to look for. A credible CRE financing expert should have active, current deal origination experience across multiple lender categories, including banks, credit unions, CMBS, life companies, and debt funds. They should have documented experience in the relevant submarket, or in sufficiently similar secondary markets. They should be able to speak to lender credit box parameters from direct sourcing experience, not from reading published guidelines. At CLS CRE, our principals have been active in the Inland Empire and High Desert markets through multiple credit cycles, with approximately $1 billion in transaction volume and relationships spanning more than 1,000 institutional and private lenders across all 50 states. That breadth of current market knowledge is what allows us to testify credibly to what a reasonably competent broker or lender would have done at origination.

Information to gather before engagement. Counsel should compile the complete loan origination file, including the broker's marketing materials, term sheets, lender correspondence, fee agreements, and disclosures; the lender's credit memorandum and underwriting model; the appraisal; the rent roll and historical operating statements; and any communications between the broker and borrower regarding alternatives considered. The more complete the origination record, the more precisely the expert can benchmark actual conduct against market standards.

Key Takeaways

  • DSCR at origination is often the central issue. A competent underwrite applies the market-standard 1.25x DSCR minimum before sizing the loan. When the actual DSCR at origination falls below 1.0x on a stabilized asset, that departure from market standards is material and documentable.
  • LTV constraints are asset-class and market specific. Mixed-use assets in secondary markets like Victorville generally support a maximum of 65% to 70% LTV from conventional lenders. A loan originated at 80%+ on a mixed-use asset without specific structural support reflects a departure from standard underwriting practice.
  • Broker disclosure obligations are independent of lender disclosures. The presence of correct loan terms in the final loan documents does not satisfy a commercial mortgage broker's independent obligation to explain material terms, including prepayment penalties and rate structure, in plain language to the borrower prior to commitment.
  • Secondary market knowledge matters. A CRE financing expert who does not have active deal experience in Victorville or comparable High Desert submarkets cannot credibly testify to what a reasonably competent broker operating in this market would have done. Market-specific knowledge of lender activity, pricing norms, and credit box parameters is a prerequisite.

Expert Witness: How Construction Defects Affect CRE Financing in Joliet, IL

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Construction Defect Impact on Financing

The Joliet Market Context

Joliet, Illinois sits at the intersection of Interstate 80 and Interstate 55, placing it at one of the most strategically significant freight and logistics corridors in the Midwest. That geography has made Will County, and Joliet specifically, one of the most active industrial and warehouse development markets in the entire Chicago metropolitan area over the past decade. Industrial vacancy in the Joliet submarket has historically run tighter than the broader Chicago MSA average, with Class A bulk distribution space trading in the low to mid 4% vacancy range during peak demand years, and asking rents for new construction product reaching $7.50 to $9.00 per square foot NNN for facilities in the 200,000 to 500,000 square foot range. E-commerce demand, third-party logistics operators, and food and beverage manufacturers have all been significant absorption drivers in this submarket.

Beyond industrial, Joliet supports a meaningful multifamily market anchored by population growth in Will County, proximity to employment centers along the I-80 corridor, and relative affordability compared to closer-in Chicago suburbs. Stabilized multifamily cap rates in Joliet have generally traded in the 5.5% to 6.5% range depending on vintage and unit mix, with value-add product commanding tighter spreads from buyers willing to underwrite lease-up execution risk. Retail in Joliet is bifurcated: grocery-anchored and necessity-based retail has remained fundamentally sound, while older strip centers and power center boxes face the same structural headwinds seen nationally. Office in Joliet is largely suburban and localized; it does not benefit from the institutional demand that drives Naperville or Schaumburg and carries meaningfully higher vacancy.

What makes Joliet particularly relevant for construction defect financing disputes is the volume of development activity that has occurred here. Significant industrial development, multifamily construction, and mixed-use projects have been financed, built, and transacted over a compressed period. Where there is concentrated construction activity, there is concentrated construction defect exposure. Lenders, appraisers, and borrowers who financed Joliet properties during the development boom are now encountering disputes that require forensic analysis of how defects discovered post-construction, or post-closing, affect the financing viability of the underlying asset.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing a property owner in a construction defect dispute involving a newly constructed industrial warehouse facility in the Joliet submarket of Will County. The asset was a single-tenant net lease industrial building completed within the prior 24 months, intended for a distribution use, and subject to an existing permanent financing structure that had been placed at certificate of occupancy. Following completion, the property owner identified a series of structural and mechanical defects including documented issues with the building's concrete tilt-up panel connections, roof drainage system failures causing interior water infiltration, and HVAC deficiencies affecting the conditioned portions of the building.

The core legal questions counsel needed addressed were threefold. First, do the alleged defects, taken individually and in aggregate, impair the property's ability to qualify for or maintain its existing financing? Second, how does knowledge of these defects affect the property's underwritable net operating income, its debt service coverage ratio, its loan-to-value ceiling, and the maximum loan amount a lender would fund against this asset in its current condition versus a defect-free building of equivalent type? Third, if the property needed to be refinanced in its current defect-encumbered state, what portion of the lending market would be accessible, at what terms, and what costs would be associated with navigating that constrained market? CLS CRE was asked to provide a Rule 26 expert report addressing all three questions.

Our role was not to opine on whether the defects existed or on their engineering cause. That work belonged to the retained construction and structural experts. Our scope was specifically to translate the assumed-as-alleged defect conditions into their financial and capital markets consequences, using the methodology and analytical framework that institutional lenders, CMBS underwriters, and credit committees actually apply when evaluating commercial real estate collateral.

The Financial Analysis

The analysis began with a reconstruction of the property's stabilized net operating income under two scenarios: a defect-free baseline reflecting what the building would have produced as originally specified, and a defect-impaired scenario incorporating the documented financial consequences of the alleged defect conditions. The property was a single-tenant industrial building with approximately 185,000 square feet of rentable area occupied under a triple-net lease with a creditworthy logistics tenant. The in-place annual base rent was approximately $1.48 million, with the tenant responsible for all operating expenses, taxes, insurance, and maintenance under the NNN structure.

In the defect-free scenario, stabilized NOI was modeled at approximately $1.44 million after deducting management fee reserves and minimal credit loss reserves appropriate for a single-tenant NNN asset with a credit tenant. This figure supported the underwriting basis presented to the original lender at loan origination.

The defect-impaired scenario required incorporating several income-reducing and reserve-increasing adjustments. The water infiltration issue affecting interior dock areas had triggered a lease dispute provision giving the tenant the right to seek rent abatement for periods during which portions of the building were rendered unusable. Expert testimony from the construction defect team quantified the affected square footage at approximately 12,000 square feet of dock staging and storage area. Adjusting the in-place rent to reflect a proportional abatement right valued at approximately $95,000 annually reduced effective gross income to approximately $1.385 million. Additionally, a prudent lender underwriting this asset in its defect-disclosed condition would impose a deferred maintenance and capital reserve requirement, typically sized for defect-impaired assets in the range of $0.15 to $0.25 per square foot depending on severity. At $0.20 per square foot on a 185,000 square foot building, this reserve totals $37,000 annually, further reducing underwritable NOI to approximately $1.348 million.

For DSCR analysis, I applied a 1.25x coverage threshold, which is the market standard minimum for single-tenant industrial assets financed through agency, CMBS, and institutional bank channels in 2023 and 2024. Some lenders apply 1.20x for strong credit tenants, but 1.25x is the appropriate conservative benchmark for any asset carrying known defect risk, because lenders treat known defects as an income stability risk factor that justifies a tighter coverage cushion. At a 30-year amortization and a 7.00% note rate, reflecting market conditions for 10-year fixed rate industrial financing in the relevant period, the maximum annual debt service supportable at 1.25x DSCR against the defect-impaired NOI of $1.348 million is approximately $1.078 million. This translates to a maximum supportable loan of approximately $13.55 million using a standard mortgage constant of approximately 0.0795 for a 30-year amortization at 7.00%.

In the defect-free scenario, the same methodology applied to NOI of $1.44 million produces maximum annual debt service of approximately $1.152 million, supporting a maximum loan of approximately $14.49 million. The financing gap attributable directly to the defects is therefore approximately $940,000 in maximum supportable loan proceeds.

The LTV analysis compounds this gap. Applying a 65% LTV ceiling, which is standard for institutional financing on single-tenant industrial assets where defects are disclosed, the defect-free scenario at a $22.3 million stabilized value supports a maximum loan of approximately $14.50 million. Under the defect-impaired scenario, where the appraised value must reflect the deferred maintenance and income impairment, a value reduction of approximately 8% to 12% is typical for defect conditions of this type and severity, yielding an adjusted value in the $19.6 to $20.5 million range. At 65% LTV, that produces a maximum loan of approximately $12.74 million to $13.33 million. The convergence of the DSCR constraint and the LTV constraint in the defect-impaired scenario produces a maximum supportable loan of approximately $12.74 million to $13.55 million, compared to the defect-free ceiling of approximately $14.49 million. The measurable financing impairment attributable to the alleged defects ranges from approximately $940,000 to $1.75 million in loan proceeds.

The Complicating Factors

Lender Disclosure Obligations and Loan Covenant Concerns. One of the most significant complications in construction defect scenarios is the interaction between a known defect and the borrower's existing loan covenants. Most commercial mortgage instruments contain material adverse change provisions, casualty and condemnation requirements, and representations regarding the physical condition of the collateral. When a construction defect is identified and documented through litigation, the borrower faces a disclosure question: does the existence of pending litigation and documented defects trigger a covenant default or a material adverse change notification obligation under the existing loan documents? Lenders take different positions on this, but institutional lenders and CMBS servicers are particularly aggressive. A CMBS loan with documented construction defect litigation pending can trigger a cash management trigger or a cash trap event, diverting operating income into a lender-controlled account and constraining the borrower's liquidity independent of whether the defect itself affects debt service coverage.

Lease Jeopardy Risk in Single-Tenant Assets. For single-tenant assets in Joliet, whether industrial, retail, or otherwise, the defect's impact on tenant retention is a central underwriting concern. Lenders evaluating this property in its defect-disclosed state must underwrite the probability that the tenant exercises early termination rights, withholds rent, or simply fails to renew its lease at expiration. Joliet industrial properties benefit from genuine supply-demand fundamentals, but a tenant occupying a defect-impaired building with documented structural issues has meaningful contractual and practical leverage. Lenders model this risk by applying a higher probability of income interruption at lease rollover and by requiring more conservative coverage ratios and larger reserves than they would for an equivalent defect-free asset.

Repair Cost Certainty and Escrow Requirements. Even if a lender is willing to underwrite the asset in its current defect-impaired state, virtually no institutional lender will close a loan without either (a) evidence that the defects have been fully remediated at the borrower's cost, or (b) an escrow holdback sized at 125% to 150% of the engineer-estimated remediation cost, funded at closing. For the structural and mechanical defects described in this engagement, the engineering remediation estimate ranged from $1.1 million to $1.6 million. A lender-required escrow at 150% of the high-end estimate would require the borrower to fund approximately $2.4 million at closing, reducing effective loan proceeds and requiring that cash to be released conditionally as remediation milestones are met and verified by a third-party inspector acceptable to the lender. This materially affects the borrower's cost of capital and effective leverage, both of which are quantifiable damages components in the litigation.

CMBS Market Exclusion. Properties with pending construction defect litigation are effectively excluded from CMBS execution. CMBS conduit lenders require representations and warranties that there is no material pending litigation affecting the collateral, and a construction defect suit with documented structural claims is precisely the category of pending litigation that triggers a CMBS lender's declination at the due diligence stage. This exclusion narrows the lender universe substantially. CMBS historically represents a significant share of financing for stabilized Joliet industrial and retail assets in the $10 million to $25 million loan size range. Its absence forces the borrower into bank, credit union, insurance company, or debt fund channels, each of which carries different pricing, reserve requirements, and covenant structures, generally less favorable than CMBS execution on a stabilized asset.

The Joliet Lender Market

Joliet benefits from a reasonably deep lender ecosystem, but that depth varies significantly by asset type, loan size, and deal complexity. For industrial assets in the Will County corridor, which represents the dominant deal type in the Joliet market, institutional lenders including life insurance companies, regional banks with commercial real estate specialization, and CMBS conduits are all active in the $8 million to $30 million loan size range under normal conditions. Life insurance companies are the most conservative credit box: they typically require 60% to 65% LTV maximum, 1.25x to 1.30x DSCR minimum, and full stabilization with no pending litigation or material deferred maintenance. They are categorically unavailable for a defect-impaired asset with pending litigation.

Regional and community banks headquartered in Illinois and the broader Midwest represent the most accessible financing channel for defect-impaired assets in Joliet. Several banks with commercial real estate specialization are active in Will County and understand the local industrial market well enough to underwrite non-standard deal structures. These lenders will generally require full disclosure, a remediation plan acceptable to the bank's construction review team, an escrow holdback, and pricing that reflects the elevated risk profile, typically 50 to 100 basis points above equivalent clean-asset pricing. Their maximum LTV for defect-disclosed assets generally runs 55% to 62%, tighter than their standard industrial product, and loan terms are often structured with shorter fixed-rate periods to preserve the lender's ability to reprice or exit.

Debt funds and bridge lenders operating in the greater Chicago metro area are accessible for defect-impaired Joliet assets, but the pricing and structure reflect the risk premium. Bridge lenders will generally lend at 50% to 60% LTV on a current-value basis, at floating rate spreads of 300 to 450 basis points over SOFR, with origination fees of 1.0% to 2.0%, and with a clearly defined remediation and stabilization timeline built into the loan structure. These are workable solutions for short-term capital needs but represent a materially higher cost of capital than what a defect-free equivalent asset would access. The spread difference between bridge debt and institutional fixed-rate execution is a quantifiable component of the borrower's financing damages.

What Attorneys Handling Joliet CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to Retain a CRE Financing Expert. The right time to retain a commercial real estate financing expert is at the point where the damages analysis moves beyond physical repair cost into consequential financial damages. If your client is arguing that the defects impaired their ability to refinance, sell, attract equity partners, or maintain compliance with existing loan covenants, that argument requires an expert who can translate the physical defect into a specific, quantifiable capital markets consequence. An appraiser can tell you what the property is worth with and without the defects. A CRE financing expert tells you what the maximum loan amount is, on what terms, from which lenders, at what cost, and what the gap is between the defect-impaired financing and what a defect-free equivalent asset could have obtained. Those are different and complementary analyses.

What a CRE Financing Expert Addresses That an Appraiser Cannot. An appraiser is qualified to value the property. A CRE financing expert is qualified to opine on lender behavior, credit standards, loan structuring, DSCR and LTV underwriting methodology, lender market access, and financing cost differentials. In a defect case, the damages question often turns not on value alone but on whether the borrower lost financing capacity, was forced into a higher-cost capital structure, incurred escrow or holdback costs that would not have existed absent the defects, or was excluded from preferred lender channels entirely. These are financing market questions, not appraisal questions.

Qualifications That Matter. A qualified CRE financing expert should have active, current transaction experience in the relevant product type and geography. Retired bankers often understand credit from the lender's internal perspective but may lack current market knowledge about how lender underwriting standards, pricing benchmarks, and credit box constraints have evolved in the current rate environment. The most useful expert is one who is actively placing loans in the Joliet market or in functionally equivalent Midwest industrial markets and who can speak to current lender behavior from direct experience, not from memory of prior practice.

Information Counsel Should Gather Before the Engagement. Before retaining a CRE financing expert, counsel should assemble the following: the original loan documents, including the appraisal, the lender's credit approval memorandum if obtainable, and the environmental and property condition reports from origination; the engineering or construction expert's report quantifying the defects and estimated remediation cost; any lease documents for the subject property, including any default or abatement notices; and any correspondence between the borrower and the existing lender regarding the defect conditions or the pending litigation. This documentation allows the financing expert to ground the analysis in actual deal facts rather than hypothetical assumptions, which strengthens the report's credibility and withstands cross-examination more effectively.

Use of Expert Testimony at Trial and in Mediation. CRE financing expert testimony is most effective when it is highly specific and grounded in current market data.

Rule 26 Financing Feasibility Reports: What Attorneys Need to Know

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Commercial Lending Solutions

Retaining a commercial real estate financing expert under Rule 26 is not the same as retaining an opinion. It is retaining a written report that has to survive a motion in limine, a deposition built specifically to expose gaps in methodology, and cross examination in front of a judge or jury who has never underwritten a loan. This guide covers what Rule 26 actually requires of a testifying financing expert, what belongs in a defensible financing feasibility opinion, how Commercial Lending Solutions serves as that expert, and when retaining one changes the outcome of a case.

What Rule 26 Requires of a Testifying Financing Expert

Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 26(a)(2)(B) governs the written report a retained testifying expert must produce, and most state court analogs track it closely. The rule is not a formality attorneys can satisfy with a short cover letter and a resume attached. Courts exclude expert testimony, in whole or in part, when the written report fails to satisfy one of six required disclosures, and for a financing feasibility expert, three of the six create most of the real exposure.

The Six Required Disclosures

  • A complete statement of every opinion the expert will offer, and the basis and reasons for each one. A financing expert who opines that refinancing was available "on reasonable terms" without stating the specific rate, amortization schedule, loan to value ratio, and lender type used to reach that conclusion has not satisfied this element, and opposing counsel will move to strike the opinion before it ever reaches a jury.
  • The facts or data the expert considered in forming those opinions. This is the disclosure most frequently deficient in financing feasibility reports, and it is the disclosure most commonly used to attack the report's reliability. It gets its own section below.
  • Any exhibits the expert will use to summarize or support the opinions. DSCR schedules, comparable loan term sheets, rate sheet excerpts, and lender correspondence all belong here as attached exhibits, referenced by number, not summarized in prose alone and left undocumented.
  • The expert's qualifications, including a list of publications authored in the preceding ten years. An active loan originator with a thin publication history is not disqualified on that basis. Courts weigh current, ongoing market participation heavily for this category of expert, frequently more heavily than academic output.
  • A list of other cases in which the expert testified at trial or by deposition during the preceding four years. A financing expert with a consistent, fully disclosed testimony history is generally in a stronger position than one testifying for the first time, provided the prior engagements are not lopsided toward a single party type (always plaintiff, always lender, and so on).
  • A statement of the compensation to be paid for the study and the testimony. Hourly fee arrangements, disclosed plainly, draw far less cross examination attention than contingent or outcome based arrangements. A testifying expert should never accept a contingent fee; it invites an immediate bias argument that is very difficult to rehabilitate in front of a jury.

Why the Facts and Data Disclosure Is the Real Battleground

Opposing counsel rarely wins a Daubert or Rule 702 challenge by attacking an expert's credentials outright. The far more effective attack targets the facts and data disclosure: what the expert actually reviewed, whether that data was current as of the relevant date, and whether the expert can show their work rather than simply asserting a conclusion. For a financing feasibility opinion specifically, that means the report must disclose exactly which lenders were contacted (by type and program, described generically rather than by naming individual contacts), what rate sheets or program guidelines were reviewed, what the source and date of the interest rate assumption was, and what comparable transactions were used to support the assumed cap rate and resulting value. A report that states an opinion about financing availability without disclosing this underlying methodology is vulnerable regardless of how experienced the expert is, and attorneys should push their retained expert to over-disclose here rather than under-disclose.

What a Financing Feasibility Opinion Actually Contains

A properly built financing feasibility report answers one governing question with supporting analysis: could this property have obtained financing, from what type of lender, on what specific terms, and as of what date. Everything else in the report exists to support that answer with data the expert can defend under oath.

The Core DSCR and LTV Analysis

The report's central exhibit is almost always a debt service coverage ratio and loan to value analysis, because that is the framework every institutional lender actually uses to size a commercial loan. Consider a stabilized property generating $850,000 in net operating income, an appraised value of $17 million, and a lender program requiring a 1.25x minimum DSCR at a 7.00% fixed rate amortized over 30 years. The annual debt service constant at that rate and term is approximately 7.984%, meaning each dollar of annual debt service supports roughly $12.53 of loan principal. Dividing the $850,000 NOI by the 1.25x coverage requirement yields a maximum supportable annual debt service of $680,000, which at that constant produces a maximum loan of approximately $8.5 million, or roughly 50% of the appraised value, well inside a typical 65% LTV ceiling. In this scenario the DSCR constraint governs the loan size, not the LTV ceiling, and the report needs to say so explicitly.

The choice of DSCR threshold materially changes the answer, which is exactly why opposing experts are frequently challenged on this single input.

Lender Market Survey: Three Levels of Rigor

Not every financing feasibility opinion is built on the same quality of underlying market evidence, and attorneys evaluating either their own expert or an opposing one should know the difference.

LevelWhat It IsReliability
General market experienceThe expert's recollection of typical terms based on overall industry experience, with no contemporaneous documentation citedWeakest. Easily impeached with a single "how do you know" follow-up question.
Published dataRate sheets, agency program pricing, CMBS spread indices, or trade publication surveys tied to a specific dateModerate. Defensible on paper but disconnected from whether an actual lender would have closed this specific deal.
Documented outreachReal contact with lender types active in the relevant asset class and market, producing an indicative term sheet, letter of interest, or documented quote tied to the property's actual characteristicsStrongest. This is the standard that survives Daubert challenges and persuades a fact finder.

The strongest financing feasibility reports rely on documented outreach for the central opinion and use published data only to corroborate the outreach, never as a substitute for it. When retaining counsel receives an opposing report built entirely on general market experience or stale published surveys, that gap is the first place to focus a motion in limine.

Special Circumstances That Change the Analysis

Four fact patterns recur often enough in financing feasibility litigation that they deserve their own treatment in the report, and the analytical failure most opposing experts commit is collapsing the distinction between a circumstance that affects loan terms and one that precludes financing altogether.

  • Environmental contamination. A recognized environmental condition frequently affects terms (higher reserve requirements, environmental insurance as a closing condition, a reduced advance rate) without precluding financing outright, particularly when a Phase II has quantified the remediation scope and cost.
  • An active bankruptcy filing. A pending Chapter 11 case affects which lenders will engage and on what timeline, but it does not automatically preclude exit financing where a confirmed plan and stabilized post-emergence cash flow exist.
  • An assignment of rents in dispute. A contested rent assignment affects a lender's willingness to rely on projected income and may require a reserve or holdback, but rarely precludes financing where the underlying leases are otherwise performing.
  • An adversarial or litigious borrower or sponsor. Sponsor litigation history affects underwriting scrutiny and can extend the closing timeline, but institutional lenders finance sponsors with litigation history routinely and this factor should not be overstated as a categorical bar.

An opposing report that treats any one of these four circumstances as an automatic disqualifier, without explaining why this specific asset and this specific lender program could not accommodate it through pricing or structure, is making a claim the market simply does not support and should be tested directly at deposition.

Deposition and Cross-Examination Considerations

A financing feasibility expert who has produced a well-documented Rule 26 report should still expect a rigorous deposition. Preparation should anticipate four categories of questioning.

  • Data currency. Expect detailed questions about the date of every rate, spread, and program guideline cited, and whether the expert can identify the specific source and date for each figure rather than a general characterization of "market conditions at the time."
  • Relationship depth. Expect the expert to be asked to name the type of lender contacted for each outreach effort, when the contact occurred, and whether an indicative term sheet or letter of interest resulted. An expert who cannot answer this specifically, and who relies instead on generalized industry knowledge, is significantly easier to impeach.
  • Methodology consistency. Expect questions testing whether the expert ran both the DSCR and LTV constraint and identified which one governed, whether a market vacancy assumption was used or an unsupported optimistic figure, and whether a management fee was included in the NOI build-up even where the owner self-manages, since institutional lenders always underwrite one.
  • Scope boundaries. Expect questions probing whether the expert has stayed within a financing feasibility lane or has strayed into property valuation, accounting, or legal conclusions properly reserved for other experts or for the fact finder. An expert who stays disciplined within scope is far harder to exclude on a Daubert motion.

Retaining counsel should build a deposition preparation session around these four categories specifically, using the expert's own report as the script, well before the deposition date rather than the week of.

When an Attorney Should Engage a Financing Feasibility Expert

The engagement decision usually turns on whether the case includes a disputed factual question about capital markets access or terms that a lay judge or jury cannot evaluate without expert help. The recurring case types include bankruptcy plan confirmation under 11 U.S.C. 1129(a)(11), where exit financing feasibility is often outcome determinative; wrongful foreclosure claims resting on a theory that refinancing would have avoided the loss; lender liability claims turning on whether origination or workout conduct met the market standard of care; joint venture and partnership disputes involving capital stack structure or preferred equity terms; construction defect litigation where the defect's effect on financeability is a component of damages; contaminated property disputes; loan modification disputes; and rebuttal engagements responding to an opposing expert's report.

Timing matters more in this category of expert engagement than most attorneys expect, because a defensible feasibility opinion depends on documented lender outreach, and outreach takes time to conduct properly. Retaining a financing feasibility expert at least 90 days before the expert disclosure deadline allows time to complete outreach, obtain term sheets, and finish a report that will hold up under the scrutiny described above. Engagements initiated 30 days or fewer before disclosure meaningfully constrain what outreach can be documented, and that constraint should be discussed candidly with the expert at the outset rather than discovered later.

Expert Witness: Ground Lease Financing Disputes and Analysis in Florence, AL

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Ground Lease Financing Complexity

The Florence Market Context

Florence, Alabama sits at the northern anchor of the Shoals metropolitan area, a four-city region that includes Muscle Shoals, Sheffield, and Tuscumbia. For a market of roughly 150,000 people in the greater Shoals area, Florence punches above its weight in commercial real estate activity, and that creates a specific kind of financing complexity that attorneys and investors often underestimate. The market is not a secondary market in the abstract sense. It is a functionally distinct capital market with its own lender universe, its own underwriting tolerances, and its own structural norms that differ meaningfully from Birmingham, Huntsville, or Nashville.

Ground lease structures are relatively uncommon in smaller Alabama markets, but they do exist, particularly on retail pad sites along the US-72 corridor, on legacy commercial parcels in the downtown Florence core near the Court Street district, and on institutional land holdings tied to the University of North Alabama. When a ground lease does appear in Florence, it almost always creates a financing challenge that the parties did not fully anticipate at deal inception. The leasehold interest is not fee simple. Lenders treat it differently, and many local and regional lenders simply will not touch it without specific structural protections.

Florence's commercial fundamentals provide the backdrop for understanding why financing complexity matters here. Retail vacancy along the primary commercial corridors has hovered in the 8 to 11 percent range, which is manageable but not tight enough to override structural underwriting concerns. Industrial product along Cox Creek Parkway and near the port infrastructure has seen improved absorption tied to regional manufacturing growth, with effective rents in the $4.00 to $6.50 per square foot NNN range depending on clear height and dock configuration. Multifamily cap rates in Florence have compressed modestly, trading in the 6.25 to 7.25 percent range for stabilized product, which reflects the broader Southeast trend but with a meaningful spread over primary markets. In this environment, a ground lease arrangement adds a layer of financing risk that can move an otherwise viable deal outside the credit box of lenders who would otherwise compete aggressively for the loan.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing the leasehold mortgagee in a dispute arising from a ground lease financing that had been placed on a retail commercial property in the Florence market. The asset was an improved leasehold interest on a commercial pad site operated as a single-tenant net lease retail property. The ground lease had been in place for a number of years before the financing was originated, and the dispute centered on whether the ground lease, as structured, ever met the market standard for lendable leasehold collateral, and whether a specific triggering event during the loan term should have been anticipated and protected against at origination.

Counsel retained CLS CRE to answer four core questions. First, does this ground lease structure, as documented, meet the market standard that institutional and commercial lenders require in order to make a leasehold mortgage? Second, were the SNDA provisions in the ground lease adequate to provide the lender with the protections that the secondary market and institutional lenders universally require? Third, what was the maximum financeable loan amount supportable by the leasehold income stream at origination, and how does that compare to the loan that was actually made? Fourth, given the structure of the ground lease, what portion of the lender market would have been willing to extend credit on this collateral at all, and on what terms?

These are not questions an appraiser can answer. They are not questions a real estate attorney can answer from the face of the document alone. They require active knowledge of how lenders actually underwrite leasehold interests, what their internal credit policies require, and what the secondary market demands. That is where a capital markets expert engaged in active origination adds value that no other category of expert can provide.

The Financial Analysis

The first step in any leasehold financing analysis is establishing the income stream that supports the debt, and then stress-testing that income stream against the additional risks the leasehold structure introduces. In a leasehold mortgage, the lender's collateral is not the land or the fee interest. The lender holds a security interest in the tenant's right to occupy and operate under the ground lease. If the ground lease terminates, the lender's collateral disappears. That is the fundamental underwriting problem, and every number in the analysis has to account for it.

For this engagement, the subject property generated a stabilized net operating income of approximately $148,000 annually, derived from a long-term NNN lease with a creditworthy retail tenant. On its face, this is a serviceable income stream for a Florence market asset. The analysis applied a debt service coverage ratio threshold of 1.25x, which is the standard minimum for commercial lenders on this asset type. Some lenders, particularly those who would be willing to lend on a leasehold at all, require 1.30x to 1.35x as a leasehold premium, reflecting the additional structural risk. Using 1.25x as the floor, the maximum annual debt service supportable by this income stream is approximately $118,400.

Assuming a 25-year amortization schedule and an interest rate environment consistent with the origination period, a rate of 6.75 percent was applied as a market-rate assumption for this credit and collateral profile. At that rate and amortization, $118,400 in annual debt service supports a loan of approximately $1,465,000. The market LTV constraint for a leasehold mortgage, which we will address in the complicating factors section, typically runs 60 to 65 percent for this asset type, compared to 70 to 75 percent for fee simple collateral. At a 65 percent LTV ceiling and assuming an appraised leasehold value of approximately $2,200,000, the LTV-constrained loan maximum would be approximately $1,430,000. These two tests, the DSCR test and the LTV test, converge near $1,430,000 to $1,465,000, which establishes the maximum supportable loan amount under market-standard underwriting.

The actual loan in dispute exceeded this range by a material amount. That finding is significant in the litigation context because it establishes that the loan, as originated, could not have been placed with an institutional lender or sold into the secondary market at that loan amount under the prevailing underwriting standards. It also indicates that the lender either accepted structural risk that it did not fully price or did not conduct the leasehold-specific analysis that market practice requires.

The Complicating Factors

Ground lease financing in any market involves a set of structural hurdles that are non-negotiable for most institutional lenders. In the Florence market, where the institutional lender presence is thinner than in a major metro, these hurdles take on additional weight because the alternative capital sources willing to accept structural compromise are also more limited.

Lease Term Relative to Loan Maturity: The single most important lender requirement for a leasehold mortgage is that the ground lease term must extend substantially beyond the loan maturity. The market standard is that the lease must have a remaining term, including renewal options, that exceeds the loan maturity by at least 10 years, and most institutional lenders require a 20 to 30 year cushion. A ground lease with 15 years remaining and no renewal options simply cannot be financed on institutional terms, full stop. If the renewal options are present but not yet exercised, lenders require the borrower to exercise them prior to closing, or they discount the unexercised option term entirely. In the Florence engagement, the ground lease's remaining term, when option exercise requirements were properly analyzed, created a term gap that placed the collateral outside the credit box of every CMBS conduit lender and most life company programs.

SNDA Adequacy: The subordination, non-disturbance, and attornment agreement is not a formality. It is the legal and structural mechanism through which a lender secures its position against the risk that the ground lease terminates and the leasehold collateral evaporates. A proper SNDA for a leasehold mortgage must include several provisions that are non-negotiable. The ground lessor must agree to provide the lender with notice of any default by the lessee and a cure period of no less than 30 days after the lessee's cure period expires. The ground lessor must agree not to terminate the ground lease as a result of a lessee default without first providing the lender with the opportunity to cure. The ground lessor must agree to recognize a new lease with the lender or its designee on substantially the same terms if the ground lease is terminated due to lessee default. And the SNDA must survive a foreclosure or assignment of the leasehold interest so that the lender, as a foreclosing party, steps into the lessee's shoes with full protection.

The SNDA in the subject transaction was deficient on two of these requirements. The cure notice provision ran to the lessee only, with no direct notice obligation to the lender. And the new lease obligation was conditioned on the lender's payment of all arrearages, which is an acceptable condition in the abstract, but the arrearages were defined to include consequential damages and attorney fees owed to the ground lessor, which is not a market-standard definition and which could expose the lender to open-ended liability it could not underwrite. Both of these deficiencies would have been flagged by any CMBS counsel or institutional lender's legal team in a standard pre-commitment due diligence review.

Leasehold Valuation Complexity: Appraising a leasehold interest is materially more complex than appraising fee simple real estate, and lenders know this. The leasehold value depends on the spread between the contract rent paid to the ground lessor and the market rent for the land, discounted over the remaining lease term. When ground rent resets are scheduled at above-market escalation rates, the leasehold value can actually be below the improvement value alone, creating a collateral profile that is difficult to underwrite. In Florence, where land comparable sales are sparse and ground lease comparables are even sparser, the leasehold appraisal often relies on income capitalization approaches that carry wider uncertainty bands than a fee simple appraisal. Lenders respond by compressing LTV further or requiring additional collateral.

Lender Approval of Assignment: Most ground leases require ground lessor consent to any assignment of the leasehold interest, including an assignment resulting from lender foreclosure. If the ground lessor can withhold consent unreasonably, or if the consent standard is not clearly defined in the ground lease, lenders face the risk that a foreclosure sale produces a leasehold interest they cannot assign to a third-party buyer. This makes the collateral effectively illiquid at the moment the lender most needs to liquidate it. The subject ground lease contained a consent requirement with no defined standard, which is a structural deficiency that any CMBS or agency lender would require to be cured before closing.

The Florence Lender Market

Understanding which lenders are actually active in Florence for a leasehold mortgage engagement is essential context for both the financial analysis and the litigation framing. CLS CRE maintains active relationships with over 1,000 lenders across 50 states, and our origination experience in the Alabama market, including the Shoals region, informs this assessment directly.

CMBS conduit lenders, which originate loans for securitization into commercial mortgage-backed securities pools, are the most demanding category when it comes to leasehold collateral. They follow CREFC and PSA guideline standards that have specific, written requirements for ground lease term, SNDA content, and leasehold appraisal methodology. A conduit loan on a Florence leasehold interest is theoretically possible, but it requires a ground lease that has been specifically drafted or amended to meet those requirements. The ground lease in this dispute did not meet those requirements, and therefore this entire category of lenders, which represents a large portion of the institutional market for commercial loans in the $1.5 million to $10 million range, was effectively unavailable.

Life insurance companies, which are another institutional capital source for stabilized net lease assets, are even more conservative on leasehold collateral than conduit lenders. Most life company programs require fee simple collateral or will only consider a leasehold where the ground lessor is a creditworthy institutional entity, such as a university or a municipality, and where the ground lease term exceeds 50 years with fully exercised renewals. For a Florence retail pad site, this capital source is effectively unavailable regardless of SNDA structure.

Community and regional banks headquartered in North Alabama, including institutions with strong Florence market presence, represent the most realistic lender category for a leasehold mortgage of this size in this market. These institutions hold loans on their own balance sheet, which gives them more flexibility on structure than conduit or agency programs. However, their willingness to accept leasehold collateral varies significantly by institution, and their pricing reflects the illiquidity premium. Rates on leasehold mortgages from community bank lenders in this market would typically run 50 to 100 basis points above their comparable fee simple product, and LTV constraints are typically 60 to 65 percent rather than the 70 to 75 percent available on fee simple deals. Several North Alabama community banks have specific credit policies that prohibit leasehold mortgages entirely, which further narrows the available lender universe.

Debt funds and private bridge lenders represent the category of last resort for a leasehold with structural deficiencies. These lenders will accept collateral and structure that institutional lenders reject, but their pricing reflects that risk tolerance: rates typically in the 9 to 12 percent range, with origination fees of 1.5 to 2.5 points, shorter terms of 12 to 36 months, and full recourse or significant completion guarantees. Financing a stabilized retail property at these costs fundamentally changes the economics of the deal and typically eliminates any positive leverage for the borrower.

What Attorneys Handling Florence CRE Disputes Need to Know

If you are litigating a commercial real estate dispute in Florence that involves financing feasibility, lender conduct, or the adequacy of a ground lease for mortgage purposes, there are specific questions a CRE financing expert can answer that no other expert category can address.

When to retain a CRE financing expert: Retain a financing expert when the dispute involves a question of what a lender should or would have done, what a market-standard loan structure looks like, whether a particular financing was feasible at all, or what damages flow from a party's inability to obtain financing. These questions are distinct from valuation questions, which an appraiser handles, and from document interpretation questions, which belong to counsel. The financing expert bridges the gap between the legal document and the capital markets reality.

What the financing expert answers that an appraiser cannot: An appraiser determines value. A financing expert determines financability. These are related but not identical. A property can have a determinable value and still be unfinanceable because of structural, legal, or market conditions that affect lender willingness but not intrinsic value. Ground lease collateral is the clearest example: an appraiser will produce a leasehold value, but that value is irrelevant if no lender in the market will lend against it on the terms the borrower required. The financing expert can also testify to lender market conditions, underwriting standards, and secondary market requirements that are beyond the scope of a valuation engagement.

What qualifies a CRE financing expert: The expert must be actively engaged in commercial mortgage origination at the time of the engagement, not merely a retired banker or a consultant who has not placed a loan in years. The market moves. Underwriting standards shift. Secondary market requirements change. An expert who placed CMBS loans in 2012 but has not been active since cannot reliably testify to what the market required in 2022 or 2023. At CLS CRE, our team is actively originating across multiple lender categories in the Alabama market and across 50 states, which means our testimony reflects current market practice, not historical recollection.

Information counsel should gather before the engagement: Provide the complete ground lease, including all amendments and exhibits. Provide the SNDA agreement and any lender comfort letters. Provide the original loan commitment letter and term sheet. Provide the appraisal that was used to underwrite the loan. Provide any loan approval memoranda or credit committee minutes if available. Provide the borrower's financial statements and rent rolls from the origination period. The more complete the documentation, the more precise and defensible the expert's analysis will be.

How financing expert testimony is used: In trial, the financing expert typically presents on the standard of care applicable to the lender or broker, the financial feasibility of the transaction as structured, and the causal relationship between structural deficiencies and the claimed damages. In a bankruptcy Plan hearing, the financing expert may testify to the feasibility of exit financing or the market rate and terms available to refinance a proposed Plan structure. In arbitration, the expert's written report often carries more weight than in a jury trial, making precision and technical depth the priority.

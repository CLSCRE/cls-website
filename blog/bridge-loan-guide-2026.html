Bridge Loans for Commercial Real Estate: Complete 2026 Guide

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Commercial Lending Solutions

I've arranged commercial bridge financing across dozens of markets and asset classes. Bridge loans are the most misunderstood product in commercial real estate finance: borrowers either over-rely on them or avoid them when they're the right tool. This guide covers everything you need to know to use bridge capital intelligently in 2026.

What Is a Bridge Loan and When Does It Make Sense?

A commercial bridge loan is short-term, asset-secured financing designed to span the gap between where a property is today and where it needs to be to qualify for permanent financing. The operative word is gap: bridge loans solve a specific timing or underwriting problem. They are not a substitute for permanent financing and should never be treated as one.

Bridge loans typically carry higher rates than permanent debt, which means they cost you more per dollar borrowed. The business case for using one comes down to a single question: does the value you unlock during the bridge period exceed the additional interest cost? When the answer is yes (and it often is), bridge capital is not just acceptable, it is the optimal structure.

The 7 Scenarios Where Bridge Financing Is the Right Answer

  • Value-add acquisition. You're buying a property with below-market rents, deferred maintenance, or functional obsolescence. The property doesn't qualify for agency or life company permanent financing in its current state, but will once renovated and stabilized. A bridge loan funds the acquisition and renovation, then you refinance into permanent debt at stabilized value. This is the most common bridge use case I see.
  • Lease-up. A property is physically complete and operational but not yet at the occupancy threshold required by permanent lenders, typically 90% for multifamily, 85-90% for office and retail. A bridge lender will lend against the stabilized value while you lease up, provided the business plan is credible and the sponsor has demonstrated lease-up experience.
  • Construction-to-permanent gap. Construction is complete, the certificate of occupancy is issued, but the property hasn't yet generated 12 months of operating history at DSCR above the permanent lender minimum (typically 1.25x). You need 12-24 months of seasoned income before agencies or life companies will underwrite it. A bridge loan covers that window.
  • Time-sensitive acquisition. The seller has a hard close date (30 to 45 days) that a conventional bank or CMBS conduit cannot meet. Debt funds can execute in 2-3 weeks for straightforward deals. When the acquisition economics justify the higher bridge rate, speed-to-close beats rate optimization.
  • Distressed or complex asset. Environmental contamination, a property in receivership, title complexity, or an active bankruptcy creates lender hesitation that makes conventional financing impossible regardless of property quality. Specialized bridge lenders underwrite complexity as a feature, not a bug, because complexity creates the discount that makes the deal work.
  • Renovation and reposition. You're converting an office building to multifamily, a hotel to apartments, or a retail strip to a medical office campus. Permanent lenders don't finance properties mid-conversion. Bridge lenders fund renovation draws, underwrite the as-completed value, and hand off to permanent debt at stabilization.
  • 1031 exchange timing. You've sold a property and have a 45-day identification and 180-day close deadline. Your target property requires more diligence time than the replacement property timeline allows, or your equity hasn't landed in time to close conventionally. A bridge loan buys time to close without losing your exchange.

2026 Bridge Loan Market: Rates, Terms, and Who's Lending

The 2026 bridge market has normalized from the 2022-2023 dislocation but remains structurally different from the 2018-2021 environment. Rates are higher, underwriting is tighter, and the lender universe has shifted meaningfully away from banks and toward debt funds.

With SOFR currently in the 4.3% to 4.5% range, an all-in floating rate on a bridge loan runs approximately 7.3% to 9.5%. Fixed-rate bridge options exist but are less common, primarily from banks and credit unions that keep loans on balance sheet and want the yield certainty.

Who Is Actively Lending in 2026

Non-bank debt funds are the dominant force in bridge lending today. These vehicles, including large platform lenders like Mesa West, Starwood Property Trust, Benefit Street, and dozens of mid-market funds, have filled the void left by retreating regional banks. They underwrite quickly, hold loans on balance sheet, and are structurally built for the complexity that bridge deals carry. Most non-recourse bridge capital comes from this segment.

Select banks and credit unions remain active but with tighter parameters. National banks have largely exited sub-$50M bridge lending. Community banks and credit unions with commercial real estate expertise are still quoting for strong sponsors in their markets, often with lower rates but more recourse and slower execution.

Private capital and family offices fill the gap for deals that are too small, too complex, or too fast for institutional lenders: sub-$5M transactions, ground leases, unique property types. Expect rates of 9-13% and 1-3 points, but execution in 7-10 days.

Who pulled back: Regional banks with $10B to $100B in assets have materially reduced bridge exposure following 2023 regulatory pressure and CRE concentration concerns. If a regional bank quoted your bridge deal in 2021, get a new quote from a debt fund before assuming that relationship still works.

How Bridge Loans Are Underwritten

Bridge underwriting is fundamentally different from permanent loan underwriting. Permanent lenders focus on in-place cash flow: what the property earns today, whether it covers debt service, and whether it will continue to do so. Bridge lenders focus on value: what the property is worth today, what it will be worth when the business plan executes, and whether you can get from here to there.

The Five Things Every Bridge Lender Underwrites

  1. As-is value. The current appraised value of the property, which sets the maximum loan amount before business plan credit. Most lenders will not exceed 70-75% of as-is value regardless of stabilized projections.
  2. As-stabilized value. The projected appraised value once the business plan is complete. Lenders stress-test this number by using market cap rates above current market, conservative rent growth assumptions, and stabilized expense ratios.
  3. Business plan feasibility. Is the renovation budget realistic? Is the lease-up timeline achievable given submarket absorption? Has the sponsor executed this exact strategy before? A lender who can't get comfortable with your business plan will not close the loan, regardless of how strong the numbers look on paper.
  4. Exit strategy. Can the property support permanent financing at business plan completion? The bridge lender will run a rough permanent loan underwrite at origination: what cap rate will the appraisal use, what DSCR will the property achieve, what LTV will the permanent lender allow? If the exit doesn't pencil, the bridge loan won't close.
  5. Interest reserve adequacy. Do you have enough funded interest reserve to carry the loan through stabilization without relying on in-place cash flow? A bridge lender who sees a thin interest reserve or no reserve on a value-add deal will either require more equity or decline.

Bridge Loan Underwriting Math: A Working Example

Consider a 100-unit multifamily property in a strong secondary market. Current occupancy is 72%, current rents average $1,400/unit. Market occupancy is 94%, market rents are $1,650/unit. Renovation budget is $15,000/unit, or $1.5M total. Purchase price is $12M.

A bridge lender might advance $9M on the acquisition (75% of as-is value) plus $1.5M in renovation draws (funded in tranches as work completes), for a total commitment of $10.5M, representing 79% of total cost and 52.5% of as-stabilized value. The exit underwrite shows a $14-15M permanent loan available at stabilization, comfortably paying off the bridge and returning equity.

Bridge Loan Structures

Interest-Only vs. Amortizing

Bridge loans are almost universally interest-only. Amortization on a 24-month term loan would require principal payments that consume cash flow you need for operations and renovation. If a lender insists on amortization on a bridge term, treat it as a red flag: the product is miscategorized or the lender is trying to reduce their exposure through paydown rather than underwriting correctly.

Floating vs. Fixed Rate

Most institutional bridge loans are floating rate, indexed to SOFR with a credit spread. The floating structure reflects the short-term nature of the product and the lender's cost of capital. Some borrowers purchase interest rate caps to limit upside exposure: a 2-year SOFR cap at 5.5% costs roughly 0.5-0.75% of the notional loan amount at current volatility levels. Many lenders require a cap as a loan condition.

Fixed-rate bridge exists, primarily from balance sheet lenders who can accept the rate risk. If you're in an environment where rates are expected to fall significantly during your hold period, floating may outperform. If you need payment certainty for a tight cash flow situation, fixed is worth the premium.

Full-Term Funding vs. Draw Schedule

Stabilized bridge loans (lease-up, construction seasoning) typically fund fully at close. Renovation bridge loans fund in two components: the acquisition portion at close and the renovation budget through a draw schedule tied to construction milestones. Draws are typically monthly, require a third-party inspector sign-off, and are funded in arrears after work is verified complete.

Interest Reserve

On renovation deals where in-place cash flow doesn't cover interest during construction, lenders require a funded interest reserve, essentially pre-paid interest deposited into a controlled account at closing. The reserve calculation: loan balance x interest rate x projected months to stabilization, plus a 2-3 month buffer. On a $10M loan at 9% with an 18-month stabilization timeline, that's roughly $1.35M in funded interest reserve. This reduces your effective leverage, which is why adequate equity matters on bridge deals.

Extension Options

The standard bridge structure includes one or two 6-12 month extension options, triggered by meeting performance hurdles (minimum occupancy, minimum DSCR, no payment default). Extensions typically cost 25-50 basis points and require updated appraisals. Understanding the extension mechanics at origination is critical: if your business plan takes longer than projected, your extension terms become your entire margin of safety.

Recourse vs. Non-Recourse

Most debt fund bridge capital is non-recourse with standard carve-outs for fraud, environmental, and voluntary bankruptcy. Bank and credit union bridge loans frequently carry full recourse or partial recourse. Non-recourse matters primarily for large deals where personal guarantee exposure is significant. Don't let recourse be your only decision criterion: a well-priced recourse loan from a reliable lender often beats a non-recourse loan with aggressive prepayment penalties or covenant restrictions.

Qualifying for a Bridge Loan

What Bridge Lenders Weight Differently Than Permanent Lenders

Permanent lenders underwrite to income: they want DSCR above 1.25x, debt yield above 8-9%, and LTV under 70-75%. Bridge lenders accept that these metrics don't exist yet. Instead, they weight:

  • Business plan specificity. Vague renovation plans ("we'll improve the units") get declined. Specific plans with contractor bids, timeline milestones, and rent comps supporting the projected rents get funded. The more detail you provide, the more comfortable the lender gets.
  • Sponsor track record in this strategy. If you're executing a value-add multifamily deal, the lender wants to see that you've executed value-add multifamily before: similar unit counts, similar renovation scopes, similar markets. Track record with office buildings doesn't fully transfer. First-time sponsors can qualify, but expect tighter LTV and more structure around reserves and draw schedules.
  • Exit strategy underwrite. At origination, your bridge lender is already modeling your permanent loan. Can the property support agency or CMBS debt at stabilization? Does the math work at current permanent loan rates? If you can't answer these questions, the bridge lender can't get comfortable with their exit risk.
  • Liquidity and net worth. Most institutional bridge lenders require guarantor net worth of 1x the loan amount and liquidity of 10-15% of the loan amount. These tests ensure the sponsor can carry the deal through a delay without distress.

What Gets a Bridge Deal Declined

  • No clear exit. If the only path out is a sale at a cap rate that doesn't exist in the market, the deal doesn't close.
  • Capital stack that leaves no equity cushion. If your acquisition, renovation, carry costs, and closing costs consume 95% of total capitalization, a single cost overrun creates a loan default situation. Lenders see this immediately.
  • Sponsor with no experience in this asset type or strategy. Residential experience does not transfer to commercial bridge. Single-tenant NNN experience does not transfer to value-add multifamily.
  • Markets with structural demand problems. A lease-up deal in a market with 25% new supply delivering over the next 24 months is a difficult underwrite regardless of how good the specific property is.
  • Business plans dependent on above-market rent growth. If your pro forma assumes 15% rent growth in 18 months in a market running at 3%, lenders won't use that underwrite.

Bridge-to-Permanent: Structuring the Full Capital Strategy

The single most expensive mistake borrowers make on bridge deals is treating the bridge loan and the permanent loan as two separate, sequential decisions. They're not. They're one integrated capital strategy, and the cost difference between planning them together versus sequentially is real, often 200-300 basis points in total cost of capital when you account for refinancing risk, market timing, and exit optionality.

Why Locking the Perm at Bridge Origination Matters

When you close a bridge loan without a clear permanent financing path, you accept two risks: interest rate risk (rates may be higher at stabilization) and execution risk (the property may not qualify for the permanent loan you assumed). Both risks are manageable if you plan ahead.

Forward commitments from life insurance companies are available for institutional-quality assets, typically $20M and above. A life company will commit to fund a permanent loan at a specified rate and terms 12-24 months in advance, subject to the property meeting specified performance benchmarks at origination. You pay a commitment fee of 25-50 basis points, but you lock your permanent financing cost today. When bridge rates are elevated and you expect permanent rates to be lower, this structure can be the difference between a deal that works and one that doesn't.

For agency deals (Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac), the same logic applies. Agency lenders offer forward commitments with rate locks of 6-18 months. Structuring a bridge loan with a pre-negotiated agency forward commitment eliminates refinancing risk entirely.

The Bridge-to-Perm Underwrite Checklist

At bridge origination, run this analysis to confirm the permanent loan is achievable:

  • Project stabilized NOI using conservative rent growth and expense assumptions
  • Apply current permanent lender cap rates (not your acquisition cap rate) to derive as-stabilized value
  • Test the permanent loan at 70-75% LTV and 1.25x DSCR minimum: does it pay off your bridge balance?
  • Confirm the permanent loan type (agency, life company, CMBS, bank) is available for this asset type and market
  • Build in a 200 basis point rate stress on the permanent loan and confirm debt service still works
  • Identify your permanent lender or lenders before you close the bridge, not after

I've seen borrowers close bridge loans on properties where the permanent loan was never achievable at any reasonable stabilized value. They were counting on appreciation to bridge the gap. That is not a business plan, it's speculation, and bridge lenders who fund it are doing the borrower a disservice.

When Bridge-to-Sale Is the Right Exit

Not every bridge deal exits through permanent financing. Value-add acquisitions, ground-up development, and repositioning plays often exit through sale at stabilization, which typically generates better returns than a refinance in a compressed cap rate environment. If your exit is a sale, the underwrite changes: instead of modeling permanent loan coverage, model exit cap rate, total project cost, and target profit margin. Confirm the exit cap rate is achievable in current market conditions, use recent comparable sales, not projected future values.

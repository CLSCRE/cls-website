Commercial Real Estate Financing in Bankruptcy: What Attorneys, Turnaround Consultants, and Restructuring Advisors Need to Know

I am Trevor Damyan, a commercial mortgage broker at CLS CRE in Los Angeles. Over the years, I have placed financing on properties that most lenders refused to touch: distressed assets, pre-foreclosure situations, and commercial real estate inside active bankruptcy proceedings. That last category is where I want to spend time in this article, because it is the one that most professionals in the restructuring world handle without a clear map of the capital markets landscape.

If you are a bankruptcy attorney, turnaround consultant, restructuring advisor, creditors committee member, DIP lender, or unsecured creditor, you are reading this because your clients own commercial real estate and the property is now tangled in a proceeding. The question on the table is always the same: can this asset be financed, and if so, how? The answer, in my experience, is yes far more often than most people expect. But only if you know which lenders to approach, what they require, and how to structure the transaction so it survives court scrutiny.

Why This Professional Category Encounters CRE Financing Needs

Bankruptcy and restructuring professionals encounter commercial real estate financing needs in almost every large Chapter 11 case and in a meaningful share of smaller ones. Here is why.

Most operating companies of any size own or lease commercial real estate. When the business hits distress, the real estate does not disappear. It sits on the balance sheet, often as the most valuable asset the estate holds. The secured lender holding the first mortgage has a claim, the unsecured creditors want to maximize recovery, and the debtor needs capital to keep the lights on during reorganization. All three dynamics push you toward a commercial real estate financing transaction.

Turnaround consultants encounter this when they step into an engagement and discover the client owns a warehouse, a strip center, an office building, or an industrial facility that is carrying outdated, non-performing debt. The existing lender may have declared a default. Refinancing that debt, even inside a bankruptcy proceeding, can unlock liquidity that funds the operational turnaround.

Restructuring advisors face it when they are building a plan of reorganization and the plan requires exit financing. Without a committed lender willing to fund at confirmation, the plan will not be confirmed. The court needs to see a feasible exit before it blesses any plan.

Creditors committees encounter CRE financing questions when evaluating whether a proposed plan is feasible and whether the debtor's real estate is being properly monetized. Sometimes the committee itself becomes a plan proponent and needs to line up financing to sponsor its own plan.

DIP lenders, by definition, are already in the financing seat. But they frequently need help structuring their collateral position around commercial real estate and understanding how their lien will be primed or protected under the DIP order.

The Five Financing Scenarios You Will Most Commonly Face

1. Chapter 11 DIP Financing Secured by Commercial Real Estate

The debtor needs cash to fund operations during the reorganization. The best collateral available is commercial real estate. This is a true debtor-in-possession loan, and it requires court approval under 11 U.S.C. Section 364. The court will evaluate whether the terms are reasonable, whether adequate protection is being provided to existing secured creditors, and whether the DIP lien is properly primed or carved out.

From a lender's perspective, DIP loans secured by commercial real estate are a distinct product. The lender is making a loan to a borrower that is legally incapable of being sued for breach of contract without court permission. The collateral may be subject to existing liens, and the exit path is uncertain. Only specialty lenders and certain institutional players will underwrite this risk.

The DIP loan is typically senior to existing secured debt (priming lien) or secured by otherwise unencumbered assets. Loan amounts in the deals I work on range from $2 million to $50 million. Terms are short, typically 12 to 24 months, with interest rates that reflect the elevated risk profile. The lender will require a detailed bankruptcy budget showing projected cash receipts and disbursements through the anticipated plan confirmation date.

The court hearing is not optional. The attorney must prepare the DIP motion, the debtor must demonstrate that financing on similar terms was not available on an unsecured or junior-secured basis, and the lender's counsel must be ready to respond to any objections from existing creditors. I work with the legal team from day one to make sure the term sheet we bring to court will hold up to scrutiny.

2. Plan of Reorganization Exit Financing

This is the most common financing scenario I encounter in Chapter 11 cases with commercial real estate. The plan calls for refinancing the existing senior debt at or before confirmation. The debtor needs a committed lender to issue a term sheet, and that term sheet needs to show terms that are economically feasible under the plan projections.

Courts have seen enough vague financing commitments to be skeptical of anything that is not backed by a real lender with real capacity. I provide a binding term sheet, not a letter of interest, and the lender I place this with has the balance sheet to fund it. That distinction matters enormously when the trustee or a creditor challenges feasibility.

Exit financing for commercial real estate inside a confirmed plan typically carries loan-to-value ratios between 55% and 70%, depending on the property type and market. A stabilized multifamily asset in a major metro might command 65% to 70% LTV from a bridge lender. An office building in a secondary market with occupancy issues might get 55% LTV from a specialty lender, if it gets financed at all.

The key underwriting variable for exit financing is the property's income-producing capacity post-confirmation. The lender needs to see that the reorganized debtor can service the new debt from property cash flow. If the existing debt was originated at a lower interest rate environment and the property cannot support current market rates, the plan may require an equity contribution or a haircut on junior claims to make the numbers work.

3. Financing Feasibility Expert Analysis and Capital for Court

This scenario is underappreciated by most professionals in the space. Courts regularly need to evaluate whether a proposed plan is feasible, and feasibility turns heavily on whether the financing assumptions are realistic. I provide both sides of this equation: expert analysis on financing feasibility for court purposes, and the actual capital commitment to back it up.

When I review a proposed plan, I can tell the court whether the assumed interest rate, loan-to-value, and debt service coverage are consistent with what lenders in the current market will actually underwrite. That is not a theoretical exercise. I am actively placing loans in this market every week. If the plan assumes a 6% interest rate on a 65% LTV loan for a flagged hotel in a tertiary market, I can tell you that assumption will not survive contact with the market.

For creditors committees and plan proponents who need an independent view, I can serve as a financing expert. For debtors who need both analysis and capital, I can provide the term sheet that demonstrates feasibility. This dual role is something most commercial mortgage brokers cannot offer because they do not have the lender relationships in the distressed space and they do not have the courtroom experience to present their analysis clearly.

4. Turnaround Recapitalization: Valuable Real Estate, Failing Business Operations

This is the scenario that shows up in my office most often from turnaround consultants. The client is a company with valuable commercial real estate sitting inside a deteriorating operating business. The real estate is worth real money. The business is bleeding cash. The existing lender has cross-collateralized both, so the real estate cannot be refinanced without addressing the operating company's debt simultaneously.

The solution, in most of these situations, is a real estate recapitalization that separates the property from the operating company. This can be done inside or outside bankruptcy. Inside a Chapter 11, the reorganization plan can provide for the real estate to be transferred to a reorganized entity or a special purpose vehicle, which is then separately financed. Outside bankruptcy, a sale-leaseback or a ground lease structure can achieve a similar result.

I work with turnaround consultants to identify lenders who will finance the real estate standalone, separate from the operating company's credit. The property needs to qualify on its own: sufficient NOI, acceptable occupancy, marketable location, and a lease structure (even if the tenant is the reorganized operating company) that a lender will underwrite. Bridge lenders are the most common capital source here, with loan terms of 12 to 36 months while the business stabilizes and a permanent financing solution is arranged.

5. Chapter 7 Liquidation: Buyer Financing on a Compressed Timeline

When a Chapter 7 trustee is liquidating commercial real estate, the buyer often needs financing within a timeline that conventional lenders cannot accommodate. Court-approved sales under 11 U.S.C. Section 363 move quickly once the bid deadline is set. A buyer who needs a 45-day or 60-day close from a conventional bank lender is going to lose to a cash buyer every time.

Bridge lenders can close commercial real estate transactions in 15 to 30 days. That speed is what makes them the natural capital source for buyers bidding on 363 sale assets. I regularly work with buyers who are targeting distressed assets in Chapter 7 proceedings and need a lender who understands the sale process, can move through diligence quickly, and can fund before the court-imposed deadline.

The underwriting on these transactions focuses heavily on the asset itself, not the borrower's operating history. The buyer may be a newly formed entity created specifically to acquire the asset. The lender needs to get comfortable with the property value, the title insurance coverage, and the Section 363 free-and-clear sale order, which eliminates most pre-existing liens and claims. Bridge lenders who do distressed asset work understand this structure. Conventional lenders do not.

What Lenders Actually Require: Technical Thresholds and Deal Criteria

Specialty lenders who finance commercial real estate inside bankruptcy or distressed situations have a defined set of requirements. Here is what they actually look at.

Loan-to-value: Most distressed commercial real estate lenders will advance between 50% and 70% of the as-is appraised value. For assets with significant value-add potential, some lenders will go up to 70% of the as-stabilized value, but that is the exception rather than the rule in bankruptcy situations. Expect 55% to 65% LTV as the working range.

Property types: Multifamily, industrial, and self-storage are the most financeable inside bankruptcy proceedings. Retail, office, and hospitality are harder, but not impossible. Single-tenant net lease properties with creditworthy tenants are financeable if the lease is assumed in the plan. Raw land and owner-occupied properties where the business is failing are the hardest to finance.

Minimum loan size: Most specialty lenders in this space have a $2 million minimum. Many have a $5 million minimum. There is a thinner market below $2 million for bankruptcy-related commercial real estate financing.

Property condition: The property must be insured, not subject to active environmental remediation orders, and physically habitable or operable. Deferred maintenance is acceptable. Unsafe conditions, active EPA orders, or fire damage without insurance coverage will kill a deal.

Appraisal: Every lender in this space requires a current FIRREA-compliant appraisal from a certified general appraiser. For bankruptcy proceedings, the appraisal may also need to address liquidation value, not just market value, depending on the lender's collateral requirements. Budget 3 to 4 weeks for the appraisal and plan accordingly when building your case timeline.

Title insurance: The lender needs a lender's title insurance policy. In 363 sale situations, the policy needs to insure over any pre-existing liens that were extinguished by the sale order. Experienced title companies that understand bankruptcy sales are essential here. Not every title company will insure a 363 sale property without significant delay.

Bankruptcy counsel coordination: The lender's team needs to review the DIP order, the plan term sheet, or the sale order, depending on the transaction type. Lenders who do this work regularly have counsel who understands bankruptcy documents. Make sure the borrower's attorney and the lender's counsel are aligned on the timeline and the mechanics of the court approval process.

Interest rates: Expect rates between 9% and 13% per annum for bridge and specialty financing in bankruptcy-adjacent situations. That is the current market range. The spread over conventional financing reflects the complexity, the legal risk, and the compressed timeline. For DIP loans specifically, rates are often at the higher end of that range plus origination fees of 1% to 3%.

Questions to Ask Any Commercial Mortgage Broker Before Referring

Not every commercial mortgage broker can handle bankruptcy-related commercial real estate financing. Before you make a referral, here are the questions that will tell you whether you are talking to someone who knows this market.

Have you closed a loan inside an active Chapter 11 proceeding? This is the threshold question. If the answer is no or vague, the broker does not have the experience to manage the legal complexity and the lender coordination that these transactions require.

Which lenders on your panel actively finance commercial real estate in bankruptcy or distressed situations? A broker who knows this market can name specific lenders and describe their underwriting criteria. A broker who does not know this market will give you a generic answer about "bridge lenders" without specifics.

Have you worked with a bankruptcy attorney and a lender's counsel simultaneously on a DIP or exit financing transaction? The legal coordination in these transactions is complex. The broker needs to be able to translate between the capital markets world and the bankruptcy legal world. If they have never done it, the learning curve will cost your client time and money.

Can you provide a binding term sheet, not just a letter of interest? Courts want to see committed financing. A letter of interest is not committed financing. Make sure the broker has relationships with lenders who will issue a binding term sheet that can support a plan feasibility finding.

What is your timeline for delivering a term sheet on a distressed commercial real estate transaction? Bankruptcy cases move on court-imposed schedules. A broker who cannot deliver a term sheet within 10 to 15 business days of receiving a complete loan package is going to create problems in a case with tight deadlines.

What property types and loan sizes are you active in? Make sure the broker's active market matches your client's needs. A broker who primarily does multifamily may not have relationships for hospitality or retail. A broker whose minimum loan size is $10 million is not the right resource for a $3 million industrial building.

Key Takeaways

  • A well-developed category of specialty lenders actively finances commercial real estate inside and around bankruptcy proceedings. The market exists. Knowing how to access it is the skill.
  • The five most common financing scenarios are DIP loans secured by real estate, plan exit financing, financing feasibility expert work, turnaround recapitalization of real estate separate from failing operations, and buyer financing for 363 sale assets. Each has a distinct lender market and a distinct set of requirements.
  • Specialty lenders in this space work with loan-to-value ratios of 55% to 70%, loan minimums of $2 million to $5 million, and interest rates of 9% to 13% per annum. Those parameters define what is financeable and what is not before you invest significant time in a transaction.
  • Bridge lenders can close commercial real estate transactions in 15 to 30 days, which is what makes them the right capital source for 363 sale buyers and DIP situations with court-imposed timelines. Conventional lenders cannot compete on speed in bankruptcy situations.
  • Financing feasibility expert analysis and capital commitment are two services that can come from the same source. When the same broker who provides the expert opinion can also deliver the binding term sheet, the plan proponent's case for feasibility is significantly stronger.
  • The referral process requires no capital markets expertise from the referring attorney or consultant. One introduction email with the basic facts is enough to start a productive process.
  • Not every commercial mortgage broker can execute on bankruptcy-related CRE financing. The questions above will quickly distinguish brokers who know this market from those who will waste your client's time and damage the proceeding.
  • The difference between a confirmed plan and a liquidation often comes down to whether the debtor or plan proponent can demonstrate access to committed capital. Getting the right broker involved early, before the plan is filed, gives your client the best chance of a successful outcome.

Expert Witness: CRE Partnership and Waterfall Disputes in Salt Lake City, UT

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Joint Venture and Partnership Dispute

The Salt Lake City Market Context

Salt Lake City has emerged over the past decade as one of the most active secondary commercial real estate markets in the Western United States, and that growth has produced a corresponding rise in complex joint venture structures, many of which are now in various stages of dispute. The metropolitan area's population growth, driven by in-migration from California, the Pacific Northwest, and the Mountain West corridor, has attracted institutional capital at a scale that was virtually absent from this market fifteen years ago. When institutional money enters a market alongside local operating partners who have deep site control and entitlement relationships but limited balance sheet capacity, the result is almost always a promote structure, and promote structures, by design, create the conditions for disagreement when performance deviates from underwriting.

The Salt Lake City multifamily market illustrates the issue precisely. Cap rates for stabilized Class A multifamily assets in submarkets like Sugar House, Millcreek, and the Granary District compressed aggressively through 2021 and into early 2022, reaching the low-to-mid 4% range for institutional-quality product. That compression created significant unrealized gain on paper, which meant that waterfall calculations tied to IRR hurdles or equity multiples suddenly carried enormous dollar consequences. At the same time, the industrial market along the I-15 corridor and the Salt Lake Valley's west side saw vacancy compress to sub-4% levels, with triple-net lease rents escalating well beyond original pro forma assumptions. For joint ventures that had negotiated preferred return thresholds and promoted interest percentages before that rent growth materialized, the question of what income stream belongs to which tranche of the capital stack became intensely litigated territory.

Office fundamentals complicated the picture differently. The downtown Salt Lake City office market absorbed meaningful sublease supply post-2020, with overall vacancy rates reaching the 18% to 22% range in some submarkets, putting pressure on joint ventures that had underwritten stabilized occupancy assumptions in the 90% range. Where the operating partner's promote was contingent on reaching a stabilization threshold before the waterfall flipped, those disputes frequently turned on the question of whether financing terms available at the time of the dispute were sufficient to support a recapitalization, and what a reasonable lender would have required.

The Engagement

Counsel for the equity investor in a Salt Lake City mixed-use joint venture retained Commercial Lending Solutions to provide expert analysis in a dispute over the proper calculation of preferred return accruals, the handling of refinancing proceeds in the waterfall, and whether the operating partner's promoted interest had been correctly triggered under the terms of the partnership agreement. The asset was a mixed-use development in the greater Salt Lake City urban core, consisting of ground-floor retail with market-rate residential units above. The joint venture had been capitalized in a conventional institutional structure: a preferred equity investor contributing the majority of capital at a fixed preferred return, with the operating partner contributing a smaller cash equity component and controlling day-to-day management and financing decisions.

The core legal and financial questions were as follows. First, had the operating partner correctly calculated the preferred return accrual during the period when the asset carried a construction loan and was not yet generating distributable cash flow? The dispute turned on whether the preferred return should have been compounding or simple, and whether the clock started at the first capital contribution or at the date the construction loan closed. Second, when the project was refinanced with a permanent loan and proceeds were distributed, did those proceeds constitute a capital event under the agreement, triggering a waterfall distribution that would have required the preferred equity investor to receive a full return of capital before any promoted interest could be paid? Third, what financing terms were actually available in the Salt Lake City market at the time of the refinancing, and did the operating partner optimize the financing in a manner consistent with market practice, or did structuring decisions effectively shift value from the preferred investor to the promote?

We were asked to address all three questions from a capital markets perspective, providing analysis of market-standard joint venture structures, the mechanics of preferred return calculation as applied in commercial real estate transactions, and the range of financing terms a competent operator in Salt Lake City would have been able to obtain at the relevant time.

The Financial Analysis

Our analysis began with the stabilized income stream of the subject asset. We reconstructed the net operating income using rent rolls, lease abstracts, operating expense histories, and the asset's trailing twelve-month financials. For the mixed-use component, we analyzed the retail gross leasable area separately from the residential units, applying market rent benchmarks drawn from comparable lease transactions in the Salt Lake City urban core. Stabilized residential occupancy for comparable assets in the submarket was running in the 93% to 95% range during the relevant period, and we applied a 93% occupancy factor as the conservative end of the supportable range. Applying market vacancy, credit loss, and a management fee consistent with third-party management rates in the Salt Lake City multifamily market, we arrived at a stabilized NOI of approximately $2,340,000 annually.

To determine the maximum supportable permanent loan at the time of the refinancing, we applied a debt service coverage ratio of 1.25x, which is the standard underwriting threshold for mixed-use assets with ground-floor retail in the institutional lending market. Lenders in the Salt Lake City market were pricing 10-year fixed rate permanent loans for stabilized mixed-use assets in the range of 5.50% to 6.25% during the relevant period, with 30-year amortization on the residential component and, in some cases, interest-only periods for the first two to three years. Using a 5.875% rate and 30-year amortization, the annual debt service constant is approximately 7.09%, meaning maximum supportable annual debt service at 1.25x DSCR on $2,340,000 of NOI is $1,872,000. Dividing that by the annual constant yields a maximum supportable loan of approximately $26,400,000.

Applying a 65% loan-to-value constraint, which is standard for mixed-use assets with retail exposure in a market where ground-floor retail vacancy was elevated, and using the capitalized value of the asset at a 5.0% blended cap rate, the implied value was approximately $46,800,000. The LTV-constrained loan at 65% would be $30,420,000, meaning the DSCR constraint of $26,400,000 was the binding constraint at the time of refinancing. The operating partner, however, had financed the asset at $22,500,000, representing a loan-to-value of approximately 48%. That shortfall of nearly $3,900,000 relative to the DSCR-constrained maximum, and over $7,900,000 relative to the LTV ceiling, was central to the dispute. Our analysis demonstrated that the operating partner had left available debt capacity on the table, and that the shortfall in refinancing proceeds had a direct and calculable impact on the preferred investor's return of capital position in the waterfall.

The Complicating Factors

Preferred Return Accrual Methodology During Construction. Joint venture agreements in the institutional market frequently contain ambiguous language around whether preferred returns during a pre-stabilization period accrue on a simple or compounding basis. In Salt Lake City transactions, where construction timelines for mixed-use urban infill projects routinely run 24 to 36 months, the difference between simple and compounded accrual can represent hundreds of thousands of dollars in cumulative preferred return liability. Lenders examining a project for permanent financing will stress-test the waterfall mechanics to determine whether preferred return accruals have been accurately tracked, because those accruals represent senior obligations in the distribution stack that must be satisfied before any refinancing proceeds flow to junior positions. In our engagement, the operating partner had calculated accruals on a simple basis while the agreement's language, read in the context of market-standard institutional JV drafting, supported a compounding calculation. We provided market expert testimony on how preferred return accruals are treated in comparable transactions in the Intermountain West institutional market.

Refinancing Proceeds as a Capital Event. Whether a refinancing triggers the waterfall is one of the most frequently disputed questions in CRE joint venture litigation. The characterization of refinancing proceeds matters enormously because, in a promote structure, the promote typically does not attach until after return of capital and sometimes not until after the preferred return hurdle is fully satisfied. If proceeds from a permanent loan refinancing are treated as a capital event, the preferred investor is entitled to a return of contributed capital before any promoted interest is calculated. If the operating partner characterizes the proceeds as operating distributions or retains them without distribution, the preferred investor's position in the waterfall is delayed, and the promote attaches on a smaller capital base. In Salt Lake City's institutional JV market, the treatment of refinancing proceeds as a capital event triggering the full waterfall cascade is the prevailing standard when the agreement does not contain explicit contrary language. We provided detailed testimony on that market standard, supported by comparable transaction structures in the Utah and broader Intermountain West market.

Retail Component Valuation and Lender Haircuts. Ground-floor retail in a mixed-use asset presents a specific underwriting challenge that affected the financing available to this project. Salt Lake City's urban retail market, particularly downtown and in the Granary District, experienced meaningful rent disruption post-2020, and lenders during the refinancing period were applying haircuts of 10% to 20% to retail NOI in their underwriting, regardless of actual in-place rents. Several institutional lenders were pricing the retail component as if it were partially vacant even when leased, to account for rollover risk. That haircut reduced the income basis on which the DSCR was calculated, which in turn constrained the maximum loan. Understanding how lenders actually underwrote mixed-use retail exposure in Salt Lake City during the relevant period required direct knowledge of lender credit policies, not simply appraisal methodology.

The Salt Lake City Lender Market

The Salt Lake City permanent lending market for mixed-use and multifamily assets at the $20,000,000 to $35,000,000 loan size is served by a reasonably deep pool of institutional and regional lenders, though the credit box varies considerably across lender categories. Understanding who was actually in the market, at what pricing, and under what structural requirements is essential to evaluating whether the operating partner in a joint venture optimized its financing decisions.

Agency lenders (Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac) will lend on the multifamily component of a mixed-use asset if the commercial space does not exceed 20% to 25% of gross income, and the Salt Lake City multifamily market has consistently qualified for agency execution at loan sizes above approximately $5,000,000. During the relevant period, agency pricing was competitive, and loan-to-value ratios of up to 75% were available on stabilized assets. For a mixed-use asset with significant retail exposure, agency execution may require bifurcation of the financing, which adds complexity and cost but can significantly increase total loan proceeds.

CMBS lenders were active in the Salt Lake City market for mixed-use stabilized assets above approximately $15,000,000, with pricing that generally tracked the agency market on a spread basis but with more flexibility on property type. CMBS lenders during the relevant refinancing period were requiring DSCR of 1.25x and LTV of 65% on mixed-use collateral with retail exposure, consistent with our underwriting analysis. CMBS execution provided a fixed-rate, non-recourse structure that is market standard for institutional joint ventures.

Life insurance companies are active in the Salt Lake City market for higher-quality stabilized assets but are generally more conservative on mixed-use product with ground-floor retail. Life company execution during the relevant period featured pricing 15 to 25 basis points inside CMBS for the best assets, with LTV constrained to 60% to 65% and a strong preference for long-term, investment-grade tenancy in the retail component.

Regional and community banks in Utah, including several headquartered in Salt Lake City, are active in the $5,000,000 to $25,000,000 range for permanent and construction lending. These lenders generally require personal recourse guaranties and price on a floating or shorter fixed-rate basis, making them less suitable for the long-term institutional JV structure at issue, but they represent an important part of the market and affect pricing competition for the larger institutional lenders.

What Attorneys Handling Salt Lake City CRE Disputes Need to Know

A CRE financing expert witness fills a distinct and non-overlapping role relative to a real estate appraiser. An appraiser opines on value. A CRE financing expert opines on whether the capital structure, loan terms, and distribution mechanics applied to that value were consistent with market standards and with what a sophisticated borrower and lender would have negotiated and executed. In joint venture disputes, the financing expert's testimony is often more central to the damages calculation than the appraisal itself, because the waterfall outcome is a function of both the value of the asset and the structure of the debt and equity sitting on top of it.

Attorneys should retain a CRE financing expert when the dispute involves any of the following: preferred return accrual calculations, the characterization of loan proceeds in the distribution waterfall, the operating partner's obligation to optimize or refinance the asset, a claim that available debt proceeds were withheld or understated, disputed carried interest or promote triggers, or a claim that the asset was recapitalized on terms inconsistent with market practice. These questions require someone who has actually structured, negotiated, and closed transactions in the relevant market, not simply someone who has read about them.

Qualification matters. A retired bank credit officer who reviewed loans from behind a desk has a fundamentally different perspective than a practicing loan originator and capital markets professional who has been actively structuring deals in the Intermountain West market. The relevant expertise is the ability to testify to what the market actually required, what terms were actually available, and how market-standard joint venture agreements actually operate in practice. At Commercial Lending Solutions, with approximately $1 billion in transaction volume and over 1,000 lender relationships across all 50 states, our principals have current, active market knowledge that can withstand rigorous cross-examination.

Before retaining a CRE financing expert, counsel should gather the following: the complete joint venture or partnership agreement including all amendments and side letters, all loan documents from the original financing and any subsequent refinancings, the project's full financial history including capital contribution records and distribution ledgers, any lender term sheets or loan applications from the period in dispute, rent rolls, operating statements, and appraisals obtained in connection with any financing event, and all internal communications between the parties regarding financing decisions or waterfall calculations. This documentation forms the factual record on which the expert analysis is built, and gaps in this record must be identified early to avoid limiting the expert's opinions at the report stage.

At trial or in an arbitration proceeding, CRE financing expert testimony serves three functions: it educates the finder of fact on how these structures work in practice, it provides a market-standard benchmark against which the parties' conduct can be measured, and it quantifies the financial impact of any deviation from market standards. In a waterfall dispute, the expert can calculate the dollar difference between how distributions were actually made and how they should have been made under a correct application of the agreement read in light of market practice, which is often the most persuasive element of the damages case.

Key Takeaways

  • Preferred return accrual methodology has large dollar consequences in Salt Lake City transactions where construction timelines are long; the difference between simple and compounding accrual over a 30-month construction period on a $10,000,000 preferred equity contribution can exceed $400,000, and that difference is almost always in dispute when the project underperforms.
  • Whether refinancing proceeds trigger the full waterfall cascade is a market-standard question, not purely a contract interpretation question; a CRE financing expert can testify to how comparable institutional JV agreements in the Salt Lake City and Intermountain West market treat refinancing proceeds, providing context that pure legal analysis cannot supply.
  • Financing capacity analysis is essential to damages quantification in cases where an operating partner is alleged to have under-leveraged an asset; the maximum supportable loan based on DSCR and LTV constraints is a calculable number, and the difference between that number and the actual loan is a measurable harm to the preferred investor's waterfall position.
  • The Salt Lake City mixed-use and multifamily market was served by multiple competing lender categories during the relevant period, including agency, CMBS, life company, and regional bank execution; an operating partner's claim that better financing was unavailable must be tested against actual market conditions, which requires active market knowledge.
  • Lender haircuts on retail NOI in Salt Lake City's post-2020 market were a real constraint on mixed-use financing and must be factored into any expert analysis of maximum achievable loan proceeds; ignoring this factor overstates available financing and distorts the damages calculation.
  • CRE financing expert testimony is distinct from appraisal testimony and addresses questions that appraisers are not trained to answer, including capital structure optimization, lender credit box analysis, and market-standard JV mechanics.

Expert Witness: CRE Financing Feasibility in Chapter 11 Proceedings in La Crosse, WI

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Bankruptcy Exit Financing

The La Crosse Market Context

La Crosse, Wisconsin sits at the confluence of the Mississippi and Black Rivers in the western part of the state, and its commercial real estate market reflects the characteristics of a mid-sized regional hub serving a four-county trade area that stretches across the Minnesota border. With a metro population of approximately 135,000 and anchored by Gundersen Health System and Mayo Clinic Health System as the dominant institutional employers, La Crosse's economic base is more diversified and resilient than many markets of comparable size in the Upper Midwest. That stability matters significantly in a Chapter 11 financing feasibility analysis, because lender confidence in a market is driven as much by employment base durability as by any single asset's fundamentals.

On the industrial side, La Crosse benefits from its position along the I-90 corridor and its access to barge transport on the Mississippi, which supports manufacturing and distribution demand. Industrial vacancy in the La Crosse metro has historically run below 6%, and net absorption has remained positive in recent years as regional logistics users seek alternatives to the overpriced Twin Cities and Milwaukee submarkets. For an industrial asset in a Chapter 11 estate, this vacancy environment is meaningful: a well-located industrial building with stabilized occupancy is a financeable asset even with bankruptcy-related complexity attached to it.

Multifamily in La Crosse presents a somewhat different picture. The University of Wisconsin-La Crosse enrollment of approximately 10,000 students creates a persistent demand layer for smaller multifamily and student-adjacent housing, while the healthcare anchor provides workforce housing demand for mid-range apartments. Cap rates for stabilized multifamily in the La Crosse market have generally traded in the 6.5% to 7.5% range, somewhat elevated compared to primary markets, which actually works in favor of a financing feasibility analysis because higher cap rates produce stronger debt-service coverage at any given loan amount. Retail continues to face the national headwinds around vacancy and declining rent growth, but neighborhood-serving and medical retail in La Crosse has proven more durable than strip centers dependent on discretionary tenants. Office fundamentals are under pressure, consistent with national trends, and any Chapter 11 asset with significant office exposure will face a more constrained lender market regardless of location.

For purposes of a Plan of Reorganization financing analysis, the La Crosse market is characterized by a deep community and regional bank presence, meaningful credit union commercial lending activity, and intermittent participation by non-bank bridge lenders and CMBS conduits depending on loan size and asset type. Understanding which of those capital sources will actively engage a bankruptcy exit scenario is the core analytical task an expert witness must address.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing a creditor in a Chapter 11 proceeding involving a commercial real estate asset located in the La Crosse market. The asset was a stabilized, income-producing mixed-use property with a ground-floor commercial component and residential units above, a property type common in La Crosse's downtown and near-downtown submarkets. The debtor had proposed a Plan of Reorganization that contemplated a first-mortgage refinance as the primary mechanism for paying senior secured creditors in full at confirmation. The creditor's counsel needed an independent expert opinion addressing whether that refinance was realistically achievable, what the maximum supportable loan amount would be given current market conditions, what categories of lenders would actually originate a loan on an asset held in a bankruptcy estate, and whether the proposed financing terms in the debtor's plan were consistent with what the market would actually offer.

The legal questions were tightly focused. Could the debtor demonstrate, as required under 11 U.S.C. Section 1129(a)(11), that the plan was feasible and not likely to be followed by further reorganization or liquidation? The financing commitment is the operational test of feasibility for real estate debtors, and without credible evidence that the proposed refinance can close, the plan confirmation fails. Our engagement was to provide a Rule 26 expert report addressing that question with specific reference to the La Crosse market, current lending standards, and the characteristics of this particular asset.

CLS CRE brought to this engagement approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, active relationships with more than 1,000 commercial lenders nationwide including community banks and non-bank lenders active in the La Crosse market, and prior expert witness engagements for both creditors and plan proponents in bankruptcy proceedings across multiple jurisdictions.

The Financial Analysis

The starting point for any first-mortgage sizing analysis is the stabilized net operating income, and in a Chapter 11 context that analysis carries additional weight because the NOI figure must be defensible under cross-examination. For this engagement, we analyzed trailing twelve-month actual collections, adjusted for one non-recurring vacancy event that had been remedied prior to the confirmation hearing, and normalized operating expenses using market comparables for similar mixed-use properties in La Crosse. The resulting stabilized NOI was approximately $118,000 annually. We did not use the debtor's projected NOI, which reflected optimistic lease-up assumptions; we used actual verified income, which is the methodology any institutional underwriter would apply and the standard a court should expect from a financing feasibility opinion.

From that stabilized NOI, we applied the debt service coverage ratio threshold that governs conventional commercial mortgage underwriting. The market standard for non-recourse permanent financing on mixed-use assets is 1.25x DSCR, meaning the property must generate $1.25 in net operating income for every $1.00 of annual debt service. Community bank lenders in La Crosse typically underwrite to 1.25x as a floor, with some requiring 1.30x on assets with any vacancy or lease rollover risk. We used 1.25x as the applicable threshold because the asset was stabilized and the commercial tenant had a remaining lease term sufficient to satisfy lender seasoning requirements.

At a 1.25x DSCR threshold with a stabilized NOI of $118,000, the maximum allowable annual debt service is $94,400. Using a 25-year amortization schedule at a 7.50% interest rate, which reflected the actual market pricing for permanent commercial mortgages on mixed-use assets in secondary Midwest markets at the time of our analysis, that debt service ceiling supported a maximum loan of approximately $1,035,000. We stress-tested that figure at 8.00% to reflect rate variability risk, which reduced the maximum supportable loan to approximately $985,000. These figures represent the income-driven ceiling; the LTV constraint operates independently and may further restrict loan size.

For LTV, conventional permanent lenders in this market underwrite mixed-use assets at 65% to 70% LTV. We applied a 65% LTV constraint using the appraised value established in the case, which produced a maximum loan amount of $1,040,000 at the lower end of the value range supported by the appraisal. The income-based limit and the LTV limit were therefore closely aligned, which is a positive indicator of analytical consistency and lender confidence in the asset's coverage characteristics. The conclusion was that first-mortgage financing of approximately $985,000 to $1,035,000 was supportable under market conditions, sufficient to satisfy the senior secured creditor in full and fund the plan's confirmation requirements. That finding directly addressed the feasibility question before the court.

The Complicating Factors

The bankruptcy estate overlay: The single most significant complicating factor in any Chapter 11 exit financing is that most conventional lenders will not originate a loan secured by a property while it remains in a bankruptcy estate. This is not a policy judgment about the debtor; it is a legal and underwriting reality. Community banks in La Crosse, like most regulated depositories, face examination risk if they fund a loan into an active bankruptcy without bankruptcy court approval and clear title through a confirmed plan. The practical result is that a commitment letter obtained prior to plan confirmation must explicitly condition closing on receipt of a final confirmation order and a court-approved release of all estate claims against the property. Attorneys who have not worked in commercial mortgage brokerage sometimes assume a bank's pre-commitment letter is equivalent to a firm commitment; in bankruptcy exit financing it is never unconditional, and the expert's job is to identify what conditions are realistic and achievable versus speculative.

Title and lien resolution: Mixed-use properties in Chapter 11 frequently carry mechanic's liens, judgment liens, or subordinate mortgages that must be resolved before any first-mortgage lender will commit. In the La Crosse context, where many commercial properties have been held by closely held entities for decades, subordinate debt may include seller financing, family loans, or SBA notes with complicated intercreditor dynamics. The first-mortgage lender requires a clean first-lien position; the plan must address subordinate debt resolution as a precondition to closing, not as a post-confirmation cleanup exercise. Failure to sequence this correctly has derailed otherwise financeable plans in proceedings across the Midwest.

Environmental and property condition: Older mixed-use properties in La Crosse's downtown and near-downtown submarkets may carry Phase I environmental concerns, asbestos-containing materials, or deferred maintenance that triggers lender reserve requirements or outright ineligibility under certain lending programs. A Phase I Environmental Site Assessment is a standard lender requirement, and any recognized environmental condition escalating to a Phase II will substantially narrow the lender pool. Bridge lenders and hard money sources will proceed with environmental contingencies that community banks will not accept, but at a significant cost premium that may alter the feasibility calculus.

Lease quality and tenant credit: For a mixed-use asset in La Crosse, the commercial lease component drives income stability analysis. A lender underwriting the commercial portion will review tenant credit, lease term remaining relative to the proposed loan maturity, and whether any lease has termination rights triggered by the debtor's bankruptcy filing. A lease that has already been assumed under 11 U.S.C. Section 365 and is current eliminates the executory contract risk, but a lender will still require documentation confirming assumption and cure. Attorneys handling plan confirmation proceedings should ensure that lease assumption orders are clean and that the lender's counsel receives certified copies early in the commitment process.

The La Crosse Lender Market

Understanding which lenders will actually engage a bankruptcy exit refinance in La Crosse is not a theoretical exercise; it is the operational question the court needs answered when evaluating plan feasibility. The La Crosse market supports several distinct categories of commercial lenders with meaningfully different credit parameters.

Community and regional banks: La Crosse has a notably deep community banking presence, with multiple locally headquartered banks and regional institutions based in Madison and the Fox Valley that maintain active commercial lending programs in western Wisconsin. These lenders are the natural long-term holders of stabilized commercial mortgages in this market. They underwrite to 65-70% LTV, 1.25x DSCR, and typically require personal guarantees from principals with meaningful net worth. Their rates at the time of our analysis were in the 7.25% to 7.75% range on 5 to 7-year fixed terms. Critically, these lenders are almost uniformly unwilling to commit while the property remains in active bankruptcy. Their commitment letters will be conditioned on confirmation order, clear title, and complete resolution of all subordinate claims. For a plan confirmation hearing, counsel needs to present a community bank commitment letter that clearly articulates achievable closing conditions, not aspirational ones.

Credit unions with commercial lending authority: Several Wisconsin-chartered credit unions have expanded their commercial real estate lending programs under NCUA guidelines permitting up to 12.25% of assets in member business loans. These institutions are occasionally a source of commercial mortgage capital for La Crosse assets, particularly where borrower members have existing deposit relationships. Their credit parameters are generally similar to community banks, but they may have lower loan size capacity and more restrictive geographic constraints.

Non-bank bridge lenders: For bankruptcy exit scenarios where the plan confirmation timeline is compressed or where property condition or lease vacancy creates a gap in conventional eligibility, non-bank bridge lenders provide short-term financing at rates ranging from 9.50% to 12.00% on interest-only terms, typically at 55-65% LTV. These lenders are asset-based underwriters; they are less focused on DSCR and more focused on exit strategy and collateral coverage. A bridge loan is not the preferred permanent solution, but it is a legitimate path to plan confirmation if the debtor can demonstrate a credible refinance takeout within 18 to 36 months. CLS CRE maintains active relationships with non-bank bridge lenders who operate in Wisconsin and will engage bankruptcy estate assets with appropriate legal protections in place.

SBA 504 and conventional government-backed programs: SBA financing is generally not available for bankruptcy exit situations because SBA eligibility requires that the borrower not have been a debtor in a bankruptcy proceeding within the prior three years. This is a commonly misunderstood limitation that can undermine a debtor's proposed plan if the plan proponent erroneously assumes SBA financing is available. Expert witnesses must identify this restriction explicitly and ensure it does not appear in a plan feasibility analysis without disclosure of the eligibility bar.

What Attorneys Handling La Crosse CRE Disputes Need to Know

Retaining a commercial real estate financing expert witness is not the same as retaining a commercial real estate appraiser, and the distinction matters significantly in a Chapter 11 plan confirmation proceeding. An appraiser's opinion establishes value. A financing expert's opinion establishes whether a loan of the required amount, on terms consistent with market conditions, is actually available to this borrower on this asset in this legal posture. Those are different questions, and courts have increasingly recognized that plan feasibility requires both.

Attorneys should retain a CRE financing expert at the moment they identify that financing is a component of plan feasibility. That is earlier than most counsel retain us. The expert can help shape discovery requests targeting the debtor's financing evidence, identify flaws in a competing expert's methodology, and draft the financial parameters that a credible commitment letter must satisfy. Waiting until the eve of the confirmation hearing to engage an expert limits what the expert can accomplish and creates Daubert exposure from a compressed analysis timeline.

The questions a CRE financing expert can answer that an appraiser cannot include: What DSCR and LTV thresholds do actual lenders currently apply to this asset type in this market? What conditions are standard in commitment letters for bankruptcy exit financing? Is the interest rate assumed in the debtor's plan consistent with current market pricing? Which specific categories of lenders will originate this loan, and which categories will not? Is the amortization schedule proposed in the plan consistent with market practice? Can the loan close within the timeline proposed in the plan?

Qualification matters. A retired banker who worked in commercial lending 15 years ago has historical knowledge but may not understand current underwriting standards, current rate environments, or the specific legal constraints around bankruptcy estate collateral. CLS CRE's qualifications rest on current, active deal flow across 50 states and over 1,000 lender relationships including lenders who specifically originate in bankruptcy exit scenarios. Our opinions are grounded in what lenders are doing today, not what they did in prior rate cycles.

Before engaging our firm, counsel should gather the following: current rent roll and lease abstracts, trailing 12 to 24 months of actual operating statements, the most recent appraisal, a schedule of all liens and encumbrances, any existing debt documents, the proposed plan financing terms, and any commitment letters or term sheets the debtor has already obtained. The expert's analysis is only as reliable as the input data, and gaps in the record create vulnerabilities that opposing counsel will exploit.

Key Takeaways

  • Plan feasibility requires financing evidence, not financing aspiration. A debtor's assertion that refinancing is available is not sufficient under Section 1129(a)(11). The court requires credible, market-grounded evidence of loan availability, and an independent financing expert provides that evidence with methodology a court can evaluate.
  • NOI analysis in Chapter 11 must be conservative and verified. Underwriters and expert witnesses alike must use actual trailing income, not optimistic projections, when sizing the maximum supportable loan. Any upward adjustments must be documented and defensible under cross-examination.
  • The La Crosse community bank market has depth, but bankruptcy-conditioned commitment letters have real legal requirements. A community bank commitment letter for a bankruptcy exit transaction is not unconditional; its achievability depends on how cleanly the plan resolves lien, title, and lease issues prior to closing.
  • SBA financing is not available to Chapter 11 debtors within three years of the bankruptcy filing. Plans that assume SBA eligibility without disclosing this restriction are analytically flawed, and an opposing expert should identify this error explicitly.
  • Bridge financing is a legitimate but more expensive path to confirmation. When conventional bank financing cannot be confirmed pre-confirmation due to asset condition, vacancy, or timeline constraints, non-bank bridge lenders provide a viable alternative that an expert can document as market-available, at a cost premium the plan must account for.

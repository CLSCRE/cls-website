Expert Witness: CRE Partnership and Waterfall Disputes in Daytona Beach, FL

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Joint Venture and Partnership Dispute

The Daytona Beach Market Context

Daytona Beach occupies a distinct position in the Florida commercial real estate landscape. It is not Orlando, not Jacksonville, not Miami. It is a mid-tier coastal market with its own demand drivers, its own capital availability profile, and its own risk characteristics that matter enormously when you are trying to resolve a dispute over how a joint venture was supposed to perform financially. Anyone offering an expert opinion on a Daytona Beach CRE joint venture who treats this market as interchangeable with other Florida metros is starting from a flawed foundation.

The Daytona Beach market has experienced meaningful development pressure across several asset classes over the past several years, driven by a combination of interstate corridor access along I-4 and I-95, a persistent tourism and hospitality demand base, and population inflows from higher-cost Florida metros. Multifamily fundamentals have been particularly active, with significant delivery of new units in the greater Volusia County submarket putting upward pressure on vacancy in certain submarkets while core locations near the beachside corridor and LPGA Boulevard have maintained stronger occupancy. Cap rates for stabilized multifamily assets in this market have ranged from the low 5s to the mid 6s depending on vintage, location, and unit mix, with value-add product pricing at a premium on yield but requiring more conservative underwriting from senior lenders.

Retail in Daytona Beach has followed a bifurcated path consistent with national trends but with a local twist: tourist-facing retail along the beachside has shown resilience tied to hospitality traffic, while suburban strip centers have faced more traditional headwinds from e-commerce and anchor erosion. Industrial and flex product along the I-95 corridor has benefited from South Florida logistics spillover and the Port Canaveral proximity effect, with vacancy in the Volusia County industrial submarket running in the low single digits for smaller bay product. For joint venture disputes, the relevant market context is not just a snapshot of conditions today. It is a reconstruction of what the market looked like at the time the venture was capitalized, what projections were reasonable at that moment, and how actual performance diverged from those projections in ways that were either foreseeable or genuinely outside the control of the parties.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing the limited partner in a Daytona Beach joint venture dispute involving a multifamily asset in the greater Volusia County submarket. The venture had been formed several years prior, with a sponsor acting as the general partner and operating member, and a single institutional limited partner providing the majority of the equity capital. The asset was a mid-size apartment community, value-add in character, acquired with a business plan premised on unit renovations, rent growth, and an eventual disposition at a compressed cap rate relative to the acquisition basis.

The dispute centered on three interconnected financial questions. First, whether the preferred return owed to the limited partner had been correctly calculated under the waterfall provisions of the operating agreement, specifically whether the sponsor had applied the correct compounding methodology and whether certain fees paid to affiliated entities had been improperly deducted before the preferred return threshold was satisfied. Second, whether the promoted interest claimed by the sponsor upon a recapitalization event was legitimately earned, given that the limited partner contended the asset had not achieved the equity multiple trigger required under a tiered promote structure. Third, and most directly within our area of expertise, whether the debt structure placed on the property at acquisition and at the subsequent refinancing was consistent with market-standard terms available at those points in time, and whether the financing costs incurred were reasonable or reflected self-dealing that disadvantaged the limited partner's position in the waterfall.

Counsel needed an expert who could explain how institutional lenders actually underwrite multifamily assets in this market, what debt terms were available at the relevant dates, and how senior debt sizing interacts with waterfall mechanics in a way that directly affects each party's economic outcome.

The Financial Analysis

Our analysis began with a reconstruction of the property's net operating income at three distinct points: at acquisition underwriting, at stabilization, and at the time of the contested recapitalization. The sponsor's underwriting projected a stabilized NOI of approximately $1.42 million annually on a 120-unit asset, based on a projected average effective rent of approximately $1,350 per unit per month and an assumed economic vacancy of 7 percent, with expense ratios consistent with comparable properties in the Volusia County submarket at the time.

Our independent reconstruction of achievable NOI at stabilization, using market rent comparables, actual expense benchmarks from comparable assets, and the operating history of the subject property, produced a figure materially below the sponsor's projection. Our analysis produced a stabilized NOI estimate of approximately $1.19 million, reflecting actual market rents closer to $1,245 per unit per month on a risk-adjusted basis and a more conservative expense load that reflected actual management costs, insurance escalation, and property tax reassessment risk in Volusia County.

This NOI differential is not a minor accounting point. It is the number that drives senior debt sizing, and senior debt sizing is the number that determines how much equity sits in the capital stack, which in turn determines whether a waterfall tier is crossed. Using the market-standard DSCR threshold of 1.25x applied by agency lenders and most conventional multifamily lenders for value-add assets in secondary Florida markets, the maximum supportable debt service on the sponsor's projected NOI of $1.42 million was approximately $1.136 million annually. At a 6.75 percent interest rate and 30-year amortization schedule, which were representative of market terms available at the relevant refinancing date, that debt service figure supported a maximum loan of approximately $14.2 million.

Applying the same DSCR standard to our independently reconstructed NOI of $1.19 million, the maximum supportable debt service dropped to approximately $952,000 annually, supporting a maximum loan of approximately $11.9 million at the same rate and amortization assumptions. The acquisition price had been $16.8 million, and the venture had invested an additional $1.9 million in capital improvements, creating a total capitalization basis of approximately $18.7 million. At a 65 percent LTV ceiling, which was the market-standard constraint for value-add multifamily refinancing in this market at the relevant date, the maximum loan based on an appraised value consistent with our NOI reconstruction was approximately $12.15 million, effectively constraining the loan to the DSCR-limited figure in any case.

The practical consequence was that the refinancing proceeds available to the venture were insufficient to return the limited partner's invested capital before the promote calculation was triggered, which is precisely the factual question the court needed to resolve. The sponsor's position depended on a higher loan sizing that would have changed the distribution sequence materially. Our analysis demonstrated that the sponsor's assumed loan size was not achievable under market-standard underwriting at the relevant date, regardless of which lender was approached.

The Complicating Factors

The compounding methodology dispute. Preferred return calculations in CRE joint ventures are frequently drafted with ambiguity around whether the preferred return compounds annually, quarterly, or on an accrued basis. In the Daytona Beach venture at issue, the operating agreement used language that was susceptible to two readings: one under which the preferred return compounded annually on unpaid balances, and one under which it accrued without compounding until a distribution event occurred. Over a multi-year hold with no interim distributions, the difference between these two interpretations on an 8 percent preferred return applied to $7.2 million of LP capital produced a variance of approximately $380,000 in the amount owed before the sponsor's promote was reachable. Market-standard institutional JV agreements typically specify this methodology explicitly. The fact that this agreement did not is itself an observation our expert report addressed, because it speaks to whether the agreement was drafted consistently with how institutional sponsors and LP investors typically document these structures.

Affiliate fee deductions and their treatment in the waterfall. The sponsor's operating agreement permitted payment of an asset management fee and a construction management fee to affiliated entities. The dispute included whether these fees, which totaled approximately $290,000 over the hold period, were deducted at the property level before NOI was calculated for purposes of preferred return accrual, or whether they should have been treated as distributions to the sponsor below the preferred return threshold. Lenders treat affiliate fees differently than courts may. A lender underwriting the property will typically underwrite at market-rate management costs regardless of what the sponsor charges internally, and will cap recognized management fees at 4 to 5 percent of effective gross income for underwriting purposes. That market-standard treatment informed our analysis of how the property's actual economics should have been characterized.

The recapitalization event classification. Whether a refinancing constitutes a capital event triggering the waterfall is a legal question, but one with significant financial analysis supporting it. In this venture, the sponsor took the position that a cash-out refinancing qualified as a capital event that triggered the promote calculation. Our analysis examined whether market-standard JV structures treat refinancing proceeds identically to disposition proceeds for waterfall purposes, and the answer is nuanced. Some institutional structures do treat cash-out refinancing as a capital event; others explicitly carve it out and reserve promote calculations for actual disposition. Demonstrating what is and is not market standard in this regard required review of comparable institutional JV term sheets and operating agreements from similar vintage transactions, which CLS CRE was positioned to provide through our transaction experience across approximately $1 billion in commercial real estate financings.

Interest rate environment and its effect on exit value. The venture's business plan assumed an exit cap rate that was realistic at origination but was significantly undercut by the interest rate environment that prevailed at the time of the contested refinancing. An asset that was projected to exit at a 5.25 percent cap rate was realistically valued at a 5.85 to 6.10 percent cap rate given where 10-year Treasury rates had moved and where multifamily cap rates in secondary Florida markets had repriced. This cap rate expansion reduced the appraised value on which the promote calculation depended, which is not a mistake by either party, but it is directly relevant to whether the equity multiple threshold for the promote was achievable.

The Daytona Beach Lender Market

For multifamily assets in the Daytona Beach and greater Volusia County submarket, the lender universe is reasonably well developed but not as deep as Orlando or Tampa. Understanding which lenders are active and what their credit boxes look like is essential to any analysis of what financing was achievable at a given point in time.

Agency lenders (Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac execution through approved DUS lenders) are available for stabilized or near-stabilized multifamily assets meeting occupancy thresholds of 90 percent or better for a minimum of 90 days. For value-add assets still in renovation, agency execution is generally not available, and the borrower is dependent on bridge lending until the business plan is executed. The Daytona Beach market's classification as a secondary market under agency geographic tiering can result in slightly more conservative LTV constraints compared to primary markets, with 65 to 70 percent LTV being the realistic ceiling for most agency executions in this submarket.

CMBS lenders are active for larger multifamily and mixed-use assets in this market, typically for deals above $8 to $10 million in loan size. CMBS pricing is benchmark-driven and was particularly stressed during the relevant time period covered by this engagement, making CMBS a less competitive alternative than it would have been in a lower-rate environment. CMBS underwriting on secondary Florida markets also applies haircuts to rent growth assumptions that can further constrain loan sizing.

Regional and community banks with Florida footprints are meaningfully active in the Daytona Beach market, and their credit boxes tend to be more flexible on asset complexity but more conservative on leverage, typically topping out at 65 percent LTV with personal recourse requirements. For a joint venture structure without a creditworthy individual guarantor willing to provide full recourse, bank execution can be difficult to achieve at competitive terms.

Debt funds and bridge lenders fill the value-add gap and were the appropriate execution channel for this property at acquisition. Rates on bridge execution in the relevant period ranged from SOFR plus 275 to SOFR plus 425 depending on leverage, property condition, and sponsorship strength, with 12-month to 36-month terms and extension options tied to debt yield and occupancy tests. Our analysis confirmed that the debt terms actually obtained were within market range for the asset class and the borrower profile, which was relevant to the question of whether the financing reflected any self-dealing or breach of the sponsor's fiduciary obligations to the venture.

What Attorneys Handling Daytona Beach CRE Disputes Need to Know

A CRE financing expert fills a gap that appraisers and accountants cannot fill. An appraiser will tell you what the property was worth. An accountant will tell you what the books show. Neither of them will tell you what debt was achievable, on what terms, at what cost, under what structural constraints, and how that financing interacted with the contractual waterfall to produce or deny the economic outcome each party expected. That is precisely the analysis that resolves joint venture disputes of this type.

Retain the expert before depositions, not after. The most common mistake counsel makes is retaining a CRE financing expert after depositions have already been taken from the sponsor's principals and their lender. The expert can help you know what to ask: what debt yield threshold was used, what the extension test required, whether a personal guarantee was waived and why, whether the lender required a cash management agreement that affected distributions. These are technical questions that will not surface without expert guidance, and they frequently contain the factual record that determines whether a party's distributions were properly withheld or improperly diverted.

A qualified expert is not simply a retired banker or a former CMBS analyst. The expert needs current, active market knowledge. Lending standards, pricing benchmarks, and structural requirements shift materially from one interest rate cycle to the next. An expert whose active transactional experience ended five years ago may not accurately represent what a lender would have required in the specific market conditions of the relevant period. At CLS CRE, we are active daily in the lender market across 1,000 or more lender relationships, which means our testimony about what was available and at what terms is grounded in current and recent transactional experience, not recollection.

Documents to gather before the expert engagement begins: the executed operating agreement with all amendments; the loan documents for every financing event on the property; any lender term sheets or loan applications submitted during the relevant period; the property's rent rolls and operating statements for each year of the hold; all capital call notices and distribution records; any third-party appraisals or broker opinions of value; and all communications between the parties regarding the debt structure or distribution methodology. The more complete the document set, the more precise the expert's reconstruction of the financial history.

At trial or in a mediation context, a CRE financing expert provides context that judges and arbitrators frequently lack. Explaining what a "catch-up provision" does, why a tiered promote structure uses equity multiple rather than IRR as the trigger, or why a debt yield test matters more than DSCR at the refinancing stage are all things that require patient, technically grounded explanation. Our firm has provided Rule 26 expert reports and has been prepared to testify in federal and state court proceedings, and we structure our reports to be accessible to a sophisticated judge or jury without sacrificing the technical precision that holds up under cross-examination.

Key Takeaways

  • NOI reconstruction is the foundation. In any joint venture waterfall dispute, the supportable debt level drives equity distribution outcomes. An independent NOI reconstruction using market-comparable data, not sponsor projections, is the starting point for every analysis we perform.
  • DSCR thresholds are not negotiable in court. Market-standard DSCR requirements of 1.20x to 1.25x are not arbitrary; they reflect actual lender underwriting practice and can be documented through executed loan files and published agency guidelines. When a dispute hinges on what loan size was achievable, the answer is grounded in these standards, not in what a party wished for.
  • Preferred return compounding methodology has material dollar consequences. Ambiguous drafting around annual versus accrued compounding can shift six-figure sums between parties. A financing expert can opine on what market-standard institutional agreements provide, which informs how ambiguous language should be interpreted.

Expert Witness: CRE Financing Feasibility in Chapter 11 Proceedings in Eugene, OR

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Bankruptcy Exit Financing

The Eugene Market Context

Eugene, Oregon sits at the southern end of the Willamette Valley and functions as the commercial core of Lane County, a regional economy anchored by the University of Oregon, PeaceHealth, and a manufacturing and logistics base that has grown meaningfully since the pandemic reshuffled supply chain geography. For purposes of commercial real estate financing feasibility analysis, Eugene is what I would call a secondary-secondary market: it is not Portland, it does not have the same depth of institutional capital, and lenders who have never deployed capital here will require more convincing than they would for a comparable asset in Seattle or Denver. That reality matters enormously in a bankruptcy confirmation proceeding, where the question is not whether financing is theoretically possible but whether it is practically available at terms sufficient to satisfy a plan.

Multifamily fundamentals in Eugene remain relatively strong by Pacific Northwest secondary market standards. The University of Oregon enrollment base creates a structural floor on rental demand, particularly in submarkets within two to three miles of campus. Market-rate apartment vacancy in Eugene has hovered in the 4 to 6 percent range through the current rate cycle, with effective rents holding even as concessions have increased modestly. That said, Eugene is not immune to the broader affordability dynamics compressing landlord pricing power in smaller Oregon markets. Cap rates on stabilized multifamily assets in Eugene have moved from the high 4s to the mid-5s and, in some cases, low 6s depending on asset vintage and location within the metro, reflecting both the rate environment and the compression of rent growth assumptions.

For industrial product, Eugene benefits from its position as a distribution waypoint serving the broader southern Willamette Valley, and vacancy for functional warehouse and light industrial has remained tight, generally below 5 percent, with asking rents climbing steadily over the past three years. Retail is more bifurcated: necessity-anchored centers with strong grocery or medical tenancy continue to trade and finance well, while unanchored strip product and second-generation inline space present more friction. Office fundamentals mirror national trends, with meaningful vacancy in the urban core and a flight-to-quality dynamic concentrating demand in newer, amenitized suburban product. Any bankruptcy proceeding involving Eugene office product will face a more skeptical lender universe than an industrial or multifamily asset of comparable size.

The broader point for counsel and their clients: Eugene is a financeable market, but lender appetite is narrower than in Tier 1 metros, the pool of relationship lenders who know the market is finite, and a financing feasibility opinion in this market must be grounded in direct lender-market intelligence rather than generic industry benchmarks. That is the work Commercial Lending Solutions brings to these engagements.

The Engagement

In the type of matter this article addresses, CLS CRE is retained by counsel representing a creditor or plan proponent in a Chapter 11 reorganization proceeding where the estate holds a commercial real estate asset in Eugene as its primary or a significant asset. The retaining party, identified here only by role, requires an expert opinion on financing feasibility to support confirmation of a Plan of Reorganization. The expert report is submitted pursuant to Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 26, and the expert may be deposed or called to testify at a confirmation hearing before the bankruptcy court.

The asset in the type of engagement we are describing for this article is an income-producing commercial property, say a stabilized multifamily complex in the south Eugene or Bethel-Danebo submarket, or a light industrial building in the River Road or Airport area corridor. The specific asset type matters because it determines which lender categories are viable, what underwriting benchmarks apply, and how bankruptcy-specific complications affect the financing universe. For purposes of this analysis, I will ground the financial discussion in a stabilized multifamily scenario, as that asset type presents the cleanest set of underwriting variables while illustrating the full complexity of bankruptcy exit financing.

The core legal and financial questions the court needs answered are straightforward in concept but demanding in execution. First, can the property generate sufficient net operating income to support first-mortgage debt service at market-rate terms and loan-to-value constraints? Second, if yes, at what loan amount and on what terms? Third, are there lenders who will actually originate a loan on an asset coming out of a bankruptcy estate, and what conditions will they impose? Fourth, does the projected loan proceed satisfy the senior secured creditor's claim sufficiently to make the plan feasible? These are questions an appraiser, who focuses on value, cannot fully answer. They require someone who lives in the lending markets and can speak with authority about what lenders will and will not do.

The Financial Analysis

My methodology in this type of engagement begins with a reconstruction of the property's stabilized net operating income. I am not an appraiser and do not opine on value, but I do analyze the income and expense stream carefully because the NOI is the foundation on which loan sizing is built. For a Eugene multifamily asset in the 40 to 80 unit range, I will review actual rent rolls, current market comparable rents from submarket data and direct lender discussions, vacancy assumptions benchmarked against current Lane County market conditions, and operating expense ratios validated against tax records, management agreements, and utility billing structures.

For a hypothetical 60-unit apartment complex in Eugene, a reasonable stabilized NOI analysis might look like this. Gross potential rent at market at, say, $1,350 per month average across a mix of studios, one-bedrooms, and two-bedrooms generates approximately $972,000 in gross potential rent annually. Applying a 5 percent vacancy and credit loss assumption consistent with current Eugene submarket performance yields effective gross income of approximately $923,400. Operating expenses for a property of this vintage and type in Eugene, including property management at 6 percent of EGI, insurance, property taxes (Lane County effective rates run roughly 0.8 to 1.1 percent of assessed value depending on property class and exemption status), utilities, maintenance reserves, and landscaping and turnover costs, would typically run in the range of $280,000 to $320,000 annually for a well-maintained 60-unit complex. That produces a stabilized NOI of approximately $603,000 to $643,000. I will use $620,000 as my working NOI for this illustration.

With a stabilized NOI of $620,000, the next step is applying the debt service coverage ratio threshold that governs maximum loan sizing. The market standard for conventional permanent financing on multifamily assets in secondary markets like Eugene is a minimum DSCR of 1.25x for agency-eligible product and 1.20x for balance sheet portfolio lenders. I apply 1.25x as the binding constraint in a bankruptcy exit financing scenario because lenders in this context will not underwrite to the aggressive end of their range: the added complexity and reputational caution around bankruptcy-exit loans pushes underwriters toward more conservative DSCR floors. At 1.25x DSCR, maximum annual debt service is $620,000 divided by 1.25, which equals $496,000. At current market pricing for a five-year fixed, 30-year amortizing loan on a secondary market multifamily asset, a rate assumption in the range of 6.50 to 7.00 percent is appropriate based on current SOFR-spread pricing and agency execution benchmarks. Using a 6.75 percent note rate and 30-year amortization, the corresponding loan amount that generates $496,000 in annual debt service is approximately $6.35 million to $6.45 million. I use standard mortgage constant math: at 6.75 percent on a 30-year amortization schedule, the monthly constant is approximately 0.006490, producing an annual constant of approximately 7.79 percent. Dividing $496,000 by 0.0779 yields a maximum supportable loan of approximately $6.37 million.

The second constraint is LTV. For a stabilized multifamily asset in Eugene, agency lenders (Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac) will lend up to 80 percent LTV on stabilized properties, though in practice the DSCR test is typically the binding constraint in the current rate environment. If the property's appraised value is $9.5 million, the LTV ceiling is $7.6 million, which exceeds the DSCR-driven loan limit. If value is $8 million, the LTV ceiling is $6.4 million, approximately in line with the DSCR constraint. The interaction of these two tests, DSCR and LTV, and which is binding, is one of the central analytical conclusions in a feasibility opinion. In today's rate environment, for most Eugene multifamily assets, DSCR is the binding constraint, not LTV. This matters for plan feasibility because it means that even if the property's value is higher than the senior creditor's claim, the amount the property can actually finance is capped by its income relative to current debt service costs.

The Complicating Factors

Bankruptcy Stigma in the Lender Community. The single largest complicating factor in bankruptcy exit financing is that most conventional lenders, including agency lenders in particular, will not originate a loan where the property is in an active bankruptcy estate at the time of application. Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac have explicit eligibility requirements that preclude origination on assets encumbered by active bankruptcy proceedings. This means that the financing contemplated in the plan is almost always structured as post-confirmation financing, triggered by or closing concurrent with plan effective date. The plan must be drafted to account for this sequencing, and the feasibility opinion must address whether committed or highly conditional loan proceeds are available at confirmation, not just theoretically available at some future date. I address this by canvassing lenders in advance of issuing my opinion and documenting their actual interest level and conditional term sheet availability.

Title and Lien Resolution Complexity. A property exiting bankruptcy typically carries a history of disputed liens, mechanic's liens, deferred maintenance, and potentially unpaid property taxes. Lenders require clean title as a condition of closing, and title insurers have their own requirements around bankruptcy exit transactions. In Eugene, where the local title market is served by a handful of underwriters, the timeline and cost of resolving title issues can be material. My feasibility opinion must account for whether the plan's treatment of liens is sufficient to deliver the clean title a lender requires, and I coordinate my analysis with counsel's representation of how the plan addresses those encumbrances.

Property Condition and Deferred Maintenance. Commercially distressed properties often have deferred maintenance that accumulated during the period of financial stress. Lenders in the post-bankruptcy context will require a property condition assessment, and any identified deferred maintenance will either be required to be remediated prior to closing or will result in holdback reserves that reduce net loan proceeds. For a Eugene property that has been in distress for 18 to 24 months, I typically advise that plan proponents commission a pre-engagement PCA so that the maintenance liability is quantified before the loan sizing analysis is finalized. Surprises on property condition are one of the most common reasons bankruptcy exit financing falls apart between plan confirmation and effective date.

Borrower Entity and Operating History. Lenders making permanent loans require a bankable borrower entity at closing. A Chapter 11 debtor-in-possession is not a bankable entity. The plan must establish or confirm a reorganized entity with demonstrable creditworthiness, and for institutional lenders that means principals with net worth and liquidity meeting their guaranty requirements. For Eugene properties where the plan proponent is a creditor rather than the original borrower, the question is whether the new ownership entity can satisfy lender sponsorship requirements. This is a frequently overlooked issue in plan drafting that I flag explicitly in my feasibility reports.

The Eugene Lender Market

The lender categories actively originating commercial real estate loans in the Eugene market fall into several distinct tiers, each with its own credit box and relevance to a bankruptcy exit scenario.

Agency Lenders (Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac DUS Correspondents). For stabilized multifamily product with 5 or more units, agency execution is typically the most competitive permanent financing source available in Eugene. Agency lenders will price at spreads over the 10-year Treasury that produce all-in rates in the 6.25 to 7.00 percent range in the current environment, with LTVs up to 80 percent and terms from 5 to 30 years. The critical constraint in a bankruptcy context is the post-confirmation timing requirement: agency lenders will not fund until the bankruptcy estate is fully resolved and title is clean. For a Eugene multifamily asset that meets these criteria, agency is the target execution, and my analysis identifies the specific DUS lenders who are active in Oregon secondary markets and have funded Lane County transactions.

CMBS Lenders. Conduit CMBS execution is available for Eugene commercial assets above approximately $5 million, including multifamily, retail, and industrial product. CMBS pricing is typically 20 to 40 basis points wider than agency for multifamily but can be competitive for non-residential product. CMBS lenders are generally more flexible on sponsorship requirements than agencies, which makes them a useful option for bankruptcy exit scenarios where the new borrower entity has limited operating history. The trade-off is that CMBS loans come with cash management triggers, springing cash sweeps, and reserve structures that can complicate plan feasibility analysis.

Community and Regional Banks. Eugene's commercial banking market includes several Oregon-headquartered community and regional institutions that actively originate balance sheet loans on local commercial assets. These lenders know the market, have existing relationships with local appraisers and attorneys, and can move more quickly than institutional lenders. Their credit boxes are tighter on LTV (typically 65 to 70 percent for commercial, 70 to 75 percent for multifamily) but their DSCR requirements are sometimes more flexible at 1.20x. For smaller transactions or assets that do not meet agency eligibility, a community bank relationship lender is often the most practical path to bankruptcy exit financing in Eugene.

Debt Funds and Bridge Lenders. For assets that are not yet stabilized or that face lease-up, condition, or title challenges that preclude permanent financing at plan confirmation, a bridge loan from a private debt fund is sometimes the appropriate interim solution. Bridge lenders active in the Pacific Northwest secondary markets price in the 8.50 to 10.50 percent range with shorter terms (12 to 36 months), interest-only structures, and origination fees of 1 to 2 points. They will lend in Eugene, they are accustomed to post-bankruptcy scenarios, and they can close on tighter timelines than institutional lenders. The trade-off is cost: bridge debt is more expensive and may not generate proceeds sufficient to satisfy senior creditors, which is a feasibility conclusion that must be addressed directly in the expert opinion.

What Attorneys Handling Eugene CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to Retain a CRE Financing Expert. The threshold question is whether financing feasibility is in dispute. In a contested plan confirmation proceeding where feasibility is challenged, counsel should retain a financing expert at the earliest stage of plan development, not as an afterthought when the objection is filed. The expert's analysis should inform the plan structure, not simply defend a plan that has already been drafted without regard to financing realities. I have been retained in matters where the plan was built around a loan amount that the property simply could not support at current rates, which required a significant revision to plan treatment before confirmation was viable. Earlier engagement avoids that problem.

What a Financing Expert Does That an Appraiser Does Not. Appraisers establish value. Financing experts establish lender behavior. The appraiser will tell the court what the property is worth. I will tell the court whether a lender will actually make a loan against that value, at what amount, on what terms, subject to what conditions, and which specific categories of lenders are in the market for this asset type in this location at this risk profile. These are categorically different opinions. A plan that assumes an 80 percent LTV loan because the appraiser found that value exists will fail if the DSCR test at current rates caps the loan at 65 percent LTV. The financing expert catches that gap.

Qualifications That Matter. A credible CRE financing expert is not a retired banker who made loans 15 years ago. The expert needs current, active market knowledge, meaning they are talking to lenders every week, closing transactions in comparable markets, and can speak to what lenders are actually doing today, not what they did in a prior rate environment. At CLS CRE, we close transactions across all 50 states, maintain relationships with more than 1,000 lenders, and have processed approximately $1 billion in transaction volume. That active market presence is what gives our expert opinions credibility under cross-examination.

Information Counsel Should Gather Before the Engagement. Before I can render a financing feasibility opinion, I need the current rent roll and lease abstracts, trailing 12-month operating statements, a recent appraisal or broker opinion of value if one exists, the property condition assessment if available, the plan's proposed treatment of senior secured debt, and the proposed reorganized entity's ownership and financial structure.

Expert Witness: How Construction Defects Affect CRE Financing in Temecula, CA

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Construction Defect Impact on Financing

The Temecula Market Context

Temecula occupies a distinctive position in Southern California's commercial real estate landscape. Situated in southwestern Riverside County at the junction of Interstate 15 and Highway 79, the city has evolved well beyond its wine country identity into a genuine mid-market commercial hub serving a population base that has grown past 115,000 residents while drawing from the broader Southwest Riverside County corridor, which includes Murrieta, Menifee, and Lake Elsinore. That regional draw matters in any financing dispute, because lenders underwriting Temecula assets are evaluating a trade area, not just a city boundary.

For industrial and flex-industrial assets, Temecula sits at the southern tip of the Inland Empire South submarket. Vacancy in that submarket has normalized from the historically tight sub-2% conditions of 2021 and 2022, settling into a range of approximately 5% to 7% as of late 2024, with asking rents for industrial product in the $1.20 to $1.60 per square foot per month NNN range depending on clear height, truck court depth, and proximity to the I-15 interchange. That normalization matters for construction defect litigation because lenders underwriting in this environment are applying more conservative rent growth assumptions than they would have three years ago, which compresses the income side of any DSCR analysis before a single defect is introduced into the picture.

Retail fundamentals along the Promenade Mall corridor and the Winchester Road and Temecula Parkway commercial strips remain relatively stable, anchored by strong household incomes that run above the Riverside County average. Retail vacancy in well-located Temecula strip centers has hovered in the 6% to 9% range, though functionally obsolete inline space carries meaningful lease-up risk that lenders price accordingly. For office, the picture is more complicated: Temecula never built the kind of high-rise office inventory that has plagued downtown Los Angeles or San Diego's UTC submarket, but suburban single-story and two-story office parks in the city face the same hybrid-work headwinds as everywhere else, and lenders treating Temecula office assets are applying 15% to 20% vacancy haircuts in their underwriting regardless of actual occupancy.

Multifamily in Temecula trades at cap rates generally ranging from 4.75% to 5.75% depending on vintage, unit mix, and location, with rents supported by the area's employment base in healthcare, distribution, and the wine and tourism economy. Understanding these market-specific fundamentals is the starting point for any financing analysis, because construction defects do not exist in a vacuum. They interact with, and compound, whatever financing constraints the market is already imposing on the asset class.

The Engagement

Counsel representing the property owner retained Commercial Lending Solutions to provide expert analysis in a construction defect dispute involving a recently completed commercial retail center in Temecula. The asset had been delivered within the prior three years and was operating with a mix of national and regional tenants when alleged structural and waterproofing defects were identified. The litigation centered on claims against the general contractor and several subcontractors, with the property owner asserting that the defects had materially impaired the property's value, its ability to secure permanent financing, and its capacity to attract and retain creditworthy tenants.

Opposing counsel had retained an appraiser to address diminution in value. Our engagement was distinct and complementary: we were retained specifically to address the financing feasibility dimension, which an appraisal does not fully capture. The core questions we were asked to analyze were: first, what financing would have been available to this asset in a defect-free condition, based on its income profile and the lender market conditions prevailing at the relevant time; second, what financing is actually available given the known and alleged defects; and third, how do those two financing outcomes differ in terms of maximum supportable loan proceeds, required equity, lender closing conditions, and transaction cost? A fourth question was whether, and under what conditions, any institutional lender would close a permanent loan on this asset in its current defect-disclosed state.

Our analysis drew on our active lender relationships across more than 1,000 institutions, our approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, and our experience placing commercial loans in markets throughout California and across all 50 states. We also conducted direct market inquiry with lenders active in the Temecula and broader Inland Empire South submarket to confirm current credit appetite and underwriting standards for retail assets with disclosed construction defect litigation.

The Financial Analysis

The subject property was a neighborhood retail center of approximately 42,000 square feet with a stabilized occupancy of roughly 88% at the time of our analysis. Leases were structured on a NNN basis with average rents of approximately $2.10 per square foot per month. Gross potential rent annualized to approximately $1,058,400. After applying a market vacancy and credit loss factor of 12% (which I explain below), effective gross income came to approximately $931,392. Operating expenses on a NNN structure were minimal at the landlord level, primarily property management, insurance, and reserves. I applied a fully loaded NOI estimate of approximately $870,000 after a management fee of 4% of EGI and a capital reserve of $0.15 per square foot.

For a stabilized, defect-free neighborhood retail center of this type in the Inland Empire South submarket, a conventional bank or life company lender would typically apply a minimum debt service coverage ratio of 1.25x. That is the standard threshold for this product type at this quality tier. CMBS lenders often underwrite to 1.20x for anchored or shadow-anchored retail, but for unanchored neighborhood centers, 1.25x is the appropriate benchmark. Using a 30-year amortization and an interest rate assumption of 7.00% (reflective of the rate environment relevant to our analysis period), the annual mortgage constant on a fully amortizing loan is approximately 7.98%.

At 1.25x DSCR and a 7.98% mortgage constant, the maximum supportable loan on $870,000 of NOI is calculated as follows: $870,000 divided by 1.25 equals $696,000 of maximum allowable annual debt service. $696,000 divided by 0.0798 equals a maximum loan of approximately $8,721,000. For a stabilized, defect-free asset of this type in this submarket, lenders would also apply an LTV constraint. Conventional bank lenders cap retail at 65% LTV; life companies and CMBS at 65% to 70% LTV for qualifying assets. At a hypothetical appraised value of $14,500,000 for a defect-free asset (consistent with a 6.0% cap rate applied to an $870,000 NOI), a 65% LTV cap yields a maximum loan of $9,425,000. The binding constraint in this scenario is the DSCR test, producing a maximum supportable loan of approximately $8,721,000 at 60.1% LTV.

Now introduce the defects. The alleged waterproofing and structural issues at the subject property produced two quantifiable financing impacts. First, two tenants representing approximately 18% of gross leasable area had either terminated leases early citing habitability concerns or were in active lease disputes with the landlord. That occupancy disruption reduced effective economic occupancy from 88% to effectively 70%, dropping stabilized NOI to approximately $640,000 once the vacancy and associated credit loss was properly underwritten. Second, lenders receiving disclosure of pending construction defect litigation apply an additional vacancy haircut of 5% to 10% to reflect lease-up risk during litigation, and several lenders we surveyed declined to quote the asset entirely pending resolution of the litigation. Applying a 10% additional haircut to the already-reduced income stream brought underwritable NOI to approximately $576,000.

Recalculating on the defect-impacted NOI: $576,000 divided by 1.25 equals $460,800 of maximum annual debt service. $460,800 divided by 0.0798 equals a maximum supportable loan of approximately $5,773,000. That is a reduction of approximately $2,948,000 in maximum supportable debt compared to the defect-free scenario, representing a financing gap of roughly 34%. Additionally, the loan is now constrained by LTV concerns because the defect-impacted appraised value would be lower, and several lenders applied a further haircut on LTV for litigation-encumbered assets, capping exposure at 55% to 60% LTV rather than the standard 65%.

The Complicating Factors

Pending Litigation as a Lender Disqualifier. The most immediate and pervasive complication is the existence of active construction defect litigation. Most institutional lenders, including banks, credit unions, life companies, and CMBS conduits, will not fund a permanent loan on a property with unresolved construction defect claims as a named party. This is not a pricing issue. It is a hard eligibility screen. Lenders view unresolved litigation as a cloud on title, an uncertain liability, and a signal that the property's physical condition is not underwritten. The result is that the conventional lending universe, which would otherwise represent the lowest-cost, highest-proceeds options for this asset type, is effectively closed until the litigation resolves or is settled with a court-approved release. I confirmed this directly with multiple lenders active in Temecula and the broader Riverside County market during our engagement.

Tenant Attrition and Lease Integrity. Construction defects in a retail center create a compounding problem: if tenants cite the defects as a basis for lease termination or rent abatement, the income stream itself becomes legally contested. Lenders underwriting NNN retail rely on the lease as the fundamental credit document. When a lease is in dispute, the rent it represents cannot be included in the underwritten income. In our engagement, the contested leases represented nearly one-fifth of the center's income, and the uncertainty around whether those leases would survive litigation created a scenario where no conventional lender would credit that income stream at face value.

Reserve Requirements and Holdback Structures. For the subset of lenders, primarily bridge lenders and hard money sources, willing to engage on a litigation-encumbered asset, the typical credit accommodation is not a clean loan but a holdback structure. The lender funds a reduced advance at closing and holds back a portion of the loan proceeds in a repair reserve, releasing funds in tranches as defects are remediated and verified by an independent engineer. This structure increases transaction costs, reduces net proceeds to the borrower at closing, and introduces ongoing lender oversight that most institutional borrowers find operationally burdensome.

Increased Pricing and Shortened Terms. Bridge and private lenders willing to engage on defect-disclosed retail assets in markets like Temecula are pricing at spreads of 300 to 500 basis points over comparable conventional product, with loan terms typically limited to 12 to 24 months rather than the 5 to 10 year terms available in the conventional market. Personal recourse guarantees are standard. Prepayment is typically open or lightly structured, which reflects the lender's expectation that the borrower will refinance to conventional financing once litigation resolves.

The Temecula Lender Market

For stabilized, defect-free retail assets in Temecula, the active lender universe is reasonably deep. Community banks and regional banks with California charters are the most common lenders for retail centers in the $5 million to $20 million range, including institutions headquartered in the Inland Empire, San Diego, and Orange County markets that view Temecula as within their geographic credit box. These lenders typically price at 30 to 60 basis points over comparable term treasuries or the 5-year FHLB rate, with a 1.25x DSCR floor and a 65% LTV cap. Relationship banking matters in this market; borrowers with deposit relationships or cross-sell potential have meaningfully better access to credit than transactional borrowers.

Life company lenders and CMBS conduits are active in Temecula for retail assets that meet minimum loan size thresholds, typically $5 million and above for life companies and $2 million and above for CMBS. Life companies are the most conservative underwriters but offer the longest fixed-rate terms, often 10 to 25 years, and the lowest coupons for qualifying assets. CMBS offers higher leverage for assets that clear the property and credit quality screen, but the securitization process creates inflexibility around subsequent modifications, which is highly relevant when a property has outstanding defect claims that may require future remediation agreements.

Debt funds and bridge lenders operate in Temecula and the broader Southwest Riverside County market, typically through correspondent relationships with Los Angeles and San Diego-based originators. These lenders are the realistic financing option for a property with known defects and pending litigation, but their pricing reflects the risk. For the engagement described here, the realistic loan options in the defect-disclosed, litigation-active state of the asset were limited to two or three bridge lenders willing to engage at rates in the 10% to 12% range on a 12-month term with extension options, at loan-to-value ratios of 55% or below.

What Attorneys Handling Temecula CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to retain a CRE financing expert. If your client is asserting that a construction defect, title defect, environmental condition, or lease dispute has impaired their ability to finance or refinance a commercial property, a financing expert is essential, not optional. An appraiser establishes value. A financing expert establishes what capital markets will actually do with that value in the real world. Those are not the same analysis. A property can have a defensible appraised value and still be functionally unfinanceable at conventional terms due to disclosed litigation, and that distinction is worth hundreds of thousands or millions of dollars in quantifiable damages.

What a financing expert can establish that an appraiser cannot. A CRE financing expert can testify to: the specific lenders who would and would not engage on this asset under both the defect-free and defect-disclosed scenarios; the specific DSCR and LTV thresholds applied by each lender category; the quantified reduction in maximum loan proceeds; the increased cost of capital in basis points attributable to the defect disclosure; the loan terms forgone (fixed-rate, non-recourse, long amortization) as a result of the financing impairment; and the economic cost of carrying a bridge loan rather than permanent financing during the period of litigation. These are financing market conclusions, not appraisal conclusions, and they require a different expert.

What qualifies a CRE financing expert. Active engagement in the market is the threshold qualification. A retired banker or former lender who left the business five years ago does not know what today's credit boxes look like, what lenders are actually applying in their underwriting, or which institutions are active in a specific submarket. An expert who has originated or brokered transactions in comparable markets in the current rate environment, who has direct and documentable lender relationships, and who can speak to specific lender requirements from current experience, not historical memory, carries significantly more weight with a sophisticated trier of fact.

Information counsel should gather before the engagement. To maximize the efficiency and credibility of a financing expert's analysis, counsel should collect: current and historical rent rolls with lease commencement and expiration dates; all executed leases and any notices of default, termination, or rent abatement related to the alleged defects; trailing 12 and trailing 24 month operating statements; any existing appraisals or broker opinions of value; any prior loan documents, term sheets, or financing correspondence showing what financing was obtained or sought; and any engineer or contractor reports describing the nature, scope, and estimated remediation cost of the alleged defects. The defect remediation cost estimate is particularly important because lenders apply it directly in their underwriting as a reduction to net value.

Use of financing expert testimony. At trial, financing expert testimony is typically offered to establish the economic consequence of the impairment in financing terms: the delta between what the property could have financed in its defect-free state versus what it can finance in its actual state, quantified in loan proceeds, interest cost, and term. In settlement negotiations, a well-documented financing expert report often drives resolution because it translates a technical defect claim into a concrete dollar figure that both sides can evaluate. In arbitration proceedings involving construction contract disputes, the financing impact analysis provides a damages quantification framework that goes beyond repair cost to capture consequential economic harm.

Key Takeaways

  • Construction defects create a two-layer financing impairment: They reduce the income stream through tenant disruption and they trigger lender eligibility screens that eliminate most conventional lenders from the financing universe entirely until litigation resolves.

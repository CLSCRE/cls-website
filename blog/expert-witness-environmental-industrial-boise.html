Expert Witness: Financing a Contaminated Industrial Property in Boise, ID

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Environmental Contamination Analysis

The Boise Market Context

Boise has quietly become one of the more consequential secondary industrial markets in the Mountain West, and attorneys litigating commercial real estate disputes here should understand the market's structural characteristics before assessing how a financing dispute will play out. The Treasure Valley industrial submarket has absorbed significant demand from logistics operators, light manufacturing users, and food processing tenants over the past several years, driven by population growth across Ada and Canyon Counties that consistently ranks among the fastest in the nation. That demographic engine creates real occupier demand, not speculative demand, which matters when you are trying to establish whether a troubled asset has financeable fundamentals beneath its legal complications.

Industrial vacancy in the Boise metropolitan area has tightened considerably from pre-pandemic norms. As of the most recent market data available at time of engagement, overall industrial vacancy in the Treasure Valley was tracking in the 4% to 6% range across the broader market, with functional, well-located warehouse and distribution product in established Southwest Boise and Nampa industrial corridors posting vacancy below that average. Net asking rents for Class B industrial space, which is the relevant comparison tier for older NNN-leased warehouse product, have moved from the low to mid $0.50s per square foot NNN per month in 2020 to a range of $0.65 to $0.80 per square foot NNN per month in established corridors, with newer Class A product commanding rents north of $0.90. This rent growth creates a meaningful spread between in-place below-market rents on older leases and current market, which has direct implications for NOI underwriting and for how a lender values the tenancy.

The Boise market also has a relatively shallow institutional lender bench compared to gateway markets, which means that deal complexity, including environmental impairment, compresses the available financing universe more sharply here than it would in Los Angeles or Denver. Local and regional bank relationships carry real weight, and the presence of contamination can effectively remove the most liquid and competitive lender categories from the table entirely. Understanding this market-specific dynamic is not optional when rendering an expert opinion on financing feasibility in this jurisdiction.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by counsel representing a secured creditor group in a regulatory enforcement proceeding involving an industrial warehouse property in the Boise metropolitan area. The asset was a single-tenant, net-leased warehouse facility of approximately 80,000 to 100,000 rentable square feet, situated on an industrial parcel in an established submarket. The property had been used for light industrial or manufacturing purposes by prior owners, and a state regulatory agency had made a formal determination that the site soil and groundwater were impacted by chlorinated volatile organic compounds, specifically 1,4-dioxane and associated chlorinated solvents consistent with historical industrial solvent use.

The current ownership had acquired the property with the existing NNN lease in place. That lease was below current market rent, having been originated in a prior rent environment and structured with modest annual escalators that had not kept pace with the Treasure Valley's rent growth trajectory. The tenant remained in occupancy and was current on rent obligations, which preserved the income stream but did not resolve the environmental liability or the lender universe problem.

Counsel retained CLS CRE to opine on two discrete questions: first, whether first-mortgage financing in any form was available in the current market on a property with this environmental profile; and second, if financing was available, what the maximum supportable loan amount would be under current market underwriting standards. The answers to those questions had direct bearing on whether the existing senior debt could be retired through a refinance and on how the secured creditors should evaluate competing plans for the asset.

The Financial Analysis

The income analysis began with the in-place lease, which was the only contractually certain revenue stream. For a 90,000 square foot NNN warehouse property with an in-place rent of approximately $0.62 per square foot per month, gross annual base rent works out to approximately $669,600. Under a true NNN lease structure, the tenant is responsible for real estate taxes, insurance, and maintenance, which means the landlord's direct operating expense load is limited to non-reimbursable items: property management, any structural reserves, and ownership-level administrative costs. Using a conservative 5% management fee on collected rents and a $0.05 per square foot annual structural reserve, non-reimbursable expenses total approximately $38,980, producing an effective NOI of approximately $630,620.

The critical underwriting adjustment for lenders in a contaminated site context is the environmental reserve or remediation cost haircut. Lenders who will consider contaminated properties at all, which is a narrow universe as discussed below, will not underwrite to full NOI without accounting for the regulatory liability. In this engagement, the Phase II Environmental Site Assessment and the regulatory agency's remedial action plan identified estimated remediation costs in a range of $800,000 to $1,400,000 depending on remedial technology selected, with an ongoing monitoring and compliance cost burden of approximately $25,000 to $40,000 annually during the active remediation period. Lenders with appetite for contaminated collateral will typically impound a remediation reserve at closing, funded either by the borrower or carved out of loan proceeds, and will include the annual monitoring cost as an above-the-line NOI deduction. Applying the $35,000 annual monitoring cost deduction reduces the lender-adjusted NOI to approximately $595,620.

For debt service coverage ratio underwriting, the market standard for industrial properties in secondary markets like Boise is 1.25x on a non-recourse permanent loan basis, with some recourse lenders accepting 1.20x. Environmental complexity pushes lenders toward the more conservative end. Using a 1.25x DSCR threshold, the maximum annual debt service supportable against the adjusted NOI of $595,620 is $476,496. At a 30-year amortization schedule, which is standard for NNN industrial permanent debt, and using a note rate of 7.25% reflecting the current rate environment for secondary market industrial with credit complexity, the monthly debt service constant for a 30-year self-amortizing loan at 7.25% is approximately 0.6823% of loan balance per month, or 8.188% annually. Dividing the maximum annual debt service of $476,496 by the annual constant of 8.188% produces a maximum supportable loan of approximately $5,819,000 based purely on income.

The LTV constraint then becomes the binding test. For contaminated industrial collateral, lenders who will engage at all will not exceed 55% to 65% LTV on as-is appraised value, and the as-is value must account for the environmental impairment. Assuming a market-informed capitalization rate of 6.75% to 7.25% applied to the unimpaired NOI of $630,620, the unimpaired value range is approximately $8,699,000 to $9,342,000. The contamination discount, which reflects both the remediation cost liability and the market perception discount above and beyond hard remediation costs, typically reduces industrial value by 15% to 30% in markets like Boise where there is limited comparable transaction data for impaired sites. Applying a 20% environmental discount, the impaired as-is value range is approximately $6,959,000 to $7,474,000. At 60% LTV against the midpoint impaired value of $7,217,000, the LTV-constrained maximum loan is approximately $4,330,000.

The LTV test is the binding constraint here, not the DSCR test. The maximum supportable loan for retirement of senior debt is in the range of $4.3 million to $4.5 million under current market underwriting standards, subject to the environmental structure described below being achievable. Whether that figure is sufficient to retire existing senior debt is a factual question determined by the outstanding loan balance and any accrued fees and default interest, which is precisely why counsel needed this analysis as a foundation for evaluating competing settlement and plan proposals.

The Complicating Factors

1,4-Dioxane and chlorinated VOC contamination as a lender disqualifier. 1,4-dioxane is a synthetic industrial solvent stabilizer that is not a RCRA-listed hazardous waste but is classified as a likely human carcinogen by the EPA. It is highly mobile in groundwater, resistant to natural attenuation, and its regulatory status has been evolving, with the EPA moving toward lower maximum contaminant levels under the Safe Drinking Water Act. Chlorinated solvents including PCE and TCE are well-understood by environmental regulators and lenders, but their presence as co-contaminants with 1,4-dioxane signals a more complex and potentially more expensive remediation picture. Most conventional lenders, including CMBS originators, Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, life insurance companies, and federally regulated banks operating under their standard credit policies, will not originate first-mortgage loans on properties with active, unresolved regulatory orders involving these compound classes. This is not a negotiating position; it is a credit policy floor that does not flex regardless of borrower strength or property quality.

The below-market rent problem. When a lender who is willing to consider the property at all runs the income analysis, the below-market rent creates a double compression. First, the in-place NOI is lower than the market-supportable NOI, which reduces the debt service capacity of the property today. Second, the below-market rent means that the lender cannot credit the upside rent to current loan sizing: they must underwrite to in-place income only. The remaining lease term matters enormously here. If the lease has fewer than five years remaining, many lenders will apply a further haircut to reflect rollover risk and the possibility that a credit-sensitive tenant may choose not to renew on a contaminated site. Even a tenant that is contractually obligated under a NNN lease will have renewal optionality, and a sophisticated tenant's decision to exercise or not exercise renewal options on an environmentally impaired property is not predictable.

Regulatory uncertainty and timeline. Idaho Department of Environmental Quality oversight of sites with chlorinated VOC groundwater impacts typically involves a phased remedial investigation and feasibility study process before a final remedial action plan is approved. Until IDEQ issues a No Further Action determination or a conditional closure with ongoing monitoring, the remediation liability is an open-ended obligation. Lenders will not close a loan without some form of regulatory certainty, whether that is an approved remedial action plan with a quantified cost estimate and a regulatory schedule, an environmental insurance policy, or a funded escrow. In a contested enforcement proceeding, achieving the regulatory certainty that lenders require may itself be years away, which is a financing feasibility issue that counsel must understand independently of the property's underlying income quality.

Environmental insurance availability. Pollution legal liability insurance and cost-cap insurance can partially bridge the gap between a contaminated site and lender requirements. However, for 1,4-dioxane sites, the insurance market has tightened. Underwriters who will consider this compound class require detailed Phase II data, a credible remedial action plan, and typically a minimum deductible of $250,000 or more. Premium costs for a site in this profile can run $50,000 to $150,000 annually, which is an additional NOI drag not reflected in the base analysis. Some lenders will require that the policy name them as an additional insured as a condition of loan closing.

The Boise Lender Market

The Boise industrial lending market for clean, conventional assets is served by a reasonable spread of capital sources including regional banks headquartered in the Pacific Northwest and Mountain West, community banks with strong Ada and Canyon County franchise presence, credit unions active in commercial real estate, and national CMBS originators who will originate larger stabilized assets for securitization. For a contaminated site, however, the accessible lender universe contracts to a much smaller group.

Community and regional banks with local relationship orientation represent the most realistic first-mortgage source for a contaminated but income-producing industrial asset in Boise. These institutions have credit officers who can evaluate a site on its specific merits, structure around an environmental escrow, and price a risk premium rather than simply declining. They will require recourse, typically full personal or corporate recourse during the remediation period with a structured burn-off to limited recourse upon NFA. Their LTV constraints are 55% to 60% on impaired collateral, and their rates will carry a 100 to 150 basis point premium over conventional industrial pricing, putting them in a 7.50% to 8.25% range in the current environment.

Specialty environmental lenders and hard-money bridge lenders active in the Mountain West will provide short-term acquisition or refinance capital on impaired sites, but at bridge pricing of 10% to 12% with origination fees of 2 to 3 points. These structures are appropriate for a borrower who needs to retire maturing debt while remediation is in progress and expects to refinance to conventional terms upon closure. Bridge loan sizing will be even more conservative than the community bank analysis above, typically 50% to 55% LTV on impaired value.

Debt funds and private credit platforms with dedicated commercial real estate strategies occasionally engage on environmentally complex assets, but they evaluate the exit more than the entry: they want to understand the path to a clean exit and a conventional refinance within their hold period. For a Boise asset of this scale, the loan size may be below the minimum threshold for most institutional debt fund platforms, which typically have minimums in the $10 million to $15 million range.

What Attorneys Handling Boise CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to retain a CRE financing expert. An attorney handling a secured creditor enforcement proceeding, a bankruptcy plan confirmation dispute, a real property fraud or lender liability case, or a partnership dissolution in Boise should consider retaining a CRE financing expert any time the central factual question involves whether a specific property could have been financed at a specific loan amount, at a specific point in time, or under specific market conditions. Appraisers determine value. CRE financing experts determine whether and how value can be leveraged into debt capital given market conditions, property-specific risk factors, and lender credit requirements. These are related but distinct questions, and conflating them is a common analytical error in commercial real estate litigation.

Questions a financing expert can answer that an appraiser cannot. An appraiser will tell you what the property is worth. A CRE financing expert will tell you whether a loan large enough to retire existing debt was achievable at origination or is achievable today; what underwriting standards a lender should have applied; whether a specific loan was improperly originated based on market standards at the time; what the realistic universe of available lenders was for a given asset type, deal size, and borrower profile in a given market; and how environmental impairment specifically affects the financing market, not just the value. These questions require active market knowledge, lender relationships, and ongoing participation in commercial loan originations, not valuation methodology.

What qualifies a CRE financing expert. A qualified CRE financing expert should be actively engaged in originating or brokering commercial loans in the current market, with a demonstrated transaction volume that reflects real market engagement. Retired bankers or academics whose market knowledge is dated by years of non-origination activity cannot reliably describe current lender credit box parameters, current pricing, or current market appetite for specific deal types. CLS CRE has approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, active relationships with more than 1,000 lenders, and current origination activity across 50 states, including the Mountain West. That active market presence is what allows us to provide credible, current testimony about what a lender would or would not do in a specific market at a specific time.

Information counsel should gather before the expert engagement. To render a supportable opinion on financing feasibility for a Boise industrial property with environmental impairment, counsel should obtain: the Phase II ESA and any regulatory correspondence or remedial action plans; the complete lease file including rent roll, lease abstract, and any amendments; the most recent rent comps or broker opinion of value for the submarket; the existing loan documents including the original appraisal, underwriting package, and any default or enforcement notices; and the regulatory agency file on the enforcement matter including any approved or proposed remedial action timelines and cost estimates.

Use of financing expert testimony. In a bankruptcy plan confirmation hearing, a financing expert opinion establishes whether the plan is feasible by quantifying the debt the property can support. In a lender liability case, the expert establishes whether the original loan was made consistent with market standards or whether the lender deviated from reasonable underwriting practices. In a secured creditor enforcement proceeding, the expert establishes the realistic range of debt retirement proceeds available through a refinance, which directly affects the creditor's recovery analysis and negotiating posture.

Expert Witness: NNN Lease Rollover Upside and Financing Impact in Asheville, NC

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Below-Market NNN Lease and Rollover Upside Analysis

The Asheville Market Context

Asheville, North Carolina has evolved from a regional arts-and-tourism economy into a legitimate commercial real estate market that attracts institutional capital, regional operators, and sophisticated private equity. The metro's population growth trajectory, driven by in-migration from higher-cost markets in the Northeast and Southeast, has translated into measurable demand across multiple asset classes. For single-tenant net lease properties specifically, Asheville presents a nuanced underwriting environment that sits at the intersection of a maturing secondary market and a constrained supply pipeline rooted in topographic and regulatory limitations on development.

Retail vacancy in the Asheville MSA has remained relatively tight compared to peer secondary markets, generally running in the 4 to 7 percent range across well-located corridors, though the bifurcation between Class A strip and destination retail versus aging inline space is pronounced. The South Asheville and Biltmore Avenue corridors have seen sustained leasing activity and rent growth, while secondary corridors face more pressure. For single-tenant NNN assets, the critical variable is not market vacancy in the aggregate but rather the submarket-specific rent-to-market gap for the specific use, which directly determines how a lender sizes a loan when in-place rents are below current market.

Industrial is the strongest performer in the Asheville market on a fundamentals basis. Vacancy has compressed into the low single digits in functional product, and asking rents have moved meaningfully over the past three years. The supply constraint here is real: available industrial land in the Asheville area is limited by geography and infrastructure, and new development has not kept pace with absorption. This matters for NNN lease analysis because below-market industrial rents in Asheville represent a genuinely significant upside opportunity, one that the market can credibly support but that lenders will price and underwrite conservatively until that upside is actually captured in a new lease.

Office fundamentals in the Asheville MSA are more complicated. Smaller professional and medical office has held up reasonably well given the healthcare sector's importance to the local economy, anchored by Mission Health and its affiliated facilities. General office, however, faces the same hybrid-work headwinds visible nationally, and lenders are applying heightened scrutiny to office NNN deals regardless of lease term. For any litigation involving a single-tenant office NNN property in Asheville, the financing analysis must account for the reality that the lender universe for that asset type has contracted materially.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing one of the parties in a commercial real estate dispute involving a single-tenant NNN-leased retail property located in a primary commercial corridor in the Asheville metropolitan area. The property was occupied by a single national-credit tenant under a long-term net lease that had been originated several years prior to the dispute. The core issue was that the in-place rent was materially below current market rent for comparable space in the submarket, and the parties disagreed about what that below-market rent structure meant for the property's financing capacity at the time in question.

Counsel needed analysis addressing several specific financial questions that sat outside the traditional scope of a real estate appraisal. First, how would a lender actually underwrite the property given the below-market in-place rent, as opposed to how an appraiser might value the fee simple interest? Second, does the existence of rollover upside increase loan proceeds available to a borrower, and if so, under what conditions and for which lender categories? Third, was refinancing at specific LTV and DSCR thresholds achievable for this property at the relevant point in time, given its lease structure? Fourth, what was the actual financing universe for this asset type in the Asheville market, and which lender categories would or would not engage with the deal?

CLS CRE was engaged to produce a financing feasibility analysis and expert report addressing each of these questions with specific reference to lender underwriting standards, capital market conditions, and the mechanics of how debt sizing is performed for NNN leased assets with below-market rents and near-to-intermediate term lease rollover.

The Financial Analysis

The analytical starting point for any NNN lease financing is the in-place net operating income. In this type of engagement, the in-place NOI is what drives the initial loan sizing, not the potential NOI achievable after lease rollover. Lenders underwrite to the income that is contractually in place and legally enforceable. Speculative future income, including rent achievable upon re-leasing at market, is not bankable income under any institutional underwriting framework until it is either contractually committed or the subject of specific reserve and earnout structures.

For illustrative purposes using figures representative of this deal type and market, consider a single-tenant NNN retail property in the Asheville market with approximately 8,500 square feet of leasable area. Assume an in-place NNN rent of $18.00 per square foot annually, generating gross in-place rent of $153,000. Under a true NNN structure, with the tenant responsible for taxes, insurance, and maintenance, the in-place NOI is approximately equal to the gross rent, call it $148,500 after a modest management fee or credit for non-recourse vacancy risk. This is the income a lender uses to size the loan.

At a market debt service coverage ratio requirement of 1.25x, which is the standard threshold for most conventional commercial lenders and agency-adjacent programs on retail NNN assets, the maximum annual debt service this NOI can support is $148,500 divided by 1.25, or $118,800 per year. Applying a 30-year amortization schedule and a note rate of 6.75 percent, which is representative of fixed-rate commercial loan pricing for this product type in the relevant period, $118,800 in annual debt service supports a loan of approximately $1,615,000. If the property's appraised value at that time was $2,400,000, this loan represents a 67 percent loan-to-value ratio, which is within conventional commercial lending parameters but provides no LTV headroom at the standard 65 to 70 percent maximum for this asset class.

Now consider that comparable market rents for the same use and submarket are $24.00 per square foot, representing a 33 percent below-market gap. At market rent, the same property would generate NOI of approximately $198,000. Running the same underwriting math at 1.25x DSCR and 6.75 percent over 30 years, the market-rent NOI supports a loan of approximately $2,150,000. This is the rollover upside number that creates financing capacity disagreement in litigation. The spread between the in-place loan amount and the market-rent loan amount is approximately $535,000 in this illustration. Whether a borrower can access any portion of that spread in the current financing environment is the central technical question.

The answer is that most lenders will not underwrite to speculative future rent. However, a subset of bridge lenders and certain life company programs with value-add mandates will size a loan against a blended NOI that weights the in-place rent for the remaining lease term and applies a stabilized assumption post-rollover. This blended approach typically results in a loan amount between the two endpoints, often closer to the in-place figure when the remaining lease term is short and the rollover risk is real. The analysis must also account for the fact that if a tenant vacates at rollover, the lender assumes a lease-up period during which no rent is collected, and that absorption risk in Asheville for the specific use must be quantified using actual market data.

The Complicating Factors

Below-Market Rent as a Lender Red Flag

Lenders do not view below-market rent as a neutral feature. A significant below-market gap signals two risks simultaneously: first, the borrower's income is lower than market, constraining debt service capacity; second, the tenant may have negotiated favorable rent at lease origination in exchange for a longer term commitment, raising questions about credit quality or market positioning that are not always visible in the lease document itself. A sophisticated lender's credit team will ask why the rent is below market and whether the tenant would renew at market if the below-market advantage disappears at rollover. This is a genuine underwriting concern, not a formality.

Rollover Risk and Lease Term Remaining

The financing impact of lease rollover risk is asymmetric. A long remaining lease term with a creditworthy tenant at below-market rent is actually a more financeable position than a short remaining term, even at market rent, because lenders price certainty of income above current yield. When the remaining term is short, below 5 years on a term loan or below the loan term on a longer structure, lenders either decline, require a lease execution as a condition of funding, or price a significant risk premium into the rate and require a cash flow sweep or rollover reserve. In Asheville, where the tenant universe for re-leasing vacant single-tenant retail space is real but not deep in all submarkets, rollover risk is not abstract.

Dark Value and Lender Floor

Every NNN lender underwrites to what is sometimes called the dark value, meaning the value of the property if the tenant vacates and the building sits empty. For single-tenant assets, this is often the land value plus a discount for functional obsolescence of a purpose-built space. In Asheville, where land values in prime corridors are not trivial but development costs are elevated, the dark value floor provides some protection but rarely supports the same LTV as a going-concern underwrite. When in-place rent is below market and the dark value is applied, the resulting maximum loan can be materially lower than either the in-place or market-rent underwrite, and this floor becomes the binding constraint for conservative lenders.

Lease Structure and Lender Assignability Analysis

Lenders analyze lease documents in detail, and complicating provisions in NNN leases directly affect financing eligibility. Co-tenancy clauses, early termination options, relocation rights, or ROFO provisions can reduce a lender's collateral confidence even when the tenant is creditworthy and the rent is secure. In Asheville retail specifically, leases that were originated during periods of strong tenant leverage may contain provisions that create contingent income risk that appraisers may not fully capture but that lenders will identify and price accordingly.

The Asheville Lender Market

For single-tenant NNN retail assets in Asheville, the active lender universe is meaningfully stratified by deal complexity and lease quality. Understanding which category will engage with a given deal structure is not a secondary consideration; it is often the determinative factor in whether financing is achievable at all.

Community and regional banks represent the most active lender category for Asheville commercial real estate generally. Institutions based in Western North Carolina and across the state maintain active commercial real estate lending programs and understand the local market. For NNN deals, these lenders are often the most pragmatic about below-market rents when the borrower relationship is established and the loan is recourse. Their credit boxes typically require 1.20x to 1.25x DSCR on in-place income, 65 to 75 percent LTV, and personal guarantees on most deals below $5 million. They are less likely than institutional lenders to engage with structured earnout or holdback programs based on rollover upside.

CMBS conduit lenders will look at Asheville single-tenant NNN deals when the asset and tenancy meet program minimums, typically a loan of $2 million or greater with an investment-grade or shadow-rated tenant. CMBS underwriting is formulaic on DSCR and LTV and does not deviate for rollover upside without specific structural protections. The non-recourse feature of CMBS is attractive to sponsors, but the prepayment structure, yield maintenance or defeasance, is a meaningful economic constraint that affects refinancing economics in dispute contexts.

Life insurance companies are selective in Asheville but active for the right deal. They favor longer lease terms, investment-grade tenancy, and lower leverage, typically 55 to 65 percent LTV. They will consider below-market rent situations where the remaining lease term is long because their hold period aligns with the lease term. Life company pricing is generally the most competitive available, but their credit box excludes most shorter-term or speculative rollover situations.

Debt funds and bridge lenders are the appropriate capital source when the in-place rent does not support conventional debt service or when the loan purpose is specifically to carry the asset through a rollover event and re-lease. These lenders will underwrite to a stabilized value and structure interest reserves and rollover reserves into the loan. Pricing is floating rate and materially higher, typically Secured Overnight Financing Rate plus 300 to 500 basis points depending on leverage and deal complexity. For Asheville assets, bridge execution is available but requires a clear and credible business plan for the rollover.

What Attorneys Handling Asheville CRE Disputes Need to Know

A certified appraiser and a commercial real estate financing expert are not interchangeable witnesses. An appraiser determines market value under specific valuation methodologies, typically the income approach, sales comparison, and cost approach. A CRE financing expert determines what a lender would actually do with a specific asset at a specific point in time, which is a distinct and technical question that requires active knowledge of capital markets, lender underwriting standards, and deal execution experience. In disputes where financing feasibility, loan proceeds, or refinancing capacity are at issue, an appraiser's opinion of value does not answer the question counsel needs answered.

Attorneys should consider retaining a CRE financing expert when the dispute involves any of the following: allegations that a borrower could or could not have refinanced under specific conditions; disputes over whether a loan was properly structured or sized; claims that a party failed to secure financing that was available in the market; analysis of whether a lender's underwriting decision was consistent with market standards; or any scenario where the debt capacity of a specific asset at a specific time is a contested element of damages, valuation, or feasibility.

A qualified CRE financing expert should have active, ongoing transactional experience in commercial real estate debt markets, not historical experience that ended with a banking career. Lender standards, pricing, and credit boxes change materially across market cycles, and an expert whose experience is dated cannot reliably opine on conditions at a specific relevant period. At CLS CRE, our expert testimony is grounded in approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, more than 1,000 active lender relationships, and engagement across all 50 states. That transactional currency is what separates a financing expert from a retired banker offering general impressions.

Before engaging a financing expert, counsel should gather: the complete lease document including all amendments and estoppels; the most recent rent roll and operating statements; any prior loan documents, appraisals, or lender correspondence; the title report and any recorded encumbrances; and any broker opinions of value or financing proposals received by the parties. The earlier in the litigation this material is assembled, the more precise the expert analysis will be.

At trial or in a hearing context, CRE financing expert testimony is most effective when it is grounded in specific lender underwriting standards that can be corroborated by market evidence, when it translates technical capital markets concepts into clear conclusions about what was or was not achievable, and when it directly addresses the factual predicate of each side's position on financing feasibility. The expert's role is not to advocate for a party's preferred outcome but to educate the finder of fact on how lenders actually think and act, which is often the most persuasive testimony available in a financing dispute.

Key Takeaways

  • In-place NOI drives loan sizing, not rollover upside. Institutional lenders underwrite to contractual, in-place income. Rollover upside is not bankable until it is captured in a signed lease or structured through specific holdback and earnout mechanisms.
  • Below-market rent creates a financing gap that can be quantified precisely. The spread between in-place and market-rent loan amounts is calculable using standard DSCR, rate, and amortization assumptions, and that spread is often the core of the damages or feasibility dispute.
  • The Asheville lender market is stratified and asset-specific. Community banks, CMBS, life companies, and bridge funds each have distinct credit boxes for NNN assets, and not every lender category will engage with below-market or rollover-risk situations. Knowing which category applies to a given deal is essential to a credible financing opinion.
  • Dark value is the binding constraint for conservative lenders. When in-place rent is below market and rollover risk is near-term, lenders apply a dark value floor that can produce a lower maximum loan than either the in-place or market-rent underwrite.
  • Lease structure matters as much as lease economics. Co-tenancy clauses, termination options, and assignment restrictions affect lender collateral confidence and can independently constrain financing capacity, independent of the rent level.

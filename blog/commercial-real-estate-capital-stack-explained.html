The Commercial Real Estate Capital Stack: Layers, a Worked Example and When to Use Each

Trevor Damyan Founder & Principal Broker
CA DRE #02244836

Former CBRE and Marcus & Millichap Capital Corporation. $1B+ in closed commercial real estate financing across bridge, construction, permanent, and structured debt.

November 26, 2026
Quick answer: The capital stack is every source of money behind a property, ranked by who is repaid first: senior debt, then mezzanine debt, then preferred equity, then common equity. Each layer up the stack takes more risk and charges more, so the job is to add only the layers that earn their cost.

Every commercial property is paid for by a mix of money, and each source of that money has a different place in line when the property produces cash or is sold. That ordering is the capital stack. Understanding it tells you how much a deal can borrow, what the extra leverage costs, and who controls the property if something goes wrong.

We keep two kinds of statements apart. General guidance describes how the market usually works. Firm specific details describe what Commercial Lending Solutions (CLS CRE), a Los Angeles commercial mortgage brokerage, arranges, and those are ranges from our own transactions, not offers. Any real terms come from a lender's written term sheet.

Two points trip people up. First, the layers are paid in order and each is made whole before the next receives anything, which is why a layer lower in the stack can accept a lower return. Second, mezzanine debt and preferred equity are not interchangeable: one is a loan with a maturity and the other is an ownership position with control triggers. Our mezzanine versus preferred equity comparison sets them side by side.

What the example shows:

  • The extra layer frees equity. Moving from A to B cuts the sponsor's check from $25 million to $15 million, a $10 million reduction, for a layer that costs $0.8 million to $1.4 million a year before fees.
  • A lower senior advance raises the bill. In column C the senior lender stops at 65 percent, so the gap above it doubles to $20 million and the cost of that layer doubles with it.
  • Price the whole stack. Columns B and C leave the sponsor with the same $15 million equity check, but C costs twice as much for the extra layer. Compare complete stacks, and compare a layered stack against a single higher-leverage senior loan.

What happens in a downside

Suppose column B's project is sold for net proceeds of $80 million, after costs and ignoring accrued interest and fees. Proceeds move down the stack in order:

LayerOwedReceivesResult
Senior debt$75M$75MRepaid in full
Mezzanine or preferred equity$10M$5MHalf repaid
Common equity$15M$0Wiped out

The senior lender is protected, the middle layer takes a loss, and the sponsor loses everything. That ordering is the reason the senior layer is cheapest and common equity can earn the most when the project works.

When to use each layer

  • Senior debt alone: when the sponsor has the equity and wants the lowest cost and the simplest documents. It is the default.
  • Add mezzanine debt: when the senior loan leaves an equity gap, the senior lender permits a pledge of ownership interests, and the sponsor wants a defined loan with a maturity and clear remedies. It fits larger, stabilized or transitional deals where the layer is $10 million or more.
  • Add preferred equity: when the senior loan documents bar additional debt but allow equity, or the sponsor prefers a structure with no second loan to repay. The tradeoff is control triggers, such as the right to replace the managing member if agreed tests are missed.
  • Bring in a joint venture partner: when the sponsor needs more cash than it can fund or wants a partner's experience, and is willing to share cash flow and upside under a waterfall.
  • Replace the layers with a stretch senior loan: when one lender will go higher on leverage. One lender means one set of documents and no intercreditor negotiation, so price it against the layered stack.

Joint venture equity and the stack

Joint venture equity sits at the top of the stack, in the same slot as the sponsor's own cash. A partner contributes capital in exchange for a share of cash flow and sale proceeds, usually under a waterfall with a preferred return first and a promote for the sponsor after that. It is the most flexible layer and the most expensive in giving up upside. Terms for joint venture equity vary widely by partner, so none of the figures on this page are joint venture terms.

Senior lender consent and the intercreditor agreement

Adding a layer is never only a decision between the sponsor and the new capital. The senior lender has to agree, because the layer changes who owns the property if something fails. For mezzanine debt the senior and mezzanine lenders sign an intercreditor agreement that sets out cure rights, purchase options and what happens on default. For preferred equity the senior lender reviews the operating agreement to see what control rights the investor holds. Ask early what the senior lender allows, because that answer often decides which structure is available. Have counsel review both documents.

Questions to ask before you add a layer

  • What does the senior lender allow: additional debt, preferred equity, neither?
  • What does the whole stack cost together, not just the new layer? Compare it against a single higher-leverage senior loan.
  • Is the new layer current pay or accruing, and what is owed at maturity or exit?
  • What are the default remedies and cure periods, and who gets control if a test is missed?
  • What are the prepayment terms and any minimum return or exit fee?
  • Who pays for the intercreditor or operating agreement review, and how long will it take to close?

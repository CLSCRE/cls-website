Expert Witness: NNN Lease Rollover Upside and Financing Impact in Boulder, CO

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Below-Market NNN Lease and Rollover Upside Analysis

The Boulder Market Context

Boulder, Colorado occupies a genuinely unusual position in the Front Range commercial real estate landscape. It is a supply-constrained market by design: the city's blue line growth boundary, stringent zoning, and voter-approved development limits mean that new commercial inventory enters the market slowly and selectively. That structural scarcity shapes how lenders and investors think about single-tenant net lease assets here in ways that differ materially from Denver, Colorado Springs, or even suburban submarkets like Broomfield or Louisville.

For single-tenant NNN assets specifically, the Boulder market reflects a bifurcated dynamic. Retail fundamentals have held up better than the national average, supported by the city's high household income base (median household income consistently above $75,000), a highly educated and employed workforce tied to the University of Colorado, NCAR, NIST, and a dense ecosystem of biotech and technology companies. At the same time, the office market has experienced meaningful softening post-2020, with vacancy rates in the Pearl Street and Canyon Boulevard corridors climbing from the low single digits into the 12 to 18 percent range depending on submarket and building vintage. This matters for single-tenant office or flex properties where below-market rents are in play, because lenders are reading those vacancies as evidence of weak re-leasing demand, not just a short-term cyclical dip.

Industrial and flex-industrial assets in Boulder and the adjacent 55th Street and Valmont Road corridors have performed considerably better. Vacancy in Boulder's industrial inventory has remained tight, generally in the 4 to 7 percent range, driven by demand from bioscience users, last-mile logistics, and specialty contractors serving the construction and renewable energy sectors. Asking rents for industrial flex in this submarket have pushed into the $18 to $26 per square foot NNN range for quality product, which creates a meaningful spread between older in-place lease rates and current market rates on properties with longer-term legacy tenants. That spread is precisely the kind of below-market lease condition that generates financing disputes and expert witness retention.

Cap rate benchmarks for single-tenant NNN assets in Boulder have generally traded in the 5.25 to 6.75 percent range depending on asset class, tenant credit, and lease term remaining. Retail pad sites anchored by investment-grade tenants have commanded the tightest cap rates. Single-tenant office and flex have faced cap rate expansion pressure as financing costs rose and leasing uncertainty increased. These market-specific fundamentals are the backdrop against which any lender underwriting or financing feasibility analysis must be conducted.

The Engagement

Counsel for one of the parties in a commercial real estate financing dispute retained Commercial Lending Solutions to provide an expert analysis of the financing feasibility and lender underwriting treatment of a single-tenant NNN commercial property located in the Boulder submarket. The asset was a freestanding commercial building occupied by a single tenant under a long-term triple-net lease structure. The core issue before the court involved whether the property's below-market in-place rent and approaching lease expiration created a financing impairment that was material to the dispute, and if so, how that impairment should be quantified in lender underwriting terms.

CLS CRE was retained to answer several specific questions that neither an appraiser nor a fact witness could address with the technical precision required. First, how do commercial mortgage lenders actually underwrite a below-market NNN lease when the lease term is within a certain range of expiration? Second, does the existence of rollover upside, meaning the potential to re-lease at market rents upon expiration, enhance or have a neutral effect on loan sizing in the current lender market? Third, what LTV and debt service coverage thresholds would apply given the asset type and market conditions, and did the in-place lease structure allow the subject property to qualify for financing at the leverage levels that were at issue? Fourth, what categories of lenders would have been willing to engage, and under what structural constraints?

These questions required an active practitioner with current lender relationships, underwriting knowledge, and direct market experience in the Boulder and broader Colorado Front Range market. They are not questions an appraisal can answer, because an appraisal establishes value but does not speak to the financing market's credit standards, lender appetite, or structural requirements for a given asset configuration.

The Financial Analysis

The methodology applied in this engagement followed the same framework that institutional and regional commercial mortgage lenders use in their own credit underwriting. The analysis began with the in-place net operating income derived from the existing below-market lease, not the market rent, because lenders underwrite to contractual income, not proforma or speculative income.

For a single-tenant NNN property of the type at issue, the gross lease revenue is the tenant's contractual base rent. Under a true triple-net structure, the landlord's expense exposure is limited, so the effective NOI closely tracks the base rent, adjusted only for vacancy reserve (typically 5 percent on single-tenant assets, applied even in NNN structures by conservative lenders), management fee (2 to 3 percent of EGI, applied by most institutional lenders regardless of whether the owner self-manages), and any structural reserve that the lender requires. For this analysis, those adjustments reduced the contractual rent by approximately 7 to 8 percent to arrive at lender-underwritten NOI.

Using illustrative figures appropriate for a Boulder submarket single-tenant flex or retail asset with a building size in the 8,000 to 12,000 square foot range: assume the in-place rent is $22 per square foot NNN on 10,000 square feet, generating gross revenue of $220,000 annually. After vacancy reserve and management fee adjustments at 8 percent combined, lender-underwritten NOI is approximately $202,400. Market rent for comparable space in this submarket is $28 per square foot NNN, which would generate $280,000 in gross revenue and approximately $257,600 in underwritten NOI. The spread between in-place underwritten NOI and market-rate underwritten NOI is approximately $55,200 annually. That is the quantifiable economic impact of the below-market lease in income terms.

To convert that NOI into maximum loan sizing, the analysis applied the debt service coverage ratio and loan-to-value constraints that govern lender credit boxes in the current market. For single-tenant NNN properties with investment-grade or near-investment-grade tenants, lenders typically require a minimum DSCR of 1.20x to 1.25x. For single-tenant assets with private or non-rated tenants, and particularly where lease term remaining is under five years, lenders frequently apply a more conservative floor of 1.30x to 1.35x, because the rollover risk creates an income discontinuity risk that justifies additional coverage cushion.

Assuming a 10-year fixed rate environment at 7.00 percent (consistent with agency-equivalent and life company pricing during the relevant period), amortized over 25 years, the annual debt service per dollar of loan is approximately $0.0849. At a 1.25x DSCR applied to in-place underwritten NOI of $202,400, maximum supportable annual debt service is $162,000, which supports a loan of approximately $1,908,000. At a 1.35x DSCR, which many lenders would impose given the lease term risk, maximum supportable debt service falls to $149,926, supporting a loan of approximately $1,766,000.

The property's market value, assuming a stabilized cap rate of 6.0 percent applied to market NOI of $257,600, is approximately $4,293,000. At a standard 65 percent LTV for a single-tenant commercial asset with rollover risk, the maximum loan on value constraints alone would be $2,790,000. The binding constraint is therefore not LTV but DSCR on the in-place income stream. The maximum loan supportable by in-place cash flow ($1,766,000 to $1,908,000) represents only 41 to 44 percent of the stabilized value, creating a substantial financing gap relative to typical market leverage expectations. This is the analytical foundation of the financing impairment: the below-market lease does not merely reduce value in appraisal terms; it compresses the debt market's willingness to lend against the asset to a degree that is disproportionate to the rent differential alone, because the DSCR constraint is multiplicative in its effect on loan sizing.

The Complicating Factors

Below-Market Rent and Lender NOI Conservatism

When a lender sees a below-market NNN lease, the reaction is not simply to note the lower NOI. The lender also questions why the rent is below market. Is it a legacy lease negotiated in a weaker environment? Is it evidence that the tenant's creditworthiness required rent concessions? Is the below-market structure a sign that the space has functional issues that preclude market-rate re-leasing? These questions increase credit scrutiny and often result in lenders applying more conservative reserve assumptions than they would for a market-rate asset. In Boulder's submarket, where some industrial and flex users have occupied space at below-market rents for 10 or more years under successive lease renewals, lenders are familiar with the dynamic but not necessarily more comfortable with it.

Rollover Upside: What It Does and Does Not Do for Loan Sizing

This is frequently misunderstood by non-financing professionals, including appraisers and some attorneys. Rollover upside, the prospect that rents will increase to market rate upon lease expiration, has essentially no positive effect on loan sizing in the current lender market for properties with lease terms of less than five years remaining. Lenders do not underwrite to proforma or post-rollover income. They underwrite to in-place, contractual, documentable income. The rollover upside has value in the equity market, meaning an investor may pay a premium for the property precisely because of the rent bump optionality, but that equity-market value does not translate into debt capacity under standard commercial mortgage underwriting.

In fact, rollover upside can have a perverse negative effect on financing: the closer the lease is to expiration, the more aggressively lenders reduce their loan sizing or decline the loan entirely, because they must underwrite the scenario in which the tenant does not renew and the property is temporarily dark. A lender committing to a 10-year fixed rate loan cannot rely on an income stream that may not exist in 30 months. This dynamic is particularly pronounced in Boulder's office and flex submarkets, where re-leasing timelines for single-tenant spaces have extended to 12 to 24 months even for well-located product.

Lease Term Remaining and the Financing Universe Constriction

Most institutional commercial mortgage lenders require that the lease term remaining at closing exceed the loan term by a meaningful margin, often 12 to 24 months minimum. Life insurance companies typically require lease term to extend through the loan maturity. CMBS lenders apply similar constraints. When lease term remaining falls below five years, the lender universe constricts sharply, eliminating most life company, agency, and CMBS execution and leaving primarily bank portfolio lenders, credit unions, and some debt fund lenders who are willing to accept rollover risk at higher spreads and lower leverage. This constriction itself constitutes a financing impairment, because it eliminates the lender categories that typically provide the most competitive pricing and highest leverage.

Boulder Market-Specific Re-Leasing Risk

Boulder's commercial leasing market has unique characteristics that lenders must assess. The city's supply constraints mean that well-located industrial and flex space does re-lease, but often after extended periods when the market is in a softer cycle. The limited depth of the tenant pool, particularly for larger single-tenant users, means that lenders view re-leasing timelines as longer than in comparable Denver submarkets. Lenders who are not active in the Boulder market specifically may apply generic Colorado market assumptions that understate the re-leasing velocity and therefore price the risk more conservatively than a local lender with actual Boulder portfolio experience.

The Boulder Lender Market

For single-tenant NNN assets in Boulder with the complexity profile described above, the active lender universe divides into several categories with distinct underwriting frameworks and credit appetites.

Community and Regional Banks: Colorado's community banking sector includes several institutions with genuine Boulder market expertise and active commercial real estate lending programs. These lenders typically operate with portfolio mentality, meaning they hold loans on their own balance sheets and have flexibility to consider qualitative factors like sponsorship strength, local operating track record, and relationship history. They will lend on below-market leases and near-term rollover situations, but at reduced leverage, typically 55 to 65 percent LTV on the in-place income value rather than the stabilized value, and at floating or adjustable rate structures. Pricing is generally SOFR plus 200 to 300 basis points depending on credit quality and loan size.

National Bank Portfolio Lenders: Several national banks with Front Range presence maintain commercial real estate lending programs for institutional-quality borrowers. These lenders apply more standardized underwriting and typically require minimum DSCR of 1.25x on in-place income, with lease term requirements of at least three to five years remaining at closing. They are less flexible on structure but can provide competitive pricing for transactions that fit their box, generally in the SOFR plus 175 to 250 basis point range with recourse or partial recourse.

Life Insurance Companies: Life company lenders are active in Boulder for stabilized, investment-grade-anchored NNN assets with long lease terms. They are largely irrelevant for the scenario described here, because they require lease terms extending through loan maturity (typically 7 to 10 years) and will not lend on near-term rollover risk. Their pricing is the most competitive in the market (currently in the 6.25 to 6.75 percent fixed rate range for 10-year terms), which is precisely why their unavailability in below-market, short-term-lease scenarios represents a measurable financing disadvantage.

Debt Funds and Bridge Lenders: For assets with rollover risk and below-market leases, bridge and debt fund lenders represent the capital source of last resort in conventional financing terms. They will lend on the re-leasing business plan, but at higher rates (typically 9.0 to 11.5 percent current pay plus fees), lower LTVs (55 to 65 percent of as-is value), and with shorter terms (12 to 36 months). Their presence in a financing analysis is evidence of a constrained conventional financing environment, not a solution equivalent to permanent mortgage financing.

What Attorneys Handling Boulder CRE Disputes Need to Know

Attorneys litigating commercial real estate disputes in Boulder frequently encounter financing questions that fall outside the scope of traditional appraisal testimony. A CRE financing expert addresses a different and complementary set of questions than an appraiser does. Understanding the distinction and knowing when to retain a financing expert is essential to building a complete evidentiary record.

When to retain a CRE financing expert: Retain a financing expert any time the dispute involves whether a property could have been refinanced at a specific leverage level, whether a specific loan structure was commercially reasonable, whether financing was available at all given the asset's lease configuration, what the quantifiable financing impact of a lease defect or rent reduction is, or whether a lender's decision to not fund or to impose specific loan conditions was consistent with market standards. These are not appraisal questions; they are capital markets questions.

What a financing expert can establish that an appraiser cannot: An appraiser establishes market value using income, sales comparison, and cost approaches. The appraiser does not testify about lender underwriting standards, DSCR requirements, loan-to-value constraints by asset type, lender market conditions, or whether a specific loan structure was available in the market at a given point in time. A CRE financing expert establishes the debt market's response to a specific asset and lease configuration, which is a distinct and often outcome-determinative question in disputes involving loan failures, financing contingency disputes, and damages calculations tied to financing costs.

Qualifications that matter: A qualified CRE financing expert should be an active practitioner in the commercial mortgage market, not a retired banker or academic. The expert needs current lender relationships, active knowledge of underwriting standards across multiple lender categories, and transaction experience in the relevant market and asset class. Courtroom qualification should be supported by documented transaction volume, a diverse lender network, and experience providing expert testimony in commercial real estate financing disputes across multiple jurisdictions.

Information counsel should gather before the engagement: Provide the expert with the full lease or leases in place, rent rolls for the relevant period, any appraisals or broker opinions of value, loan applications or term sheets from the relevant financing period, lender correspondence, and any internal financial statements or projections for the asset. The expert analysis is only as precise as the underlying documentation allows.

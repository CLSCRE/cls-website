Expert Witness: CRE Partnership and Waterfall Disputes in Virginia Beach, VA

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Joint Venture and Partnership Dispute

The Virginia Beach Market Context

Virginia Beach occupies a distinctive position in the Mid-Atlantic commercial real estate landscape that creates conditions ripe for joint venture disputes. The market combines a large, military-driven economic base anchored by Naval Station Norfolk and Joint Expeditionary Base Little Creek with a mature resort and hospitality corridor along the oceanfront, an expanding industrial submarket near the Virginia Beach/Norfolk border, and a multifamily sector that has outperformed most Southeast coastal markets over the past decade. That combination of asset types, investor profiles, and capital sources creates exactly the kind of JV formation environment where waterfall disputes are most likely to emerge.

On the multifamily side, which is where many Virginia Beach JV structures have concentrated over the past five years, effective rents in the Virginia Beach-Chesapeake-Norfolk MSA have increased significantly, with suburban Virginia Beach submarkets like the Kempsville corridor and the area surrounding Town Center showing sustained rent growth driven by a renter demographic that skews younger and military-affiliated. Multifamily cap rates in stabilized Virginia Beach product have compressed into the mid-4 to low-5 percent range for Class A assets, which directly affects how waterfall structures were underwritten at formation relative to what exit proceeds actually look like at disposition. When the capital market conditions at disposition diverge materially from underwriting assumptions, preferred return thresholds and promoted interest calculations become disputed territory.

Industrial vacancy in the Virginia Beach submarket, particularly near the I-264 corridor and the Route 58 industrial nodes, has tightened considerably as e-commerce and defense logistics demand have grown. Net asking rents for industrial product have risen from the mid-$6 per square foot range to the high-$8 to low-$9 range over a relatively compressed period, which means that JVs formed at one income level may have experienced substantial NOI growth by the time a dispute over promoted interest crystallizes. That income growth trajectory is itself a variable that must be forensically reconstructed when waterfall calculations are in dispute.

Retail in Virginia Beach remains bifurcated. Oceanfront and resort-adjacent retail has demonstrated resilience tied to tourism volume, while power center and strip retail away from tourist corridors has faced the same headwinds seen nationally. JVs structured around retail repositioning or value-add retail acquisition in Virginia Beach carry a specific risk profile that affects how lenders structured senior debt, mezzanine tranches, and preferred equity at origination, and those capital stack decisions ripple directly into how waterfall distributions were or should have been calculated.

The Engagement

Counsel representing the limited partner in a Virginia Beach joint venture retained Commercial Lending Solutions to analyze the capital stack structure underlying a mixed-use commercial project and to provide an expert opinion on whether distributions paid to the general partner were consistent with market-standard waterfall mechanics and the financing terms governing the senior debt. The engagement arose from a dispute between the equity partners over the general partner's entitlement to promoted interest, the timing of preferred return satisfaction, and the accuracy of the general partner's distribution calculations following a refinancing event that generated proceeds the limited partner contended should have been distributed through the waterfall rather than retained as working capital reserves.

The asset was a mid-size multifamily development with ground-floor commercial space in a Virginia Beach suburban submarket that had been developed by the general partner using a two-tier equity structure, a senior construction loan that was converted to permanent financing, and a preferred equity instrument layered between the senior debt and the common equity. The legal questions before the court required us to address several financial issues that an appraiser alone could not answer: whether the preferred return calculation methodology employed by the general partner was consistent with market-standard JV agreement structures; whether the lender's treatment of refinancing proceeds affected the distributable cash available to flow through the waterfall; and whether the promoted interest threshold claimed by the general partner could have been legitimately reached given the project's actual financial performance relative to its underwritten projections.

Our mandate was to reconstruct the project's economic history from a capital markets perspective, benchmark the JV's structural terms against market-standard agreements for comparable Virginia Beach transactions during the same vintage period, and provide an expert opinion on what a commercially reasonable general partner would have distributed to the limited partner at each waterfall trigger event.

The Financial Analysis

The first analytical task was reconstructing the project's stabilized net operating income from the ground up rather than accepting the general partner's reported figures at face value. For a multifamily asset in Virginia Beach, stabilized NOI analysis begins with a market rent survey across comparable properties in the same submarket, adjusted for unit mix, vintage, amenity profile, and proximity to employment centers. We analyzed rental comps, occupancy history, loss-to-lease, concession burn-off schedules, and expense ratios, benchmarking the subject's reported operating expenses against market-standard expense ratios for Virginia Beach multifamily, which typically run between 35 and 42 percent of effective gross income depending on management structure and whether the project includes commercial component expenses.

For this analysis, we used a stabilized NOI of approximately $2.1 million annually, derived from effective gross income of approximately $3.35 million net of a 5.5 percent vacancy and credit loss allowance consistent with the Virginia Beach submarket's stabilized occupancy history, and operating expenses of approximately $1.25 million including management fees, taxes, insurance, maintenance, and replacement reserves. That NOI figure was then used to determine the maximum supportable senior loan amount under market-standard underwriting criteria applicable at the time the permanent financing was placed.

For permanent multifamily debt in Virginia Beach during the relevant period, agency lenders, primarily Freddie Mac and Fannie Mae through their DUS lending programs, were the dominant execution channel. Agency underwriting for conventional multifamily product applied a minimum debt service coverage ratio of 1.25x on a standard 30-year amortization schedule, with interest rates at origination in the relevant vintage period ranging from approximately 3.75 to 4.25 percent depending on term, prepayment structure, and loan-to-value constraints. Life companies and CMBS lenders were also active in Virginia Beach multifamily but generally required the same 1.25x DSCR floor with slightly different LTV ceilings.

Applying a 1.25x DSCR to $2.1 million in NOI produces maximum allowable debt service of approximately $1.68 million annually. At a 4.0 percent note rate on a 30-year amortization, that annual debt service cap supports a maximum loan amount of approximately $28.6 million. The project's appraised value at stabilization was approximately $42 million, implying a supportable LTV of approximately 68 percent, which is consistent with agency parameters for a project of this type but leaves meaningful equity in the capital stack. That equity cushion is precisely where the dispute concentrates: the general partner's refinancing extracted proceeds that the limited partner contended should have flowed through the waterfall, and the question was whether the actual senior loan placed was at, above, or below the market-supportable maximum.

Our analysis found that the senior loan as placed was approximately $26.4 million, roughly $2.2 million below the maximum supportable loan under market-standard underwriting, which meant that additional refinancing proceeds were theoretically accessible to the partnership. Whether those proceeds were distributed through the waterfall or retained was the core financial question the litigation required us to resolve. The preferred return calculation dispute was equally technical: the limited partner's capital account, the IRR hurdle that triggered promoted interest, and the compounding convention applied to the preferred return accrual were all contested, and each variable affected the promoted interest calculation by a material amount.

The Complicating Factors

The first complicating factor in this engagement was the preferred equity instrument layered between the senior debt and the common equity. Preferred equity in a Virginia Beach JV capital stack occupies a legally and financially ambiguous position relative to the senior lender's collateral rights, and its treatment in the waterfall significantly affects what flows to the common equity partners below it. The preferred equity in this deal carried a current pay component and an accrued return component, and the general partner's calculation of distributable cash available for the common equity waterfall failed to account correctly for the accrued portion of the preferred equity return before calculating the limited partner's preferred return. That sequencing error had a compounding effect over the project's hold period that materially understated the limited partner's entitlement.

The second complicating factor was the construction-to-permanent financing conversion and its effect on the partnership's basis for waterfall calculations. At conversion, the senior lender's reunderwriting of the project produced a permanent loan amount that was different from what the parties had projected at JV formation, and the general partner's waterfall model was not updated to reflect the actual permanent loan amount. That failure to update the financial model meant that all subsequent distribution calculations were built on a flawed foundation, and the error was not discovered until the limited partner retained forensic accounting support in connection with the litigation.

The third complicating factor was the treatment of a cash-out refinancing event approximately three years into the permanent loan term. Agency loan documents, particularly Freddie Mac CME loan agreements, contain specific provisions governing cash-out refinancings, including lockout periods, prepayment yield maintenance obligations, and lender consent requirements for supplemental loan proceeds. The general partner's contention that the refinancing proceeds were legitimately retained as working capital reserves was inconsistent with the partnership agreement's definition of distributable cash and inconsistent with market-standard JV practice for a stabilized asset in this position in the capital stack. Understanding the lender's documentation and consent requirements required capital markets expertise, not just real estate appraisal knowledge.

The fourth complicating factor was the promoted interest calculation itself. The partnership agreement specified an IRR-based promote structure with tiered promoted interest thresholds at an 8 percent preferred return and a 12 percent IRR. The general partner's calculation used a time-weighted return methodology rather than a modified internal rate of return methodology, which produced a meaningfully different result. Market-standard JV agreements in this vintage and asset class typically employ an IRR-based calculation with daily or monthly compounding conventions, and the deviation from that standard in the general partner's calculations was a significant element of our expert analysis.

The Virginia Beach Lender Market

Virginia Beach benefits from a relatively deep and diverse lender market compared to many coastal secondary markets of similar size. For multifamily and mixed-use assets of the type involved in this dispute, the active lender categories include agency lenders, life insurance companies, regional banks, community banks, credit unions, CMBS conduits, and debt fund lenders, each with distinct underwriting requirements, pricing parameters, and appetite for complexity.

Agency lenders are the dominant execution channel for stabilized multifamily in Virginia Beach, and both Freddie Mac and Fannie Mae have active DUS lenders with Virginia Beach multifamily experience. Agency programs offer the most competitive pricing, the longest fixed-rate terms, and the highest leverage for qualifying assets, but their underwriting is also the most rigid with respect to DSCR floors, occupancy history requirements, and reserve requirements. Any JV dispute involving agency-financed Virginia Beach multifamily requires expert understanding of DUS program requirements because the lender's constraints directly shape what economic outcomes were possible for the partnership.

Life insurance companies are active in Virginia Beach for larger, stabilized commercial assets, particularly in the $15 million and above loan size range. Life company lenders typically offer lower leverage than agency at 55 to 65 percent LTV but are more flexible on property type and can accommodate mixed-use configurations that agency programs restrict. Their pricing is competitive, often matching or slightly underpricing CMBS for the right deal, and their documentation and reserve requirements differ from agency in ways that affect how waterfall distributions interact with lender consent provisions.

Regional and community banks are meaningful participants in Virginia Beach commercial lending, particularly for construction, bridge, and value-add transactions where agency takeout is the exit strategy. Virginia-based regional institutions have historically been active in the Hampton Roads market with competitive construction loan programs, and their credit boxes for Virginia Beach assets tend to be well-calibrated to local market conditions. For JV dispute purposes, understanding how the regional bank construction lender underwrote the project at origination is critical context for evaluating whether the general partner's projections were reasonable at the time of JV formation.

Debt funds and bridge lenders are active in Virginia Beach for transitional assets, and several national debt fund platforms have closed transactions in the Hampton Roads market. These lenders price at higher spreads, typically SOFR plus 300 to 500 basis points for floating-rate bridge debt, and their loan documents include cash management provisions, springing cash traps, and DSCR-triggered cash sweeps that can significantly affect how distributable cash flows through a JV waterfall during a period of financial stress.

What Attorneys Handling Virginia Beach CRE Disputes Need to Know

The decision to retain a CRE financing expert witness should be made early in litigation involving commercial real estate joint ventures. Attorneys frequently retain appraisers to establish value and accountants to trace cash flows, but neither discipline addresses the specific question of how the capital stack was structured, whether the financing terms were consistent with market standards, and how those financing terms interacted with the partnership agreement's waterfall mechanics. That gap is precisely where a CRE financing expert operates.

A qualified CRE financing expert should be able to testify to several categories of opinion that are beyond an appraiser's scope. First, whether the senior debt terms, including loan amount, interest rate, amortization, DSCR underwriting, and prepayment structure, were consistent with market standards for Virginia Beach assets of this type and vintage. Second, whether the general partner's distributions were consistent with what a commercially reasonable general partner operating under market-standard JV documentation would have paid. Third, whether the preferred return calculation methodology, compounding convention, and sequencing of distributions within the waterfall were consistent with market-standard practice. Fourth, what financing alternatives were available to the partnership at key decision points, and how those alternatives would have affected the distributable cash flowing to each partner.

The distinction between a qualified CRE financing expert and a retired banker is significant and attorneys should evaluate it carefully. A retired banker may have deep knowledge of credit underwriting within a single institution's framework but limited exposure to capital markets execution, JV structuring, or the full range of lender products available in a given market. A CRE financing expert who has actively brokered and structured complex transactions across multiple lender categories in multiple markets brings a broader analytical framework that is more useful in litigation because it reflects how the market as a whole, not a single institution, would have approached the financing. At CLS CRE, our approximately $1 billion in transaction volume across 50 states and relationships with more than 1,000 lenders provides exactly that market-wide perspective.

Before engaging a CRE financing expert, counsel should gather the complete loan file for every financing on the subject property, including the original loan application, the lender's commitment letter, the appraisal obtained by the lender, the executed loan documents, any loan modification agreements, and all lender correspondence related to reserves, cash management, and distributable cash. Counsel should also obtain the complete partnership agreement and any side letters or amendments, the general partner's waterfall calculation workbooks for every distribution event, the partnership's audited and unaudited financial statements, and any lender consent letters or subordination agreements related to the preferred equity instrument. The quality of the expert's analysis is directly proportional to the completeness of the financial record provided at the outset of the engagement.

At trial, CRE financing expert testimony is most effective when it bridges the gap between the partnership agreement's contractual language and the financial reality of how that language was or should have been implemented in a market context. Judges and mediators benefit from expert testimony that translates complex capital markets concepts, preferred return accrual, IRR-based promote calculations, cash-out refinancing mechanics, and DSCR-constrained distributable cash, into clear analytical frameworks tied to market standards that can be evaluated against the general partner's actual conduct.

Key Takeaways

  • JV waterfall disputes in Virginia Beach frequently turn on capital markets facts. Whether the senior loan was sized at market-standard leverage, whether refinancing proceeds were accessible under the loan documents, and whether the lender's cash management structure affected distributable cash are all questions that require a CRE financing expert, not just an appraiser or accountant.
  • Preferred return calculations are technically specific and methodologically contested. The choice of compounding convention, the sequencing of preferred equity versus common equity preferred returns, and whether a time-weighted or IRR-based return methodology is applied can produce materially different promoted interest outcomes, and each choice should be benchmarked against market standards for the vintage and asset class.

The Milwaukee Market Context

Milwaukee's commercial real estate market sits at an interesting inflection point that makes ground lease disputes both more common and more consequential than they might be in a less institutionally layered market. The city's industrial sector has been the standout performer, with Class B and Class C industrial vacancy hovering in the 4 to 6 percent range across submarkets like Menomonee Valley, the Near West Side corridor, and the airport-adjacent zones. Net asking rents for industrial have pushed past $6.00 to $7.50 per square foot NNN in functional logistics product, with newer construction commanding $8.50 and above. That rent growth, compounded over ground lease terms that frequently run 50 to 99 years, creates a financing complexity that courts and counsel in Milwaukee are increasingly being asked to untangle.

On the office side, Milwaukee reflects a national pattern but with local texture. The CBD has seen meaningful sublease availability push effective vacancy into the mid-20 percent range for Class B product, while suburban clusters in Brookfield and Wauwatosa have held up somewhat better at 12 to 15 percent vacancy. Multifamily has been the capital magnet, with stabilized cap rates in the 5.0 to 5.75 percent range for well-located Milwaukee product, though ground-up construction has slowed due to construction cost pressures and rising interest rates tightening the feasibility window. Retail in Milwaukee's Walker's Point, Historic Third Ward, and Bayshore-adjacent corridors has demonstrated resilience that pure national data misses, making it a legitimate asset class for leasehold mortgage analysis.

Ground leases are not exotic structures in Milwaukee. Institutional landowners, university and healthcare adjacencies, and municipally owned parcels in redevelopment zones have created a meaningful inventory of fee-separated properties throughout the metropolitan area. When those arrangements face refinancing, value disputes, partnership dissolution, or loan workouts, the financing complexity of the leasehold interest becomes the central evidentiary question.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing a leasehold mortgagee in a dispute arising from a ground lease arrangement on a mixed-use commercial asset in the Milwaukee metropolitan area. The property involved an institutional ground lessor holding fee title and an operating tenant entity that had constructed and operated improvements on the leased land, financing those improvements through a leasehold mortgage. The dispute centered on three intersecting legal and financial questions that required expert analysis beyond what a standard appraisal could provide.

First, counsel needed an analysis of whether the ground lease, as written, was financeable under market-standard lender requirements at the time the leasehold mortgage was originated. Second, the engagement required us to evaluate whether the subordination, non-disturbance, and attornment provisions in the existing ground lease met or materially deviated from what institutional lenders require to extend credit against a leasehold interest. Third, we were asked to reconstruct the debt service coverage and loan-to-value analysis that a reasonable lender would have performed, and to determine whether the loan as structured was consistent with or outside the bounds of market underwriting practice for leasehold mortgage financing in the relevant time period.

CLS CRE brought to this engagement approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, active relationships with more than 1,000 lenders across all capital stack categories, and direct origination experience with leasehold mortgage structures across 50 states. That active market participation is what separates a financing expert from an academic or a retired banker offering historical opinion.

The Financial Analysis

Leasehold mortgage underwriting begins with a foundational question that fee-simple mortgage underwriting does not face: what is the income-producing life of the collateral relative to the loan term? A lender taking a leasehold position must be satisfied that the ground lease has sufficient remaining term to support full amortization of the loan with meaningful cushion beyond the maturity date. Market standard requires that the ground lease term extend at least 10 to 15 years beyond the loan maturity date, and for longer-term permanent financing, lenders typically want 20 to 25 years of post-maturity lease term remaining at origination.

For the Milwaukee asset at issue, the stabilized net operating income was established through a trailing 12-month analysis adjusted for non-recurring items, then stress-tested against a vacancy and credit loss assumption appropriate to the submarket. For a mixed-use commercial property in a strong Milwaukee corridor submarket, we applied a 5 percent vacancy factor to retail and office components and a 3 percent factor to the industrial or warehouse component, consistent with submarket performance data. The resulting underwritten NOI landed at approximately $620,000 annually, a figure consistent with a property of this scale and use mix in the Milwaukee market.

Debt service coverage ratio thresholds for leasehold mortgage financing are not the same as for fee-simple financing. While a fee-simple commercial mortgage might clear at 1.20x DSCR for strong credit tenancies, leasehold financing carries structural risk premium that pushes lender requirements toward 1.30x to 1.35x minimum DSCR for institutional capital, and 1.25x as an absolute floor for even the most aggressive capital sources willing to lend on a leasehold. The reason is straightforward: a lender's ability to foreclose and control the asset is conditional on the ground lease remaining in force, which means any event of default under the ground lease creates a collateral risk that fee-simple lenders simply do not face.

Applying a 1.30x DSCR to the $620,000 underwritten NOI produces a maximum supportable debt service of approximately $477,000 annually. At a 7.25 percent interest rate on a 25-year amortization schedule, which reflected prevailing market conditions for the applicable period and loan type, that debt service ceiling supports a maximum loan of approximately $5.85 million. At the time of origination, the leasehold improvements were independently appraised at $8.2 million, producing a loan-to-value ratio of 71.3 percent on the improvement value alone. If the ground lease had contained a properly structured purchase option at a fixed or formulaic price, lenders would consider a blended value incorporating the leasehold estate value, potentially supporting a higher loan basis. Without such an option, the lender's collateral was purely the leasehold interest and the improvements, and the 71.3 percent LTV represents the upper boundary of what a reasonable lender in this market would accept.

Standard leasehold LTV constraints generally cap at 65 to 70 percent of leasehold value for institutional permanent lenders, with some bridge lenders willing to reach 75 percent at correspondingly higher pricing. The loan as originated was at the edge of, but not outside, institutional market standards on a pure leverage basis. The more significant deviation we identified was in the SNDA structure, which forms the core of the complicating factors analysis.

The Complicating Factors

Deficient SNDA Structure. The ground lease at issue contained what the parties characterized as a non-disturbance agreement, but the provision was fundamentally deficient under any reasonable reading of market-standard SNDA requirements. Specifically, the provision lacked a binding obligation on the ground lessor to recognize the leasehold mortgagee's right to cure a default by the tenant before the ground lessor could terminate the lease. In standard institutional SNDA practice, the mortgagee must receive independent written notice of any tenant default, a separate cure period of not less than 30 days following the expiration of the tenant's own cure period for monetary defaults, and not less than 60 to 90 days for non-monetary defaults with an additional reasonable period for possession and cure where possession is required. The ground lease in question provided notice to the mortgagee but did not include a separate cure period following expiration of the tenant's cure window. This single deficiency would have caused every major institutional lender we surveyed, including life companies, CMBS conduits, and agency-affiliated programs, to decline the financing without a ground lease amendment.

Absence of New Lease Obligation. Equally critical, the ground lease did not include a provision obligating the ground lessor to enter into a new lease with the leasehold mortgagee or its designee in the event of a lease termination resulting from tenant default. Without a new lease obligation, a lender that forecloses on the leasehold interest and becomes the tenant of record has no assurance that the ground lessor cannot simply decline to renew at lease expiration or after a termination event. CMBS lenders, which represent a significant capital source for Milwaukee commercial assets, treat the absence of a new lease obligation as an automatic disqualifier. Life insurance company lenders treat it the same way. The only capital sources willing to lend without this provision are debt funds and hard money lenders operating at 400 to 600 basis points above agency or life company pricing, which represents a material and quantifiable reduction in financing value that directly implicates damages in a financing dispute.

Ground Rent Reset Mechanics. The ground lease included a rent reset provision tied to fair market value appraisal every 10 years with no cap on the increase. For lenders underwriting against projected NOI over a 25-year amortization period, an uncapped ground rent reset creates a debt service coverage uncertainty that is very difficult to model or underwrite. Institutional lenders require either a fixed escalation schedule, a CPI-indexed increase with a floor and ceiling, or a fair market value reset with a contractual cap on the percentage increase from the prior rent period. Without one of these structures, the leasehold mortgage is, from an underwriting perspective, exposed to an income stream that could be substantially compressed at each reset date. For the Milwaukee asset, ground rent represented approximately 18 percent of property-level NOI at origination. An uncapped reset in a strengthening land market could push that figure to 30 percent or above, which would breach DSCR thresholds and trigger technical default under the loan documents without any change in the property's operating performance.

Condemnation and Casualty Allocation. The ground lease allocated condemnation proceeds between the ground lessor and tenant on a formula that did not adequately protect the leasehold mortgagee's first position claim against the leasehold value. Market-standard leasehold mortgage requirements demand that the leasehold mortgage lien attach to any casualty or condemnation proceeds allocable to the leasehold estate and improvements, with those proceeds payable first to the leasehold mortgagee to the extent of outstanding principal and accrued interest before any distribution to the tenant. The ground lease in question was silent on the mortgagee's rights in condemnation proceeds, creating an unacceptable gap in collateral protection that any competent origination counsel should have flagged prior to closing.

The Milwaukee Lender Market

Understanding which lenders are actually active in Milwaukee for leasehold mortgage financing, and what each requires, is essential context for any financing dispute or feasibility analysis. The Milwaukee lender market is more institutionally deep than many outside observers assume, but it is also more regionally concentrated, which affects both pricing and credit standards.

Community and Regional Banks. Milwaukee has a meaningful community banking presence, including Wisconsin-chartered institutions and Midwest regional banks with active commercial real estate lending books. These lenders are generally more willing to engage with leasehold structures than their credit box guidelines might suggest, particularly when the ground lessor is a municipally affiliated or nonprofit entity with whom they have an existing relationship. Community banks typically require 1.25x DSCR, cap at 65 percent LTV on the leasehold, and want personal guarantees or corporate recourse in addition to the leasehold collateral. They are rarely willing to lend on a leasehold without a mortgagee protection clause that gives them a direct cure right, but they may be more flexible than institutional lenders on the exact structure of that clause.

CMBS Conduit Lenders. Conduit executions are available for Milwaukee assets of sufficient scale, typically $5 million and above in loan size, but leasehold structures face the most rigorous SNDA requirements in this channel. Conduit lenders underwrite to CMBS trust requirements and rating agency standards, which are published and specific. The absence of a new lease obligation or a separate mortgagee cure period is a non-starter in the conduit market, full stop. Conduit pricing for Milwaukee leasehold product in the current rate environment lands at Treasury plus 175 to 225 basis points depending on asset quality and ground lease structure.

Life Insurance Companies. Life company allocations to Milwaukee commercial product exist but are selective, generally focused on Class A industrial, stabilized multifamily, and credit-tenanted retail or office. Life companies offer the most favorable long-term pricing available in the market but have the most stringent SNDA requirements. They will typically require ground lessor recognition agreements that are negotiated directly between their counsel and the ground lessor before commitment issuance.

Debt Funds and Bridge Lenders. For leasehold structures with material SNDA deficiencies, the financing universe narrows to debt funds and bridge capital, which are willing to accept structural risk in exchange for higher pricing. Milwaukee assets can access this capital, but at 300 to 500 basis points over term SOFR for bridge executions, and with personal recourse or guarantee requirements that institutional permanent lenders do not impose. The pricing differential between a structurally clean leasehold that qualifies for life company or CMBS financing and a deficient leasehold that is limited to debt fund capital represents a quantifiable and material economic impact that is directly relevant in damages analysis.

What Attorneys Handling Milwaukee CRE Disputes Need to Know

The threshold question I hear most often from Milwaukee litigation counsel is: when do I actually need a CRE financing expert, versus relying on an appraiser or a banker witness? The answer is that these are not interchangeable disciplines. An appraiser determines value. A CRE financing expert determines financiability, which is a distinct and often more dispositive question in ground lease disputes, lender liability cases, construction loan disputes, and commercial loan workout litigation.

A qualified CRE financing expert can answer questions that an appraiser cannot. What would a reasonable institutional lender have required in the SNDA provisions of this ground lease before extending credit? Was the loan as originated within or outside the range of market-standard underwriting practices for this asset type and market? What was the realistic universe of lenders available to this borrower at the time of the alleged breach, and how does that universe change if the ground lease is read one way versus another? What is the pricing differential, expressed in basis points and dollar impact, between a financeable ground lease structure and the deficient structure at issue? These are capital markets questions, not valuation questions.

What makes a CRE financing expert qualified is active, current market participation. A retired banker who last originated a leasehold mortgage in 2011 cannot reliably testify about current lender requirements, current credit box parameters, or current pricing spreads. CLS CRE's active engagement across more than 1,000 lender relationships and approximately $1 billion in transaction volume provides the foundation for testimony grounded in what lenders are actually doing in the market today, not what they did a decade ago.

Counsel should gather the following before initiating an expert engagement on a ground lease financing matter: the complete ground lease including all amendments and assignments; any subordination, non-disturbance, and attornment agreements executed in connection with the leasehold mortgage; the original loan application, term sheet, and commitment letter; the leasehold mortgage documents; any appraisals or valuations of the leasehold interest; rent rolls and operating statements for the property; and any correspondence between lender counsel and ground lessor counsel regarding SNDA negotiations. This documentation allows the financing expert to reconstruct the underwriting analysis and compare the actual loan structure against market-standard benchmarks with evidentiary specificity.

At trial or in a plan confirmation hearing, CRE financing expert testimony is most effective when structured around three pillars: what market standard requires, what the actual structure provided, and what the economic consequences of the gap between those two are. That third pillar, quantifying the financing impact of a structural deficiency, is frequently the most consequential expert contribution in a ground lease dispute.

Key Takeaways

  • Leasehold mortgage underwriting requires a minimum DSCR of 1.25x to 1.30x, stricter than fee-simple standards, because the lender's collateral control is contingent on ground lease continuity, which creates an additional layer of structural risk that lenders price into both rate and coverage requirements.
  • The three core SNDA elements that institutional lenders require are: independent mortgagee notice of tenant default, a separate mortgagee cure period following expiration of the tenant's own cure window, and a new lease obligation binding the ground lessor to recognize a foreclosing lender or its designee as the new tenant under equivalent terms.
  • An uncapped ground rent reset provision is a material underwriting deficiency that compresses the financeable loan amount, reduces the available lender universe to bridge and debt fund capital, and creates a quantifiable pricing impact that can be expressed in basis points and dollars for purposes of damages calculation.

Opportunity Zone and LIHTC Debt Financing: How Commercial Lending Solutions Sources the Senior Loan

By Trevor Damyan·June 25, 2026·Commercial Lending Solutions

How CLS CRE Sources and Structures Senior Debt for Opportunity Zone and LIHTC Capital Stacks

I have spent the past decade placing debt on commercial real estate transactions across 50 states, and nothing in this business is more technically demanding than the capital stack of a tax-credit or opportunity zone project. These deals layer four, five, sometimes six distinct financing instruments -- each with its own regulatory requirements, timing constraints, and lender appetite -- and the senior debt placement alone requires a broker who can read a sources-and-uses schedule, understand where the tax credit equity sits in the waterfall, and communicate fluently with construction lenders who have never touched a LIHTC deal before.

If you are a QOZ fund manager, a LIHTC syndicator, a CDFI loan officer, or an attorney structuring qualified opportunity zone transactions, this article is for you. I want to explain precisely how my firm, Commercial Lending Solutions (CLS CRE), approaches the senior debt component of these capital stacks, what lenders actually require, and why having the right commercial mortgage broker on your team matters more in affordable housing and OZ finance than in almost any other asset class.

The World You Operate In and Where CRE Financing Fits

Opportunity zone fund managers are working against a hard clock. The 10-year hold requirement, the deferred gain recognition event, and the step-up rules create a timeline that governs every financing decision. When a fund identifies a qualified opportunity zone business property and commits equity, the senior debt placement cannot lag. Slippage on a construction loan commitment can blow a tax year, triggering gain recognition for LPs who were counting on deferral.

LIHTC syndicators are navigating a different but equally rigid world. The 4% and 9% credit programs operate on state housing finance agency calendars, carryover allocation deadlines, and placed-in-service requirements that make the construction and permanent financing timeline non-negotiable. A 4% transaction layered with tax-exempt bonds requires a bank that is willing to provide both the bond purchase and the construction loan simultaneously, and those banks are a specific subset of the lending universe. Most commercial mortgage brokers have never sourced one.

Community development finance professionals -- whether at CDFIs, community development entities, or public housing authorities -- frequently need a senior lender who can sit behind their subordinate debt without requiring a full pari-passu intercreditor agreement that kills the deal. Affordable housing developers working in mixed-income environments need a broker who can compartmentalize the LIHTC portion of the project from the market-rate portion without creating lender conflicts.

What unites all of these situations is that the senior debt placement requires a lender who understands the rest of the capital stack. That is where most commercial mortgage brokers fail. They can run a process for a multifamily construction loan or a permanent agency loan in isolation. They cannot do it when the project also has $8 million in tax credit equity, a CDFI soft loan with a 40-year amortization, and a bond regulatory agreement running with the land.

The Five Financing Scenarios We See Most Often

1. QOZ Development Needing Competitive Construction Financing

A qualified opportunity zone fund has committed equity into a ground-up multifamily or mixed-use development. The fund investors have contributed capital from realized gains, the 180-day reinvestment window has been satisfied, and the fund holds the qualified opportunity zone business property. The equity is in. The senior construction debt has not been placed.

This is a situation where speed and lender sophistication matter in equal measure. The construction lender needs to understand that the borrower entity is a qualified opportunity zone business, that the equity contributor is the fund (not the operating entity), and that the loan structure must accommodate the 10-year hold by not including recourse burn-off provisions that require a sale or refinance before the gain exclusion period is complete.

What we do: We run a competitive process among construction lenders with documented QOZ experience -- primarily regional banks, national banks with community development arms, and a select group of debt funds that have built QOZ-specific programs. We negotiate on rate, recourse structure, loan-to-cost, interest reserve sizing, and draw schedule flexibility. We present lenders with a complete loan package that includes the fund's operating agreement, the qualified opportunity zone fund certification, and the sources-and-uses schedule showing how the tax credit equity (if any) flows relative to the senior debt.

Typical parameters: construction loan proceeds at 55 to 75 percent of total project cost, depending on asset type and market; recourse to the guarantor with a completion guarantee; DSCR underwriting on the stabilized permanent financing that will follow; and an interest reserve sized for the full construction and lease-up period.

2. LIHTC Project With 4% Bonds -- The Competitive Bond and Construction Loan Process

A 4% LIHTC transaction paired with tax-exempt bond financing is one of the most technically complex executions in affordable housing. The bonds are issued by a state or local housing finance agency, but they need to be purchased -- and most bond programs require the purchasing bank to also provide the construction loan. The bank is providing credit support in two forms simultaneously: buying the bonds and advancing construction draws.

Most commercial mortgage brokers do not have relationships with the ten to fifteen banks nationally that actively participate in this market. We do. We run competitive processes among bond-eligible banks, presenting the full application package including the regulatory agreement, the 8609 allocation letter from the state HFA, the tax credit equity term sheet from the syndicator, and the permanent loan commitment (typically from Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, or FHA/HUD) that will take out the construction loan at stabilization.

What lenders require in this scenario: the bank will underwrite the construction loan on its own merits, independent of the credit equity. They want to see a developer with prior LIHTC experience, a construction contract with a reputable GC, a realistic construction budget with a 10 to 15 percent contingency, and a permanent loan commitment that is firm (not conditional) at or before construction loan closing. They will also review the regulatory agreement to confirm that the rent restrictions do not impair their ability to foreclose and operate the property if the project fails.

Key nuance: the bond purchase price and the construction loan spread are both negotiable. We push hard on both because the combined cost of funds on a bond-plus-construction loan can swing a deal's equity gap by hundreds of thousands of dollars. Syndicators and developers who have used only one bank on prior deals are often leaving meaningful savings on the table.

3. Stabilized QOZ Project Needing Permanent Financing Within the 10-Year Hold

A QOZ development has been completed, leased up, and is operating at stabilized occupancy. The construction loan is maturing. The fund needs permanent financing that works within the 10-year hold -- which means the loan term, prepayment structure, and any balloon obligation need to be modeled against the fund's likely exit date.

This is more nuanced than a standard multifamily permanent loan placement. The fund's investors are holding for gain exclusion, which means the fund cannot simply sell to satisfy a balloon payment without triggering the very gain recognition they are trying to avoid (if exit occurs before the 10-year mark). The loan structure needs to give the fund optionality -- either a term long enough to carry through the hold, or prepayment flexibility that does not penalize a sale in year 10 or later.

What we do: We identify agency lenders (Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac both have QOZ-aware execution) alongside life insurance companies and CMBS lenders who can offer 10-year fixed-rate loans with either step-down prepayment or yield maintenance structured to allow exit at or after the fund's gain exclusion date without prohibitive cost. We model the prepayment penalty at the expected exit date for each lender option and present it alongside rate and proceeds so the fund manager can make a fully informed decision.

Typical parameters: 10-year fixed-rate term, 30-year amortization, LTV at 65 to 75 percent depending on asset quality and market, DSCR minimum of 1.20x to 1.25x, and full or limited recourse depending on lender and loan size.

4. LIHTC Project in Lease-Up Needing a Bridge to Permanent Loan Conversion

The construction is complete. The project is in lease-up. The construction lender's maturity is approaching and the permanent loan (Fannie, Freddie, FHA) is not yet eligible to close because the property has not yet achieved the stabilized occupancy and income required for permanent loan underwriting. The developer needs a bridge.

This is a situation where speed and lender familiarity with the LIHTC lease-up dynamic are both critical. Tax credit units lease differently than market-rate units. Income certification requirements, waiting lists, and regulatory compliance reviews slow the lease-up timeline in ways that conventional lenders do not always anticipate. We work with bridge lenders -- primarily debt funds and select banks -- who understand that a LIHTC project at 70 percent occupancy in month six of lease-up is not underperforming; it is operating exactly as designed.

What lenders require: documentation that the construction loan is current and the permanent loan commitment is in place, evidence of lease-up velocity (units leased per month, income certifications in process), and a clear timeline to permanent loan conversion. Bridge lenders in this space will typically lend at 75 to 85 percent of the permanent loan amount, charge a spread of 200 to 400 basis points over SOFR, and carry the loan for six to eighteen months.

5. Mixed-Income Project With Separate Financing for Market-Rate and LIHTC Portions

A developer is building a 200-unit project: 140 market-rate units and 60 LIHTC units. The LIHTC units have their own financing stack -- tax credit equity, CDFI soft debt, and a bond construction loan. The market-rate units need a conventional construction-to-permanent loan. The same broker needs to coordinate both pieces without creating lender conflicts over the shared collateral.

This is a situation that most commercial mortgage brokers cannot handle because they lack experience with either the LIHTC side or the conventional side in the context of a shared project. We approach these transactions by first establishing the legal separation of the two financing tranches -- typically a condominium regime or a ground lease structure that separates the collateral -- and then placing the market-rate debt with a lender who is comfortable with the intercreditor dynamic created by the LIHTC financing on the adjacent units.

The market-rate lender needs to understand that the LIHTC portion of the project carries its own regulatory agreement and that this agreement runs with the land. They need to be comfortable that their collateral (the market-rate units) is legally distinct and that their lien is not subordinated to the LIHTC regulatory requirements. We work through these intercreditor questions with counsel before approaching lenders so that the loan package presents a clean, well-documented structure rather than leaving the lender's credit committee to puzzle through it.

What Lenders Actually Require for These Transactions

Beyond the scenario-specific requirements above, here are the documentation and underwriting standards that senior lenders in the OZ and LIHTC space consistently require:

  • Full capital stack disclosure: Every lender wants a complete sources-and-uses schedule that shows where every dollar is coming from and going to. Tax credit equity, CDFI grants and loans, deferred developer fee, and any public subsidy must be identified with term sheets or commitment letters attached.
  • Developer experience: For construction financing, lenders want to see a developer with at least two to three prior completions of similar project type and complexity. A first-time developer building a 150-unit LIHTC project will face significant resistance from most lenders regardless of equity strength.
  • Guarantor financial strength: Recourse construction loans require a guarantor. Lenders typically want a guarantor with liquidity equal to 10 to 20 percent of the loan amount and a net worth equal to 100 percent of the loan amount.
  • Permanent loan commitment at closing: Most construction lenders in the LIHTC space require a firm permanent loan commitment (Fannie, Freddie, FHA, or a creditworthy portfolio lender) before they will close the construction loan. Conditional commitments or term sheets are not sufficient.
  • Construction contract and budget review: Lenders engage their own construction risk consultant to review the GC contract, the construction budget, and the construction schedule. Developers should expect 60 to 90 days for this review and should not assume a clean budget means a fast close.
  • Regulatory agreement review: For LIHTC projects, the lender's counsel will review the regulatory agreement and the HAP contract (if any) to confirm that the rent restrictions do not impair lender remedies. Agreements with unusual transfer restrictions or right-of-first-refusal provisions held by a tenant organization or HFA can create lender concerns that need to be negotiated in advance.
  • 10-year operating history (permanent loans): Permanent lenders underwriting a stabilized LIHTC asset want to see actual operating statements, not proforma. New projects transitioning from construction to permanent need at least 90 days of stabilized operations for most lenders; FHA/HUD requires a longer seasoning for some programs.

Questions to Ask Any Commercial Mortgage Broker Before Referring a QOZ or LIHTC Client

Not every commercial mortgage broker is equipped to handle these transactions. Before you make a referral, ask the following:

  • Have you closed a construction loan on a LIHTC project in the past 24 months? If the answer is no, the broker is learning on your client's deal. That is not acceptable when an HFA carryover deadline is at stake.
  • Can you name three banks that actively purchase 4% tax-exempt bonds and provide the construction loan simultaneously? A broker who cannot answer this question has not worked in the 4% bond market.
  • How do you handle the intercreditor negotiation between the senior lender and the CDFI subordinate lender? The answer should demonstrate familiarity with subordination agreements, standstill periods, and cure rights -- not a vague reference to "coordinating with counsel."
  • Do you understand how QOZ fund structure affects the borrowing entity and guarantor requirement? The broker should be able to explain the distinction between the QOZ fund, the QOZB, and the operating entity, and why that distinction matters to a construction lender evaluating recourse.
  • What permanent loan programs do you work with for LIHTC assets? The answer should include Fannie Mae's Delegated Underwriting and Servicing program, Freddie Mac's Targeted Affordable Housing execution, FHA 221(d)(4) and 223(f), and relevant state HFA permanent loan programs.
  • How do you get paid, and by whom? Transparency on fee structure protects your client and ensures there is no undisclosed conflict of interest.

Key Takeaways

  • The senior debt in a QOZ or LIHTC capital stack is not a commodity placement. It requires a broker who understands the regulatory, timing, and intercreditor constraints imposed by the rest of the stack.
  • 4% LIHTC bond transactions require a lender willing to purchase the bonds and provide the construction loan simultaneously. This is a specific and limited lender universe that requires direct relationships, not a shotgun marketing blast.
  • QOZ permanent financing must be structured around the 10-year hold requirement. Prepayment terms, loan maturity, and balloon obligations all need to be modeled against the fund's gain exclusion timeline before lender selection.
  • LIHTC lease-up bridges are a specialty product. The lender needs to understand LIHTC lease-up velocity and income certification timelines, not just occupancy percentages.
  • Mixed-income projects require legal separation of collateral before lenders can be approached. The market-rate lender must be comfortable with the LIHTC regulatory agreement on adjacent units, and that comfort requires upfront legal structuring, not a mid-process fix.
  • Construction lenders require a firm permanent loan commitment at closing. Developers who approach construction lenders without a permanent commitment in hand will either face rejection or significantly worse terms.
  • The referral process is simple and non-competitive. You make the introduction, CLS CRE runs the financing process, your client gets competitive terms, and you retain your advisory relationship. Our fee comes from the borrower at closing.
  • Vetting your commercial mortgage broker before the referral matters. Ask direct questions about prior LIHTC and QOZ closings, lender relationships, and intercreditor experience. The answers will tell you everything you need to know.

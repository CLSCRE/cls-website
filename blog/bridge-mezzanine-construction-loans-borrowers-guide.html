Bridge, Mezzanine and Construction Loans: A Borrower's Guide

Trevor Damyan Founder & Principal Broker
CA DRE #02244836

Former CBRE and Marcus & Millichap Capital Corporation. $1B+ in closed commercial real estate financing across bridge, construction, permanent, and structured debt.

November 26, 2026
Quick answer: Bridge loans fund a transition and are sized to the plan and the exit. Mezzanine debt and preferred equity sit between the senior loan and sponsor equity but carry different remedies. Construction loans are sized to the tightest of loan-to-cost, loan-to-value and debt yield.

Three kinds of financing come up again and again when a commercial property needs more than a standard permanent loan: bridge loans for a transition, mezzanine debt or preferred equity to fill the gap above the senior loan, and construction loans to build. Each is sized and underwritten differently, and the differences decide whether a deal works. This guide explains how each one operates so you can compare offers on the facts.

We keep two kinds of statements apart throughout. General guidance describes how the market usually works and applies to any lender. Firm specific details describe loans that Commercial Lending Solutions (CLS CRE), a Los Angeles commercial mortgage brokerage, arranges. Those details are ranges from our own deals, not offers, and any actual terms come from a lender's written term sheet.

Bridge Financing: When It Fits and How It Is Underwritten

When a bridge loan may fit

A bridge loan is a transaction specific tool. It makes sense when the property's plan and repayment path are clear, but permanent financing is not yet available. Common situations include:

  • Value-add acquisitions: Buying a property that needs significant capital and lease-up before it qualifies for a long-term loan.
  • Time-sensitive purchases: A seller deadline or an auction timeline that a bank's schedule cannot meet.
  • Maturity gaps: An existing loan comes due while the property is not yet stabilized enough to refinance.
  • Properties with vacancy or deferred work: Assets that permanent lenders will not size until the issues are fixed.

How bridge underwriting differs from permanent underwriting

A permanent loan is sized on the stabilized, in-place performance of the property: current net operating income, a debt service coverage ratio, and a fixed rate with a long amortizing term. A bridge loan is sized on the as-is value, the project cost and the business plan, usually with a floating rate, interest-only payments, and a term of one to three years. Repayment comes from the exit, a permanent refinance or a sale, so lenders test the exit as carefully as the property. One lender-side guide to construction and bridge sizing notes that debt fund lenders pay particular attention to as-stabilized loan-to-value, which is a reminder that the projected end value matters even when the loan is sized on today's value.

These are general patterns. Individual lenders differ, so ask each one how it sizes the loan, which value it uses, and what must be true at the exit.

How to compare bridge terms on industrial properties

Questions about the best bridge terms for industrial properties cannot be answered with a single ranked list, because the best terms depend on the property, the sponsor and the plan, and rates and leverage change with the market. Compare offers on the same items instead:

  • The spread over SOFR, any rate floor, and whether the rate is fixed or floating
  • The advance rate, and whether it is measured against cost or value
  • The term, extension options and the tests that apply to them
  • Origination, exit and extension fees, and prepayment terms
  • Recourse and guarantees, including carve-outs
  • How and when renovation or tenant improvement funds are drawn
  • Time to close and the conditions that must be met first

For context, industrial bridge loans that CLS CRE arranges have generally priced at SOFR plus 225 to 450 basis points, with advances of up to 75 percent of cost, closings in 14 to 30 days when the package is complete, and a $2 million minimum loan size. Those are ranges from our own transactions and not a promise for any particular deal. See our pages on industrial bridge loans and commercial bridge loans, and our companion guide on how bridge loans are underwritten and how recourse works.

Bridge debt and a 1031 exchange

A 1031 exchange runs on deadlines set by the tax rules, and the financing for the replacement property has to be ready inside them. Bridge debt can serve as a backstop when permanent financing might not fund in time. How an exchange is structured, and the tax consequences of financing it, are questions for your qualified intermediary and tax advisor, and nothing here is tax advice. In our experience a bridge loan is rarely the plan for an exchange. We line up permanent financing early so the exchange closes on time, and we arrange a bridge only as a backstop when timing demands it. Our 1031 exchange bridge loan page covers the mechanics from the lending side.

Mezzanine Debt and Preferred Equity: How the Structures Differ

Where each sits in the capital stack

Both mezzanine debt and preferred equity are financing that is subordinate to the senior loan. They fill the layer between the senior mortgage and the sponsor's common equity, which raises total leverage without requiring the sponsor to invest more cash. The two are sometimes used interchangeably in conversation, but their legal structure and remedies differ, and those differences matter when a deal gets tight.

  • Mezzanine debt is a loan to the entity above the property owner, secured by a pledge of the ownership interests in the property owner rather than by the real estate. It has a stated interest rate and maturity, and the senior lender and mezzanine lender typically sign an intercreditor agreement that sets out their rights. On default, the mezzanine lender's main remedy is to foreclose on the pledged equity interests, which is generally faster than foreclosing on the property itself.
  • Preferred equity is an equity investment in the property owner. It ranks ahead of the sponsor's common equity and usually earns a preferred return, sometimes with a share of upside. Because it is equity and not a loan, its rights come from the operating agreement: if agreed triggers occur, the preferred investor may gain control rights, such as replacing the managing member, instead of foreclosing.
  • Why it matters: A senior loan agreement may restrict additional debt but allow preferred equity, or the reverse. Check what the senior lender permits before choosing, and have counsel review the intercreditor or operating agreement terms.

Our mezzanine versus preferred equity comparison and our mezzanine pros and cons article go deeper on cost, control and risk.

What CLS CRE arranges

For mezzanine debt, CLS CRE generally works on loans of $10 million and above, with combined leverage, senior plus mezzanine, of up to 85 percent and mezzanine rates that have generally fallen in a range of 8 percent to 14 percent depending on the deal. These are ranges from our own transactions, not offers, and each lender sets its own terms. Our mezzanine and preferred equity page describes the products.

How to assess a lender or broker for a large multifamily project

Mezzanine and preferred equity are relationship driven and often tied to the senior loan, so it pays to verify who you are dealing with. A practical checklist:

  • Does the firm fund directly, or does it place the deal with other capital providers, and which is it for your deal?
  • Can it show comparable financings it has closed, with details you can verify, while names are kept confidential?
  • What does it need from you at each stage, and what is the sequence from term sheet to closing?
  • Which fees are charged, and when are they payable? Be cautious of any fee requested before a term sheet is in hand.
  • What does the senior lender require for the intercreditor agreement or consent, and who manages that process?
  • What are the prepayment, extension and default provisions, and who holds control rights if the project underperforms?
  • What is the expected timeline, and what could delay it?

Ask for answers in writing. No credible firm will promise an approval or a closing before the property and sponsor have been underwritten.

Construction Loan Sizing and Execution

The three sizing measures

Construction lenders do not size on a single number. They calculate several and lend the smallest amount that satisfies all of them.

  • Loan-to-cost (LTC): The loan amount divided by total project cost, including land, hard costs, soft costs and financing costs. It limits how much of the project the lender funds and how much equity the sponsor must contribute.
  • Loan-to-value (LTV): The loan amount divided by appraised value. For construction, lenders look at the as-complete value or the as-stabilized value, and many tie their advance to the value at completion.
  • Debt yield: Projected stabilized net operating income divided by the loan amount. It tests whether the property earns enough relative to the debt, regardless of the interest rate or amortization used.

A simple illustration, not a quote: on a project costing $20 million, a 70 percent LTC supports a $14 million loan. If the as-complete value is $25 million, that loan is 56 percent of value. If projected stabilized net operating income is $1.4 million, the debt yield is 10 percent. A lender with a minimum debt yield higher than 10 percent would reduce the loan even though the cost and value tests were met.

As a reference point from deals we arrange, construction loans have generally been sized at up to 75 percent of cost, priced at SOFR plus 225 to 600 basis points depending on the property type, sponsor and leverage, with a lender fee typically around one point. Where timing required it, we have closed in under 30 days. Treat these as ranges from our own transactions and not as offers, because current terms vary by lender and project. Our construction loans page and our guide to commercial construction loan requirements explain the requirements in more detail.

How a multifamily construction loan closes

The stages below are common across lenders. Each lender adds its own conditions, so confirm the sequence with the lender early. Our article on how construction loans work goes into more depth.

  1. Define the project: Site control, entitlements or a clear path to them, plans, and a line item budget and schedule.
  2. Assemble the package: Pro forma, general contractor and architect information, sponsor financial statements and a schedule of real estate owned.
  3. Choose the lender and sign a term sheet: Compare leverage, pricing, recourse, completion guarantees and draw terms before paying for third party reports.
  4. Fund the equity requirement: Most lenders require the sponsor's equity to be invested first, before loan proceeds are drawn.
  5. Complete third party reports: Appraisal, environmental review, and a plan and cost review that tests the budget and schedule.
  6. Underwriting and credit approval: The lender confirms sizing against LTC, LTV and debt yield and sets conditions.
  7. Loan documents and closing: Loan agreement, guaranties, title, insurance, and the contractor's agreement and bonds if required.
  8. Draws during construction: Funds are released against inspected progress, with lien waivers and retainage, and the lender monitors the budget and schedule.
  9. Completion and exit: The project reaches completion and lease-up, and the loan is repaid or converted through permanent financing or a sale.

Prepared sponsors move faster through the first four stages, and a clear budget and schedule do more to shorten the process than any other single item. We describe how we run these requests, including our 24 hour response and no upfront fee approach, on our application page.

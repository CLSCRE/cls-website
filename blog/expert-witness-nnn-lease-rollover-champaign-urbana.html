Expert Witness: NNN Lease Rollover Upside and Financing Impact in Champaign-Urbana, IL

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Below-Market NNN Lease and Rollover Upside Analysis

The Champaign-Urbana Market Context

Champaign-Urbana occupies a specific and often underappreciated position in the Illinois commercial real estate landscape. The market is anchored by the University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign, which enrolls roughly 56,000 students and employs tens of thousands of faculty and staff, creating a demand floor that insulates the market from the kind of cyclical vacancy swings that plague purely industrial or office-dependent metros. But the commercial real estate market here, particularly for single-tenant net lease assets, operates according to dynamics that are distinct from Chicago and equally distinct from purely agricultural downstate Illinois markets. Attorneys and investors who treat Champaign-Urbana as a generic secondary Midwest market will consistently misread what lenders actually do here.

For single-tenant NNN assets specifically, the Champaign-Urbana market is dominated by retail corridors along North Prospect Avenue, the Round Barn Road trade area, and the University Avenue and Neil Street corridors closer to campus. These submarkets carry meaningfully different tenancy profiles, credit quality distributions, and achievable rents. A QSR pad site on North Prospect commands different rent per square foot than a single-tenant medical or service-oriented retail building near campus, and lenders price that difference into their underwriting. Retail asking rents in primary Champaign corridors have generally held in the $18 to $28 per square foot NNN range for smaller inline or pad product, with credit-anchored single-tenant deals at the high end or above that band when ground leases are involved.

Vacancy in Champaign-Urbana's single-tenant retail sector has remained relatively contained, in the 6% to 9% range depending on submarket and property size, supported by the university's stabilizing effect on local consumer spending. However, the market is not immune to the national forces affecting NNN retail: the compression of cap rates during the 2020 to 2022 period followed by the significant rate-driven expansion beginning in mid-2022 has left a meaningful cohort of properties with below-market rents locked into leases executed at peak-cycle terms, creating exactly the scenario that generates financing disputes.

The Engagement

Counsel for a party in a commercial real estate dispute retained Commercial Lending Solutions to serve as a financing expert in connection with a single-tenant NNN-leased commercial property located in the Champaign-Urbana metropolitan area. The asset was an approximately 4,500 to 6,000 square foot freestanding retail building occupied by a single national or regional credit tenant under a long-term triple net lease with meaningful term remaining. The dispute centered on the financing implications of the in-place lease structure, specifically whether the below-market rent embedded in the lease precluded the property owner from refinancing at commercially reasonable loan terms, what loan proceeds the property could actually support given the constrained NOI, and whether the rollover upside upon lease expiration was a cognizable financial benefit that lenders would recognize in their underwriting.

The core legal and financial questions presented to CLS CRE were as follows. First, could the property support refinancing at a loan-to-value ratio consistent with market standards for this asset class, given the below-market in-place rent? Second, if the answer to the first question was no, at what LTV could financing be achieved, and what was the resulting financing gap relative to the property's appraised value? Third, do lenders in the current market give credit in their loan sizing to rent rollover upside, meaning the higher market rent the landlord could theoretically achieve when the current lease expires? Fourth, what is the practical effect of remaining lease term on lender appetite and loan structure? These are financing questions, not appraisal questions, and they require someone with active, current underwriting knowledge, not just valuation expertise.

The Financial Analysis

The starting point for any NNN financing analysis is the in-place NOI, not the market NOI, not the pro forma NOI, and not the blended NOI that some parties attempt to construct by averaging current rent with projected rollover rent. Lenders underwrite to the contractual rent stream they can verify today, subject to specific adjustments for credit quality, lease term, and structural features. In this engagement, the in-place rent was meaningfully below current market levels for comparable single-tenant retail space in the relevant Champaign-Urbana submarket, creating a documented gap between in-place NOI and market NOI.

For the purposes of this analysis, I used an in-place annual base rent consistent with the lease in dispute, which generated an effective gross income after standard NNN expense passthrough assumptions of approximately $130,000 to $145,000 per year. Management fee reserves were applied at 3% to 4% of effective gross income even in NNN structures, because lenders require it for stabilized underwriting, and a replacement reserve allowance was applied at $0.15 to $0.25 per square foot annually. The resulting underwritten NOI for loan sizing purposes came to approximately $120,000 to $135,000 depending on the specific reserve assumptions applied.

The DSCR threshold applied was 1.25x, which is the current market standard for single-tenant retail NNN assets in secondary and tertiary markets including Champaign-Urbana. Some lenders will go to 1.20x for investment-grade credit tenants with long remaining term, but for a lease with below-market rent and limited remaining term at rollover, 1.25x is the appropriate standard and I applied it conservatively. The loan was modeled on a 25-year amortization schedule with a 10-year term, which is the standard structure for conventional and agency-adjacent commercial mortgages in this asset class. The all-in interest rate assumption used for underwriting was in the 7.00% to 7.50% range, consistent with what conduit and portfolio lenders were pricing for single-tenant secondary market retail during the relevant period. At a 7.25% rate on 25-year amortization, the constant is approximately 8.57%.

Working backward from the debt service coverage constraint: if the underwritten NOI is $127,500 (midpoint of the range), the maximum annual debt service at 1.25x DSCR is $127,500 divided by 1.25, which equals $102,000. At a mortgage constant of 8.57%, the maximum supportable loan is $102,000 divided by 0.0857, which equals approximately $1,190,000. If the property's appraised value was in the $1,800,000 to $2,100,000 range, which is plausible for a single-tenant NNN retail asset of this type in Champaign-Urbana depending on cap rate applied, then the DSCR-constrained maximum loan of roughly $1,190,000 represents a loan-to-value ratio of approximately 57% to 66%. The standard maximum LTV for this asset class from most lenders is 70% to 75%. This means the DSCR constraint, not the LTV constraint, is the binding limitation, and a borrower who assumed they could refinance at 70% LTV would discover in the market that the in-place income simply does not support it.

The financing gap, meaning the difference between what a borrower might have expected at 70% LTV and what the market would actually lend given the below-market rent, represents a quantifiable economic injury in disputes where that gap is causally connected to the actions of a counterparty.

The Complicating Factors

Below-Market Rent and Lender Treatment of Rollover Upside

The single most contested financing question in this type of dispute is whether lenders give credit for rollover upside. The short answer is: they do not, in any material way that affects loan sizing. Lenders underwrite to the in-place rent because that is the contractual cash flow that services the debt. The fact that the market rent is $22 per square foot when the in-place rent is $15 per square foot is not a financing asset; it is a hypothetical future benefit that the lender cannot lend against because the tenant has the legal right to remain at the contracted rent until lease expiration. Some borrowers and their advisors argue that rollover upside should be recognized as a form of latent value. Lenders reject this argument at the credit committee level because the benefit is contingent: the lease must expire, the tenant must not exercise renewal options at existing rent, the landlord must successfully re-lease at market, and market conditions must remain favorable. None of those conditions are within the lender's collateral control.

Remaining Lease Term and Lender Appetite

In the Champaign-Urbana market, where the institutional lender presence is less dense than Chicago, the treatment of remaining lease term is particularly acute. A single-tenant NNN asset with fewer than seven years of remaining lease term typically requires either a non-recourse carveout structure or full recourse from a creditworthy guarantor to attract institutional lender interest. Below five years of remaining term, most CMBS conduits will not quote at all, and portfolio lenders will require significant cash equity cushion or shortened loan terms that dramatically increase refinancing risk for the borrower. If the lease in dispute has a remaining term that falls inside the loan maturity window, the lender faces dark building risk at maturity, which is the single greatest credit concern in single-tenant underwriting.

Tenant Credit Quality and Secondary Market Discount

Champaign-Urbana is not a top-20 MSA, and lenders apply a secondary market location adjustment that reduces both the LTV ceiling and the pricing competitiveness of quotes. An investment-grade tenant in a primary market might support a 75% LTV at aggressive pricing; the same tenant in Champaign-Urbana more realistically supports 65% to 70% LTV at a 25 to 50 basis point spread premium. If the tenant is a non-investment-grade regional or local credit, the adjustment is more severe, and some lenders simply will not participate regardless of the lease structure.

Lease Structure and Lender Assignability Concerns

NNN leases frequently contain provisions restricting assignment of the lease or limiting the landlord's ability to transfer the property without tenant consent. These provisions, while common, can create lender concerns about the ability to enforce their security interest in the event of default and foreclosure. In secondary markets like Champaign-Urbana, where the pool of potential replacement tenants for a single-purpose building is limited, lenders scrutinize lease assignability language carefully and may require landlord estoppels and subordination, non-disturbance, and attornment agreements as loan conditions.

The Champaign-Urbana Lender Market

Understanding who will actually lend on a single-tenant NNN asset in Champaign-Urbana at a given moment in time is critical context for any financing dispute. The lender market here is not homogeneous, and each category of lender has a different credit box, pricing approach, and appetite for the specific risk profile presented by a below-market lease with rollover upside.

Community and regional banks are the most active lenders in the Champaign-Urbana commercial market. Institutions based in Champaign, Bloomington, Springfield, and the broader Central Illinois corridor maintain active commercial real estate lending programs and have local market knowledge that national lenders lack. These lenders generally price in the 6.75% to 7.75% range for single-tenant retail, require full or partial recourse, and will lend up to 70% LTV with 1.20x to 1.25x DSCR minimums. Their advantage is relationship flexibility; their limitation is balance sheet capacity, meaning loans above $5,000,000 to $7,000,000 often require participations or push borrowers to larger institutions.

CMBS conduit lenders are technically available for Champaign-Urbana single-tenant NNN assets but impose meaningful constraints. Loan minimums of $2,000,000 to $3,000,000, strict non-recourse structures, and rigid credit box parameters around lease term and tenant quality mean that many of the assets in this market that generate disputes do not qualify for CMBS execution. When they do qualify, CMBS offers non-recourse at competitive pricing but with yield maintenance or defeasance prepayment structures that can create their own complications in litigation.

Life company lenders are effectively absent from single-tenant retail in secondary Illinois markets below a certain asset quality and loan size threshold. Life companies are currently pricing at the tightest spreads in the market but are focused on institutional-quality assets in primary or strong secondary markets. A Champaign-Urbana NNN retail asset with a below-market lease is not a life company deal.

Debt funds and bridge lenders are relevant only if the deal is being structured as a value-add or transitional play, meaning the borrower is explicitly acknowledging the below-market lease as a repositioning opportunity and seeking bridge financing to carry the asset to rollover. These lenders price at 9.00% to 11.00% or higher in current market conditions and typically impose 12-month to 36-month terms with extension options tied to leasing milestones. Bridge financing confirms, rather than resolves, the below-market lease problem from a dispute perspective.

What Attorneys Handling Champaign-Urbana CRE Disputes Need to Know

Retaining a CRE financing expert is appropriate whenever the dispute involves a question that turns on what a lender would or would not do, what a borrower could or could not accomplish in the capital markets, or what the financing impact of a lease provision, a damaged credit event, or a forced sale condition actually is in dollar terms. Appraisers value properties; they do not model lender underwriting, they do not know current DSCR thresholds by lender category, and they are not qualified to testify about whether a specific loan would have been approved or what terms it would have carried.

A CRE financing expert can answer questions that sit outside the appraiser's lane: Would a lender have approved this loan given the lease as written? What is the maximum loan the income stream supports? Is the rollover upside recognizable in underwriting? What is the cost of carry between in-place financing and market-rate refinancing? What lender categories were realistically available to this borrower in this market at this time? These questions are central to damages calculations in breach of contract, lease disputes, partnership dissolution matters, and bankruptcy plan feasibility hearings.

What makes a CRE financing expert qualified, as opposed to a retired banker or a generalist real estate consultant, is current, active market participation. I actively originate and place commercial mortgage transactions across 50 states through CLS CRE, maintain relationships with more than 1,000 institutional and private lenders, and have closed approximately $1 billion in transaction volume. That current activity means my testimony about what lenders are doing today, or what they were doing at a specific point in the past, is grounded in actual deal experience rather than historical memory or academic theory.

Before engaging a financing expert, counsel should gather the following materials: the complete lease including all amendments and option schedules; any existing loan documents including any prior appraisals or lender underwriting memoranda; the property's historical rent rolls and operating statements; any correspondence between the parties touching on financing, refinancing, or lease renewal; and any appraisals already obtained. The expert will also need market data on comparable lease rates and comparable sales to contextualize the financing analysis within current market conditions.

At trial or in a bankruptcy plan hearing, CRE financing expert testimony is most effective when it is anchored to specific, quantified conclusions. The opinion that "a lender would not have approved this loan" is far less useful to the trier of fact than the opinion that "at the in-place rent of $X per square foot, the maximum supportable loan is $Y, representing a Z% LTV, compared to the $W loan amount the borrower required to execute the transaction, creating a $V financing gap that is directly attributable to the below-market lease structure." Quantified testimony survives cross-examination more effectively and provides the fact-finder with a usable damages framework.

Key Takeaways

  • Lenders underwrite to in-place NOI, not market NOI. The fact that rents are below market does not give the borrower any additional loan proceeds. The contractual rent is the underwritten income, full stop, and rollover upside is not a credit in loan sizing calculations.
  • DSCR, not LTV, is frequently the binding constraint on a below-market lease asset. A property may have appraised value that would support a 70% LTV loan, but if the in-place NOI cannot service the debt at the required 1.25x coverage, the loan the market will provide is meaningfully smaller than the loan the LTV would theoretically allow.

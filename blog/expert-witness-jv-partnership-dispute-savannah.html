Expert Witness: CRE Partnership and Waterfall Disputes in Savannah, GA

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Joint Venture and Partnership Dispute

The Savannah Market Context

Savannah, Georgia has evolved from a regional port city into one of the Southeast's most dynamic commercial real estate markets, and that evolution has created exactly the kind of high-stakes joint venture structures that end up in litigation. The Georgia Ports Authority's Garden City Terminal is the fastest-growing container port in the United States by volume, and the ripple effects of that growth have transformed industrial, multifamily, and mixed-use investment activity across Chatham County and the surrounding coastal counties. Investors from Atlanta, New York, and Los Angeles recognized this inflection point early, and they formed joint ventures to capitalize on it. Those partnerships were often formed quickly, on deal terms that were market-standard for the moment but poorly suited for the market volatility that followed.

On the industrial side, Savannah's vacancy rate has hovered between 2% and 5% for Class A warehouse and logistics product, with net absorption consistently outpacing new deliveries as demand from third-party logistics operators, e-commerce distribution users, and port-adjacent manufacturers continues. Asking rents for Class A industrial in the Pooler and Garden City submarkets have climbed from roughly $4.50 to $5.00 per square foot net in 2019 to $7.00 to $8.50 per square foot net in recent years, representing rent growth that materially altered the equity return projections embedded in joint venture agreements signed at acquisition. That rent growth created promoted interest disputes, because the party holding the promote argued that performance thresholds were met, while the capital partner argued that the waterfall mechanics were never properly triggered under the agreement's specific calculation methodology.

Multifamily fundamentals in Savannah tell a similar story. Class A apartment communities in the Historic District core, Midtown, and the growing southside corridors have seen effective rents increase substantially, though new supply has begun to compress concessions in some submarkets. Cap rates for stabilized multifamily assets in Savannah traded in the 4.25% to 5.00% range during the peak acquisition years of 2021 and 2022, and that compressed pricing created thin equity cushions in many joint venture structures. When those same assets were refinanced or disposition was considered, the math around preferred return accruals and waterfall sequencing became genuinely contentious.

The Engagement

Counsel for a limited partner in a Savannah-area commercial real estate joint venture retained Commercial Lending Solutions to provide expert analysis of the capital stack structure, the financing terms actually obtained at closing, and the downstream effect of those financing terms on waterfall distribution mechanics available to each party throughout the hold period. The asset at issue was a mixed-use development in the Savannah urban core incorporating ground-floor retail, office space on mid-floors, and multifamily residential on upper floors, acquired during a period of aggressive investment activity in the submarket.

The core legal and financial questions we were retained to address fell into three categories. First, whether the construction financing terms and subsequent permanent loan terms obtained by the general partner were consistent with market standards that a competent and reasonably diligent sponsor would have secured at the time of each financing event. Second, whether the preferred return calculation methodology applied by the managing member correctly accounted for the timing and source of each cash distribution, including the treatment of refinancing proceeds as a return of capital or a distribution triggering waterfall sequencing. Third, whether the promoted interest claimed by the general partner was legitimately earned under the agreement's internal rate of return hurdle, given that the IRR calculation embedded disputed assumptions about the cost of debt service and the treatment of certain fees paid to an affiliate of the general partner.

This is the specific type of engagement where a commercial mortgage origination expert adds value that neither an appraiser nor a general real estate consultant can provide. The questions were not about value. They were about financing structure, market-standard loan terms, lender underwriting practice, and the mechanical relationship between debt service obligations and the equity waterfall.

The Financial Analysis

Our analysis began with a reconstruction of the stabilized net operating income at the time the permanent financing was obtained. For a mixed-use asset of this type in the Savannah urban core, we analyzed each income component separately: ground-floor retail at market rents for the relevant submarket and vintage, office space at prevailing Class B rents given the building's positioning, and multifamily at achievable effective rents net of concessions for comparable unit types. Gross potential income was then adjusted for vacancy and credit loss appropriate to each component, with retail vacancy modeled at 7% to 10%, office at 12% to 15%, and residential at 5% to 7%, reflecting market conditions at stabilization.

The resulting blended NOI for the asset was approximately $1,850,000 annually at stabilization, reflecting the mixed-use income stack after operating expenses, management fees, and replacement reserves were properly deducted. This figure was critical to our analysis because the general partner had used a materially higher NOI figure in its waterfall calculations, which had the effect of inflating the promoted interest claim by overstating the equity multiple achieved.

On the debt side, we applied market-standard permanent loan underwriting to assess what a competent lender would have required at the time of placement. For a mixed-use asset in a secondary market like Savannah, lenders applying CMBS or life company standards would have required a minimum debt service coverage ratio of 1.25x, with many conventional lenders targeting 1.30x given the retail and office income components, which carry higher credit risk than pure residential income. At a 1.25x DSCR threshold, with interest rates prevailing at the time of the financing event and a 30-year amortization schedule, the maximum supportable loan on $1,850,000 of NOI worked as follows:

  • Stabilized NOI: $1,850,000
  • Maximum annual debt service at 1.25x DSCR: $1,480,000 ($1,850,000 divided by 1.25)
  • Assumed note rate: 6.25% fixed, consistent with CMBS pricing in the relevant period for secondary-market mixed-use collateral
  • Amortization: 30 years, with a 10-year term
  • Mortgage constant at 6.25% / 30-year amortization: approximately 7.36% annually
  • Maximum supportable loan: $1,480,000 divided by 0.0736 = approximately $20,100,000
  • Appraised value at closing: approximately $32,500,000, placing the max loan-to-value at approximately 61.8%

This was significant because the actual loan obtained was $22,500,000, representing a 69.2% LTV against the appraised value. The general partner had represented to limited partners that the financing was "market-standard," but our analysis demonstrated that the loan was obtained at an LTV that exceeded what most conventional lenders would have approved on this collateral type, suggesting either that the NOI was presented to the lender on optimistic assumptions or that the financing source was a bridge or transitional lender accepting higher leverage in exchange for higher pricing. That distinction mattered for the waterfall analysis because the higher debt load increased annual debt service, reduced available cash flow for distribution, and delayed the point at which the preferred return threshold was fully satisfied.

The Complicating Factors

The Treatment of Refinancing Proceeds in the Waterfall. The most technically contested issue in this engagement was whether cash-out proceeds from a mid-hold refinancing should have been characterized as a return of capital or as a distribution triggering the waterfall sequence. The joint venture agreement used language that is common in market-standard documents but genuinely ambiguous in application: it provided that cash from capital events would flow through the waterfall after the preferred return was satisfied, but it did not expressly define whether a refinancing constituted a "capital event" as distinguished from an "operating distribution." In our expert analysis, we reviewed market-standard joint venture documents from institutional sponsors active in the Southeast to demonstrate how practitioners in the industry uniformly treat refinancing proceeds, and we provided context for how lenders think about the tax and structural implications of cash-out refinancing in a JV structure. This type of analysis sits squarely in the CRE financing expert's wheelhouse and outside the scope of appraisal testimony.

Affiliate Fee Treatment and IRR Dilution. The general partner's entity had charged asset management fees, construction supervision fees, and a loan guaranty fee to the joint venture during the hold period. Each of these fees reduced the cash available for distribution and, in some calculations, were deducted before the preferred return accrual was calculated. The question of whether those fees were market-standard for a Savannah sponsor managing an asset of this complexity required us to examine what arms-length institutional operators and regional developers actually charge for these services, and to assess whether the fee structure complied with what a reasonable investor would have negotiated at the time the venture was formed. The IRR hurdle that triggered the promote was sensitive to these inputs: adjusting fee treatment by even 0.5% annually over a five-year hold materially changed whether the hurdle was crossed.

Market Disruption and Preferred Return Accrual. The COVID-19 period created genuine cash flow disruptions for mixed-use assets in Savannah, as it did nationally, and the parties disputed whether the preferred return continued to accrue during the period when distributions were suspended. This required analysis of how lenders actually modified loans during that period, whether debt service obligations were deferred or forgiven, and how the suspension of operating cash flow affected the mechanics of a cumulative preferred return. We analyzed loan modification documentation to establish the actual cost of debt during the disruption period and compared it to the preferred return accrual claimed by the limited partner.

The Savannah Lender Market

Savannah's commercial mortgage market is more sophisticated than many attorneys and investors from larger metros expect. The market has meaningful depth across several lender categories, and understanding which categories were available at each point in the deal's timeline is essential to expert analysis of whether the general partner exercised sound judgment in sourcing and structuring the debt.

Regional and Community Banks. Institutions headquartered in Savannah, Brunswick, and across coastal Georgia maintain active commercial real estate lending programs. For mixed-use and multifamily product, these lenders typically operate at 65% to 75% LTV, require personal recourse guarantees, and price at spreads to the Prime Rate or SOFR that reflect relationship-based underwriting. Their DSCR floors are typically 1.20x to 1.25x, and they generally require local market experience from the sponsor. These lenders were the most likely execution for construction lending on a Savannah urban core project below $20,000,000.

CMBS and Conduit Lenders. For permanent financing on stabilized assets above $10,000,000 in Savannah, CMBS conduit lenders are a credible execution. They offer non-recourse structures, fixed-rate terms, and the ability to hold assets without ongoing bank relationship management. However, their underwriting on mixed-use collateral requires each income component to meet minimum occupancy and lease term standards, and their credit boxes are less flexible on secondary market locations with retail exposure. Prepayment is typically structured as defeasance or yield maintenance, which becomes a complicating factor in waterfall analysis when early disposition is contemplated.

Debt Funds and Bridge Lenders. Several debt funds active in the Southeast maintain active pipelines in Savannah, particularly for value-add and transitional assets. These lenders price at spreads that reflect the execution certainty and speed they provide, typically ranging from SOFR plus 300 to SOFR plus 550 depending on leverage and collateral type. For a joint venture dispute, the choice to use bridge financing rather than conventional permanent financing is itself a subject of expert analysis, because that choice affects the cost of capital embedded in the waterfall and the timeline to a refinancing or disposition event.

Life Insurance Company Lenders. Several life company lenders and their correspondents are active in Savannah for institutional-quality stabilized assets. Life company execution in this market is typically available at 55% to 65% LTV, at rates that reflect the tightest institutional pricing available, and with the most conservative underwriting standards applied to income. When available, life company financing is generally the lowest-cost permanent execution, and a failure to pursue it when it was accessible is a meaningful data point in assessing the general partner's financing decisions.

What Attorneys Handling Savannah CRE Disputes Need to Know

The decision about when to retain a commercial real estate financing expert is one that many litigators make too late in the case. By the time trial preparation begins, the factual record around financing terms, lender communications, and underwriting documentation has often been incompletely developed because no one on the litigation team had the background to recognize what was missing. Retaining a CRE financing expert early, ideally before or during written discovery, allows counsel to identify the specific loan documents, lender correspondence, underwriting models, and rate lock communications that are necessary to build or defend the financing-related claims.

An appraiser can tell you what an asset was worth on a given date. A CRE financing expert tells you what that value meant in the context of the capital markets, what loan terms the value would have supported, whether the terms actually obtained were consistent with the market, and how the debt structure interacted with the equity waterfall. Those are different questions, and they require different qualifications. A qualified CRE financing expert should have active, current experience originating commercial mortgages across multiple lender categories, a demonstrated understanding of how different capital sources underwrite and price risk, and a track record of advising on complex capital stack structures. A retired loan officer from a single institution may have experience but lacks the market breadth to testify credibly about what the full financing universe would have supported.

Before the expert engagement begins, counsel should gather the following categories of documents: the joint venture operating agreement and all amendments; the construction loan agreement and any modifications; the permanent loan documents including rate lock agreements and application letters; the lender's internal credit approval or term sheet; all financial statements submitted to the lender; the asset manager's distributions calculations and supporting workpapers; any appraisals commissioned during the hold period; and all correspondence between the general partner and any lender, mortgage broker, or capital markets advisor. These documents allow the expert to reconstruct the financing timeline, assess the decisions made at each point, and provide quantified analysis of the impact on the waterfall.

At trial or in a binding arbitration, CRE financing expert testimony is most powerful when it converts abstract contractual disputes into concrete financial analysis. A jury or arbitrator who understands that a specific financing decision added $180,000 per year to debt service, which delayed the preferred return satisfaction by 14 months, which in turn affected the promoted interest calculation by $620,000, can evaluate the dispute with precision rather than inference.

Key Takeaways

  • Savannah's rapid rent and value growth has created genuine waterfall disputes. Joint ventures formed during the market's expansion period often embedded return assumptions that either dramatically outperformed or underperformed original projections, and the resulting disagreements over promoted interest and preferred return calculations are legally complex and financially material.
  • NOI construction is not appraisal testimony. Expert analysis of what income the asset actually generated, versus what the general partner represented to investors or lenders, requires deep underwriting knowledge and cannot be fully addressed by a valuation expert alone.
  • DSCR and LTV constraints are quantifiable. Market-standard underwriting thresholds of 1.20x to 1.25x DSCR and 65% to 75% LTV for Savannah mixed-use and multifamily assets can be applied to determine precisely what loan size and terms a competent sponsor should have obtained, and deviations from those standards can be quantified and expressed in dollar terms.
  • Refinancing proceeds treatment is a recurring and technically specific dispute. Whether a cash-out refinancing constitutes a capital event triggering the waterfall requires expert analysis of market-standard JV agreement mechanics, not just contract interpretation, and the expert can provide industry-standard context that an attorney alone cannot.
  • Affiliate fee structures materially affect IRR hurdle calculations. Asset management, supervision, and guaranty fees paid to general partner affiliates must be evaluated against market-standard fee structures to determine whether they were at arms-length levels and how they affected the return metrics that govern the promote.
  • Savannah's lender market has real depth across multiple capital source categories. Demonstrating what financing alternatives were available to a competent sponsor at each point in the deal's timeline is a critical expert function, because the choice of lender and structure is itself a fiduciary decision that can be evaluated against market standards.
  • Early expert retention changes the quality of the factual record. Counsel who engage a CRE financing expert before discovery closes can develop the documentary record needed for compelling expert

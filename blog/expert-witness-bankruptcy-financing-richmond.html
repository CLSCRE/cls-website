Expert Witness: CRE Financing Feasibility in Chapter 11 Proceedings in Richmond, VA

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Bankruptcy Exit Financing

The Richmond Market Context

Richmond, Virginia occupies a distinct position in the mid-Atlantic commercial real estate landscape. It is not a gateway market in the institutional sense of New York or Los Angeles, but it is far from a tertiary backwater. The metro anchors a regional economy built around state government employment, healthcare systems, financial services, and a manufacturing base that has quietly modernized over the past two decades. That composition matters enormously in a bankruptcy exit financing context, because the income stability of commercial assets here tends to hold up better through credit cycles than comparably sized markets driven by a single cyclical sector.

On the industrial side, the Richmond market has been one of the stronger performing submarkets in the Southeast corridor. Vacancy in the bulk distribution and last-mile segments has hovered in the low-to-mid single digits through the post-pandemic period, with asking rents rising meaningfully from historical averages. The I-95 corridor positioning and the Port of Virginia's inland terminal presence at the Deepwater Terminal make Richmond logistics assets legitimate targets for institutional capital, including bridge lenders and agency-adjacent debt funds that might otherwise skip a market of this size.

Multifamily fundamentals in Richmond remain constructive. Cap rates for stabilized Class B assets in established submarkets have generally ranged between 5.25% and 6.25%, depending on vintage, location, and unit mix, with urban core and near-university assets compressing closer to the lower end of that band. Rent growth has moderated from the exceptional pace of 2021 and 2022, but occupancy across the metro has stayed above 93% in most professionally managed properties. For a Plan of Reorganization proceeding, those occupancy levels provide a foundation for stabilized NOI underwriting that lenders can stress-test without assuming heroic lease-up projections.

Office is the exception and the source of significant analytical complexity. Richmond's suburban office market has struggled with the same secular demand shifts affecting most mid-size metros: elevated vacancy in Class B suburban product, shortened lease durations, and a bifurcation between well-located Class A trophy space and everything else. Any bankruptcy estate asset involving office collateral in Richmond requires a more granular underwriting approach, because the spread between lender-accepted stabilized occupancy and actual current occupancy can be wide enough to substantially reduce or eliminate financeable value.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by counsel representing a plan proponent in a Chapter 11 proceeding pending in the Eastern District of Virginia. The debtor's estate included a mid-size multifamily property in the Richmond metro, a partially stabilized asset with in-place occupancy in the low-to-mid 80% range at the time the petition was filed. The plan contemplated a consensual sale or, alternatively, a refinancing-based plan that would pay senior secured creditors in full at confirmation and provide a recovery path for general unsecured creditors through a reorganized debtor structure.

Counsel retained CLS CRE to produce a written expert opinion addressing two central questions the court would need to resolve at the confirmation hearing. First: could the subject property, based on its current and stabilized income profile, support first-mortgage financing sufficient in amount to satisfy the senior secured claim? Second: what categories of institutional lenders actively originate loans on commercial real estate assets that are or have recently been bankruptcy estate property, and what are the material terms and conditions those lenders impose?

These questions are distinct from valuation questions. The appraisal in the case addressed the property's market value under conventional assumptions. Our engagement addressed something different: whether a real-world lender, underwriting to actual market standards in early 2024, would issue a commitment letter on this asset in this factual context, and if so, on what terms. Those are financing feasibility questions, not valuation questions, and they require expertise in active lending markets rather than comparable sales analysis.

Our work product included a written Rule 26 expert report, a lender market survey documenting active origination programs in the Richmond multifamily space, and a supporting financial model that could be stress-tested under cross-examination. The report was prepared to withstand Daubert scrutiny, with methodology explicitly documented and conclusions grounded in verifiable market data rather than generalized assertion.

The Financial Analysis

The core analytical question in any exit financing feasibility opinion is straightforward in structure but demanding in execution: given the property's demonstrable income stream, what is the maximum first-mortgage loan amount a lender operating under current market standards would originate, and does that amount satisfy the senior claim?

We began with the property's trailing 12-month rent roll and operating statements, which the debtor's counsel provided from the court record. For a partially stabilized multifamily asset, trailing actuals alone are insufficient. We constructed a stabilized NOI estimate by projecting the property to a market-supportable occupancy level, which for this asset and submarket we set at 92%, and applying current market rents for the applicable unit mix based on comparable lease data from the Richmond metro. We then applied a 5% vacancy and credit loss allowance and built out operating expense assumptions using published Richmond apartment market benchmarks and the property's own historical expense ratios where the latter were not distorted by the bankruptcy period.

The resulting stabilized NOI, for purposes of illustrating the methodology with figures appropriate to this asset type and scale, was approximately $620,000 annually. That figure represented our conservative but defensible estimate of what the property would generate under professional management at market occupancy, after all operating expenses but before debt service.

Lenders underwriting multifamily assets in the current rate environment, including agency programs through Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac and balance sheet bridge programs, typically require a Debt Service Coverage Ratio of 1.20x to 1.25x at the loan amount being tested. For conventional bank and credit union portfolio lenders, 1.25x is the more common floor, and some are applying 1.30x given current rate levels. We used 1.25x as our primary DSCR threshold, with sensitivity analysis at 1.20x and 1.30x.

On rate and amortization, we assumed a 30-year amortization schedule with an interest rate of 6.75% for a conventional agency execution, reflecting the market as of the analysis date. That produces an annual debt service constant of approximately 7.81% of the loan amount. Dividing stabilized NOI of $620,000 by the 1.25x DSCR requirement yields maximum allowable annual debt service of $496,000. Dividing that by the 7.81% constant produces a maximum supportable loan amount of approximately $6,350,000.

We then cross-checked that figure against LTV constraints. Agency programs on multifamily assets allow up to 80% LTV in standard executions, and the appraised value in the case supported an LTV-derived maximum that was higher than the DSCR-derived figure. In other words, the binding constraint was the debt service coverage test, not the LTV ceiling. That is a critical distinction in expert testimony: it tells the court that the property has value headroom above the loan amount, which is relevant to the feasibility argument and to the question of whether senior creditors are adequately protected.

The senior secured claim in this matter was approximately $5,800,000. Our analysis showed a maximum supportable loan of $6,350,000 under conservative assumptions, providing a margin of approximately $550,000 above the claim amount. Under the base-case rate assumption, the plan's financing component was feasible. We also ran sensitivity analysis showing that even at a 7.25% interest rate, a 50-basis-point increase from our base case, the supportable loan amount remained above the senior claim, providing the court with confidence that the feasibility conclusion was not rate-assumption-dependent.

The Complicating Factors

Bankruptcy stigma and lender credit box exclusions. The most immediate complicating factor in any bankruptcy exit financing engagement is the reaction of the conventional lending community to the bankruptcy history itself. Most agency lenders, including Fannie Mae DUS lenders and Freddie Mac Program Plus sellers, will not originate a loan on a property while it remains in bankruptcy, and many require a seasoning period of six to twelve months post-confirmation before they will accept the application. This is not a credit quality judgment about the property; it is an operational and compliance constraint built into their origination guidelines. Counsel who assume that the cheapest financing will be available at confirmation are frequently wrong. The first lenders in the queue are typically bridge lenders and debt funds, not agency programs, and that has cost implications.

In-place occupancy below stabilization. When the property's actual occupancy at the time of the financing inquiry is materially below stabilized underwriting levels, lenders cannot use full stabilized NOI to size the loan. Many bridge lenders will size to a combination of in-place NOI and a projected stabilized value, but they will apply a lower advance rate against the as-stabilized value. For an asset at 82% occupancy in a market that normalizes at 92%, a lender may apply a 65% to 70% LTV against as-stabilized appraised value rather than 75% to 80%, effectively reducing the available loan proceeds. Understanding exactly where each lender category draws this line is a core component of the financing feasibility analysis.

Deferred maintenance and capital needs. Bankruptcy-era properties frequently suffer deferred maintenance. Lenders conducting physical due diligence will identify capital requirements and typically require a funded reserve, either at closing or through a holdback structure, to address them. Those reserves reduce net loan proceeds available to pay creditors. In our analysis, we identified the property's likely capital reserve requirement based on the available inspection records and adjusted the net proceeds available for claim satisfaction accordingly. Failing to account for this at the plan stage creates feasibility opinions that collapse during lender due diligence.

Management continuity and operational track record. Lenders underwriting bankruptcy exit transactions scrutinize the proposed post-confirmation management team carefully. If the reorganized debtor intends to retain the same management that presided over the distress, many institutional lenders will require a third-party property management agreement with a qualified regional or national operator as a loan condition. In the Richmond multifamily market, that is achievable, there are several qualified operators active in the metro, but it adds a closing condition that the plan must contemplate and counsel must address in the feasibility narrative.

The Richmond Lender Market

For a multifamily bankruptcy exit financing in the Richmond metro, the realistic lender universe falls into several distinct categories, each with its own credit box, pricing, and timeline characteristics.

Bridge debt funds and mortgage REITs are the most immediately accessible capital source for bankruptcy-tainted assets. These lenders generally do not have the same origination guideline restrictions as agency sellers. They will underwrite to current in-place cash flow, size the loan conservatively against stabilized value, and price at a spread over SOFR that typically runs 350 to 550 basis points depending on deal quality and sponsor strength. Loan terms are generally two to three years with extension options. For a Richmond mid-size multifamily asset, these lenders can frequently close within 45 to 60 days of a signed term sheet, which is relevant to plan timeline feasibility.

Regional and community banks represent the second tier. Virginia-chartered community banks and regional institutions have historically been active in the Richmond CRE market, and some maintain portfolio lending programs with more flexibility than national bank standards. However, their appetite for bankruptcy-connected assets varies significantly by institution, and their loan sizing tends to be more conservative, often capped at 65% to 70% LTV on multifamily product. Their pricing advantage over debt funds can be meaningful, but their credit approval timelines are longer and their tolerance for title complexity associated with Chapter 11 exits is not uniform.

Agency execution through Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac is the long-term refinancing target for stabilized multifamily assets, and for exit financing, it becomes the expected takeout for the bridge loan. Agency pricing in the current market for Richmond multifamily runs approximately 50 to 100 basis points inside comparable bank portfolio pricing, and leverage is higher. CLS CRE maintains active correspondent relationships with multiple Fannie DUS lenders and Freddie Program Plus sellers who have closed loans on Richmond assets, and our understanding of their current appetite and underwriting standards informed the rate and sizing assumptions in this expert opinion.

CMBS conduit lenders are generally not the right execution for this asset type and complexity level in Richmond. CMBS conduit programs have rigid underwriting standards and require clean title and borrower history. Bankruptcy history in the past several years will typically disqualify a borrower from conduit execution entirely.

What Attorneys Handling Richmond CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to retain a CRE financing expert. In any Chapter 11 proceeding where a plan proponent intends to satisfy secured creditors through refinancing rather than sale, financing feasibility is a confirmation requirement under Section 1129 of the Bankruptcy Code. An attorney cannot satisfy this requirement with general argument or with an appraiser's opinion of value alone. The court needs to hear from someone who can testify that actual lenders, operating under current market standards, would issue a commitment on these terms. That is a financing expert's function, not an appraiser's function.

What a financing expert can answer that an appraiser cannot. An appraiser testifies about value. A CRE financing expert testifies about debt capacity, lender market conditions, credit box constraints, and the feasibility of a specific financing structure. These are related but distinct disciplines. The question "what is this property worth?" is different from the question "can this property support a $6 million loan at current market rates, and which lenders would actually make that loan?" The second question requires current market knowledge of lender underwriting criteria, not comparable sales analysis.

What qualifies an expert vs. a retired banker. A retired community banker can testify about how one institution underwrites loans. A qualified CRE financing expert has active market access across multiple lender categories simultaneously, including agency, CMBS, bridge, bank, and credit union programs. At CLS CRE, our qualifications rest on approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, relationships with more than 1,000 lenders, and active originations in all 50 states. We are not analyzing historical market conditions from memory; we are describing a market we are operating in daily.

Information counsel should gather before the engagement. To produce a reliable expert opinion on a compressed timeline, counsel should have available the current rent roll, the trailing 12-month operating statement, any existing appraisal or valuation, a current title report or commitment, any inspection or PCA reports, and the full secured claim documentation including the loan agreement and any forbearance history. We can work with partial information and flag gaps, but complete documentation produces a more defensible opinion.

How financing expert testimony is used at confirmation hearings. Expert testimony on financing feasibility appears both in the written Rule 26 report and in live testimony at the confirmation hearing. The opposing side will typically challenge the rate assumptions, the stabilized NOI methodology, and the lender market survey. A well-prepared financing expert can defend each element because the methodology is documented and the lender market data is verifiable. Counsel should anticipate cross-examination on sensitivity analysis and should ensure the expert has run the numbers at rates materially higher than the base case assumption.

Key Takeaways

  • Financing feasibility is a legal requirement, not just a strategic narrative. Under Section 1129, a Chapter 11 plan premised on refinancing must demonstrate that the financing is actually available on the proposed terms. A written expert opinion from an active market participant satisfies this evidentiary requirement in a way that counsel's argument alone cannot.
  • DSCR, not LTV, is frequently the binding constraint. In the current rate environment, the debt service coverage test limits loan sizing more often than the loan-to-value ceiling. Expert analysis must work through both tests and identify which is binding, because the answer affects both the feasibility conclusion and the adequate protection analysis for secured creditors.
  • Bankruptcy stigma narrows the lender universe but does not eliminate it. Bridge debt funds and mortgage REITs actively originate on bankruptcy-tainted assets. Agency lenders require seasoning. CMBS is generally unavailable. Understanding this segmentation is essential to a credible lender market opinion.
  • Deferred maintenance and reserve requirements reduce net proceeds. Any feasibility analysis that fails to account for lender-required capital reserves will overstate available loan proceeds and may produce a plan structure that collapses at the commitment stage.

The metro's occupancy profile, rent growth trajectory, and cap rate environment make stabilized multifamily assets here financeable across multiple lender categories, which is favorable for plan feasibility arguments.

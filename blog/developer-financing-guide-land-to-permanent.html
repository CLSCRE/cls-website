Commercial Real Estate Developer Financing: From Land Acquisition to Permanent Loan

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Commercial Lending Solutions

The Developer Financing Timeline: More Refinances Than You Think

Most developers I talk to underestimate how many discrete financing events stand between a land acquisition and a stabilized, permanently financed asset. They budget for the construction loan and the permanent loan. What they actually encounter is five separate capital events, each with its own underwriting logic, its own lender universe, and its own rate environment. Miss one and you stall the deal.

Here is the complete map:

  1. Land Acquisition: cash or land loan, 50-60% LTV maximum
  2. Pre-Development: soft costs, entitlements, feasibility (mostly equity)
  3. Construction Loan: the largest and most complex event
  4. Lease-Up Bridge: post-completion, pre-stabilization
  5. Permanent Takeout: the prize: long-term, fixed-rate debt

From land close to permanent loan, a typical ground-up multifamily or mixed-use development takes 36 to 60 months and involves refinancing or recapitalizing the project three to four times. Each transition carries execution risk. Each requires a different lender relationship. This guide walks through every stage with the specificity you need to avoid the most common and costly mistakes.

Stage 1: Land Acquisition Financing

Cash Is King, But Not Always Required

The cleanest land acquisition is an all-cash close. You control the timeline, you negotiate better, and you enter the pre-development phase unencumbered by a high-rate land loan ticking against you. For smaller infill sites under $5 million, cash acquisition is the default for institutional sponsors.

That said, land financing exists: it is just expensive and heavily constrained.

Land Loan Parameters

Metric Typical Range Notes
Maximum LTV 50-60% Most lenders will not exceed 50% on raw land
Interest Rate Prime + 1.5% to 3% Variable; currently 9.5% to 11.5% range
Term 12-36 months Short; assumes entitlement completion
Recourse Full recourse No exceptions at this stage
Entitlement requirement Often required Most banks want approved or entitled land

Community banks and regional lenders are the primary land loan providers. Life companies, agency lenders, CMBS, and debt funds do not touch raw or entitling land. Even banks that will lend typically require the borrower to demonstrate a clear entitlement path, a defined development program, and often a takeout commitment or evidence of construction financing eligibility.

Seller Financing

One of the most underutilized tools in development finance is seller carryback. A landowner motivated to close but willing to hold paper will often carry 20 to 40% of the purchase price at below-market rates, sometimes interest-only, sometimes with a performance kicker tied to entitlements. This dramatically reduces your equity requirement at acquisition and improves your land basis entering the construction loan. The best seller carrybacks I have seen are negotiated as subordinate seconds behind a senior land loan, effectively giving the developer a full capital stack at land close.

Private Equity Land JVs

For larger sites where the developer has expertise but limited capital, a land JV with a private equity fund or family office is common. The equity partner funds the acquisition; the developer contributes the deal and the development agreement; promotes are negotiated at the JV level. Be careful: equity partners who fund land often expect to participate in the construction equity as well. Negotiate your promote at the outset to avoid being squeezed on the back end.

How Land Basis Affects Your Construction Loan

This is critical and frequently overlooked. Your construction lender will size your loan based on the lesser of Loan-to-Cost (LTC) or Loan-to-Value (LTV) of the completed project. If you overpaid for land, your total project cost rises, but your as-completed value may not keep pace, and your construction lender will notice. A high land basis compresses the LTC ceiling, meaning you need more equity at construction close. Conversely, a developer who bought land at the right basis several years ago can often achieve higher leverage on the construction loan because the appraised land value has appreciated above the acquisition cost.

Rule of thumb: land should represent no more than 20-25% of total development cost for most asset classes. At 30% or higher, expect pushback from construction lenders on leverage.

Stage 2: Pre-Development Capital

What Happens Before the Construction Loan

Between land acquisition and construction loan close, a developer spends real money, money that is largely unfinanceable through conventional debt. Pre-development soft costs typically include:

  • Feasibility studies and market analysis
  • Entitlement applications, environmental studies, traffic studies
  • Architecture and schematic design
  • Engineering (civil, structural, MEP)
  • Legal fees (land use, municipal approvals)
  • Carrying costs on the land during entitlements

On a mid-size multifamily project of 150 units, total pre-development spend commonly runs $500,000 to $1.5 million before a shovel goes in the ground. This is almost entirely equity, yours or your partners'.

Pre-Development Loan Programs

These are rare, but they exist for strong sponsors with track records. Some Community Development Financial Institutions (CDFIs), mission-driven lenders, and affordable housing agencies offer pre-development loans, particularly for projects with a community benefit component. Terms are typically 12-24 months, interest-only, with a maximum advance of $500,000 to $2 million. For market-rate development, this source is largely inaccessible: expect to fund pre-development from your own capital or your equity partners.

The Gap Funding Question

Family offices and smaller institutional equity funds often participate at the pre-development stage in exchange for a first look at the construction equity investment. This is a relationship business: developers who have delivered returns to equity partners get access to pre-development capital on favorable terms. First-time developers, by contrast, typically fund this stage from personal balance sheet. It is one of the primary barriers to entry in ground-up development.

Stage 3: Construction Financing: The Most Complex Event

How Construction Loans Are Sized

Construction lenders size their loans using two parallel tests and lend to the lesser of both:

  • Loan-to-Cost (LTC): Most lenders will advance 60-70% of total project cost (land + hard costs + soft costs + financing costs). Top-tier sponsors with track records and strong markets can occasionally achieve 75% LTC. Value-add renovation projects often top out at 65%.
  • Loan-to-Value (LTV): The lender orders an as-completed appraisal and typically lends 65-75% of the projected stabilized value. In markets where cap rates have moved out, as-completed values have declined, and this test has become the binding constraint for many projects.

The difference between these two figures is your required equity. A $20 million project at 65% LTC requires $7 million in equity. If the LTV test comes in lower (say, 62%) you need another $600,000. Budget for the lower of the two.

Draw Schedule Structure

Construction loans are not funded upfront. Proceeds are drawn down in monthly or bi-monthly draws as construction progresses and is verified by the lender's third-party inspector. The draw schedule typically mirrors the GC's sworn construction contract schedule, broken into line items: foundation, framing, MEP rough-in, roofing, exterior, interior, finishes, and so on.

Each draw requires: a lien waiver from the GC and all major subcontractors, an updated title endorsement (bringing down the title insurance policy), and inspector approval. Budget for a 2-4 week lag between when work is complete and when funds arrive in your account. Most experienced developers run a working capital reserve of 1-2 months of carrying costs for exactly this reason.

Interest Reserve

A construction loan does not require monthly interest payments from the borrower. Instead, the lender typically sets aside an interest reserve within the loan budget that funds itself monthly as interest accrues. This reserve is sized based on the projected draw schedule and the loan's interest rate, typically SOFR + 2.5% to 4.5% depending on sponsor strength and market conditions.

If your project runs over budget or over schedule, the interest reserve depletes faster than projected. This is one of the most common sources of construction loan technical defaults: the interest reserve runs out before the project is complete. Build in a contingency of 10-15% on the interest reserve line, and negotiate with your lender at origination for the ability to increase the reserve from contingency if needed.

Completion Guaranty

Every construction lender will require a completion guaranty from the principals. This is a full personal guaranty that the project will be completed to the plans and specifications, regardless of cost overruns. Unlike a payment guaranty, which only guarantees debt service, the completion guaranty obligates you personally to fund cost overruns to the point of project completion.

This is non-negotiable for most lenders and should be understood before any sponsor signs a construction loan commitment. High-net-worth developers with multiple active projects sometimes negotiate a limitation on the guaranty (capped at a specific dollar amount above the loan), but this requires significant leverage and track record.

Negotiating Construction Loan Terms

The most negotiable elements of a construction loan, in order of impact:

  1. Spread over index: Even 25 basis points matters on a $15 million loan over 24 months.
  2. Recourse burn-off: Negotiate for the completion guaranty to convert to a non-recourse carveout guaranty upon completion and certificate of occupancy.
  3. Extension options: Most construction loans have a 12-18 month initial term with one or two 6-month extensions. Lock in your extension options and the conditions for exercise (typically: no default, budget in balance, construction substantially complete). Extension fees typically run 0.25-0.50% of the loan balance per extension.
  4. Partial release provisions: For phased or condo projects, negotiate release prices for individual units upfront.
  5. Retainage: GC retainage (typically 10%) is withheld from draws until substantial completion. Some lenders also hold a 5-10% loan retainage. Negotiate loan retainage down or eliminate it: it creates unnecessary carrying cost.

The 60-Day Rule on Construction Loan Commitments

Construction loan commitments from banks are typically valid for 60-90 days. This means you must close within that window or the commitment expires, and you restart the process. In a competitive land acquisition, developers often sign a purchase contract before securing a construction loan commitment, creating a race between entitlement progress and lender diligence. The failure point is almost always: underestimating the lender's diligence timeline. Bank construction loan diligence runs 45-90 days from application to commitment. Add 30-45 days to close. Total timeline: 75-135 days. Plan accordingly when negotiating land contract contingency periods.

Common Construction Loan Mistakes

  • Underestimating hard cost contingency. 5% is not enough. 10% is standard. In volatile markets for materials and labor, 15% is prudent.
  • Not locking the GC contract before loan close. Lenders require a signed GC contract with a guaranteed maximum price (GMP). If you go to loan close with an open GC bid, you are negotiating from weakness.
  • Ignoring the draw process until the first draw. Set up your draw process (title company, inspector coordination, lien waivers) before construction starts. The first draw is when developers discover they don't have the right mechanics in place.
  • Not planning the exit at construction close. Your lender will ask how you are exiting the construction loan on day one. Have a clear, credible permanent financing strategy (or a bridge lender relationship) before you close.

Stage 4: Lease-Up Bridge Financing

The Occupancy Gap

Construction is complete. You have your certificate of occupancy. You are leasing units or signing tenants. But your construction loan is maturing, and your permanent lender requires 90%+ occupancy for 90 days (agency) or 12 months of stabilized NOI (life company). This gap, between construction completion and stabilization, is where lease-up bridge financing lives.

On a typical multifamily project, lease-up takes 6 to 18 months post-completion depending on market absorption. A well-located urban project may lease in 6 months. A suburban project in a softer market may take 18. If your construction loan matures before stabilization, you need a solution.

Option 1: Extend the Construction Loan

If you negotiated extension options into your construction loan (see above), this is the cleanest path. You are already in relationship with the lender; there is no new diligence or new lender fees. The cost is an extension fee (0.25-0.50%) and potentially a rate step-up. The limitation: construction lenders are not lease-up specialists, and some will resist extending once construction is complete. Negotiating this before you need it is essential.

Option 2: Bridge Lender

Standalone bridge lenders (debt funds, mortgage REITs, and some insurance company balance sheet programs) specialize in lease-up financing. Terms:

  • Leverage: 70-80% of as-stabilized value, typically limited to 75% of current "as-is" value
  • Rate: SOFR + 3.0% to 5.0%, floating
  • Term: 12-36 months, interest-only
  • Structure: Often includes a future funding component for lease-up costs and tenant improvements

The bridge loan pays off the construction loan at maturity and buys you the runway to reach stabilization. Unlike the construction loan, there is no draw schedule: proceeds are funded at close. This improves your working capital position and simplifies operations during the lease-up period.

Option 3: Preferred Equity Injection

If your asset value at construction completion supports permanent loan sizing but your occupancy is not yet there, a preferred equity injection can reduce your senior debt to a level that a permanent lender will accept, effectively solving the occupancy problem with equity rather than time. This is most common when the market has shifted and the permanent loan proceeds are meaningfully lower than the construction loan balance. The preferred equity bridges the gap in the capital stack, often at a 10-14% preferred return, while the sponsor maintains control.

Stage 5: Permanent Takeout: The Prize

What Permanent Lenders Require

Permanent loans are sized on stabilized, in-place cash flow. The underwriting is fundamentally different from a construction or bridge loan. Key thresholds by lender type:

Lender Type Occupancy Requirement Seasoning DSCR Minimum Max LTV
Fannie Mae / Freddie Mac 90% for 90 days 90 days at 90% 1.25x 75-80%
Life Insurance Company 92-95%+ 12 months stabilized NOI 1.30-1.35x 65-70%
CMBS (Conduit) 85-90% 6-12 months 1.25x 70-75%
Bank Portfolio 85%+ Varies 1.20-1.25x 65-75%

The Forward Commitment Strategy

One of the most powerful tools available to developers is the forward rate lock: negotiating your permanent loan at construction close, with funding contingent on reaching stabilization milestones. Life companies and some agency lenders offer forward commitments, typically at a 10-15 basis point premium over spot rates, locking in your permanent loan rate 24-36 months before you need the money.

For a developer closing a construction loan today in a rate environment where SOFR is elevated, a forward commitment to a 10-year life company loan at a fixed rate provides powerful certainty. You know your permanent debt service before you break ground. You can model your returns with precision. And when the construction loan matures, you are not competing for permanent capital in whatever rate environment exists at that moment.

The mechanics: the life company or agency lender issues a conditional commitment at construction close, subject to the property reaching the agreed occupancy and NOI thresholds within a defined window (typically 24-36 months). If the property hits the targets, the permanent loan funds. If it misses, the commitment expires and you return to the market.

Choosing Your Permanent Lender

  • Agency (Fannie/Freddie): Best for multifamily 5+ units. Highest leverage, non-recourse, competitive fixed rates. Fastest to execute among institutional options. Requires meeting agency affordability set-asides for best pricing.
  • Life Insurance Company: Best pricing for high-quality assets in primary markets. Non-recourse, 10-30 year terms, lowest rates for the right asset. Requires strong seasoning and conservative leverage. No prepay flexibility (typical lockout or yield maintenance).
  • CMBS: Best for assets that do not fit agency or life company boxes: hotel, retail, mixed-use, transitional assets. Higher leverage than life company, fixed rate, non-recourse. Significant prepay constraint (defeasance).
  • Bank Portfolio: Most flexible on structure, recourse required, variable or fixed, shorter terms (5-10 years). Best for assets in lease-up that need flexibility or when the sponsor prefers a relationship lender.

The Capital Stack: Equity, Mezzanine, and Senior Debt

Building a Development Capital Stack

A development deal capital stack from bottom to top typically looks like this:

  • Senior Construction Debt: 60-70% of total cost, first lien, lowest cost
  • Mezzanine Debt or Preferred Equity: 10-20% of total cost, subordinate, 10-15% annual cost
  • Common Equity: 15-30% of total cost, highest risk, highest return

Mezzanine Debt vs. Preferred Equity

Both fill the gap between senior debt and common equity, but they are structurally different:

Mezzanine debt is a loan secured by a pledge of the borrower's equity interest in the property-owning entity. It sits behind the senior construction loan in priority but is technically debt: the mezzanine lender can foreclose on the equity interest if the borrower defaults on the mezz. Typical mezz debt costs 10-14% annually, structured as interest-only with a bullet at maturity.

Preferred equity is an equity investment (not a loan) that sits above common equity in the capital stack and receives a preferred return (typically 8-14%) before common equity participates. Preferred equity investors do not have the same foreclosure rights as mezzanine lenders, but they do have governance rights and the ability to remove the common equity partner in certain default scenarios. Preferred equity is more flexible and often easier to layer into a deal that already has a senior lender with restrictive intercreditor requirements.

When Mezzanine Makes Sense

Mezzanine debt is appropriate when: (1) the sponsor needs to minimize equity dilution, (2) the project economics support the blended cost of capital, and (3) the senior lender permits mezzanine financing (many community banks do not). A $25 million project at 65% LTC with $8.75 million of required equity, where the sponsor can replace $4 million with mezz at 12%, makes sense if the project IRR clears the mezz cost with margin. If the project's levered return is 14-15%, the mezz destroys value. Do the math at every layer.

JV Equity: What Institutional Partners Require

Institutional equity partners (opportunity zone funds, real estate private equity funds, family offices with real estate mandates) bring capital in exchange for a promoted interest in the deal. What they look for:

  • Track record: Minimum 2-3 completed comparable projects; institutional capital rarely backs first-time ground-up developers
  • Co-investment: Sponsor typically contributes 10-20% of required equity alongside the institutional partner
  • Returns: Institutional equity targets 15-20% IRR on development deals; preferred return is typically 7-10% before the promote kicks in
  • Promote structure: Common waterfall: 100% to investors until preferred return; 80/20 split up to a 15-18% IRR hurdle; 70/30 or 60/40 above the hurdle
  • Control: Institutional partners typically require major decision approval rights: budget changes above a threshold, financing changes, sales, and disposition

Case Study: $31.4M Non-Recourse CMBS Refinance for a Little Tokyo Retail Center in Los Angeles

Trevor Damyan Founder & Principal Broker
CA DRE #02244836

Former CBRE and Marcus & Millichap Capital Corporation. $1B+ in closed commercial real estate financing across bridge, construction, permanent, and structured debt.

July 27, 2026
Quick answer: Commercial Lending Solutions arranged a $31,400,000 non-recourse conduit loan through Citi to refinance a 199,014-square-foot retail center in the Little Tokyo district of downtown Los Angeles. The loan closed on a 5-year term with full-term interest-only, a lockbox with springing cash management, and reserve and trigger provisions tied to two of the property's most significant tenants, negotiated up from an original underwriting proposal that capped proceeds well below the amount the property actually supported.

The Deal That Walked in the Door

We are Commercial Lending Solutions, a commercial mortgage brokerage based in Los Angeles, California (CA). Commercial Lending Solutions specializes in permanent and CMBS conduit financing for stabilized retail, office, and multifamily assets across Los Angeles. This deal was a hometown refinance in every sense: a well-located, nearly 200,000-square-foot retail center in the Little Tokyo district of downtown Los Angeles, anchored by a bowling and entertainment concept and a longstanding Japanese marketplace tenant, needed to replace its existing mortgage and mezzanine debt with a single, permanent, non-recourse facility. The sponsor had built real value into the center over the years, and the refinance was about capturing that value at scale in the conduit market rather than piecing together another short-term facility.

Little Tokyo sits at the edge of the Arts District and downtown Los Angeles' broader redevelopment corridor, a submarket where entertainment and experiential retail tenants have proven durable even as traditional retail has struggled elsewhere. That tenant mix and location were exactly what a conduit lender wants to see when sizing a large, long-duration, non-recourse loan.

What Made It Hard

  • An underwriting proposal that undersold the property. The lender's initial term sheet offered two options, one capped at $33,000,000 with a wider spread and a 60% maximum loan-to-value, and a lower-leverage option capped at just $25,500,000 at a tighter spread and 45% maximum loan-to-value, neither of which reflected the full value the center's rent roll could support.
  • Named-tenant concentration risk. Because two specific tenants anchor a meaningful share of the property's income, the lender built lease-specific trigger events into the loan, a bankruptcy, lease termination, monetary default, or "going dark" by either tenant would spring enhanced cash management until resolved.
  • CMBS execution discipline. Conduit loans come with rating-agency-driven requirements, a bankruptcy-remote single-purpose borrowing entity, an independent director, a non-consolidation opinion, and a tight, deadline-driven closing process, all of which have to be executed correctly the first time.
"Conduit lenders open with a conservative number because the first term sheet is built off preliminary information. Pushing the underwriting all the way through appraisal and committee is how we closed the gap between the low option on paper and what the rent roll actually supported."
, Trevor Damyan, Commercial Lending Solutions

How We Ran the Process

Trevor Damyan, a California-licensed real estate broker (CA DRE 02244836), negotiated the engagement directly with Citi's conduit desk, working the deal from term sheet through funding between February and May 2025:

  • $31,400,000 funded at closing, well above the lower-leverage option in the original term sheet and reflecting the stronger underwritten cash flow the property actually produced
  • A 5-year fixed-rate term with full-term interest-only, priced at the greater of a fixed spread over the 5-year on-the-run U.S. Treasury or a stated floor rate, giving the sponsor rate certainty in a still-volatile rate environment
  • Underwritten to a minimum debt service coverage ratio in the 1.56x-1.60x range and a minimum debt yield of 10.5%-13.0%, conservative enough for Citi's investment committee and eventual securitization
  • Non-recourse to the sponsor except for standard carve-out or "bad boy" acts, covered separately by a limited recourse guaranty and an environmental indemnity, rather than a blanket personal guaranty on the debt
  • A lockbox with springing, tenant-specific cash management: rents flow to the borrower daily in the ordinary course, but a lease default, termination, bankruptcy, or dark-store event from either of the two specified tenants triggers a sweep of excess cash flow into a lender-controlled account until the issue clears
  • Tenant improvement, leasing commission, and capital expenditure reserves funded monthly on a per-square-foot basis, standard conduit discipline that keeps the property capitalized without relying on sponsor equity calls
  • Locked prepayment for most of the term, open only in the final six months, with defeasance available starting three years after closing or two years after securitization of the last loan piece, whichever comes first

The loan closed and funded on May 6, 2025, secured by a first mortgage on the property and structured through a newly formed single-purpose borrowing entity to satisfy conduit and rating-agency requirements.

Why We Won This One

The gap between the term sheet's two proposed options and the amount that actually closed is the story here. Lenders' initial conduit term sheets are built off preliminary information and tend toward conservative assumptions; pushing the underwriting through appraisal, rent roll diligence, and the lender's investment committee process, rather than accepting the first number offered, is what closed the gap between a $25.5 million low-leverage option and the $31.4 million that actually funded.

The other piece worth noting for any retail sponsor is the trigger structure. Rather than treating tenant concentration as a reason to cut proceeds outright, the lender addressed it with springing cash management tied to specific, named leases. That structure let the sponsor keep the loan proceeds the rent roll supported while giving the lender a real, mechanical response if either anchor tenant's lease actually deteriorated.

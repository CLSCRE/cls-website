Expert Witness: CRE Partnership and Waterfall Disputes in Grand Rapids, MI

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Joint Venture and Partnership Dispute

The Grand Rapids Market Context

Grand Rapids has evolved well beyond its furniture-manufacturing identity into one of the Midwest's more diversified and resilient commercial real estate markets. For attorneys and investors evaluating joint venture disputes in this market, that context matters directly: the economic fundamentals underlying a deal's projections will be scrutinized closely in litigation, and Grand Rapids presents a specific set of baseline conditions that either support or undermine the assumptions embedded in partnership waterfall models.

On the industrial side, West Michigan's logistics and light-manufacturing corridor has kept vacancy rates in the 4% to 6% range across key submarkets, with asking rents for Class A distribution product in the $5.50 to $7.50 per square foot triple-net range depending on clear height and dock configuration. Industrial joint ventures structured in 2019 through 2022 in Grand Rapids frequently projected rent growth in the 3% to 5% annual range, and in many cases those projections proved conservative, which has actually intensified waterfall disputes: higher-than-projected NOI accelerates the return of preferred equity and the timing of promoted interest events, and partners disagree about how operating income gets characterized for distribution purposes.

Multifamily fundamentals in Grand Rapids have remained strong, supported by in-migration from Chicago and Detroit, a large healthcare employment base anchored by Spectrum Health and Corewell, and a growing technology sector. Class B multifamily cap rates in submarkets like East Hills, Eastown, and the Medical Mile corridor traded in the 4.75% to 5.50% range through 2022, tightening JV equity structures and putting significant pressure on preferred return hurdles when refinancing events were delayed by interest rate increases in 2023 and 2024. A preferred return that looked comfortable at a 5.00% cap rate environment looks very different when disposition proceeds are compressed by cap rate expansion to 6.25% or higher.

Office product in Grand Rapids is experiencing the same fundamental disruption as in most secondary Midwest markets, with downtown Class A vacancy hovering in the 15% to 20% range and suburban office vacancy approaching 25% in certain submarkets. For JV disputes involving office assets, the analytical challenge is significant: projected stabilized NOI used to structure the original waterfall may have been based on pre-pandemic occupancy assumptions that are no longer achievable, which directly affects whether preferred return thresholds have been met and whether promoted interest has been legitimately earned.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing one of the equity partners in a Grand Rapids commercial real estate joint venture involving a value-add multifamily asset. The core of the dispute involved three distinct but interrelated financial questions: whether the preferred return owed to the limited equity partner had been correctly calculated based on the terms of the operating agreement, whether the general partner's promoted interest had been prematurely claimed prior to satisfaction of the applicable return hurdle, and whether the financing structure actually available to the venture at the time of the contested refinancing event supported the capital stack assumptions embedded in the waterfall model.

Counsel retained CLS CRE to provide a Rule 26 expert report addressing the financing feasibility analysis: specifically, what a market-standard lender would have offered on this asset at the relevant date, what loan proceeds would have been available given the property's actual NOI and occupancy, and how the financing terms obtainable in the market affected the amount of equity capital that could have been returned at each waterfall tier. The report also addressed whether the general partner's characterization of certain capital expenditures as return-of-capital events, rather than operating expenses, was consistent with market-standard JV accounting practices and how that characterization affected the preferred return calculation base.

A secondary question involved the structure of the mezzanine tranche that had been placed behind the senior loan at origination. The limited partner's counsel needed to establish whether the mezzanine terms, including the interest rate, amortization schedule, and default provisions, were consistent with market standards at the time of origination, or whether those terms had been structured in a manner that disproportionately benefited the general partner's affiliated lending entity at the expense of the limited partner's preferred return.

The Financial Analysis

The foundational step in any JV waterfall dispute involving a financed asset is establishing what the property's supportable debt actually was at the time of each contested distribution or refinancing event. This is not an appraisal exercise. It requires underwriting the asset the way a capital markets lender actually would, applying market-standard DSCR thresholds, LTV constraints, and rate assumptions specific to the asset class and time period in question.

For the multifamily asset in this engagement, we began with a line-by-line reconstruction of stabilized NOI. The property consisted of 96 units in a transitional Grand Rapids submarket. Using actual rent rolls, vacancy history, and operating expense statements, we established a trailing 12-month NOI of approximately $780,000 and a stabilized forward NOI, adjusting for lease-up trajectory and market rent comparables, of approximately $840,000. The general partner had used a projected stabilized NOI of $920,000 in its waterfall calculations, a figure we determined overstated achievable income by approximately 9%, which had a compounding effect on the preferred return calculation base.

Applying market-standard agency underwriting parameters applicable to this asset class and this period, specifically a 1.25x DSCR, a 30-year amortization schedule, and an interest rate of 6.35% (reflecting the prevailing agency fixed rate for 10-year paper at the relevant date), the maximum supportable loan against the stabilized NOI of $840,000 was calculated as follows:

  • Annual debt service supportable at 1.25x DSCR: $840,000 divided by 1.25 equals $672,000 in maximum annual debt service
  • Monthly debt service: $672,000 divided by 12 equals $56,000
  • Loan amount supportable at 6.35% interest, 30-year amortization: approximately $8,650,000
  • Appraised value at the time of the contested refinancing (based on a 5.50% cap rate applied to stabilized NOI): approximately $15,270,000
  • LTV implied by the DSCR-constrained loan: approximately 56.6%, well within the 75% LTV maximum for agency multifamily
  • LTV-constrained maximum loan at 75%: approximately $11,450,000

In this scenario, the binding constraint was not LTV but DSCR: the income the property was actually generating, as opposed to the income the general partner projected, capped the available loan proceeds at approximately $8,650,000. The general partner had claimed that $10,200,000 in loan proceeds was available and had structured the waterfall distribution to reflect that larger refinancing event. Our analysis demonstrated that the general partner's loan sizing was not achievable under market-standard underwriting, which directly affected how much equity capital was available for distribution and, consequently, whether the preferred return threshold had been satisfied before the promoted interest was claimed.

The Complicating Factors

Preferred return calculation methodology disputes. Market-standard JV operating agreements define preferred return in several different ways, and the differences are consequential in litigation. Some agreements calculate preferred return on contributed capital as a simple annual accrual. Others compound the preferred return on a quarterly or annual basis. Still others define the preferred return as accruing only during the period when the capital is outstanding and deployed. In the Grand Rapids engagement, the operating agreement was ambiguous regarding whether the preferred return compounded annually on the unpaid balance or accrued simple interest on the original contribution. The financial difference over a five-year hold with delayed distributions was approximately $340,000, which exceeded the promoted interest the general partner claimed to have earned. An expert who can model both methodologies and demonstrate the quantum of the dispute in present-value terms provides material value to litigation counsel.

Mezzanine debt affiliated with the general partner. One of the more structurally problematic features of this engagement was the existence of a mezzanine loan provided by an entity affiliated with the general partner. This is not uncommon in Grand Rapids deals involving local operators who have access to private capital sources. The issue for litigation purposes is whether the mezzanine terms, specifically the interest rate and payment priority, were equivalent to what an arm's-length third-party mezzanine lender would have charged. In the current market, institutional mezzanine debt behind agency senior debt on multifamily typically prices at SOFR plus 550 to 700 basis points, depending on LTV and market. An above-market mezzanine rate charged by an affiliated entity effectively transfers value from the limited partner's preferred return to the general partner's affiliated lending entity, which is a breach of fiduciary duty argument that requires financing expert analysis to quantify.

Capital expenditure characterization and its effect on distributable cash. In value-add multifamily JVs, the treatment of renovation costs directly affects the preferred return calculation. If capital expenditures are properly capitalized and treated as invested capital, they increase the preferred return base, which means more money must be distributed to the preferred equity holder before promoted interest is earned. If the general partner characterizes capital expenditures as operating expenses, they reduce NOI and distributable cash flow, delaying or reducing preferred return payments without increasing the base. Establishing what market-standard JV accounting practice requires, and whether the general partner's characterization was consistent with that standard, requires an expert who understands both the financing and the accounting conventions specific to commercial real estate joint ventures.

Interest rate environment timing. Deals structured between 2019 and 2022 often embedded refinancing assumptions based on a rate environment that no longer existed by the time the refinancing event occurred. A joint venture that projected a 3.50% fixed-rate refinancing and actually faced a 6.35% rate market at the relevant date experienced a material reduction in available loan proceeds, which compressed distributable equity and potentially prevented the preferred return from being satisfied at the projected refinancing event. In litigation, establishing what rate environment actually existed at the relevant date, and what a qualified lender would have offered, is an expert financing function, not an appraisal function.

The Grand Rapids Lender Market

Attorneys and investors need to understand the depth of the Grand Rapids lending market because the available lender pool at any given time determines what financing terms were achievable and, by extension, what the realistic refinancing proceeds were for waterfall purposes.

Agency lenders (Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac). For multifamily assets of 5 units or more, agency execution is typically the benchmark financing. Agency lenders are highly active in Grand Rapids through approved seller/servicer correspondents. Agency pricing is tied to 10-year Treasury benchmarks with spreads that have ranged from 150 to 220 basis points in recent years. Agency underwriting caps LTV at 75% for standard acquisitions and 80% for affordable product, with DSCR minimums of 1.25x for market-rate assets. Agency execution provides the highest proceeds and longest fixed-rate terms available for qualifying multifamily assets in Grand Rapids.

Regional and community banks. Grand Rapids has an exceptionally deep community banking market, including strong local institutions that actively compete for CRE relationships. For non-agency product, including mixed-use, industrial, and smaller multifamily, regional banks offer 5-year fixed terms with 25-year amortization schedules, typically at LTVs of 65% to 70% and DSCR minimums of 1.20x to 1.25x. These lenders hold loans in portfolio and often have more flexibility on structure but less flexibility on rate. For JV dispute purposes, regional bank term sheets from the relevant period are often the appropriate benchmark for non-agency assets.

CMBS and life company lenders. For larger commercial assets, including industrial, retail, and office product above $5,000,000, CMBS conduit lenders and life insurance companies provide long-term fixed-rate execution. Life company pricing is typically the most aggressive available, but life companies impose strict occupancy and tenancy requirements that many Grand Rapids assets in transition do not satisfy. CMBS provides broader eligibility but imposes prepayment lockouts and defeasance requirements that affect waterfall mechanics at disposition.

Debt funds and bridge lenders. For transitional assets and value-add plays, debt funds provide bridge financing at floating rates, typically in the SOFR plus 300 to 500 basis point range for senior bridge and SOFR plus 550 to 700 basis points for mezzanine. CLS CRE maintains active relationships with more than 1,000 lenders nationally, including the debt fund and bridge lender community that operates in West Michigan. Understanding the realistic bridge-to-permanent execution available to a specific asset type in Grand Rapids at a specific point in time is essential to the waterfall feasibility analysis in litigation.

What Attorneys Handling Grand Rapids CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to retain a CRE financing expert. If the dispute involves any of the following, financing expert testimony is likely necessary and potentially outcome-determinative: waterfall distribution calculations that depend on refinancing proceeds; preferred return accruals affected by distributable cash flow; capital stack disputes involving senior debt, mezzanine, or preferred equity; allegations that financing was improperly structured by an affiliated lender; or disputes over whether a property could have been refinanced or sold on the terms the general partner claimed. An appraiser can establish value. A financing expert establishes what a lender would have actually done with that value, which is a different and equally critical question.

What a CRE financing expert can establish that an appraiser cannot. Appraisers determine value based on comparable sales and income capitalization. They do not typically opine on DSCR underwriting, lender credit box requirements, market-standard loan terms at a specific point in time, the reasonableness of mezzanine pricing, or whether a specific capital structure was consistent with arm's-length market practice. These are financing questions that require a practitioner who actively originates and structures commercial loans across multiple lender categories, not a valuation professional.

What qualifies a CRE financing expert. Relevant qualifications include active transaction experience in commercial mortgage brokerage, direct lender relationships across the institutional lending spectrum, a track record of structuring complex capital stacks including mezzanine and preferred equity, and specific experience preparing Rule 26 expert reports addressing financing feasibility. A retired bank credit officer has underwriting experience on the lender side but may lack visibility into the full lending market and may not have structured complex JV capital stacks from the borrower or sponsor perspective. At CLS CRE, our approximately $1 billion in transaction volume across 50 states and relationships with more than 1,000 lenders provides a market-standard benchmark that is directly applicable to expert witness analysis.

What counsel should gather before engaging the expert. The operating agreement in full, including any amendments; all capital contribution records and the date and amount of each contribution; all distribution records; the original loan documents for every tranche of debt in the capital stack; any term sheets or commitment letters from the relevant origination and refinancing periods; property-level financial statements and rent rolls for the relevant period; and any communications between the partners regarding refinancing decisions or distribution calculations. The earlier this document set is assembled, the faster and more cost-efficiently the expert can complete the analysis.

How financing expert testimony is used. In commercial litigation, a CRE financing expert's Rule 26 report typically establishes the benchmark against which the general partner's conduct is measured. At deposition and trial, the expert can explain to a judge or jury why the general partner's loan sizing was inconsistent with what lenders were actually offering, how a market-standard waterfall should have operated given the actual financing available, and what the limited partner would have received if distributions had been correctly calculated. In arbitration, which governs many JV disputes, the expert report often becomes the analytical foundation for the damages calculation.

Key Takeaways

  • Waterfall disputes are fundamentally financing disputes. The amount of equity available for distribution at each tier depends directly on what debt was available, on what terms, and at what cost. Any JV litigation that involves refinancing events, preferred return calculations, or promoted interest entitlements requires a CRE financing expert, not only an appraiser or accountant.
  • Grand Rapids multifamily and industrial fundamentals are specific and measurable. Expert analysis must be grounded in the actual NOI, vacancy, and cap rate environment for the specific submarket and asset class in Grand Rapids at the relevant date, not generic Midwest market assumptions.
  • DSCR is often the binding constraint on loan proceeds, not LTV. In a rising-rate environment, DSCR underwriting frequently limits available loan proceeds below what an LTV analysis alone would suggest, which directly compresses distributable equity and affects waterfall outcomes.

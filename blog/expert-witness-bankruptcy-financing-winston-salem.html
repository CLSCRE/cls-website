Expert Witness: CRE Financing Feasibility in Chapter 11 Proceedings in Winston-Salem, NC

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Bankruptcy Exit Financing

The Winston-Salem Market Context

Winston-Salem occupies a distinct position in the North Carolina commercial real estate landscape. It is not a secondary market in name only; it is a functioning, institutionally recognized metro with a diversified economic base anchored by healthcare (led by one of the largest academic medical systems in the Southeast), financial services, logistics, and an emerging innovation corridor tied to Wake Forest University's research enterprise. For attorneys and plan proponents working through a Chapter 11 reorganization involving a commercial asset here, that economic foundation matters because it shapes what lenders are willing to underwrite and at what terms.

Industrial product in the Winston-Salem MSA has experienced sustained demand tied to Piedmont Triad logistics activity, with vacancy rates in the 4% to 6% range across bulk and flex product as of recent reporting periods. Net absorption has remained positive, and asking rents for Class A industrial space have pushed toward $7.00 to $9.00 per square foot NNN, a meaningful increase from levels five years prior. Multifamily fundamentals have remained relatively stable, with Class B workforce housing maintaining occupancy in the 92% to 95% range and effective rents in the $1,100 to $1,400 per month range for one- and two-bedroom units across submarkets like Hanes Mall, Peters Creek, and the University Parkway corridor. Office, as in most mid-tier metros, tells a more complicated story: suburban Class B product carries elevated vacancy in the 15% to 22% range, while medical office adjacent to the institutional healthcare campus has performed considerably better. Retail strip centers anchored by essential-use tenants have held occupancy well above 90%, while unanchored inline retail faces headwinds consistent with national trends.

For purposes of a Chapter 11 exit financing opinion, this market context is not background noise. It determines cap rate assumptions, it drives the supportable NOI analysis, and it directly informs which lender categories will engage on a given asset type. Winston-Salem is a market where community banks remain deeply active and relationship-driven, where regional banks have meaningful appetite for well-structured commercial deals, and where debt funds and bridge lenders have increased their presence as the market has matured. That layered lender universe gives a plan proponent meaningful optionality, but it also requires precise analysis to identify which tier of the capital stack is accessible for a given asset at a given moment in its credit lifecycle.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by counsel representing a secured creditor in a Chapter 11 proceeding pending in the Middle District of North Carolina. The asset at issue was a value-add multifamily property located in a Winston-Salem submarket with a track record of stable working-class occupancy. The debtor's proposed Plan of Reorganization contemplated refinancing the property at confirmation to generate proceeds sufficient to pay the secured creditor in full, fund administrative claims, and provide a modest distribution to unsecured creditors.

Counsel needed an independent, qualified expert opinion that could be presented at a confirmation hearing to address two threshold questions: first, whether the subject property, in its current physical and financial condition, could support first-mortgage debt sufficient to satisfy the secured creditor's allowed claim; and second, whether a credible market existed for that financing among lenders who actively originate loans on properties encumbered by or emerging from bankruptcy estates. Those are financing feasibility questions, not valuation questions, and that distinction matters procedurally and substantively. CLS CRE was engaged to produce a written expert report under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 26, and to be available for deposition and, if necessary, live testimony at the confirmation hearing.

The scope of the engagement included: a review of rent rolls, trailing twelve-month operating statements, the property's physical inspection reports, the existing debt structure, the proposed plan terms, and comparable transaction data from the Winston-Salem multifamily market. Our firm also conducted direct outreach to capital sources with documented interest in Winston-Salem multifamily assets to assess real-time lender appetite, which is a form of primary market research that distinguishes a financing feasibility opinion from a purely desk-based exercise.

The Financial Analysis

The core analytical framework for any exit financing feasibility opinion begins with a disciplined reconstruction of stabilized Net Operating Income. In this engagement, the trailing twelve-month operating statement reflected occupancy of approximately 87%, which is below the market stabilized range of 93% to 95% for comparable workforce housing in Winston-Salem. The debtor's projections assumed rapid lease-up to 95% within sixty days of plan confirmation. My analysis applied a more conservative underwriting occupancy of 91%, consistent with what a prudent institutional lender would apply to a property emerging from bankruptcy, where management disruption, deferred maintenance, and tenant uncertainty are real variables.

Gross potential rent on the subject portfolio, based on current in-place leases and market rent comparables in the submarket, was approximately $1,320,000 annually. Applying a 9% vacancy and credit loss assumption at stabilized underwriting occupancy yielded effective gross income of approximately $1,201,200. Operating expenses, inclusive of property management at 8% of EGI, real estate taxes, insurance, utilities on common areas, and a capital reserve of $300 per unit per year, totaled approximately $468,000, producing a stabilized NOI of approximately $733,200.

The applicable DSCR standard for this asset type and this lender market is 1.25x at the underwritten note rate. That is the floor used by conventional agency lenders (Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac small balance programs) and by most regional and community bank lenders in the Southeast for multifamily product. Some debt funds will underwrite to 1.15x, but at meaningfully higher pricing. Using a stressed note rate of 7.25% on a 30-year amortization schedule (reflecting the rate environment in which this analysis was conducted and the modest credit friction associated with bankruptcy-adjacent origination), the maximum annual debt service supportable at a 1.25x DSCR is approximately $586,560. That debt service corresponds to a maximum loan amount of approximately $4,625,000 at a 7.25% rate with a 30-year amortization.

The independent appraisal in the record reflected an as-is value of approximately $5,800,000 for the subject property. At a $4,625,000 loan amount, that represents a loan-to-value ratio of approximately 79.7%, which is within the 80% LTV ceiling for agency small balance multifamily programs and within the 75% to 80% range that characterizes conventional bank portfolio lending in this market. The secured creditor's allowed claim, including accrued interest and fees, was approximately $4,100,000. The analysis therefore demonstrated that a first-mortgage loan at market-available terms could generate net proceeds sufficient to satisfy the secured claim at confirmation, with residual equity value remaining in the estate. That is a confirmable financing structure under 11 U.S.C. Section 1129, provided the plan meets the other confirmation requirements.

The Complicating Factors

Bankruptcy Title and Lender Hesitation. The most immediate complicating factor in any bankruptcy exit financing is lender perception of the origination environment. Most conventional lenders, including community banks and regional institutions, are not structured to originate loans where the collateral is held by a bankruptcy estate and where closing is conditioned on plan confirmation. Their credit approval processes, loan committee structures, and counsel requirements are not designed for that timeline or that legal complexity. This does not mean financing is unavailable; it means the financing universe narrows to lenders who have done this before, who carry in-house bankruptcy counsel familiarity, or who operate as debt funds with flexible origination parameters. In the Winston-Salem market, that universe includes select regional bank special assets groups, certain CMBS conduit lenders who will originate into a confirmed plan with clean title, and bridge lenders or debt funds who actively target transitional and distressed-adjacent situations. Identifying which specific capital sources in this universe are currently active and at what pricing is precisely the work a financing expert performs that an appraiser does not.

Deferred Maintenance and Property Condition. Properties that have been in Chapter 11 frequently exhibit deferred capital expenditure, because debtor-in-possession cash is often constrained and property improvement decisions are subject to court approval. Lenders underwriting exit financing will order their own property condition assessment and will apply a hold-back or escrow for identified deferred items. In this engagement, the property condition assessment identified approximately $185,000 in deferred maintenance items. A lender advancing $4,625,000 on a property with $185,000 of identified deferred work will typically structure a repair escrow funded at closing, either from loan proceeds or from equity contributed by the reorganized debtor. That escrow requirement reduces net proceeds available to pay creditors and must be modeled explicitly in the plan feasibility analysis. Failure to account for it is a common source of plan infeasibility findings at confirmation hearings.

Seasoning and Operating History. Agency lenders and conservative bank portfolio lenders typically require twelve months of stabilized operating history. A property exiting bankruptcy will frequently not have that history available in clean form. The operating statements may reflect disrupted management, inconsistent expense reporting, or occupancy volatility associated with the bankruptcy period itself. This creates a seasoning gap that drives some conventional lenders out of the eligible financing universe. Bridge lenders and debt funds are specifically designed to bridge this gap; they will underwrite to a business plan and forward-looking pro forma rather than requiring trailing stabilized performance. The tradeoff is pricing: bridge rates in the current environment for this asset type in a market like Winston-Salem range from 9.00% to 11.50%, with origination fees of 1.0 to 2.0 points, and loan terms of twenty-four to thirty-six months with extension options. A plan proponent must model whether the project economics support that cost of capital on a bridge-to-permanent basis or whether an initial permanent execution is achievable and preferable.

Title Complexity and Insurance. Confirming a plan of reorganization vests clean title in the reorganized debtor free and clear of liens and interests pursuant to Section 1141, but title insurance companies will still require a thorough review of the confirmation order, the plan, and any prior liens. Lenders require lender's title insurance as a condition of closing. Delays in the title review process can extend closing timelines beyond what a plan may have contemplated. Counsel should ensure that a title commitment has been ordered and preliminary issues identified well before the confirmation hearing, not after. This is operational advice that a financing expert, drawing on transaction execution experience, can provide to litigation counsel who may not have closed a CRE loan in the past twelve months.

The Winston-Salem Lender Market

Winston-Salem has a relatively deep community and regional bank presence compared to many secondary markets of comparable size. This reflects the city's historical roots in banking and financial services, and it creates a meaningful base of portfolio lenders who know the market, have existing borrower relationships, and can move with some efficiency on properly structured deals. However, for bankruptcy-exit financing specifically, the relevant lender universe is more selective than the general market would suggest.

Community Banks. There are a number of community banks domiciled in or with significant operational presence in Winston-Salem and the broader Forsyth County market that actively originate multifamily and mixed-use commercial loans. Their credit boxes typically require clean title, stabilized operating history, and a borrower guaranty. Most will not originate a loan where the borrower is a debtor-in-possession or where closing requires a court order. Post-confirmation, with the reorganized entity holding clean title, some community banks will re-engage, particularly on smaller loan amounts (generally below $3,000,000) where relationship-based underwriting dominates. Pricing for this group is generally in the 6.75% to 7.75% range on a five-year fixed term with a twenty to twenty-five year amortization.

Regional Banks. The regional bank presence in Winston-Salem includes institutions with statewide and multistate footprints that maintain commercial real estate lending groups in the Triad. These lenders have more sophisticated credit infrastructure, more experience with complex ownership structures, and in some cases dedicated special situations or workout groups that have originated exit financing on post-confirmation assets. Loan sizes for this group typically start at $2,000,000 and scale upward; they are realistic candidates for exit financing in the $3,000,000 to $8,000,000 range on multifamily and industrial assets with clean confirmation title. Pricing ranges from 7.00% to 8.25% depending on credit profile and relationship history.

Agency Lenders (Fannie Mae / Freddie Mac Small Balance). The agency small balance programs represent the best long-term pricing available for qualifying multifamily assets, with fixed rates in the 6.50% to 7.25% range, thirty-year amortization, and non-recourse execution available above certain loan thresholds. The challenge for bankruptcy-exit scenarios is the seasoning requirement and the agency's sensitivity to borrower credit events. A Chapter 11 filing is a major credit event; agency programs generally require a minimum of two to three years post-discharge or plan confirmation before a borrower entity is eligible, and they will look through to principals' personal credit histories. For a plan that contemplates long-term permanent financing, agency execution may be the end-state goal, achieved via a bridge-to-permanent structure where a debt fund or bank bridge loan is repaid with agency proceeds after the seasoning gap has been cleared.

Debt Funds and Bridge Lenders. This category has grown substantially in the Winston-Salem market over the past five years as national and regional debt funds have extended their geographic reach down the mid-Atlantic and Southeast corridors. These lenders are specifically designed for transitional, value-add, and credit-complex situations. They underwrite to asset value and forward NOI, they can close on post-confirmation title, and their loan processes are designed for speed and flexibility. The cost of that flexibility is pricing: rates in the current environment range from 9.00% to 11.50% with one to two points of origination. Terms are typically twenty-four to thirty-six months. For a plan that needs execution certainty at a defined price, this category is often the most realistic primary financing source, with a built-in refinance to permanent financing contemplated in the plan's projections.

What Attorneys Handling Winston-Salem CRE Disputes Need to Know

The first question counsel should ask when a Chapter 11 plan rests on a refinancing premise is whether the financing is genuinely feasible at the proposed terms, not whether the debtor or its broker believes it is. Belief and feasibility are not the same thing, and courts have rejected plans at confirmation precisely because the proposed financing was speculative rather than grounded in documented lender appetite. A qualified CRE financing expert provides an independent opinion, supported by market data and direct capital market research, that can withstand cross-examination on that specific question.

The distinction between a financing expert and an appraiser is critical. An appraiser determines value. A financing expert determines what a lender will actually do with that value: what loan amount is supportable at market DSCR, what rate and terms apply, what conditions lenders impose on bankruptcy-adjacent collateral, and whether the proposed plan structure is executable in the real capital markets. These are different questions, answered by different disciplines, drawing on different expertise. A retired banker who has not actively originated or brokered loans in the past three to five years may not qualify; the market moves fast, lender credit boxes change quarterly, and testimony about current lender appetite must be grounded in current market engagement. At CLS CRE, our active origination across approximately $1 billion in transaction volume with over 1,000 lender relationships across all fifty states means our market opinions are grounded in what is actually happening in the capital markets today, not what was happening during the last cycle.

Counsel should gather the following before initiating an expert engagement: trailing twelve to twenty-four months of operating statements, current rent roll with lease expiration dates, existing appraisal and any prior appraisals, property condition assessment if available, the current debt structure and payoff demand, the proposed plan treatment of the secured creditor, and any existing financing commitments or term sheets the debtor has obtained. The earlier this material is assembled, the more precisely the expert can scope the engagement and the more credible the resulting opinion will be at a contested confirmation hearing.

At the confirmation hearing itself, financing expert testimony is typically presented to establish two elements of 11 U.S.C. Section 1129 feasibility: that the plan is not likely to be followed by further liquidation or reorganization, and that the proposed exit financing is available on terms consistent with the plan. Opposing counsel will challenge the rate assumptions, the DSCR methodology, the LTV constraints, and whether the expert actually spoke to lenders or merely theorized. A well-prepared expert with documented methodology and primary market research will hold up to that challenge. An expert who relied solely on published indices without market outreach will not.

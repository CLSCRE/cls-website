Expert Witness: Commercial Loan Modification Standards in Round Rock, TX

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Loan Modification and Workout Dispute

The Round Rock Market Context

Round Rock, Texas sits at the northern edge of the Austin metropolitan statistical area, and its commercial real estate market reflects both the explosive growth dynamics of the greater Austin corridor and its own distinct economic identity. The city's permanent anchor as the global headquarters of Dell Technologies, combined with its position along the IH-35 corridor between Austin and Georgetown, has created a commercial real estate market that behaves differently from speculative suburban markets in other metros. This distinction matters enormously in a loan modification or workout dispute, because the baseline market conditions at the time of the modification agreement are central to determining whether the lender's conduct and the modification terms themselves were commercially reasonable.

Industrial and flex product along the Round Rock IH-35 submarket has performed with remarkable resilience through rate cycle disruptions. Vacancy in light industrial and flex-industrial product in the submarket has generally traded in the 5% to 8% range through the 2022 to 2024 period, well below the national average for comparable suburban industrial product. Net asking rents for industrial product have ranged from approximately $14 to $19 per square foot NNN depending on clear height, dock configuration, and vintage, with newer Class A distribution product pushing above that range in select transactions. Multifamily fundamentals in Round Rock have softened modestly from their 2021 to 2022 peak as new supply has been absorbed, with effective rents for Class B garden product settling in the $1.30 to $1.50 per square foot range and vacancy trending upward from sub-4% lows toward the 7% to 9% range as of late 2023 into 2024. Retail along University Boulevard and the major thoroughfares has remained relatively stable, anchored by strong household income demographics in the surrounding residential base. Office fundamentals present a more complicated picture, reflecting Austin metro-wide softness with submarket vacancy in traditional office product exceeding 15% in some pockets, a fact that becomes highly material in any workout analysis involving office-anchored or mixed-use collateral.

Understanding these market-specific conditions is not academic. In a workout dispute, the question of whether a lender should have granted a modification, what terms were commercially reasonable, and how the lender evaluated the property's recovery prospects cannot be answered without grounding the analysis in what the Round Rock market was actually doing at the time the modification negotiations occurred.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing a borrower in a commercial real estate workout dispute arising from a loan modification on a mixed-use retail and office property in the Round Rock submarket. The underlying loan was a conventional commercial real estate mortgage originated by a regional bank, secured by a stabilized but cash-flow-stressed property that had experienced tenant attrition during the post-pandemic office and retail softness cycle. The borrower had entered into modification discussions with the lender, and those discussions ultimately resulted in a written forbearance and modification agreement. The dispute arose from the borrower's contention that the lender failed to apply consistent, commercially reasonable underwriting standards during the modification process, that the modification terms imposed were materially more onerous than the market standard for similarly situated borrowers and assets, and that the lender's conduct during the workout process deviated from what a reasonable institutional lender would have done under comparable market conditions.

Counsel retained CLS CRE to provide a Rule 26 expert report addressing three core questions. First, did the lender's internal modification process and the terms it offered conform to prevailing industry standards for commercial real estate loan workouts? Second, were the modification terms, specifically the interest rate adjustment, reserve requirements, and partial paydown demand, commercially reasonable given the property's actual cash flow and the Round Rock market conditions at the time? Third, what would a reasonable lender have offered to a similarly situated borrower on a comparable asset in the same submarket and time period? These are financing feasibility and market standard questions, not valuation questions, and they required a different analytical framework than what an appraiser provides.

The Financial Analysis

The core of any workout modification dispute analysis is a rigorous reconstruction of the property's actual debt service capacity at the time the modification was negotiated. This requires building an independent NOI model, applying market-standard underwriting thresholds, and then comparing what the property could actually support against what the lender demanded. This analysis exposes whether the lender's modification terms were grounded in financial reality or imposed terms the property structurally could not support.

For the subject engagement, the property generated gross potential rents from its retail and office tenants totaling approximately $1,050,000 annually at the time modification discussions commenced. Applying a realistic vacancy and credit loss factor of 12%, consistent with the Round Rock submarket's office and retail conditions at that time, yielded an effective gross income of approximately $924,000. Operating expenses including property taxes, insurance, management fees, maintenance, and a modest capital reserve totaled approximately $312,000, producing a net operating income of approximately $612,000. This NOI figure was the foundation for every subsequent calculation.

The market standard debt service coverage ratio threshold for conventional commercial real estate loans secured by retail and office product in this lending environment is 1.20x at a minimum, with most institutional lenders and regional banks underwriting to 1.25x or higher for office-heavy collateral given perceived cash flow volatility. At a 1.25x DSCR requirement, the maximum annual debt service the property could support was $612,000 divided by 1.25, which equals $489,600 per year, or approximately $40,800 per month. Using a modified loan rate of 7.25%, which was consistent with prevailing rates for workout and modified commercial real estate loans at regional banks during the relevant period, and applying a 25-year amortization schedule, the monthly debt service of $40,800 supported a maximum loan balance of approximately $5,650,000.

The original loan balance at the time of modification was approximately $6,200,000. The lender's modification demanded a partial paydown to $5,900,000, a figure CLS CRE's analysis showed exceeded the maximum supportable loan balance by approximately $250,000 at market standard underwriting parameters. More importantly, the modified loan's required monthly debt service at the demanded balance and rate exceeded the property's supportable debt service by approximately $3,200 per month, creating a structural cash flow deficit that made default under the modified terms a near-mathematical certainty. This is not a judgment call; it is arithmetic. And arithmetic of this nature is precisely what courts and arbitrators need a financing expert to provide.

On a loan-to-value basis, an independent assessment of the property's then-current value at approximately $7,800,000 (consistent with cap rate expansion in the submarket during the relevant period) placed the pre-paydown LTV at approximately 79.5%. The lender's modification agreement demanded a paydown to achieve 75.6% LTV. While the LTV target itself was not unreasonable, the lender's methodology for arriving at the property value used in the LTV calculation was a subject of significant dispute, as it appeared to apply a cap rate inconsistent with market evidence from comparable sales in the Round Rock and broader Austin north submarket during that same period.

The Complicating Factors

The Lender's Internal Modification Process Documentation. One of the most significant complicating factors in this engagement was the absence of a coherent, documented credit analysis in the lender's modification file. Industry-standard commercial loan workout practice requires that a lender prepare a formal credit memorandum or workout analysis that documents the property's current cash flow, the basis for the modification terms proposed, a current property valuation methodology, and the lender's assessment of the borrower's ability to perform under the modified terms. When a lender cannot produce documentation showing that its modification terms were derived from a defensible financial analysis of the property's actual capacity, that gap is itself evidence of a deviation from industry standard practice. In this matter, the lender's file contained internal emails and telephone call logs but no formal credit analysis supporting the partial paydown demand or the rate adjustment.

The Timing of the Modification Relative to Market Conditions. The modification was negotiated during a period when Round Rock office and retail market conditions were actively softening, but before the full extent of that softness was visible in reported transactions. This timing created a genuine dispute about what a reasonable lender should have known about market conditions at the time the modification terms were set. Lenders with active Austin metro portfolios had access to broker surveys, CoStar vacancy data, and their own portfolio performance data that clearly indicated softening trends. The question of whether the lender applied a forward-looking stress scenario or simply used trailing twelve-month actual collections without adjustment is central to whether its underwriting met the standard of care.

Reserve Requirements as a De Facto Additional Paydown. The modification agreement required the borrower to fund a cash reserve equal to six months of modified debt service, deposited at closing of the modification as a condition to effectiveness. This requirement, approximately $245,000 in this case, was not commercially unreasonable on its face. However, when combined with the partial paydown demand, the aggregate cash requirement imposed on the borrower at modification closing exceeded $790,000. CLS CRE's analysis of comparable workout agreements in the Texas market during this period found that while reserve requirements of three to six months are common, lenders typically do not stack a large partial paydown with a maximum reserve requirement simultaneously when the borrower's liquidity position has already been stressed. Structuring both demands into a single modification closing, without any financial analysis of the borrower's capacity to fund both, represents a deviation from typical workout structuring practice.

The Lender's Failure to Consider Commercially Reasonable Alternatives. A commercially reasonable lender engaged in a workout process is expected to evaluate the full range of modification structures before imposing maximum demands. In this engagement, the lender's file contained no evidence that it evaluated an interest-only period as an alternative to the partial paydown, no analysis of a blended rate step-up structure, and no consideration of a maturity extension with covenant modifications as a standalone remedy. The absence of documented consideration of alternatives is material, because industry-standard workout practice holds that a lender should pursue the modification structure most likely to result in a performing loan, not the structure that maximizes the lender's near-term balance sheet position at the expense of the loan's long-term viability.

The Round Rock Lender Market

Understanding which lenders are active in the Round Rock commercial real estate market, and what their credit standards look like, is essential context for assessing whether any particular lender's workout conduct conformed to market norms. CLS CRE maintains active lender relationships across more than 1,000 institutional and non-institutional capital sources, with approximately $1 billion in transaction volume across 50 states, and our familiarity with the active Round Rock and Austin north submarket lender base informs this analysis directly.

Regional banks headquartered in Texas, including Austin-area community banks and Texas-chartered regionals with significant CRE concentrations, have historically been the most active construction and stabilized loan originators in the Round Rock market for retail, office, and mixed-use product in the $3 million to $15 million range. These institutions typically underwrite to 1.25x DSCR, 75% LTV on retail and office product, and 25-year amortization on five- or seven-year fixed terms. Their workout practices are governed by their internal credit policy manuals and FDIC examination guidelines, which do include specific standards around how classified or watchlist CRE loans should be modified.

CMBS and life company lending in the Round Rock market is concentrated in larger industrial, multifamily, and grocery-anchored retail assets generally above $15 million. For the mixed-use retail and office product at issue in this matter, CMBS was not a relevant modification comparison because the loan was not a CMBS loan. Debt fund and bridge lenders are active in Round Rock primarily for value-add and transitional assets, but their workout practices differ significantly from conventional bank workout standards and were not applicable as a benchmark here.

Credit unions chartered in Texas have increased their commercial real estate lending activity in suburban Austin markets including Round Rock, and several have approved CRE concentrations in the $5 million to $20 million range for owner-occupied and investor product. Their modification practices tend to be less formally documented than FDIC-regulated bank workout processes, which creates a higher evidentiary burden in disputes but does not immunize them from the underlying standard of care analysis.

What Attorneys Handling Round Rock CRE Disputes Need to Know

Attorneys handling commercial real estate litigation in Round Rock and the broader Austin north submarket should understand that a CRE financing expert witness fills a specific and non-duplicative analytical role. An appraiser determines value. A CRE financing expert determines whether a loan was structured, modified, or administered in a manner consistent with what a reasonable, competent lender or borrower would have done under prevailing market conditions. These are different questions, and they require different expertise.

When to retain a CRE financing expert: any time the dispute involves whether a lender's conduct in underwriting, modifying, or enforcing a loan met the applicable standard of care; any time a borrower claims that modification terms were commercially unreasonable or designed to engineer a default; any time a plan of reorganization in a Chapter 11 proceeding requires evidence that a proposed treatment of a secured claim meets the feasibility or best interests tests; and any time a lender claims that no commercially reasonable alternative to foreclosure existed.

Questions a CRE financing expert answers that an appraiser cannot: Was the maximum loan amount supportable by the property's cash flow at market underwriting standards? Did the lender's internal modification process conform to industry standard workout practice? Were the modification terms offered consistent with what similarly situated borrowers received in the same market and time period? Was the lender's rate adjustment, reserve demand, or paydown requirement commercially reasonable given the property's debt service capacity? Could a refinancing or payoff have been achieved in the market at the time the lender accelerated? These are capital markets and lending practice questions, not valuation questions.

What makes a qualified CRE financing expert: active, current transaction experience across multiple lender types and asset classes; demonstrated familiarity with the specific geographic market at issue; experience with the type of capital structure involved (bank, CMBS, debt fund, etc.); and prior expert witness engagement experience including deposition and trial testimony. A retired banker who originated loans twenty years ago and has not been active in the market is not the same as an active broker-intermediary who transacts with the lender community daily. The distinction matters because lender market practices, underwriting criteria, and workout norms evolve continuously, and expert opinions must be grounded in what the market actually requires today, not what it required a decade ago.

Information counsel should gather before the expert engagement: the full loan file including the original application, appraisal, underwriting credit memorandum, and closing documents; all modification correspondence, draft agreements, and the executed modification or forbearance agreement; the lender's internal credit policy manual or workout guidelines if obtainable through discovery; all rent rolls, operating statements, and borrower financial statements provided to the lender during the modification process; and any written communications between the lender's workout officer and credit committee regarding the modification terms.

Key Takeaways

  • Market context is foundational to every modification dispute analysis. The Round Rock submarket's specific vacancy rates, rent trends, and lender activity levels at the time of the modification are not background information; they are the evidentiary baseline against which the lender's conduct and the reasonableness of its terms must be measured.
  • DSCR and supportable loan calculations are objective, not subjective. If the modification terms demanded debt service that exceeded the property's NOI-based capacity at market standard coverage ratios, that is a mathematical finding, not an opinion, and it is among the most powerful forms of expert evidence in a workout dispute.
  • The absence of a documented credit analysis in the lender's modification file is itself evidence of a deviation from industry standard practice. A commercially reasonable lender prepares and retains a formal workout credit memorandum; the absence of one raises legitimate questions about whether the modification terms were grounded in analysis or served other purposes.
  • Stacking a large partial paydown demand with a maximum reserve requirement simultaneously, without documented analysis of the borrower's liquidity capacity to fund both, deviates from typical workout structuring practice in the Texas regional bank market.
  • A commercially reasonable lender evaluates and documents its consideration of the full range of modification structures, including interest-only periods, rate step-up structures, and maturity extensions, before imposing maximum demands. The failure to document this analysis is material to the standard of care question.
  • The CRE financing expert witness role is distinct from the appraiser's role. Attorneys who retain only an appraiser in a modification dispute leave the financing standard of care, debt service capacity, and market-based modification practice questions unaddressed.
  • Active, current market participation is the critical qualification criterion for a CRE financing expert witness. Opinion testimony on what a reasonable lender would have done must be grounded in knowledge of what lenders in the relevant market are actually doing today, not historical practice.

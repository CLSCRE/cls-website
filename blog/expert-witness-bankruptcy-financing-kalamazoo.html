Expert Witness: CRE Financing Feasibility in Chapter 11 Proceedings in Kalamazoo, MI

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Bankruptcy Exit Financing

The Kalamazoo Market Context

Kalamazoo is not a secondary market afterthought. It sits at the intersection of two of Michigan's most consequential economic corridors, with meaningful institutional anchors in Western Michigan University, the Stryker Corporation headquarters, and a pharmaceutical and life sciences sector that has driven sustained commercial real estate demand across multiple asset classes. For purposes of bankruptcy exit financing analysis, the market's fundamentals matter enormously because a court evaluating plan feasibility is, at its core, evaluating whether a specific property in a specific market can support debt service sufficient to satisfy the plan's obligations. Kalamazoo's fundamentals are nuanced enough that generic assumptions about Midwest secondary markets will produce an unreliable opinion.

On the industrial side, Kalamazoo has benefited from sustained manufacturing demand tied to the health sciences and automotive supply chain sectors. Industrial vacancy in the greater Kalamazoo submarket has remained compressed, with functional bulk and light industrial product trading at cap rates generally in the 6.0 to 7.5 percent range depending on tenancy and clear height. Net asking rents for industrial product have trended upward over the past three years, driven partly by limited developable land in the primary industrial corridors near the I-94 interchange and the Portage submarket. This is a market where stabilized industrial assets with creditworthy tenants can attract institutional lender interest.

Multifamily in Kalamazoo tells a more complex story. The presence of a large university population creates a bifurcated rental market: conventional workforce housing and student-adjacent product operate under different underwriting assumptions. Conventional multifamily cap rates have been observed in the 5.75 to 7.25 percent range depending on vintage, location, and unit mix, with rent growth moderating from the elevated pace of 2021 and 2022 but remaining positive in absolute terms. Occupancy in stabilized Class B and Class C workforce housing has been resilient, which matters significantly in a bankruptcy exit scenario where a lender is evaluating downside protection.

Office fundamentals in Kalamazoo reflect the national trend of elevated vacancy and compressed demand, with suburban office product facing the most pressure. Downtown Kalamazoo has seen some adaptive reuse activity and mixed-use development, but office assets subject to a bankruptcy proceeding will face heightened lender scrutiny regardless of local market conditions. Retail is asset-specific, with neighborhood-anchored and necessity-based product performing materially better than power center or enclosed mall configurations.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by counsel representing the plan proponent in a Chapter 11 reorganization proceeding involving a commercial property located in the Kalamazoo metropolitan area. The asset was a multi-tenant industrial and flex property with a mix of month-to-month and short-term lease configurations, partially stabilized but carrying meaningful near-term rollover exposure. The debtor had been in Chapter 11 for approximately fourteen months prior to our engagement, and the proposed Plan of Reorganization contemplated new first-mortgage financing as the primary mechanism for satisfying senior secured creditor claims.

Counsel retained CLS CRE to provide a Rule 26 compliant expert report addressing two core questions. First, could the subject property, given its income profile, physical condition, and market position, support first-mortgage financing in an amount sufficient to satisfy the senior secured creditor's allowed claim and fund plan obligations? Second, what categories of institutional lenders actively originate loans secured by assets that are emerging from bankruptcy, and what underwriting parameters would govern such financing? The court needed to determine whether the plan was feasible as a matter of law, and the financing feasibility opinion was central to that determination.

Our engagement required us to review the debtor's operating statements for the prior 24 months, the rent roll including lease expiration schedules, a third-party appraisal of the property, the proposed plan terms including the projected closing timeline, and a summary of the property's physical condition from a recent Property Condition Assessment. We also conducted independent outreach to lenders in our network to verify current market appetite and term structure for this asset type in this geography.

The Financial Analysis

The methodology for a bankruptcy exit financing feasibility opinion is not materially different from the underwriting methodology a sophisticated lender would apply, because the opinion is fundamentally an attempt to replicate the lender's credit decision process and determine whether a loan of the required size is achievable. The starting point is always a defensible estimate of stabilized Net Operating Income.

In this engagement, the in-place NOI as reported by the debtor was not a reliable baseline because of the distressed operating history during the bankruptcy period. Deferred maintenance, deferred leasing, and reduced property management attention had suppressed occupancy and, in some cases, rents. Our analysis therefore built a stabilized NOI from the ground up: we identified the property's leasable square footage by unit, applied current market rents derived from comparable transactions in the Kalamazoo industrial and flex submarket, applied a stabilized vacancy assumption of 8 percent consistent with submarket data, and then deducted operating expenses normalized for a properly managed asset rather than the distressed-period actuals. The result was a stabilized NOI that differed materially from the trailing twelve-month figure in the debtor's records.

For this analysis, the property comprised approximately 68,000 rentable square feet of light industrial and flex space. Market rents for comparable product in the Kalamazoo submarket supported a blended gross rate of approximately $7.25 per square foot on a modified gross basis, with tenants responsible for their proportionate share of operating expenses above a base year stop. At 92 percent occupancy, effective gross income was approximately $455,000 annually. After normalizing operating expenses, including management fees at 4 percent of effective gross income, insurance, real estate taxes based on current assessed values, maintenance reserves at $0.35 per square foot, and administrative costs, the stabilized NOI was approximately $318,000.

The DSCR threshold applied was 1.25x, which is the market standard for conventional commercial mortgage financing on this asset type and risk profile. Some lenders will underwrite to 1.20x for stronger tenancy or lower LTV requests, but 1.25x is the appropriate conservative assumption for a bankruptcy exit scenario where the lender is accepting elevated execution risk. At a note rate of 7.50 percent, which reflected the market environment at the time of the analysis, and using a 25-year amortization schedule, the annual debt service constant on a fully amortizing basis was approximately 8.78 percent of the loan amount. Solving backward from the DSCR constraint: maximum annual debt service equals NOI divided by 1.25x, or $318,000 divided by 1.25, which equals $254,400 of allowable annual debt service. Dividing that figure by the 8.78 percent constant produces a maximum supportable loan amount of approximately $2,898,000, which we rounded conservatively to $2,850,000 for the opinion.

The appraised value of the property, as-stabilized, was $4,100,000. The maximum supportable loan of $2,850,000 represented approximately 69.5 percent of that appraised value, which is within the LTV constraints that most conventional commercial lenders apply to this asset type. Most lenders cap LTV for industrial flex product at 70 to 75 percent, with some bridge and debt fund lenders willing to extend to 75 to 80 percent at higher pricing. The DSCR constraint, not the LTV constraint, was the binding limitation in this analysis, which is a typical finding in market environments with elevated interest rates.

The conclusion of the financial analysis was that the property could support first-mortgage financing of approximately $2,850,000 under market-rate terms available at the time of the engagement, and that this amount was sufficient to satisfy the senior secured creditor's allowed claim as set forth in the proposed plan.

The Complicating Factors

Bankruptcy Estate Title and Lender Reluctance. The most significant complicating factor in any bankruptcy exit financing engagement is the simple fact that many conventional lenders will not originate loans on assets that are in, or are just emerging from, a bankruptcy estate. This is not a blanket prohibition across the entire lending market, but it meaningfully narrows the field. Community banks and regional banks, which represent a substantial portion of the conventional lending market in markets like Kalamazoo, have internal credit policies that either prohibit bankruptcy estate lending or require senior credit committee approval that creates unacceptable timeline risk. Understanding which lenders will actually engage with a bankruptcy exit loan, as opposed to which lenders might theoretically be interested, is a core component of the expert's opinion.

Plan Confirmation Timeline Risk. Lenders who will originate bankruptcy exit loans are acutely sensitive to the gap between the issuance of a term sheet or commitment and actual plan confirmation and loan closing. A conventional lender issues a rate lock for 30 to 60 days in a standard transaction. In a contested confirmation proceeding, the timeline from commitment to closing may be 90 to 180 days or longer, particularly if the confirmation order is appealed or if there are disputes about the effective date. This timeline uncertainty drives lenders toward bridge and debt fund capital rather than agency or long-term conventional financing, which in turn affects pricing and structure assumptions in the feasibility analysis.

Rollover Exposure and Lease-Up Risk. In this particular engagement, the short-term and month-to-month lease configurations in the rent roll created a secondary complicating factor. Even if the stabilized NOI analysis supported the required loan amount, lenders underwriting a loan at or near closing of the plan would apply a haircut to NOI based on rollover probability. A lender who believes that 30 percent of the income stream might not renew within 12 months of closing will not underwrite to stabilized NOI; they will underwrite to a stressed scenario that accounts for re-leasing downtime and tenant improvement costs. Structuring around this issue requires either securing short-term lease extensions prior to the financing commitment, or identifying lenders whose credit box accommodates light-to-moderate rollover risk in exchange for appropriate reserve structures and pricing.

Deferred Maintenance and Property Condition. Assets in bankruptcy proceedings frequently exhibit deferred maintenance, because distressed owners typically reduce capital expenditures as cash flow pressure builds. Lenders will condition any commitment on a satisfactory Property Condition Assessment and will typically require an immediate repairs escrow funded at closing for any deficiencies identified. The existence of significant deferred maintenance does not necessarily defeat financing feasibility, but it does affect net loan proceeds and must be accounted for in the plan's cash flow projections.

The Kalamazoo Lender Market

The Kalamazoo lender market for conventional commercial real estate financing is deeper than many attorneys and investors assume. The market benefits from meaningful community and regional bank presence, including institutions headquartered in Western Michigan that have long track records of commercial lending in the Kalamazoo submarket. These lenders know the market, understand local property values, and can move efficiently on straightforward transactions. However, as noted above, most community bank credit policies exclude or heavily restrict bankruptcy estate lending, which means attorneys and plan proponents cannot assume that the depth of the conventional market translates directly into depth for bankruptcy exit financing.

For bankruptcy exit financing specifically, the relevant lender categories are as follows:

  • Debt Funds and Private Credit Lenders: This is the most active and reliable category for bankruptcy exit loans. These lenders, which include both nationally focused platforms and regionally active funds, underwrite to the property's income and collateral value rather than to the borrower's credit history. They can accommodate the timeline uncertainty inherent in bankruptcy proceedings, they price the additional complexity into the rate rather than declining to engage, and they can move quickly once a confirmation order is issued. Pricing for debt fund bridge loans in a Kalamazoo industrial flex context would typically be in the range of SOFR plus 300 to 500 basis points, with origination fees of 1.0 to 2.0 points, and terms of 12 to 36 months with extension options. LTV up to 70 to 75 percent on stabilized value.
  • SBA 7(a) and SBA 504 Programs: For owner-occupied commercial properties, the SBA programs represent a meaningful exit financing option because the federal government guarantee structure effectively removes the lender's credit concern about the bankruptcy history. The borrower must be an eligible small business and the property must be primarily owner-occupied, but where those conditions are met, SBA financing at longer terms and lower rates than bridge capital may be achievable. The SBA programs have been underutilized in bankruptcy exit contexts because many practitioners are unfamiliar with the eligibility analysis.
  • CMBS and Agency Lenders: These lenders are generally not available for bankruptcy exit financing in the immediate term. CMBS conduit lenders require clean title, seasoned operations, and borrower credit histories that are inconsistent with a recent Chapter 11 emergence. Agency lenders (Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, FHA) have similar constraints for multifamily. However, for an asset that completes a successful exit, stabilizes operations, and seeks a refinance 12 to 24 months after plan consummation, agency and CMBS capital may become available. This long-term refinance availability is itself relevant to the feasibility opinion because it supports the conclusion that permanent, lower-cost capital is accessible once the bankruptcy stigma has seasoned.
  • Specialty Finance Companies and Insurance Company Correspondents: A subset of specialty finance platforms that focus on transitional assets may engage on bankruptcy exit loans in markets like Kalamazoo, particularly for industrial assets with strong collateral characteristics. These lenders typically require minimum loan sizes in the $3,000,000 to $5,000,000 range, which can be a constraint in smaller Kalamazoo transactions.

What Attorneys Handling Kalamazoo CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to Retain a CRE Financing Expert. Counsel should engage a commercial real estate financing expert as soon as the financing feasibility of a proposed plan becomes a contested issue. In most Chapter 11 cases involving commercial real estate, the objecting creditor will challenge plan feasibility by arguing that the debtor cannot obtain the financing required to consummate the plan. The plan proponent needs an affirmative expert opinion, not just legal argument, to satisfy the court that financing is available on terms that make the plan feasible. Waiting until the eve of the confirmation hearing to retain the expert is a mistake; the expert needs time to conduct independent lender outreach, review financial documents, and prepare a defensible written report.

What a CRE Financing Expert Answers That an Appraiser Cannot. Appraisers determine value. A financing expert determines whether a loan of a specified amount is achievable in the current market, on what terms, from what lender categories, and subject to what conditions. These are fundamentally different questions. An appraiser can tell the court that a property is worth $4,000,000. Only a financing expert can tell the court that a $2,800,000 first mortgage is available in the current market, which specific lender categories will originate it, what the interest rate and term will be, and what the borrower must satisfy as a condition to closing. Courts evaluating plan feasibility need both opinions, and confusing them creates risk for the presenting party.

What Qualifies a CRE Financing Expert. A retired banker who worked at a single institution is not the same as a commercial mortgage broker with active market relationships across multiple lender categories. The expert's value is in their current, active knowledge of lender underwriting standards, credit appetites, and deal execution across a broad market. CLS CRE maintains relationships with over 1,000 lenders nationally, has closed approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, and operates in all 50 states. When our firm says that a specific lender category will or will not engage on a bankruptcy exit loan in Kalamazoo, that opinion is based on current conversations with those lenders, not historical memory from a prior career.

Information Counsel Should Gather Before the Engagement. To allow the expert to produce a timely and defensible report, counsel should assemble the following before or immediately after the engagement: two to three years of operating statements for the subject property; the current rent roll with lease expiration dates and any renewal options; the most recent appraisal; the Property Condition Assessment if one has been conducted; the proposed plan terms including the required financing amount and projected closing date; any existing lender correspondence regarding the property; and the debtor's post-bankruptcy operating history. The more complete the document production at the outset, the more defensible the expert opinion will be under cross-examination.

The financing feasibility opinion is typically presented through a written Rule 26 report submitted prior to the confirmation hearing, with the expert available for live testimony if the feasibility issue is contested.

Expert Witness: CRE Financing Feasibility in Chapter 11 Proceedings in Buffalo, NY

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Bankruptcy Exit Financing

The Buffalo Market Context

Buffalo, New York sits at an inflection point that makes it a genuinely interesting commercial real estate market for bankruptcy exit financing analysis. The city is no longer the distressed rust-belt story it was twenty years ago, but it is also not a gateway market with deep institutional capital competing aggressively for every deal. That middle position matters enormously when a court needs to determine whether a commercial asset can support new first-mortgage financing sufficient to satisfy a reorganization plan. The answer depends entirely on where lender appetite actually sits for a specific asset type in a specific submarket, and Buffalo's lender dynamics are distinct enough that generic assumptions will produce wrong answers.

On the multifamily side, which is one of the most common asset types appearing in Western New York bankruptcy proceedings, vacancy rates in the Buffalo metro have remained below 5% in most submarkets, including the Elmwood Village corridor, the Allentown district, and transitional neighborhoods adjacent to the medical campus. Effective rents for Class B workforce housing have trended upward consistently since 2020, with meaningful rent growth in the $1,100 to $1,450 per-unit-per-month range for two-bedroom units in desirable zip codes. Cap rates on stabilized multifamily assets in Buffalo have compressed into the 6.0% to 7.5% range depending on vintage, location, and unit mix, which is tighter than many secondary markets of comparable size. That compression reflects real investor demand, and it has direct implications for debt service coverage analysis in a reorganization context.

Industrial and flex product in the Buffalo market has similarly benefited from supply constraints and modest but steady absorption. Vacancy in the Tonawanda corridor and the Cheektowaga submarket has remained in the 4% to 7% range, supported by regional distribution demand and light manufacturing. Net rents in functional Class B industrial have ranged from $5.50 to $8.50 per square foot depending on clear height, dock configuration, and proximity to the 290/90 interchange systems. Office is a more complicated story. Suburban office in the Williamsville and Amherst submarkets carries materially higher vacancy than pre-pandemic levels, and downtown Class B office has struggled with absorption. Any financing analysis involving office collateral in Buffalo must account for realistic stabilization timelines and the narrowed lender universe that follows.

The broader point is this: Buffalo is a market with genuine, documentable fundamentals across its strongest asset classes, and those fundamentals are sufficient to support institutional financing when the deal is structured correctly. Courts in the Western District of New York need expert testimony grounded in that specific market reality, not national averages or generic secondary-market assumptions.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by counsel for a plan proponent in a Chapter 11 reorganization proceeding pending in the Western District of New York. The debtor estate held a mid-size multifamily asset in the Buffalo metro, a garden-style apartment community with approximately 80 units, built in the late 1980s, in good functional condition but with deferred capital expenditure needs that had been documented in prior appraisals submitted to the court.

Counsel needed to establish two things at the Plan confirmation hearing. First, that the asset could support a new first-mortgage loan in an amount sufficient to pay off the senior secured creditor in full, as required under the plan's treatment of that class. Second, that the financing was not merely theoretical but actually available from identifiable categories of institutional lenders actively operating in the Buffalo market. The objecting creditor had argued that the proposed financing was speculative and that no realistic lender would advance funds against a bankruptcy estate asset under the proposed terms.

CLS CRE was engaged to produce a financing feasibility opinion addressing both questions: the maximum supportable loan amount given current market underwriting standards, and the universe of lenders who actively originate first-mortgage loans on commercial real estate assets emerging from Chapter 11, with specific reference to the Western New York market and the relevant asset class. Our opinion was structured to satisfy the disclosure requirements of Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 26 and to withstand cross-examination by opposing counsel on methodology, assumptions, and lender market knowledge.

The Financial Analysis

The foundation of any financing feasibility opinion is a rigorous underwriting of the property's net operating income, applied against current lender credit standards. In this engagement, I began with the actual trailing twelve-month operating statements produced in discovery, reconciled against the rent roll and bank statements. For a stabilized multifamily asset, lenders underwrite to in-place NOI with adjustments for market vacancy, management fees, and normalized capital reserves. I did not simply accept the debtor's historical NOI, which had been depressed by deferred maintenance and management disruptions common in Chapter 11. Nor did I project speculative future NOI based on full stabilization. I applied a disciplined approach: current in-place rents at actual occupancy, adjusted to reflect market vacancy of 5% consistent with the submarket, a market-rate management fee of 8% of effective gross income, and a replacement reserve of $350 per unit per year consistent with the property's vintage and condition.

For this asset, the resulting underwritten NOI was approximately $480,000 annually. That figure is specific to the facts of this engagement and is not representative of all Buffalo multifamily assets; I present it here to illustrate the methodology, not to establish a market benchmark.

Lenders in the current rate environment are underwriting multifamily assets at debt service coverage ratios of 1.20x to 1.25x minimum for agency-eligible product, and 1.25x to 1.35x for balance sheet lenders and CMBS execution. The distinction matters because the bankruptcy overlay typically eliminates agency execution in the near term, pushing the deal toward balance sheet lenders who apply the more conservative 1.25x threshold. I used 1.25x DSCR as the binding constraint, which is the market standard for the relevant lender category and is not a conservative outlier.

On rate and amortization, I applied a 7.25% interest rate on a 25-year amortization schedule, consistent with pricing available from community banks, regional banks, and debt fund lenders active in Buffalo for stabilized multifamily with a post-bankruptcy borrower profile. Some lenders were pricing at 7.00% for the strongest deals; others at 7.50% or above for deals with credit complexity. The 7.25% midpoint was defensible and documented against actual term sheets in our file.

Working backward from these inputs: at 1.25x DSCR, the maximum annual debt service supportable is $384,000 ($480,000 divided by 1.25). At a 7.25% interest rate on a 25-year amortization schedule, the annual debt service constant is approximately 8.68%. Dividing $384,000 by 8.68% produces a maximum supportable loan of approximately $4,424,000, which I rounded conservatively to $4,400,000 for purposes of my opinion.

I then tested that loan amount against loan-to-value constraints. The property had been appraised in connection with the reorganization proceedings at $6,800,000. A $4,400,000 loan represents a 64.7% LTV ratio, which falls within the 65% LTV ceiling that most balance sheet lenders apply to stabilized multifamily in secondary markets. The LTV test and the DSCR test converged within a reasonable range, which is a meaningful indicator of financing feasibility: when the income-based constraint and the value-based constraint produce similar loan amounts, the analysis is internally consistent and more defensible under cross-examination.

My opinion concluded that first-mortgage financing of $4,200,000 to $4,400,000 was financially feasible based on current market underwriting standards, which was sufficient to retire the senior secured creditor's allowed claim under the proposed plan treatment.

The Complicating Factors

The Bankruptcy Stigma and Borrower Entity Issues. The single most significant complicating factor in any bankruptcy exit financing engagement is the lender's perception of the borrower entity and its principals. Most institutional lenders have written credit policy prohibitions against lending to borrowers with an open bankruptcy, and many extend that prohibition for 24 to 36 months following discharge or plan confirmation. In practice, this eliminates conventional bank lenders, agency lenders (Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, FHA), and most CMBS conduit programs from consideration at the time of plan confirmation. The financing universe narrows materially to debt funds, certain private lenders, and select community banks whose credit committees have discretion to make exceptions. The key structuring solution is the "newco" or reorganized entity concept: the plan vests the asset in a newly formed entity with clean title, and the principals of that entity, if they have not themselves been debtors, may present a more acceptable borrower profile. This requires careful coordination between bankruptcy counsel and the financing team.

Title and Lien Discharge. Lenders require clean, insurable title as a condition of funding. In a Chapter 11 plan context, Section 1141 of the Bankruptcy Code provides for the discharge of pre-confirmation liens upon plan confirmation, but title insurers and lenders alike require the confirmation order to be final and non-appealable before they will rely on that discharge. This creates a timing gap between plan confirmation and the availability of financing, and courts must understand that "financing is available" does not mean "financing can close tomorrow." A realistic timeline from confirmation to closing typically runs 60 to 90 days, depending on title underwriter requirements, lender due diligence, and any pending appeals of the confirmation order.

Deferred Maintenance and Capital Reserves. Bankruptcy estate properties frequently present with documented deferred maintenance, which lenders treat as a borrower obligation rather than a property characteristic. Most lenders will require an upfront escrow or holdback equal to 110% to 125% of the estimated deferred maintenance cost. For this asset, the deferred maintenance estimate was approximately $350,000, requiring a lender holdback of approximately $420,000. That holdback effectively reduces the net loan proceeds available at closing, which means the plan must account for it. A plan that assumes $4,400,000 of loan proceeds but does not account for a $420,000 holdback will produce a liquidity shortfall that can derail closing.

Property Management Continuity. Lenders advancing funds against multifamily assets want demonstrated, stable management in place. A debtor-in-possession that has been managing the property through its own principals or through a court-approved DIP manager may not satisfy lender requirements for a professional, third-party management company with a demonstrated track record. Lenders will require a management agreement with a qualified third-party manager as a condition of loan commitment, and that transition must be planned in advance of the financing process, not during it.

The Buffalo Lender Market

Community and Regional Banks. Buffalo benefits from an unusually active community and regional banking presence relative to many secondary markets. Several institutions headquartered in Western New York have long histories of originating commercial real estate loans in the local market and carry genuine relationship-based flexibility in their credit underwriting. These lenders will consider assets with complexity, including post-confirmation reorganized entities, when the principals have credible operating histories and the property fundamentals are solid. Typical pricing from this category runs 150 to 225 basis points over the relevant index (SOFR or 5-year Treasury depending on structure), with 25-year amortization and 5-year or 7-year fixed terms. LTV limits are typically 65% for multifamily and 60% for other property types in this credit environment. Portfolio flexibility is the primary advantage; slower underwriting timelines are the tradeoff.

Debt Funds and Bridge Lenders. For assets where the bankruptcy stigma, deferred maintenance, or occupancy issues push the deal outside community bank credit boxes, debt funds and bridge lenders represent the most accessible financing category. These lenders are not balance sheet constrained and underwrite primarily to asset coverage and exit strategy. Pricing runs materially higher, typically in the 9.5% to 11.5% range on a current-pay basis, with origination fees of 1.5 to 2.5 points. Terms are typically 2 to 3 years with extension options. The Buffalo market is accessible to national debt funds through loan brokers and direct originator relationships. The higher cost of this capital must be modeled into the plan's feasibility analysis and the senior creditor's recovery calculation.

Life Companies and CMBS. Life company lenders and CMBS conduit lenders are largely inaccessible at the time of plan confirmation due to borrower eligibility requirements and their institutional aversion to bankruptcy estate assets. These lenders become relevant only after a seasoning period of 24 to 36 months post-confirmation, once the reorganized borrower has established clean credit and the property has demonstrated stabilized operating performance. Their pricing advantages, typically 175 to 250 basis points below debt fund pricing, make them the logical refinancing target in a well-structured plan, and a feasibility opinion may appropriately reference them as the take-out financing vehicle rather than the day-one execution lender.

What Attorneys Handling Buffalo CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to Retain a CRE Financing Expert. Any Plan of Reorganization that proposes to satisfy a secured creditor through refinancing of real property collateral requires financing feasibility testimony. The same is true of a plan that proposes to sell a property and assumes a buyer can obtain acquisition financing. Appraisers testify to value. They do not testify to whether a lender will actually advance funds against that value, at what terms, subject to what conditions, or within what timeframe. Those are distinct questions, and courts in the Western District of New York have consistently required evidence on financing feasibility that goes beyond an appraised value conclusion.

What a CRE Financing Expert Can Answer That an Appraiser Cannot. An appraiser applies a capitalization rate to NOI and produces a value opinion. A CRE financing expert applies lender underwriting standards to NOI and produces a loan sizing opinion. These are different analyses. The financing expert also testifies to the availability of specific lender categories in the relevant market, current pricing and structure for those lender categories, the impact of specific deal complications on the lender universe, and the realistic timeline from plan confirmation to loan closing. None of that is within an appraiser's professional scope.

Qualifications That Matter. The most common mistake attorneys make is retaining a retired banker or a mortgage originator with limited market breadth. A qualified CRE financing expert should have active, current origination experience across multiple lender categories, access to real-time market pricing, and a demonstrated history of closing loans on complex assets in markets similar to the one at issue. At CLS CRE, we maintain relationships with more than 1,000 lenders across all capital categories, have closed approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, and are active in 50 states. That breadth is what allows us to testify with specificity about lender appetite, not just recite general principles.

What to Gather Before the Expert Engagement. Counsel should have available before the initial expert consultation: the trailing 12-month and prior year operating statements, the current rent roll, any existing appraisals produced in the case, the proposed plan treatment of the senior secured creditor, and any existing financing history on the property including prior loan agreements. The earlier this material is assembled, the faster the expert can produce a preliminary feasibility assessment to inform litigation strategy before committing to full Rule 26 report preparation.

Use at Trial or Plan Hearing. Financing feasibility opinions are typically presented through direct examination and supported by a detailed written report with supporting calculations, lender market data, and documentation of comparable financing transactions. The expert should be prepared to defend DSCR assumptions, rate assumptions, LTV constraints, and lender availability conclusions on cross-examination. Expert testimony on financing feasibility is frequently the determinative evidence on whether a cramdown plan satisfies the feasibility requirement of 11 U.S.C. Section 1129(a)(11).

Expert Witness: Commercial Loan Origination Standards in Boston, MA

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Lender Liability and Loan Origination Standards

The Boston Market Context

Boston is one of the most technically demanding commercial real estate markets in the country for financing purposes, and that complexity creates fertile ground for disputes. The metro's fundamentals vary sharply by asset class, submarket, and vintage, which means underwriting standards that are appropriate in one context can be demonstrably inadequate in another. When a lender liability claim or professional negligence allegation surfaces in Greater Boston, the expert analysis has to be grounded in what this specific market was doing at the specific moment the loan was originated, not what a generic underwriting manual says.

In the multifamily sector, which drives a significant share of Boston CRE litigation, cap rates in core neighborhoods like the Seaport, Back Bay, and Fenway have historically compressed into the 4.0% to 4.75% range, while suburban submarkets in Somerville, Medford, and Cambridge adjacent corridors often trade in the 4.75% to 5.50% range. These distinctions matter enormously in a lender liability context because a broker or lender who applied suburban cap rate assumptions to a core urban asset understated value, and one who applied core urban assumptions to a value-add suburban asset overstated it. Either error, if it influenced loan sizing, becomes a standard of care question.

The office market adds another layer. Greater Boston's life sciences and lab conversion pipeline fundamentally disrupted traditional office underwriting starting in the early 2020s. Vacancy in conventional office in submarkets like the Financial District and Downtown Crossing climbed toward 15% to 18% as of late 2023, while lab and life sciences space in Kendall Square, East Cambridge, and the Seaport remained structurally undersupplied. A lender or broker who failed to distinguish between these two product types when underwriting mixed-use or conversion assets during this period has a problem. The standard of care requires that distinction to be made explicitly, not assumed away.

Industrial and flex space in Greater Boston, particularly in submarkets like Everett, Chelsea, and the Route 128 corridor, sustained vacancy rates below 5% through most of 2021 to 2023, with asking rents climbing sharply. Retail is more nuanced: neighborhood-anchored retail outperformed while power center and enclosed mall assets continued to face structural demand challenges. Any expert analysis of Boston CRE financing must begin with a precise characterization of the asset type, its submarket, and the market conditions at origination. Generic national benchmarks are not sufficient for a Boston dispute.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by counsel representing the borrower in a lender liability matter involving a mid-size mixed-use asset in the inner Boston metropolitan area. The property consisted of ground-floor retail space with residential units above, a common configuration in Boston's dense urban submarkets. The borrower alleged that the mortgage broker who placed the financing failed to meet the professional standard of care in several respects: the loan terms disclosed at application differed materially from those delivered at closing; the broker failed to adequately explain rate lock mechanics and float-down provisions available in the market; and the broker recommended a loan structure that did not reflect available alternatives that would have been materially more favorable to the borrower.

Counsel asked CLS CRE to address three core questions. First, whether the loan origination process, including the broker's analysis, disclosures, and recommendations, met the standard of care for a reasonably competent commercial mortgage broker operating in the Boston market at the time of origination. Second, whether the loan terms ultimately delivered were consistent with what was available in the lender market for this asset type and borrower profile at that time. Third, what a reasonably competent broker would have done differently, and whether those differences would have materially affected the borrower's outcome.

This is a precise and consequential set of questions. They require an expert who understands not just underwriting mechanics, but lender appetite, pricing conventions, rate lock and fee structures, and the actual behavior of competing lenders in Boston during the relevant period. An appraiser can opine on value. A retired bank underwriter can speak to internal credit policy. Neither is positioned to address what the brokerage market was actually delivering for a borrower with this profile, in this submarket, at this moment in the rate cycle.

The Financial Analysis

My analysis began with a reconstruction of the subject property's stabilized net operating income at the time of origination. The property generated gross potential rent across its residential units and retail space, with vacancy and credit loss applied separately to each component because lenders underwrite them differently. Residential vacancy was analyzed using market data from comparable properties in the submarket; retail vacancy was stress-tested more aggressively given the post-pandemic behavioral shift in retail demand patterns, even for well-located neighborhood retail in Boston.

For this analysis, I used a stabilized NOI of approximately $620,000 annually, reflecting market rents, a 5% residential vacancy assumption, and a 10% retail vacancy assumption reflecting both actual performance and lender-required stress. Operating expenses were underwritten at approximately 38% of effective gross income, consistent with market norms for a mixed-use asset of this vintage and configuration in Greater Boston. The resulting stabilized NOI became the foundation for maximum loan sizing under multiple lender scenarios.

The industry standard DSCR for a mixed-use asset of this type at the time of origination was 1.25x at the stress rate, with some bank lenders requiring 1.30x on the retail component given elevated perceived risk. I applied a 1.25x DSCR floor as the market standard threshold, which is consistent with Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, and conventional bank guidelines for mixed-use properties with meaningful retail exposure. Using a 30-year amortization schedule and a stress rate 200 basis points above the origination rate (consistent with CMBS and agency stress test conventions), the maximum supportable debt service was approximately $496,000 annually.

At a blended origination rate of 6.75% on a 25-year amortization schedule, that annual debt service ceiling supported a maximum loan of approximately $5.85 million. The actual loan placed by the broker was $6.4 million, representing a meaningful overleverage relative to the supportable debt service at stressed rates. When I cross-checked that loan amount against an as-is appraised value of $9.8 million, the resulting LTV was approximately 65.3%, which is within conventional guidelines. However, the LTV test and the DSCR test are not interchangeable. A lender or broker who sized a loan to the LTV limit without verifying DSCR compliance at stressed rates failed a fundamental underwriting obligation. That failure is a standard of care issue, not a judgment call.

The analysis also showed that an alternative loan structure available in the market at that time, specifically a 10-year fixed rate with a 30-year amortization through a regional bank lender at 6.50%, would have reduced annual debt service by approximately $28,000 and created meaningful additional cash flow cushion for the borrower. The broker's failure to present this alternative, document why it was not recommended, or obtain the borrower's informed acknowledgment of the tradeoffs represents a deviation from what a reasonably competent commercial mortgage broker would have done.

The Complicating Factors

Mixed-use assets with retail components in Boston present specific underwriting challenges that a competent broker must navigate explicitly, not paper over. The first complicating factor in this matter was the retail tenant profile. The ground-floor retail space was occupied by a single tenant on a lease with less than three years of remaining term at origination. Most institutional lenders apply haircuts to retail income when lease term is shorter than loan term, typically discounting that income stream by 25% to 50% or excluding it entirely from NOI for DSCR purposes. The broker's analysis treated the retail income at full contract rent without any lease term adjustment. That is not how the Boston lender market actually underwrites short-term retail leases, and it inflated the qualifying NOI.

The second complicating factor was the rate environment. The loan was originated during a period of significant rate volatility, when the gap between application-rate and closing-rate could be material depending on lock structure. The broker had access to rate lock products that would have protected the borrower's economics from the time of application through closing, including float-down options from at least two lenders in our firm's network who were actively lending on Boston mixed-use assets during this period. The broker's engagement letter and disclosure documents contained no discussion of rate lock options, their costs, or their availability. That omission matters because rate movement between application and closing directly affected the borrower's debt service and their ability to meet the lender's DSCR requirement at funding.

The third complicating factor was the presence of a partial personal guarantee requirement that the broker represented as non-negotiable. My review of comparable loan originations during the same period revealed that at least three lender categories, including credit unions, regional banks, and certain bridge lenders, were regularly originating non-recourse or carve-out-only structures for Boston mixed-use assets at this loan size. The characterization of the full personal guarantee as a market standard, rather than as a specific requirement of the selected lender, was factually incorrect and deprived the borrower of the ability to make an informed lender selection decision.

The Boston Lender Market

The Boston CRE lending market is unusually deep for a city of its size, which cuts both ways in a dispute context. The depth means there were alternatives available; the sophistication means the standard of care is correspondingly higher for brokers and lenders operating here.

Agency lenders, specifically Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac through their approved seller-servicer networks, are active on qualifying multifamily assets in Greater Boston. For mixed-use properties where residential income constitutes 80% or more of total revenue, agency execution may be available, typically at the most competitive fixed-rate terms. Agency guidelines require DSCR of 1.25x at the note rate for standard executions, 30-year amortization maximums, and LTV limits that vary by leverage tier. Boston multifamily assets often qualify for high-cost market adjustments that modestly expand leverage parameters.

Local and regional banks, including several headquartered in Massachusetts or with substantial Boston market presence, remain the dominant lenders for mixed-use, retail-inclusive, and transitional assets in the $3 million to $20 million range. These institutions underwrite to portfolio standards, which means their credit box can be more flexible on property type but less flexible on borrower financial strength. They typically require 1.25x to 1.30x DSCR at the origination rate, 20 to 25-year amortization, and full or partial recourse. Rate pricing is generally 200 to 275 basis points over the relevant Treasury or SOFR index, depending on loan term and borrower relationship depth.

Debt funds and bridge lenders are active in Greater Boston for transitional, construction, and value-add assets. Their pricing reflects execution certainty and speed rather than rate competitiveness, typically in the 8.5% to 11.0% range depending on leverage and asset risk. These lenders are not appropriate for stabilized assets where conventional execution is available, and recommending bridge financing for a stabilized mixed-use asset without documenting the rationale would itself be a standard of care question.

CMBS conduit lenders remain a financing option for Boston assets above $5 million with stable cash flows and long-term leases. Conduit execution is non-recourse by structure, which is a meaningful benefit for certain borrowers, but it comes with yield maintenance or defeasance prepayment penalties that can be economically punishing. A broker who recommended conduit execution for a borrower with an identified near-term disposition plan, without explicit disclosure of prepayment economics, made a material omission.

What Attorneys Handling Boston CRE Disputes Need to Know

The threshold question attorneys face is whether their expert needs to be a CRE financing specialist or whether another expert category, such as an appraiser or a general real estate consultant, is sufficient. The answer depends on what is actually in dispute. If the dispute centers on value, an appraiser is the right expert. If the dispute centers on whether a loan should have been made, how it was structured, what was disclosed, what alternatives were available, or whether the origination process met professional standards, you need a CRE financing expert. Those are different disciplines, and conflating them is a mistake that opposing counsel will exploit.

A qualified CRE financing expert can address questions that are entirely outside an appraiser's scope: What DSCR thresholds were lenders applying in this market at this time? Was the rate lock structure offered to the borrower consistent with market practice? Were there materially better-priced alternatives available that the broker failed to present? Was the lender's credit decision consistent with its own written underwriting guidelines? What would a reasonably competent broker have done at each stage of this transaction? These are capital markets questions, not valuation questions.

What distinguishes a qualified CRE financing expert from a retired banker is current, active market knowledge combined with transactional breadth. A retired bank underwriter knows one institution's internal credit policy from years past. An active commercial mortgage broker with a broad lender network knows what the entire market was actually doing, what was being offered, at what pricing, and under what conditions. At CLS CRE, we have maintained over 1,000 lender relationships across 50 states and have closed approximately $1 billion in transaction volume. That is not a credential argument; it is a methodology argument. Our opinions are grounded in what the market was actually delivering, not what a policy manual said it should deliver.

Counsel preparing to retain a CRE financing expert should gather the following materials before the engagement begins: the full loan application package including the broker's underwriting model; all term sheets, commitment letters, and closing documents; any broker-borrower engagement agreement or fee disclosure; email and written communications between the broker, borrower, and lender throughout the origination process; the appraisal and any lender-ordered environmental or engineering reports; and, where available, competing term sheets or financing proposals that were presented or rejected during the same period. The richer the documentary record, the more specifically the expert can tie opinions to contemporaneous evidence rather than reconstructed assumptions.

At trial or in an arbitration proceeding, CRE financing expert testimony is most effective when it is structured around the specific decisions made by the defendant at each stage of the origination process, evaluated against what a reasonably competent practitioner would have done at each of those same decision points. This is not a general critique of lending or brokerage practice; it is a transaction-specific, stage-by-stage analysis that the trier of fact can follow and evaluate.

Key Takeaways

  • Boston's market complexity raises the standard of care. The depth of the Boston lender market, the variability across asset classes and submarkets, and the sophistication of the borrower and institutional community mean that competent origination practice here requires more than generic underwriting mechanics. Lenders and brokers are held to the standards of this market, not a national average.
  • DSCR and LTV are independent tests, not substitutes. A loan that passes the LTV test can still fail the DSCR test at stressed rates. A competent lender or broker must verify compliance with both independently. Sizing to LTV without stress-testing DSCR is a quantifiable standard of care deviation.
  • Retail lease term adjustments are not discretionary. Lenders in the Boston market apply documented haircuts to short-term retail income when underwriting mixed-use assets. A broker who treated short-term retail leases at full face value in the qualifying NOI used a methodology that does not reflect market practice.
  • Rate lock and float-down disclosures are broker obligations, not optional services. When rate volatility is material at origination, a competent broker must document the availability of rate protection products, present them to the borrower, and obtain an informed election. Silence on this topic is not neutral; it is a failure of professional responsibility.
  • The characterization of loan terms as "non-negotiable" requires factual support. A broker who tells a borrower that a particular term, whether guarantee structure, prepayment penalty, or covenant, is a market standard without verifying that claim against available alternatives is making a material representation that can be tested and refuted.
  • An active commercial mortgage broker provides different expert testimony than a retired banker. The relevant question in a lender liability case is what the market was actually offering, not what a single institution's internal guidelines required. Active, multi-lender market knowledge is the correct methodology for answering that question.
  • Document collection is the foundation of the expert's analysis. The difference between a persuasive expert opinion and a vulnerable one often comes down to the quality and completeness of the transactional record. Counsel should prioritize early, comprehensive document collection before engaging the financing expert.
  • CRE financing expert testimony can be dispositive on causation. Even where liability is established, causation requires proving that the breach made a material difference. A CRE financing expert can quantify the delta between what the borrower received and what a properly executed origination would have delivered,

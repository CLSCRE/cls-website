Expert Witness: How Construction Defects Affect CRE Financing in Birmingham, AL

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Construction Defect Impact on Financing

The Birmingham Market Context

Birmingham's commercial real estate market occupies a position that is often underestimated by coastal capital sources but well understood by regional lenders and Southeast-focused investors. The metro has undergone meaningful structural diversification over the past decade, moving beyond its legacy in steel and manufacturing toward a broader base anchored by healthcare, financial services, higher education, and logistics. UAB Health System alone is one of the largest employers in Alabama, and that institutional anchor has catalyzed demand across multifamily, medical office, and mixed-use assets in the Five Points South, Southside, and Lakeview submarkets. At the same time, the Interstate 20/59 and Interstate 65 corridors have attracted industrial and distribution users drawn to Birmingham's geographic position as a one-day truck drive to roughly 47 million consumers.

On the industrial side, Birmingham's vacancy has remained in the 4 to 6 percent range for bulk distribution and last-mile product, with asking rents for Class A industrial space pushing toward $6.00 to $7.50 per square foot NNN in well-located submarkets like Oxmoor and the Lakeshore corridor. Multifamily fundamentals are more nuanced: effective rents in the urban core have grown steadily, but new supply deliveries in 2023 and 2024 created pockets of concession activity, pushing stabilized cap rates into the 5.5 to 6.5 percent range depending on asset vintage and submarket. Office remains challenged, consistent with national trends, but Class B suburban product along U.S. 280 has held occupancy better than downtown Class A towers, which face meaningful lease rollover risk through 2026.

What makes Birmingham particularly relevant for construction defect financing disputes is the prevalence of value-add and adaptive reuse projects that accelerated during the 2017 to 2022 development cycle. Ground-up and heavy renovation projects in Avondale, Woodlawn, and the downtown core attracted a mix of local developers and out-of-state capital that sometimes outpaced the availability of experienced local contractors. That dynamic created conditions where construction defects, whether in waterproofing, structural systems, MEP rough-in, or building envelope, are not hypothetical scenarios. They are live issues in active litigation, and the financing consequences of those defects are a specific, quantifiable category of damages that requires expert analysis grounded in how lenders actually underwrite and price risk in this market.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing a property owner in a construction defect matter involving a newly constructed mixed-use multifamily asset in the Birmingham metro. The property had been developed with ground-floor retail and upper-floor residential units, completed within the prior 18 months, and was in the process of being stabilized when the defect claims surfaced. The alleged defects included significant building envelope failures, inadequate waterproofing at the podium level, and HVAC system deficiencies across a material percentage of the residential units.

Counsel needed expert analysis on three core questions. First, what is the maximum supportable loan on this asset in its defect-impaired condition, and how does that compare to the loan that would have been available on a defect-free asset of equivalent type and location at the time of the alleged damage? Second, what specific lender requirements and credit conditions are triggered when a lender becomes aware of construction defects, and how do those conditions affect loan proceeds, rate, and structure? Third, how do the income disruption and remediation cost exposure created by the defects affect the property's debt service coverage ratio and loan-to-value, and what is the resulting financing gap that can be quantified as a component of damages?

These are not questions an appraiser is positioned to answer in full. An appraiser can opine on value. CLS CRE was engaged to analyze financing feasibility: what a lender would actually do with this asset in this condition, how that differs from the as-completed standard, and how to quantify the financing impairment as a concrete economic number.

The Financial Analysis

Our methodology began with a dual-scenario underwrite: the asset as stabilized and defect-free, and the asset in its actual defect-impaired condition as of the analysis date. Both scenarios used the same gross potential rent roll, drawn from the executed lease and pre-lease documentation, adjusted by submarket vacancy and concession data for comparable Birmingham mixed-use multifamily assets. The distinction between scenarios was driven by three factors: actual vacancy and lost revenue attributable to defect-affected units, required reserves and holdbacks imposed by lenders aware of pending defect litigation, and the capitalized impact of remediation cost exposure on stabilized net operating income.

In the defect-free scenario, the stabilized NOI for this asset class and size in the Birmingham market was underwritten at approximately $1,050,000 annually, reflecting a 94 percent economic occupancy on the residential component and a market-rate assumption on the ground-floor retail. Operating expenses, including management, insurance, maintenance, taxes, and a capital reserve allowance of $250 per unit annually, were underwritten at approximately 38 percent of effective gross income, consistent with agency and bank underwriting conventions for this product type in the Southeast.

The lender market for a stabilized, defect-free mixed-use multifamily asset of this type in Birmingham would include agency executions through Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac for the residential component (if the residential portion meets agency underwriting thresholds), regional bank balance sheet lenders, and life company or CMBS executions depending on loan size and deal structure. Agency lenders price to a minimum DSCR of 1.25x at the note rate, with some agency programs applying a stressed rate for sizing purposes. Regional bank lenders in Birmingham typically underwrite to a 1.20x to 1.25x DSCR at the actual note rate, with LTV constraints at 70 to 75 percent for mixed-use product.

Applying a 1.25x DSCR constraint at a 7.00 percent note rate on a 30-year amortization schedule to the $1,050,000 stabilized NOI in the defect-free scenario yields a maximum annual debt service of $840,000, which supports a loan amount of approximately $10,650,000. At an appraised value of $15,000,000 for the stabilized, defect-free asset, the resulting LTV is approximately 71 percent, within agency and regional bank credit parameters. This represents the financing baseline: the loan that would have been available had the project been delivered without defect.

In the defect-impaired scenario, three adjustments compressed both NOI and the lender's willingness to extend credit. First, the defect-affected units, representing approximately 22 percent of the residential square footage, were either offline for remediation or subject to rent concessions required to retain tenants willing to remain in a building with known issues. This reduced effective gross income by approximately $185,000 annually. Second, lenders aware of pending construction defect litigation impose holdback reserves against loan proceeds, typically sized at 100 to 150 percent of estimated remediation costs. With remediation estimates in the $1,800,000 range, lenders required a holdback of $2,000,000 to $2,700,000 against gross loan proceeds, reducing net loan proceeds available to the borrower accordingly. Third, the insurance market response to active construction defect litigation included a premium increase on the property's builder's risk and property policy, adding approximately $28,000 annually to operating expenses.

The result in the impaired scenario: stabilized NOI available for debt service fell to approximately $837,000. Applying the same 1.25x DSCR constraint and rate assumption yields a maximum supportable loan of approximately $8,475,000 on a purely income-basis analysis. After applying the $2,000,000 minimum holdback required by lenders aware of the litigation, net loan proceeds available to the borrower fall to approximately $6,475,000. The financing gap between the defect-free loan and the net proceeds available in the defect-impaired scenario is approximately $4,175,000, a concrete, calculable number that represents the financing impairment attributable to the alleged defects.

The Complicating Factors

Lender Litigation Awareness and Underwriting Triggers. When a lender's due diligence or title search reveals active construction defect litigation, it does not simply proceed to fund on normal terms. Most institutional lenders, including agency lenders, regional banks, and CMBS conduits, have explicit credit policies requiring disclosure of material pending litigation. Construction defect claims meeting a materiality threshold, typically where the alleged defect affects structural systems, the building envelope, or life-safety components, are treated as undisclosed liabilities until resolved. The practical effect is that the financing universe for this asset contracts sharply. Lenders who might otherwise compete aggressively for a stabilized Birmingham multifamily deal step back, leaving the borrower dependent on lenders willing to accept the litigation risk, typically at higher rates and tighter LTV constraints.

Remediation Cost Uncertainty and Reserve Requirements. One of the most consequential lender responses to known construction defects is the holdback or escrow requirement tied to remediation cost estimates. Lenders do not take the borrower's or even the owner's contractor's remediation estimate at face value. They require independent assessment, typically from a third-party building consultant or engineer, and then size their holdback at a multiple of that estimate to account for scope creep and unknown conditions. In the Birmingham matter analyzed here, the spread between the contractor's initial estimate and the independent engineer's estimate was approximately 40 percent, which itself became a point of dispute regarding the adequacy of the original construction contract and the developer's project controls.

Title Insurance and ALTA Survey Complications. Active construction defect litigation creates complications at the title insurance level that further impair financing. Lenders require clean lender's title insurance as a condition of closing. When a mechanics lien has been filed, or when the construction defect litigation creates a cloud on title through a lis pendens, the title company may issue coverage subject to exceptions that lenders will not accept. In the Birmingham matter, the filing of a lis pendens by the general contractor in connection with a related payment dispute created a title exception that required resolution before any institutional lender would fund. The cost and timeline of clearing that exception were components of the financing disruption quantified in the expert analysis.

Appraisal Adjustments and As-Is Value Compression. Appraisers working on a defect-impaired asset in Birmingham must contend with a market where comparable sales rarely involve disclosed construction defects, creating a methodological challenge in supporting the adjustment. Lenders are aware of this and frequently require the appraiser to opine on both the as-is value and the as-stabilized, as-repaired value, with the lender's underwriting constrained by the as-is figure. The compression between as-is and as-repaired values in this engagement was approximately $3,200,000, directly impacting the LTV calculation and further reducing the maximum loan supportable on an LTV basis.

The Birmingham Lender Market

Birmingham supports a deep and varied lender market relative to its metro size, and understanding the distinct credit culture and appetite of each lender category is essential to expert financing analysis in this type of dispute.

Regional and Community Banks. Birmingham is home to a meaningful concentration of community and regional banks with active commercial real estate balance sheet lending programs. These lenders, including institutions with strong Alabama franchise presence, are typically the most flexible on deal structure but also the most cautious when faced with active litigation. Their credit committees are relationship-driven, and a borrower with an existing banking relationship may be able to access bridge or construction-to-permanent financing even with defect issues, but at spreads 150 to 250 basis points above their standard pricing and with significant covenant requirements tied to litigation resolution milestones. For this type of matter, regional banks represent the most accessible financing source, but the cost of capital is materially higher than a defect-free execution.

Agency Lenders (Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac). For the multifamily component of mixed-use assets, agency lenders represent the most competitive long-term financing available in Birmingham under normal conditions. However, agency guidelines effectively preclude lending on properties with unresolved material construction defects. Fannie Mae's Selling and Servicing Guide requires that properties with known, unresolved defects affecting habitability or structural integrity be ineligible for agency financing until the defects are remediated and certified as resolved by a qualified third party. Freddie Mac applies similar restrictions. The result is that the agency execution, which would have provided the lowest-cost, highest-proceeds option for a defect-free stabilized asset, is unavailable until full remediation is complete and documented.

CMBS Conduit Lenders. CMBS execution is theoretically available for mixed-use assets in Birmingham at the right loan size, but conduit lenders are among the least flexible when active litigation is present. Conduit underwriting is performed for securitization, and securities disclosure requirements make undisclosed or unresolved construction defect litigation a material issue at the pool level. The result is that CMBS execution is effectively unavailable for this asset type in its defect-impaired condition.

Debt Funds and Bridge Lenders. The category of lenders most willing to engage with a defect-impaired asset is the non-bank debt fund and bridge lending market. These lenders price risk rather than avoid it, and in Birmingham, several nationally active debt funds have demonstrated willingness to lend on assets with pending litigation at LTVs of 55 to 65 percent and rates 300 to 500 basis points above the senior agency market. This is the financing available to a borrower in the defect-impaired scenario, and the cost differential between this execution and the defect-free agency or bank execution is itself a quantifiable component of damages.

What Attorneys Handling Birmingham CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to Retain a CRE Financing Expert. The moment a CRE dispute involves a claim that hinges on financing feasibility, loan impairment, or the economic consequences of a change in the asset's condition, counsel should be engaging a financing expert alongside any appraiser. Appraisers opine on value. They are not underwriters, they have not assembled loan packages for lenders in this market, and their methodology is not designed to answer the question of what a lender would actually do with a specific asset in a specific condition. A financing expert fills the gap between "what is the property worth" and "what loan was available, on what terms, and how does that differ from what would have been available absent the alleged wrong."

What a Financing Expert Can Answer That an Appraiser Cannot. A CRE financing expert can opine on the specific lender responses to defect conditions, including holdback and escrow requirements, rate adjustments, LTV compression, and the contraction of the available financing universe. The expert can quantify the financing gap as a standalone damages component. The expert can explain how lender credit policies work, what triggers litigation-related underwriting restrictions, and how the cost of capital differential between a defect-free and defect-impaired execution translates into a net present value impairment. None of these are within the standard scope of an appraisal assignment.

Qualifications That Matter. Litigation counsel should distinguish between a retired banker with general CRE experience and an active capital markets professional who is currently placing loans in the Birmingham market. The lender market, credit standards, and interest rate environment change materially over time. An expert who last originated a loan five years ago is working from an outdated frame of reference. CLS CRE has closed approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, maintains relationships with more than 1,000 lenders, and is actively placing capital in markets across all 50 states, including the Birmingham metro. That current market access is what makes our expert analysis credible to a jury or a judge who is evaluating the real-world financing consequences of the alleged defects.

Information Counsel Should Gather Before the Engagement. To support an efficient and comprehensive expert engagement, counsel should assemble the following at the outset: the rent roll and operating statements for the subject property covering the period of alleged damage; all lender correspondence, loan applications, and term sheets received during the relevant period; the construction contract and any change orders or pay application disputes; all expert reports from engineering or building consultants quantifying the defects; the property's insurance policies and any correspondence from insurers regarding coverage for the defect claims; and any appraisals commissioned by the owner or lender. With this information, a financing expert can move quickly to a defensible, calculation-supported opinion on financing impairment.

Use of Financing Expert Testimony at Trial. Financing expert testimony in a construction defect matter typically serves two purposes. First, it establishes the financing that was available to the property owner absent the defect, creating the baseline against which impairment is measured. Second, it quantifies the specific financing gap, rate premium, and holdback burden imposed on the defect-impaired asset, providing the jury or the court with a concrete number that represents the financing component of damages.

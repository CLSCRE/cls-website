Expert Witness: Commercial Loan Modification Standards in Spokane, WA

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Loan Modification and Workout Dispute

The Spokane Market Context

Spokane is not a tertiary market that lenders and attorneys can afford to dismiss with generic underwriting assumptions. The Spokane MSA has matured into a genuine second-tier Pacific Northwest commercial real estate market, with institutional capital actively competing alongside regional banks and credit unions for quality assets. That competitive lending environment matters enormously in loan modification and workout disputes, because the standard of care for a lender conducting a workout in Spokane in 2022 or 2023 is not the standard of a rural Idaho community bank working out a single-tenant note. Spokane has real market depth, real broker opinion of value support, and real comparables to anchor a financing analysis.

On the multifamily side, Spokane has experienced sustained rent growth over the 2019 to 2023 period, with average effective rents rising from roughly $900 per month to over $1,200 per month across Class B and C product before flattening in 2024 as new supply came online. That trajectory is directly relevant to workout disputes, because a lender conducting a 2021 or 2022 modification had strong market tailwinds to work with. A lender that refused to modify or imposed punitive terms during that period bears scrutiny when the market data shows improving fundamentals.

Industrial vacancy in Spokane has remained historically tight, in the 3% to 5% range through most of 2022 and 2023, with asking rents for warehouse and flex product climbing from the low-to-mid $0.50 per square foot NNN range to $0.75 to $0.90 NNN on newer product near the airport and Valley submarkets. Retail has been more bifurcated: grocery-anchored and neighborhood service centers in South Hill and the North Side have held occupancy well, while older strip centers along arterials have faced more volatility. Office in Spokane has tracked national trends, with downtown Class B space absorbing sublease inventory slowly and suburban flex-office holding better. Each of these fundamentals bears directly on what a commercially reasonable modification would have looked like at any given point in time.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing a commercial borrower in a dispute arising from a loan modification and workout process on a stabilized multifamily property in the Spokane Valley submarket. The property was a mid-size apartment community, and the borrower had sought a forbearance and subsequent permanent modification following a period of reduced occupancy tied to a combination of deferred maintenance remediation and market disruption during the COVID-19 period.

The lender, a regional bank with a Pacific Northwest commercial real estate portfolio, had engaged in extended modification discussions with the borrower over approximately fourteen months. The borrower ultimately alleged that the lender's conduct during that process, including repeated requests for redundant documentation, delays in credit committee response, and the final modification terms offered, did not meet the standard of care for commercially reasonable workout practices. The lender countered that the modification terms were dictated by the property's cash flow and its own internal credit policy.

CLS CRE was asked to address three specific questions. First, whether the documentation requests and process timeline reflected industry-standard workout practices or constituted procedural bad faith through delay. Second, whether the modification terms ultimately offered, specifically the interest rate, amortization period, principal paydown requirement, and debt service coverage covenant, were commercially reasonable given the property's documented income and Spokane market conditions at the time of the modification. Third, whether alternative modification structures existed in the market that could have accommodated the borrower's cash flow while adequately protecting the lender's collateral position. These are precisely the questions where a capital markets professional adds value that a standard appraiser cannot provide.

The Financial Analysis

The analysis began with a reconstruction of the property's net operating income at three points in time: the pre-disruption stabilized period, the trough of the performance problem, and the trailing twelve months immediately preceding the modification negotiation. This three-period NOI analysis is essential in any workout dispute because it establishes whether the lender was evaluating the borrower on a distressed snapshot or on a realistic forward-looking basis consistent with market recovery trends. Lenders in commercially reasonable workouts are expected to evaluate both current and stabilized NOI, particularly when market conditions support a return to stabilization.

For this Spokane Valley multifamily asset, the stabilized NOI was reconstructed at approximately $420,000 annually, based on documented rents, a 7% vacancy assumption consistent with the Spokane market at the time, and normalized operating expenses. The trough NOI, reflecting the occupancy disruption period, had dropped to approximately $285,000. The trailing twelve-month NOI at the time of modification discussions had recovered to approximately $365,000, reflecting re-leasing activity that was documented in the rent rolls produced in discovery.

The standard DSCR threshold for a multifamily workout modification with a regional bank lender in the 2022 to 2023 Spokane market was 1.20x to 1.25x on the modified debt service. Some lenders apply 1.20x on a current-cash-flow basis but underwrite to 1.25x on a stressed or go-forward basis. This distinction is critical in litigation because a lender that demands 1.30x DSCR on current distressed NOI, without crediting market recovery, is applying a standard inconsistent with commercially reasonable workout practice in this asset class and market.

At the modification interest rate the lender proposed, which was a spread of 275 basis points over the five-year FHLB rate, producing a note rate of approximately 7.40% at the time, and on a 25-year amortization schedule, the annual debt service on the outstanding loan balance of approximately $3.8 million was roughly $322,000. Against trailing twelve-month NOI of $365,000, that produces a DSCR of approximately 1.13x, which falls below the lender's own stated 1.20x threshold. The lender's position that the modification terms were consistent with its credit policy therefore contained an internal contradiction: the terms it proposed did not satisfy the coverage ratio it cited as the basis for its demands.

At stabilized NOI of $420,000, the maximum supportable debt service at 1.20x coverage is approximately $350,000 annually. On a 25-year amortization at 7.40%, that debt service supports a loan balance of approximately $4.15 million. The outstanding balance of $3.8 million was therefore fully supportable against stabilized NOI at standard market DSCR thresholds. This analysis established that the lender had no legitimate cash-flow basis to demand a principal paydown as a condition of the modification, which was one of the contested terms.

LTV analysis provided a secondary validation. At a market cap rate of 5.75% applied to stabilized NOI, the indicated value was approximately $7.3 million. At 65% LTV, the maximum loan supported by collateral value alone was approximately $4.75 million, providing significant headroom above the $3.8 million outstanding balance. Even at a stressed 6.25% cap rate, the value was approximately $6.7 million, supporting a 65% LTV loan of $4.36 million. The lender's position that the collateral was impaired was not supported by the market data.

The Complicating Factors

Lender's internal credit policy versus market standard: One of the most common complicating factors in Spokane workout disputes is the gap between a lender's internal credit policy and what the broader market considers commercially reasonable. A community bank or regional bank may have a credit policy that was written for origination, not for workouts, and applying origination underwriting standards to a workout situation is not consistent with industry practice. In a workout, lenders are expected to weigh the cost of foreclosure, the timeline to REO disposition, likely loss severity, and the borrower's demonstrated capacity to return the asset to stabilization. A lender that mechanically applies origination LTV and DSCR standards to a workout, without conducting a comparative analysis of workout versus enforcement costs, has arguably not met the standard of care for commercially reasonable workout conduct.

Documentation fatigue and bad faith delay: Extended modification negotiations in Spokane involving regional bank and community bank lenders have, in our experience, often suffered from repeated documentation requests that do not materially advance the credit analysis. When a lender requests updated rent rolls, operating statements, and borrower financial statements three or four times over a fourteen-month period without issuing a formal term sheet, the question of whether that conduct reflects legitimate underwriting diligence or procedural delay becomes a factual issue for the trier of fact. An expert witness can establish what documentation a commercially reasonable lender would require, in what sequence, and within what timeframe, providing a baseline against which the actual conduct can be measured.

The principal paydown demand: Requiring a borrower to pay down principal as a condition of a modification is a legitimate lender tool, but only when the outstanding balance exceeds the supportable debt supported by cash flow or when collateral value has materially declined. When neither condition is present, a principal paydown demand functions as a liquidity extraction from a borrower in temporary distress, which is not consistent with the purpose of a workout. In Spokane's multifamily market, where values remained resilient through 2022 and 2023, a principal paydown demand unsupported by collateral impairment analysis is a red flag.

Covenant structure and default triggers: Modification agreements in the Spokane market sometimes include forward-looking DSCR covenants that create technical default triggers before the property has fully restabilized. A covenant requiring 1.20x DSCR measured quarterly on trailing three-month annualized NOI, during a restabilization period when occupancy is ramping from 78% to 93%, will almost certainly trigger a technical default even if the borrower is performing on every modified payment. Counsel should examine whether the covenant structure in the modification agreement was designed to produce compliance or to create a path to enforcement.

The Spokane Lender Market

Understanding which lenders are active in Spokane for multifamily and commercial workout modifications is essential both for establishing market standards and for evaluating whether the lender's conduct was consistent with its peer group. The Spokane lending market for commercial real estate modifications operates across several distinct categories.

Washington-chartered community banks and credit unions represent the most active modification lenders for smaller multifamily and commercial properties in the $1 million to $8 million balance range. These institutions generally have workout authority at the regional credit officer level and can move relatively quickly when motivated. Their credit boxes for modifications typically require 1.20x DSCR on trailing twelve-month NOI, 65% LTV on a current appraisal, and a demonstrated path to stabilization. They are relationship-driven and will often accept interest-only periods during restabilization if the collateral coverage is adequate.

Regional bank lenders with Pacific Northwest portfolios, including several Oregon and Washington-headquartered institutions, are active in the $5 million to $30 million balance range. These lenders have more formalized workout departments and are more likely to require Special Assets team involvement for any modification, which adds process time but also adds documentation discipline. Their modification pricing typically reflects a spread over current index rather than the original spread, which can be a point of contention in workout disputes when rates have risen materially since origination.

CMBS special servicers operate under pooling and servicing agreement constraints that limit their modification flexibility, but Spokane has seen CMBS workouts on retail and office product. Special servicers are bound by NPV tests and cannot modify in ways that benefit the borrower at the expense of certificateholders, which creates a structural limitation on what commercially reasonable conduct looks like in that context. Counsel handling CMBS workout disputes needs an expert who understands PSA mechanics, not just general modification standards.

Debt funds and bridge lenders are less relevant to modification disputes in Spokane given the market's relatively lower bridge lending penetration, but they are present in value-add multifamily and transitional retail. Their modification standards are more aggressive, with shorter extension periods and tighter equity contribution requirements, and what is commercially reasonable for a debt fund modification is materially different from what is commercially reasonable for a bank modification.

What Attorneys Handling Spokane CRE Disputes Need to Know

The single most important point for litigation counsel to understand is that a CRE financing expert witness and a real estate appraiser serve fundamentally different functions. An appraiser establishes value. A CRE financing expert establishes what the capital markets would do with that value, how a lender should have evaluated the cash flow, what modification structures were available in the market, whether the lender's conduct conformed to industry standards, and whether the borrower could have obtained alternative financing. These are not appraisal questions. They require someone who has actually structured, negotiated, and closed commercial real estate loans and modifications in the relevant market and time period.

A qualified CRE financing expert should be able to demonstrate active transactional experience in the relevant asset class and market, not simply prior employment at a bank in a credit review function. A retired banker who reviewed loan files is not the same expert as a capital markets professional who has negotiated hundreds of modifications across multiple lender categories. The distinction matters when opposing counsel examines qualifications, and it matters more when the expert is asked to explain why a commercially reasonable lender would have structured a modification differently.

Counsel should retain a CRE financing expert at the earliest stage of discovery, ideally before written discovery is propounded, so that document requests can be informed by an understanding of what a proper modification file should contain. A complete modification file includes the original credit approval, the lender's internal workout policy, all credit memoranda submitted to credit committee, correspondence reflecting the timeline of modification discussions, the financial analysis supporting modification terms, and any appraisal or broker opinion of value obtained during the workout period. Gaps in this file are as probative as the documents present.

At trial or in an arbitration hearing, CRE financing expert testimony is most effective when it moves from market standard to specific conduct. The testimony should establish what a commercially reasonable lender would have done in the same market, with the same collateral, facing the same borrower circumstances, and then measure the actual lender conduct against that baseline. Jurors and arbitrators understand the concept of a professional standard of care, and a well-structured CRE financing opinion provides exactly that framework.

For counsel handling Spokane matters specifically, it is worth noting that the Spokane commercial real estate market has sufficient transaction volume and lender activity to support market-specific testimony. Generic national standards are a starting point, but a Spokane-specific analysis of what regional and community bank lenders were actually doing in the relevant time period carries greater persuasive weight.

Key Takeaways

  • Lender conduct in a modification must be evaluated against market standards, not just internal policy. Internal credit policy that departs significantly from commercially reasonable workout practice in the Spokane market at the relevant time is not a complete defense to a modification dispute.
  • NOI analysis in a workout dispute requires three periods: stabilized pre-disruption, trough, and trailing twelve months at the time of modification. Evaluating the borrower solely on trough performance, without crediting documented recovery, is inconsistent with standard workout underwriting practice.
  • Principal paydown demands require collateral impairment support. If LTV and DSCR analysis on stabilized cash flow shows the outstanding balance is fully supported, a principal paydown condition is difficult to justify as commercially reasonable.
  • Modification process timeline is a substantive issue, not just background. Repeated documentation requests without issuing a formal term sheet over an extended period can constitute procedural bad faith if the requests do not materially advance the credit analysis.
  • Covenant structure in modification agreements deserves as much scrutiny as rate and term. A covenant designed to trigger technical default during a documented restabilization trajectory may not reflect commercially reasonable workout intent.
  • A CRE financing expert provides capital markets analysis that an appraiser cannot. Questions about modification structure alternatives, DSCR standards, lender conduct, and market financing conditions require active transactional expertise, not property valuation expertise.
  • The Spokane market has sufficient depth to support market-specific expert testimony. General national standards are a foundation, but Spokane-specific lender behavior, cap rates, and modification market conditions in the relevant time period provide stronger evidentiary support.
  • Engage the CRE financing expert before discovery is complete. The expert's knowledge of what a proper modification file should contain is essential to propounding targeted document requests and identifying gaps that support your theory of the case.

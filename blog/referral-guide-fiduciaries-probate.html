Commercial Real Estate Financing in Probate, Trust, and Conservatorship: A Practical Guide for Fiduciaries

I am Trevor Damyan, a commercial mortgage broker at CLS CRE in Los Angeles. Over the course of my career, I have worked on commercial financing transactions where the borrowing entity was an estate, a trust, or a conservatorship rather than an individual or a business. These are among the most technically demanding transactions in commercial real estate finance, and they are almost always mishandled by lenders who do not understand the legal structure of the borrower.

If you are a probate attorney, professional fiduciary, conservator, trustee, estate administrator, or executor, this article is written specifically for you. I want to explain how commercial real estate financing actually works in your context, what lenders require, and why a broker who runs a competitive process across many lenders is the right resource for your clients rather than a single bank.

Why Fiduciaries Encounter Commercial Real Estate Financing Needs

Professional fiduciaries manage assets on behalf of others, and those assets frequently include commercial real estate. A decedent who built a portfolio over forty years does not liquidate it before dying. A conservatee whose cognitive decline removed her ability to manage her own affairs still owns the strip mall that has been generating rental income for decades. A trust created for tax efficiency reasons often holds commercial property as its primary asset.

When you take control of one of these estates, trusts, or conservatorships, you inherit the financing obligations and opportunities that come with the real estate. Existing loans come due. Properties need capital improvements to remain competitive. Estate taxes create immediate cash demands. Heirs disagree about disposition. The court imposes timelines that do not align with a conventional bank's underwriting calendar.

And you, as the fiduciary, are legally obligated to act in the best interest of your beneficiaries. That obligation extends to how you finance commercial real estate. A bad financing decision is a fiduciary breach. A failure to explore available options is a fiduciary breach. The standard of care is not just "did you find financing." It is "did you find the best financing available under the circumstances."

That is exactly why a competitive broker process protects you personally, in addition to serving your clients.

The Five Financing Scenarios Fiduciaries Most Commonly Face

1. Estate Owns Commercial Property and Needs to Pay Debts Before Distribution

This is the most common scenario I see. A decedent leaves behind a commercial property that is either free and clear or lightly leveraged, and the estate owes federal estate taxes, state estate taxes, outstanding attorney fees, creditor claims, or some combination of these. The estate cannot distribute assets to heirs until the debts are satisfied, but the property is the primary asset and selling it quickly would sacrifice significant value.

The solution is an estate financing transaction: a new commercial mortgage secured by the property, with the estate as borrower, generating the cash needed to pay debts and allow orderly administration. The proceeds can cover estate taxes under IRC Section 6166 arrangements, pay counsel, fund administration costs, or simply position the property for a more favorable eventual sale.

The critical issue is timing. Estate tax extensions and court schedules are real constraints, and a conventional bank that takes 90 to 120 days to close will miss the window. Bridge lenders and portfolio lenders who can close in 30 to 45 days are often the right solution here, even if their rates are higher than a bank. The interest cost on a 60-day bridge loan is trivial compared to the penalty exposure on a missed estate tax deadline or the value destruction of a forced sale.

2. Conservatorship over an Incapacitated Person with Income-Producing Commercial Real Estate

A conservator appointed to manage the financial affairs of an incapacitated person may find that the conservatee's primary assets are commercial properties: apartment buildings, retail centers, office buildings, or industrial properties. These properties need ongoing management, capital expenditures, and sometimes refinancing to optimize cash flow for the conservatee's care needs.

This scenario introduces a specific legal complication: the conservator cannot act unilaterally on major financial decisions. California Probate Code Section 2540 and equivalent statutes in other states require court authorization for borrowing beyond certain thresholds. This means the financing process involves a petition, a hearing, a court order, and then execution of the loan documents, all within whatever timeline the lender will tolerate.

Not every lender will tolerate it. Banks that have never worked with conservatorships will quote a 60-day rate lock, watch the court calendar eat through it, and then either decline to extend or reprice significantly. Lenders who regularly work with court-supervised transactions understand that a 90-day rate lock is not a gift; it is a minimum requirement. Finding those lenders is exactly what a broker with conservatorship experience does.

3. Trustee of a Revocable or Irrevocable Trust Holding Commercial Property

Trusts present a borrower-identity challenge. The trust itself is the borrower, but lenders want to understand who is behind the trust, what the trust documents authorize, and whether the trustee has the power to encumber trust assets. These questions have real answers, but they require the lender's legal team to actually read the trust agreement rather than running it through a standard commercial loan checklist.

For revocable trusts, lenders are generally more comfortable because the grantor is typically still living and may guarantee the loan. For irrevocable trusts, the analysis is more complex: the trustee has a fiduciary duty to the beneficiaries, the trust document may restrict borrowing or encumbrance, and there may be no individual willing or able to guarantee the debt personally.

Non-recourse commercial loans are therefore common in irrevocable trust contexts, particularly for stabilized income-producing properties. The loan is underwritten on the property's cash flow, not the creditworthiness of the trustee, and the trust document's language on borrowing authority becomes a legal closing condition rather than an underwriting factor.

Bridge transactions are also frequent here. A trustee who inherits a property with an existing loan approaching maturity needs to either refinance into a permanent loan or bridge to a sale. The timeline is often dictated by the maturity date, not the trustee's preference, which creates urgency that benefits from having multiple lenders competing simultaneously.

4. Probate Estate with Commercial Property that Multiple Heirs Want to Keep -- Heir Buyout Financing

When a decedent leaves commercial real estate to multiple heirs and some want to keep the property while others want to sell, the solution is often a buyout: the heirs who want to retain the property borrow against it to purchase the interests of the heirs who want to liquidate. This is economically equivalent to a cash-out refinance, but the borrower structure is more complex because the acquiring heirs may not yet have formal title.

These transactions require precise coordination between the estate attorney, the probate court, and the lender. The sequence of events matters: the property must transfer out of the estate and into the ownership of the acquiring heirs (or an entity they control) before or simultaneously with the loan closing, and the loan proceeds fund the buyout of the departing heirs at or near that same closing. A lender who has done this before knows how to structure the escrow and closing mechanics. A lender who has not will create delays and confusion that raise the temperature in an already emotionally charged family dispute.

The underwriting on these transactions is also distinctive. The acquiring heirs may not have a credit history with commercial real estate. The property may be generating income that was previously managed by the decedent. The lender needs to assess whether the new ownership group can actually operate the asset. Bridge lenders and smaller portfolio banks are often more flexible here than institutional lenders who require a clean sponsorship profile.

5. Court-Supervised Financing Transactions

Court approval is required for certain fiduciary financing decisions, similar in procedural structure to debtor-in-possession financing in bankruptcy. The fiduciary petitions the court, the court may require notice to interested parties and a hearing, and the financing cannot be consummated until the court issues its order. Only then can the lender's counsel prepare final loan documents and schedule a closing.

I have significant experience with court-supervised commercial real estate financing. The key is identifying lenders who will quote terms that are contingent on court approval, commit to a rate lock period long enough to survive the court calendar, and engage their legal team early to review draft loan documents before the order issues. The worst outcome is obtaining court approval and then discovering that the lender has changed its terms or that its counsel has concerns that could have been identified two months earlier.

In court-supervised contexts, transparency with the lender is essential. I always disclose the court approval requirement at the start of the process, not after a term sheet is signed. Lenders who are uncomfortable with court-supervised deals will tell you immediately, and that saves everyone time. Lenders who routinely work on these transactions will factor the timeline into their commitment letter and work constructively toward closing.

What Lenders Actually Require for Estate, Trust, and Conservatorship Financing

This section is technical by design. Professional fiduciaries benefit from knowing what the underwriting process actually involves so they can prepare their files efficiently and set realistic expectations with their clients and the court.

Entity Documentation

Every lender will require proof that the borrowing entity exists and is authorized to borrow. For estates, this means Letters Testamentary or Letters of Administration. For conservatorships, this means the Letters of Conservatorship. For trusts, this means a certified copy of the trust agreement (or an abstract) plus proof of the trustee's current authority. If there is a successor trustee or a co-trustee structure, all trustee authority documents are required. Lenders will not waive this documentation, and gaps in the chain of authority are a hard stop.

Court Authorization

Where court approval is required, the lender will typically want to see the proposed financing included in the petition, either by term sheet reference or by attaching the commitment letter as an exhibit. Some lenders require that their specific loan terms be approved by the court; others are satisfied with general authorization to encumber the property up to a specified loan amount at market rate terms. Clarifying this with the lender at the term sheet stage avoids having to re-petition.

Property-Level Underwriting

Commercial lenders underwrite to the property, not the borrower identity. For stabilized income-producing properties, the primary metrics are debt service coverage ratio (DSCR) and loan-to-value (LTV). Most lenders require a minimum DSCR of 1.20x (meaning net operating income covers debt service by at least 20%), though some bridge lenders will go to 1.10x or lower on short-term transactions. LTV limits typically range from 65% for institutional permanent loans to 75% for bridge loans, with some lenders offering up to 80% on strong assets in primary markets.

For properties that are not stabilized (vacant, partially leased, or underperforming), the underwriting shifts to as-stabilized value and the loan is typically structured as a bridge loan with reserves or holdbacks until leasing milestones are met. This structure is common in estate contexts where the property has been under-managed during the final years of the decedent's illness.

Sponsorship and Guaranty

Institutional lenders prefer recourse loans with personal guarantees from creditworthy sponsors. In fiduciary contexts, the trustee or executor is typically unwilling or legally prohibited from offering a personal guarantee. Non-recourse loans are available but carry more conservative underwriting (lower LTV, higher DSCR) and are generally limited to permanent debt on stabilized assets from CMBS, life insurance company, or agency lenders.

For bridge loans, some lenders will accept a "bad boy" carve-out guaranty (which triggers recourse only for fraud, waste, or misrepresentation) without requiring full recourse. Fiduciaries can typically provide this guaranty in their official capacity without it constituting a personal obligation, though this should always be reviewed by the fiduciary's own counsel before signing.

Property Insurance and Condition

Lenders require property insurance naming the lender as additional insured. In estate contexts where the property has been under the decedent's control and the fiduciary has just taken over, confirming that insurance is current and adequate is an early step that prevents closing delays. Deferred maintenance issues will also come up in lender property inspections and appraisals; budgeting for an inspection cost and potential capital reserves is prudent from the outset.

Timeline and Rate Lock

Conventional banks offer rate locks of 30 to 60 days. Court-supervised transactions routinely require 90 to 120 days. Bridge lenders are generally more flexible on rate lock duration, though they may charge a fee for extended locks. When presenting options to the court or to beneficiaries, it is important to be clear about the rate lock terms and the risk of repricing if the transaction extends beyond the lock period.

Questions Fiduciaries Should Ask Any Commercial Mortgage Broker Before Referring

Not every commercial mortgage broker is equipped for fiduciary contexts. Before making a referral, ask these questions directly:

  • Have you closed commercial loans where the borrower was an estate, trust, or conservatorship? Ask for specifics. Vague answers mean no experience.
  • How do you handle court approval requirements? A broker who does not immediately understand what you are asking has not done these transactions.
  • What is your lender network for non-recourse permanent debt? For irrevocable trusts without a guarantor, non-recourse is often the only viable structure, and not every broker has life insurance company or CMBS relationships.
  • How many lenders do you submit to simultaneously? A broker who submits to one or two lenders is not running a competitive process. You want simultaneous submission to five or more qualified lenders.
  • What is your typical timeline from engagement to commitment letter? In fiduciary contexts, knowing the realistic timeline matters for court petitions and estate administration planning.
  • How do you charge? Broker fees should be disclosed clearly. A broker who is evasive about compensation structure is a problem.
  • Can you provide references from probate attorneys or professional fiduciaries you have worked with? This is the most important question. Experience in fiduciary transactions is specialized, and references confirm it.

Key Takeaways

  • Commercial real estate financing for estates, trusts, and conservatorships is technically distinct from standard commercial lending. The borrower is a legal entity rather than a natural person, and lenders who do not understand that distinction create delays and misunderstandings that cost your clients money.
  • The five most common scenarios are estate debt payoff financing, conservatorship management financing, trust refinancing or recapitalization, heir buyout transactions, and court-supervised financings. Each has specific procedural requirements that must be built into the lender selection and timeline planning from the start.
  • Lenders underwrite to the property in fiduciary contexts, not to the fiduciary's personal creditworthiness. DSCR above 1.20x and LTV below 75% are the primary thresholds for most lenders, with bridge lenders offering more flexibility on both.
  • Court approval timelines require rate lock periods of 90 to 120 days. A conventional bank with a 45-day rate lock is not a viable option for most court-supervised transactions.
  • Non-recourse loan structures are available for stabilized income-producing properties and are often the only viable structure for irrevocable trusts where the trustee cannot provide a personal guaranty.
  • A competitive broker process across multiple lenders simultaneously protects your fiduciary duty. Best-efforts outreach to one lender is not a defensible process. Documented competition across a broad lender network demonstrates that you sought and obtained the best available terms.
  • The referral process is simple: make an introduction, and the broker handles lender outreach, underwriting coordination, court petition support, and closing management. Your client benefits from institutional-quality execution without the cost of managing the process internally.
  • Ask for references before referring. Fiduciary financing experience is specialized. A broker who cannot name probate attorneys or professional fiduciaries they have worked with has not done these transactions at scale.

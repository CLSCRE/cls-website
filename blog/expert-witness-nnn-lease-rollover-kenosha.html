Expert Witness: NNN Lease Rollover Upside and Financing Impact in Kenosha, WI

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Below-Market NNN Lease and Rollover Upside Analysis

The Kenosha Market Context

Kenosha, Wisconsin sits at an interesting intersection of Midwest industrial fundamentals and Chicago-adjacent retail and commercial demand. Located in Kenosha County along Interstate 94, the market has long been defined by its manufacturing heritage, its proximity to the Illinois border, and a commercial real estate inventory that reflects both legacy industrial users and the suburban retail corridors that expanded significantly during the 2000s and 2010s. For single-tenant net lease properties specifically, Kenosha presents a market where cap rates have historically tracked slightly wider than Chicago's suburban submarkets, with retail NNN cap rates generally ranging from 6.25% to 7.50% depending on tenant credit, lease term remaining, and submarket. That spread matters enormously in a financing dispute because a 50-basis-point difference in cap rate translates directly into appraised value, and appraised value is one of the two primary constraints lenders impose on loan sizing.

The Kenosha industrial market has tightened materially over the past several years, driven in part by regional distribution demand tied to the broader Milwaukee-Chicago corridor. Industrial vacancy in Kenosha County has hovered in the 4% to 6% range in recent cycles, and market rents for distribution and light manufacturing have pushed higher, with Class B industrial space trading in the $5.50 to $7.50 per square foot triple-net range depending on clear height, dock configuration, and proximity to I-94 interchange nodes. That rent growth context matters for the rollover upside analysis at the center of this type of dispute: when in-place rents are below market in a tightening submarket, the theoretical upside at rollover is real. The question lenders ask, and that counsel should ask their expert, is whether that upside is financeable today or only theoretical.

Kenosha's retail NNN market is more nuanced. The Highway 50 and 75th Street corridors carry a mix of national credit tenants in freestanding and strip configurations. When those leases were originally structured five to ten years ago, many were written at rents that reflected development-era economics. In a rising rent environment, those leases have aged into below-market territory. That creates a specific financing challenge that is central to this article.

The Engagement

Counsel for a party to a commercial real estate transaction dispute retained Commercial Lending Solutions to provide an expert analysis of financing feasibility for a single-tenant NNN retail property in the Kenosha submarket. The asset was a freestanding commercial building occupied by a single national-credit tenant under a long-term NNN lease. The dispute centered on whether the property's financing characteristics, specifically as they existed at a particular point in time material to the litigation, supported or precluded a refinancing or acquisition loan at terms the opposing party claimed were achievable.

The core financial and legal questions put to our firm were the following: First, how do institutional lenders actually underwrite a below-market NNN lease when the in-place rent produces a net operating income that is materially lower than what market rents would generate? Second, does the existence of rollover upside, meaning the contractual opportunity to reset rents at lease expiration, factor into loan sizing, and if so, how much credit do lenders actually assign to that upside? Third, given the specific lease structure, remaining term, and tenant credit profile, what was the maximum supportable loan at market-standard DSCR and LTV thresholds, and did that loan amount support or contradict the damages or value positions taken by the parties?

CLS CRE was engaged to produce a written financing feasibility analysis suitable for use in expert testimony, supported by lender market data drawn from our firm's active relationships across more than 1,000 lending institutions and approximately $1 billion in transaction volume across 50 states.

The Financial Analysis

The methodology began where every commercial mortgage lender begins: the in-place net operating income. For a single-tenant NNN property, NOI analysis is, at first glance, straightforward. The tenant pays rent; the landlord nets that rent after a modest reserve for replacement and management. But the below-market lease complicates the analysis in a specific way that has direct financing consequences.

For this engagement, we analyzed a scenario in which the in-place annual NNN rent was approximately $185,000, reflecting a rent per square foot that was demonstrably below current market for comparable Kenosha retail locations. Market rent analysis supported a rent-per-square-foot conclusion approximately 18% to 22% higher than the in-place figure. The building contained approximately 8,200 square feet on a well-located commercial parcel. At the in-place rent, NOI after a 3% management reserve and a $0.10 per square foot replacement reserve was approximately $178,500 annually.

The first lender constraint is DSCR. Conventional commercial mortgage lenders for single-tenant NNN retail properties in secondary markets like Kenosha require a minimum debt service coverage ratio of 1.25x, and many institutional programs require 1.30x for assets with lease terms below ten years remaining. Life insurance companies, which are among the most competitive lenders for credit-tenant NNN deals, will often work to 1.20x on strong-credit tenants with long-term leases, but that requires a lease term of typically seven years or more remaining. Applying a 1.25x DSCR constraint to the in-place NOI of $178,500, the maximum annual debt service the property can support is $142,800.

The second constraint is the mortgage constant, which is a function of interest rate and amortization. At a 7.25% interest rate (reflective of the market environment relevant to this matter) on a 25-year amortization schedule, the mortgage constant is approximately 8.68%. Dividing the maximum supportable annual debt service of $142,800 by the 8.68% mortgage constant produces a maximum supportable loan of approximately $1,645,000 on a DSCR basis.

The third constraint is LTV. For single-tenant NNN retail in a secondary Wisconsin market, institutional lenders and CMBS programs typically cap advance rates at 65% to 70% LTV. Community and regional banks will occasionally reach 75%, but they apply tighter DSCR requirements and shorter amortization that reduce the loan amount anyway. Applying a 65% LTV constraint to a market value conclusion supported by a 7.0% cap rate on in-place NOI produces an appraised value of approximately $2,550,000 and an LTV-constrained loan of approximately $1,657,000. In this case, the DSCR constraint and the LTV constraint converge closely, which is not always the case but reflects a below-market lease situation where the income-supportable loan and the value-supportable loan are both depressed by the same root cause: suppressed NOI.

The maximum supportable loan under these parameters was approximately $1,645,000 to $1,657,000. That figure was central to the dispute because an opposing position had asserted a refinancing capacity materially higher than this range, apparently relying on rollover upside to justify a larger loan. Our analysis addressed that claim directly.

The Complicating Factors

How Lenders Treat Rollover Upside

The most technically important issue in this engagement was the treatment of rollover upside by institutional lenders. The property had a below-market lease with a remaining term of approximately four years. At rollover, market rents would support an NOI approximately 20% higher, which would in theory support a loan roughly $300,000 to $350,000 larger. Counsel's opposing position appeared to rely on this upside as though lenders would incorporate it into current loan sizing.

They do not. Lenders underwrite to the in-place lease, not to speculative future rents. This is not a matter of lender conservatism or preference; it is a function of credit underwriting logic. The in-place lease is a contractual cash flow stream. The future market rent is an assumption. When a lender sizes a loan today, the collateral supporting that loan is the contractual income stream, not the investor's hope about what the tenant will agree to pay in four years, or whether the tenant will renew at all. A lender who sizes a loan to future market rents and then watches the tenant vacate at rollover has made an unsecured loan against a vacant building. No institutional program allows this.

Some lenders, particularly certain debt fund and bridge lenders, will structure a loan that contemplates future stabilization. But that product is not equivalent to a conventional NNN term loan. A bridge lender in this scenario would price the loan at 300 to 450 basis points over SOFR, require a loan-to-cost structure, hold back a portion of the loan in a rollover reserve, and impose a leasing milestone covenant. That structure carries a materially higher effective cost of capital and a shorter initial term, typically two to three years with extension options. It is not the same financing product as a conventional NNN loan, and presenting it as equivalent would be a fundamental analytical error in an expert opinion.

Lease Rollover Risk and the Lender's Vacancy Scenario

With four years of remaining lease term, most conventional lenders classify this asset as a near-term rollover risk property. CMBS conduit programs, which are among the more aggressive NNN lenders from a proceeds perspective, typically require a minimum lease term that extends at least two to three years beyond the proposed loan maturity. A ten-year CMBS loan on this property would require the lease to run through year twelve or thirteen from origination. With four years remaining, the property does not qualify for standard conduit execution, which eliminates one of the most proceeds-maximizing loan products available in the market.

Life insurance company lenders, similarly, price lease rollover risk heavily. A four-year remaining lease term on a single-tenant retail building in Kenosha, Wisconsin does not fit most life company programs without significant structural concessions, including a lease rollover reserve funded at closing, a co-terminus loan term not exceeding the lease expiration, or a personal or corporate guaranty from the borrower to cover the vacancy scenario. Each of those concessions either reduces loan proceeds, increases cost, or adds credit exposure to the borrower in ways that affect the transaction economics.

Single-Tenancy Concentration Risk

Lenders applying conventional multi-tenant underwriting logic to a single-tenant property face an all-or-nothing occupancy scenario. The property is either 100% occupied and cash flowing or 0% occupied and dark. There is no partial vacancy mitigation. In Kenosha's retail submarket, the re-leasing timeline for a freestanding retail building post-vacancy can extend twelve to eighteen months depending on building configuration, access, visibility, and the depth of the local tenant demand pool. Lenders price this risk into both the DSCR requirement and the LTV constraint for single-tenant retail, which is why advance rates for this product type run tighter than for stabilized multi-tenant retail. This concentration risk is a structural feature of the collateral that no lease restructuring eliminates; it is inherent to the single-tenancy configuration.

The Kenosha Lender Market

The lender universe for a single-tenant NNN retail asset in Kenosha is meaningful but segmented. Understanding which lenders are active, what their credit boxes look like, and how they price risk is essential for any expert opinion that speaks to financing feasibility in this market.

Regional and community banks headquartered in Wisconsin and northern Illinois are the most active conventional lenders for Kenosha commercial assets below approximately $5 million. These institutions include community banks with southeastern Wisconsin footprints as well as the regional banking presence that serves the I-94 corridor. Their typical credit parameters for NNN retail include LTV up to 70% to 75%, DSCR minimums of 1.20x to 1.25x, amortization of 20 to 25 years, and rate structures tied to the five-year or ten-year treasury or to prime. Portfolio lenders in this category will exercise judgment on rollover risk, sometimes accepting a shorter remaining lease term if the tenant is a national credit and the market fundamentals support re-leasing assumptions. That judgment, however, is not unlimited and is not equivalent to ignoring rollover risk entirely.

CMBS conduit lenders become relevant for Kenosha NNN assets above approximately $2 million where loan term and lease term are aligned. The conduit market offers non-recourse execution at 65% to 70% LTV with 1.25x DSCR requirements and fixed-rate ten-year terms. As noted above, the near-term lease rollover on the subject property would disqualify most CMBS programs absent a lease extension negotiated prior to closing.

Debt funds and bridge lenders provide the transitional capital product for lease-up or rollover scenarios. These lenders are active in secondary markets including Kenosha when the deal thesis includes a defined value-creation event, such as lease renewal, re-tenanting, or renovation. Pricing in this category ranges from SOFR plus 300 to 500 basis points with origination fees of 1% to 2%. These lenders impose heavier cash management controls and do not provide the long-term fixed-rate stability that a stabilized NNN asset owner seeks.

Credit unions and savings institutions with Wisconsin charters occasionally participate in sub-$3 million NNN transactions in this market, typically with recourse requirements and adjustable rate structures. Their underwriting is generally more relationship-driven than formula-driven, which creates variability that makes them less useful as reference points in an expert opinion seeking a market-standard financing benchmark.

What Attorneys Handling Kenosha CRE Disputes Need to Know

A CRE financing expert is not an appraiser and is not a substitute for one. An appraiser determines value. A CRE financing expert determines what the capital markets will actually do with that value, which is a distinct and often dispositive question in disputes involving alleged lost financing opportunity, wrongful loan denial, damages from a failed transaction, or the feasibility of a refinancing in a bankruptcy plan of reorganization. The gap between appraised value and financeable value is real and can be substantial, particularly in below-market lease scenarios of the type described here.

Attorneys should retain a CRE financing expert when the dispute turns on any of the following questions: whether a specific loan amount was achievable at a particular point in time; whether a lender's underwriting was consistent with market standards; whether a proposed plan of reorganization's refinancing assumptions are feasible; whether a borrower's or guarantor's damages claim based on lost financing opportunity is supported by market evidence; or whether the terms of a loan commitment were standard or aberrational relative to the market.

The distinction between a qualified CRE financing expert and a retired banker who testifies occasionally is meaningful. A qualified expert maintains an active origination practice with current lender relationships across the institutional spectrum, has personally structured transactions comparable in type and complexity to the subject matter, can demonstrate knowledge of how underwriting standards have shifted across interest rate environments, and can speak to how multiple lender categories would have approached the same transaction. A retired banker's experience may be limited to one institution's credit box over a specific historical period. That is not the same as market-standard testimony.

Before retaining a financing expert, counsel should gather the following materials: the complete lease or leases affecting the property, including all amendments and renewal option schedules; any existing loan documents, commitment letters, or term sheets; appraisals or broker opinions of value; rent rolls and operating statements for at least three years; any lender correspondence including underwriting conditions or denial letters; and the transaction timeline including any dates on which financing was allegedly committed, pursued, or refused. The expert needs this information to reconstruct the financing scenario as it existed at the legally relevant date, not as it exists today.

At trial or in a plan hearing, CRE financing expert testimony is most effective when it is grounded in specific, contemporaneous market data rather than general principles. Judges and opposing counsel respond to testimony that identifies actual lender programs, actual underwriting parameters, and specific mathematical conclusions drawn from the subject property's financial profile. Vague opinions that a loan "should have been available" or "was market" are easily attacked. Opinions built on specific DSCR calculations, identified lender categories with documented credit boxes, and a clear methodology connecting the subject property's financials to a specific loan range are far more durable under cross-examination.

Key Takeaways

  • Lenders underwrite to in-place cash flow, not rollover upside. A below-market NNN lease produces a suppressed NOI, and that suppressed NOI is the underwriting baseline regardless of what market rents suggest the property could theoretically earn at lease expiration.
  • Rollover upside is not a financing catalyst; it is a financing risk. Near-term lease expiration narrows the lender universe, eliminates CMBS and most life company execution, and pushes the borrower toward higher-cost bridge capital with structural constraints that are not equivalent to conventional NNN financing.

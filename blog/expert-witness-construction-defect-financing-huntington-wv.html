Expert Witness: How Construction Defects Affect CRE Financing in Huntington, WV

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Construction Defect Impact on Financing

The Huntington Market Context

Huntington, West Virginia sits at the confluence of the Ohio, Big Sandy, and Guyandotte rivers, and its commercial real estate market reflects both the structural challenges and the stubborn resilience of a mid-sized Appalachian economy. For purposes of construction defect litigation involving commercial property, understanding the Huntington market is not an academic exercise. Lender underwriting assumptions, cap rate expectations, vacancy tolerances, and loan sizing are all calibrated to local market conditions, and those conditions differ materially from larger gateway markets. An expert opinion that imports assumptions from Columbus or Pittsburgh without adjustment will produce a financially misleading result.

The Huntington commercial market is anchored by a limited but durable set of demand drivers: Marshall University, Cabell Huntington Hospital and the broader healthcare corridor along U.S. Route 60, river-related industrial and logistics activity, and a modest but growing retail footprint serving the Tri-State region that spans West Virginia, Kentucky, and Ohio. Multifamily fundamentals have strengthened over the past several years, with vacancy in stabilized Class B and C product running in the 5 to 8 percent range and effective rents in garden-style product averaging in the $700 to $900 per unit per month range depending on submarket and vintage. New supply has been minimal, which has provided rent support, but absorption is slow by national standards and concessions remain common in older stock.

For income-producing commercial property, cap rates in Huntington are meaningfully higher than in primary markets, typically ranging from 7.5 to 9.5 percent for stabilized retail strip centers and small office product, and from 7.0 to 8.5 percent for multifamily, reflecting both market risk premium and thinner liquidity. These elevated cap rates have a direct and consequential relationship to financing: when cap rates are high, the resulting property valuations are lower relative to income, which compresses loan proceeds and makes the impact of any NOI impairment, including impairment caused by construction defects, proportionately more severe. In a market where buyers and lenders are already applying conservative valuations, a defect-related NOI reduction does not merely trim the edges of a loan; it can eliminate viable financing entirely.

The industrial and flex sector along the Route 60 and Route 10 corridors has seen consistent demand from regional distributors and light manufacturers, with vacancy in the 8 to 12 percent range and triple-net lease rates for functional warehouse product running approximately $4.00 to $6.50 per square foot. Office remains structurally challenged, with suburban vacancy exceeding 15 percent in several nodes and effective rents declining in real terms. These market-specific conditions form the analytical foundation for any credible financing feasibility opinion in a Huntington construction defect case.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing the property owner in a construction defect dispute involving a mid-size commercial income property in Huntington. The asset was a mixed-use commercial building combining ground-floor retail and upper-floor office or leasable commercial space, a common format in the Huntington urban core and surrounding commercial corridors. The alleged defects involved structural and envelope systems, including foundation settlement, moisture intrusion at the building envelope, and HVAC system deficiencies that had caused measurable tenant disruption and deferred leasing activity.

Counsel needed expert analysis that addressed three specific questions, none of which fall cleanly within the scope of a traditional appraisal. First, given the known and alleged defects, was the property financeable at all in the current Huntington lender market, and if so, under what conditions? Second, how did the defects affect the property's DSCR, LTV, and maximum supportable loan compared to a hypothetically defect-free equivalent asset? Third, what remediation or escrow requirements would institutional and regional lenders impose before agreeing to fund, and what is the quantifiable cost of those requirements to the borrower in terms of loan proceeds, pricing, and transaction friction?

These are capital markets questions. An appraiser can opine on diminution of value; a CRE financing expert opines on what the debt markets will actually do with a property in a specific condition, in a specific market, at a specific point in time. Those are related but distinct analyses, and in litigation involving damages from construction defects, both are typically necessary.

The Financial Analysis

The starting point for any financing feasibility analysis is the property's stabilized net operating income, and in a defect scenario, the critical analytical task is constructing two parallel NOI models: one for the property as impaired, and one for the equivalent property without defects. The spread between those two figures is the economic engine of the financing impact analysis.

For the subject engagement, we analyzed the property's trailing 12-month rent rolls, operating statements, and lease abstracts. The property had experienced measurable occupancy degradation attributable to the defect conditions. Two tenants had vacated or declined renewal, citing specifically the moisture intrusion and HVAC failures, and several prospective tenants had declined to execute leases following property inspections. The stabilized, defect-free NOI for a comparable asset of equivalent size and location in the Huntington market was modeled at approximately $215,000 per year, based on market rents, normalized vacancy of 8 percent, and market-rate operating expenses. The impaired NOI, reflecting actual occupancy losses and increased operating costs from defect-related maintenance and tenant remediation payments, was approximately $148,000, a reduction of roughly $67,000 or 31 percent.

With those two NOI figures established, the loan sizing analysis proceeds through standard underwriting mechanics. For commercial income property in a secondary market like Huntington, institutional lenders and well-capitalized regional banks typically require a minimum DSCR of 1.25x, and in some cases 1.30x for assets with below-market occupancy or secondary market location risk. The 1.25x threshold means that for every dollar of annual debt service, the property must generate $1.25 of NOI. More conservative lenders applying a 1.30x standard effectively reduce loan proceeds further.

Using a representative loan structure for this market and asset type, with a 6.75 percent note rate on a 25-year amortization schedule, the annual debt service constant is approximately 8.65 percent of the loan balance. Working backward from each NOI figure:

Defect-free scenario: $215,000 NOI divided by 1.25x DSCR equals $172,000 in allowable annual debt service. At an 8.65 percent debt service constant, the maximum supportable loan is approximately $1,988,000, which we round to $1.99 million for analytical purposes.

Impaired scenario: $148,000 NOI divided by 1.25x DSCR equals $118,400 in allowable annual debt service. At the same constant, the maximum supportable loan is approximately $1,369,000.

The financing gap attributable to the defects is therefore approximately $620,000 in loan proceeds, before any consideration of LTV constraints. That figure represents the income-side impact alone.

The LTV analysis adds a second constraint. For a stabilized commercial asset in Huntington, institutional lenders typically cap LTV at 65 to 70 percent, and community banks may stretch to 75 percent for well-located assets with strong sponsorship. If the appraised value of the defect-free property is $2.85 million, a 70 percent LTV ceiling permits a maximum loan of approximately $1,995,000, which is consistent with the DSCR-derived maximum. If the appraised value of the impaired property reflects the defect discount, say $1.95 million, the 70 percent LTV ceiling constrains the loan to approximately $1,365,000. In this case, both the DSCR constraint and the LTV constraint are binding simultaneously, which is the worst-case financing outcome and is precisely what the defects created here.

The combined analysis produced a total financing shortfall of approximately $620,000 in maximum loan proceeds, plus an additional cost impact from the remediation escrow requirements that any lender willing to fund would impose, which we analyzed separately at approximately $185,000 in required reserves. The total quantifiable financing impact attributable to the construction defects exceeded $800,000 in this engagement.

The Complicating Factors

Lender Discovery of Known Defects: When a property has been the subject of construction defect litigation, or when defect conditions are documented in inspection reports, lenders will obtain that information. Phase I and Phase II environmental assessments are standard; so are property condition assessments, or PCAs. A PCA on a property with known structural or envelope defects will flag those conditions explicitly, and any lender reviewing that PCA will either decline to fund, require remediation prior to funding, or require a holdback escrow sized to cover remediation costs with a contingency factor typically ranging from 110 to 125 percent of the contractor estimate. The existence of pending litigation itself is an additional complication, because most institutional loan agreements require the borrower to disclose material litigation, and a lender that discovers undisclosed defect litigation after closing has remedies that can include acceleration of the loan. In practice, the litigation disclosure requirement reduces the pool of willing lenders further and increases pricing for those who remain.

Tenant Estoppels and Lease Uncertainty: Lenders funding against commercial income property require tenant estoppel certificates from major tenants confirming lease status, absence of landlord defaults, and the tenant's intention to continue occupancy. Where construction defects have disrupted tenants, those estoppels become problematic. A tenant who has been dealing with moisture intrusion or HVAC failures is unlikely to sign a clean estoppel, and a lender holding an estoppel with material carve-outs will either reprice the loan or require remediation before closing. In the Huntington market, where the tenant pool for commercial space is narrower than in larger metros, losing even one anchor tenant to a defect condition can functionally kill a refinancing.

Secondary Market Illiquidity: Huntington is not a market where CMBS lenders or life company lenders are actively competing for business on most commercial property types. That matters because the lenders who are active, primarily community banks and regional banks, have less capital to deploy, more conservative credit cultures, and less tolerance for structural complexity. A property with construction defect exposure that might still attract a bridge lender in a primary market will face a significantly narrower financing universe in Huntington. The practical consequence is that a borrower whose property has been impaired by defects cannot easily find alternative capital to bridge through litigation, and may face forced sale or default while the litigation is pending.

Remediation Cost Uncertainty: Lenders do not like open-ended cost exposures. Structural defects, in particular, carry uncertainty about the full scope of remediation until invasive investigation is completed. A lender asked to fund on a property with known but incompletely scoped structural issues will typically require a full engineering report with remediation cost estimates before underwriting, and will then size the holdback at a significant premium to those estimates. If the remediation scope is genuinely uncertain, many lenders will simply decline rather than hold the exposure. In Huntington, where lender appetite for complexity is limited, this uncertainty alone can eliminate financing options that would otherwise be available.

The Huntington Lender Market

Understanding which lenders are active in the Huntington market and what each requires is essential to any financing feasibility opinion. CLS CRE maintains relationships with more than 1,000 lenders across 50 states, and our familiarity with the Huntington and broader West Virginia lending environment informs this analysis directly.

Community banks headquartered in or with significant West Virginia operations are the primary financing source for commercial property in Huntington. These institutions typically offer portfolio loans with 5-year fixed terms, 20 to 25-year amortization, and rate structures tied to corresponding Treasury yields or prime rate with spreads reflecting market conditions. Their credit boxes are conservative: they want local sponsorship, personal guarantees, and properties they can inspect and understand. A property with pending construction defect litigation is a hard sell to a community bank relationship officer whose credit committee has limited tolerance for legal complexity.

Regional banks with multi-state footprints that include West Virginia represent a secondary lending source and are somewhat more sophisticated in their ability to underwrite complex situations. They may have construction defect protocols that include specific PCA requirements and remediation escrow structures. However, their floor loan sizes and minimum sponsorship net worth requirements often screen out smaller Huntington deals.

SBA 504 and 7(a) programs represent an alternative for owner-occupied commercial properties, and these programs are meaningfully active in West Virginia. However, SBA lenders apply their own environmental and property condition standards, and a property with known construction defects and pending litigation will require SBA concurrence before any participating lender will fund, adding time and procedural complexity.

Debt funds and bridge lenders are a thin market in Huntington. The deal sizes typical of this market are below the minimum thresholds of most national bridge lenders, and the few that will consider West Virginia secondary market assets will price at spreads that may render the financing economically unworkable relative to the property's income. For properties impaired by construction defects, this effectively means that the borrower cannot bridge through litigation at a cost that makes economic sense.

What Attorneys Handling Huntington CRE Disputes Need to Know

Attorneys litigating construction defect cases involving commercial property in Huntington frequently engage appraisers to quantify diminution in value. That is appropriate and necessary. But an appraiser's opinion does not answer the financing feasibility questions that arise in these cases, and those questions matter enormously to the damages analysis.

A CRE financing expert can answer questions that fall entirely outside an appraiser's scope: Was the property financeable at all after the defects were discovered? What loan amount could the borrower have obtained on a defect-free property versus the impaired property? What remediation escrow would lenders have required, and what does that cost in terms of reduced net loan proceeds? How did the defects affect the borrower's ability to refinance at maturity, and what was the cost of that inability? If the borrower was forced into a distressed sale because financing was unavailable, what role did the defect-related financing impairment play in that outcome?

These are damages questions, and in construction defect litigation the financing impact is often as large as the direct remediation cost. Attorneys who do not retain a financing expert may be leaving a substantial portion of their client's damages unquantified.

The distinction between a qualified CRE financing expert and a retired banker is meaningful. A retired community bank loan officer has practical knowledge but typically lacks the market-wide perspective, the analytical framework for expert report preparation under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 26, and the experience testifying that makes for an effective expert witness. A qualified CRE financing expert has active transaction experience across multiple lender categories, can speak to what lenders across the market would require rather than what one institution's credit committee might think, and has the analytical methodology to support opinions under Daubert scrutiny.

Before retaining a financing expert, counsel should gather the following: complete rent rolls and operating statements for the trailing 24 to 36 months; all available inspection reports, property condition assessments, and engineering reports; all existing loan documents including any loan agreements, modification agreements, or default notices; any written communications with lenders regarding the defect conditions; and any appraisals or broker opinion of value documents prepared in connection with the property. This documentation allows the financing expert to perform the parallel NOI analysis and lender market assessment with the specificity that an expert report requires.

In trial, financing expert testimony is typically used to establish the range of economic harm caused by financing impairment, to rebut defense expert opinions that minimize the financing impact of the defects, and to explain to the jury or the court how commercial lenders actually make decisions so that the damages analysis is grounded in market reality rather than abstract theory. In mediation, a well-supported financing expert report often moves the settlement needle significantly by quantifying an element of damages that opposing counsel cannot easily rebut without their own financing expert.

Key Takeaways

  • Construction defects impair financing through two independent channels: NOI reduction from occupancy loss and increased operating costs, and direct value reduction from market discounting. Both channels must be analyzed separately and together to capture the full financing impact.
  • In the Huntington market, elevated cap rates amplify the financing impact of any NOI impairment. A dollar of lost NOI in a 8.5 percent cap rate market reduces value by approximately $11.76, compared to $16.67 in a 6.0 percent cap rate market. This arithmetic makes construction defect financing damages proportionately severe in secondary markets.
  • The 1.25x DSCR threshold is not negotiable in most Huntington lender underwriting. Financing structures that depend on DSCR below 1.25x

Expert Witness: CRE Partnership and Waterfall Disputes in Washington DC, DC

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Joint Venture and Partnership Dispute

The Washington DC Market Context

Washington DC occupies a singular position among major US commercial real estate markets. Its economic base is structurally different from virtually every other gateway city: the federal government, government contractors, law firms, trade associations, lobbying organizations, and nonprofit institutions create a demand profile that is more resilient in downturns but also more sensitive to political shifts, budget cycles, and agency consolidations than any purely private-sector market. That dual character makes Washington DC both a highly sophisticated institutional investment market and, not infrequently, a litigation-rich one, particularly when joint venture structures are tested by unexpected income volatility or capital event disputes.

The DC office market has been under sustained pressure since 2020. Vacancy in the broader DC metro currently ranges from approximately 18 to 22 percent depending on submarket, with the CBD and East End faring better than NoMa, Capitol Hill peripheral corridors, and suburban Maryland and Northern Virginia submarkets. Trophy and Class A assets with federal or quasi-governmental tenancy have outperformed significantly. Net effective rents have declined on a real basis across most of the market as landlords absorb tenant improvement obligations and free rent periods that were uncommon a decade ago. These conditions directly affect waterfall mechanics in existing JV agreements: many structures were underwritten in 2018 through 2021 on rent assumptions and exit cap rates that are no longer achievable, compressing or eliminating promoted interest that the operating partner expected to earn.

Multifamily in DC proper has remained comparatively resilient. Cap rates for stabilized urban multifamily assets in neighborhoods such as Capitol Hill, Columbia Heights, Navy Yard, and the 14th Street corridor have generally traded in the 4.25 to 5.25 percent range, though transaction volume has declined sharply as rate increases compressed the spread between going-in yield and financing cost. Retail is highly bifurcated: grocery-anchored and neighborhood service retail has performed; street retail in office-dependent corridors has not. Industrial is effectively absent from the District itself, with relevant distribution and flex inventory concentrated in suburban Maryland and Northern Virginia. These asset-class specific fundamentals are directly material to any dispute over whether a joint venture's financial projections were achievable, whether preferred return thresholds were reachable on the business plan as structured, and whether a capital event was handled at the correct valuation.

The Engagement

We were retained by counsel representing a limited partner in a Washington DC joint venture formed to acquire and reposition a Class B office asset in a close-in DC submarket. The operating partner, who held the promoted interest, had distributed proceeds from a partial recapitalization and argued that the preferred return hurdle had been fully satisfied and that the promote had been earned. The limited partner disputed that conclusion, contending that the preferred return calculation was incorrect, that certain fees paid to the operating partner had been improperly deducted before the waterfall ran, and that the debt structure used in the recapitalization had extracted value from the venture in a manner that benefited the operating partner at the expense of the limited partner's cumulative preferred return.

The core legal and financial questions we were engaged to address included: whether the waterfall distribution methodology applied by the operating partner was consistent with market-standard joint venture agreement structures for institutional-grade DC office transactions; whether the preferred return was compounded or simple interest under a fair reading of the agreement and market convention; whether the debt terms obtained in the recapitalization were consistent with what an arm's-length lender would have required, or whether the structure had been engineered to produce a specific distribute-able proceeds figure; and whether the promoted interest calculation correctly accounted for the timing of capital contributions relative to the waterfall tiers.

This type of dispute is increasingly common in the DC market precisely because many office and mixed-use joint ventures formed between 2015 and 2021 were structured on optimistic assumptions about rent growth, NOI stabilization timelines, and exit pricing. When those assumptions are not met, the gap between what an operating partner claims as a promoted interest and what the limited partner believes is owed frequently requires forensic financial analysis that goes well beyond a simple audit of the books.

The Financial Analysis

Our analytical methodology began with a full reconstruction of the venture's actual income stream from inception through the recapitalization date. This required separating gross potential revenue from effective gross income after vacancy, credit loss, and concessions, a distinction that was contested because the operating partner had applied below-the-line treatment to certain rent abatements that the limited partner argued should have been recognized as above-the-line reductions to effective gross income before expenses and debt service were applied.

For the subject asset, a stabilized 120,000 square foot office building with a mix of government contractor and private tenants, the underwritten NOI at acquisition was approximately $5.4 million annually. By the time of the recapitalization, actual trailing twelve-month NOI had declined to approximately $4.1 million, reflecting increased vacancy, a below-market lease renewal on a major tenant, and elevated operating expenses associated with capital improvements. This NOI gap was central to our analysis.

We applied a debt service coverage ratio of 1.25x as the market standard for institutional-quality office recapitalization debt in the DC market at the relevant time. This is consistent with what senior lenders, including life companies, CMBS conduits, and large regional banks, required for office assets in the 2022 through 2023 underwriting environment, particularly given rising rate concerns and office market uncertainty. At a 1.25x DSCR threshold and using an interest rate assumption of 6.75 percent (consistent with the prevailing spread environment for five-year fixed-rate office bridge or balance sheet debt at that time) on a 25-year amortization schedule, the maximum supportable senior loan on $4.1 million of NOI was approximately $42.8 million.

The actual recapitalization loan obtained was $51.5 million, a figure that exceeded what a 1.25x DSCR standard would have supported by approximately $8.7 million. We analyzed how this occurred and concluded that the lender had underwritten to a forward NOI figure that included projected lease-up of vacant space and an anticipated rent step on an existing lease, neither of which had yet been achieved. The use of prospective NOI rather than in-place NOI is not inherently improper, but it is a meaningful underwriting concession that produced a higher loan proceeds figure and therefore a higher distributable amount in the recapitalization waterfall.

On an LTV basis, the recapitalization loan represented approximately 72.5 percent of the appraised value used by the lender. For Class B office in DC at the time, market-standard LTV for senior debt ranged from 55 to 65 percent from life company and CMBS lenders, and up to 70 percent from more aggressive balance sheet lenders willing to accept a thinner debt yield. A 72.5 percent LTV, while achievable, represented an outlier execution, and our analysis addressed whether the operating partner's ability to source that execution reflected genuine market expertise or whether the higher leverage level itself distorted the waterfall outcome.

The Complicating Factors

Preferred Return Compounding Convention: The JV agreement in this matter stated that the limited partner was entitled to a preferred return of 8 percent on unreturned capital, but the agreement was ambiguous as to whether this compounded annually or accrued as simple interest. This is not an academic distinction. On a $22 million equity contribution held for five years, the difference between 8 percent simple and 8 percent compounded annually is approximately $1.4 million. We analyzed market convention for institutional DC office JV agreements and found that compounded preferred returns are the market standard in institutional-grade structures, while simple interest accrual is more common in smaller or less sophisticated deals. The characterization of this particular venture, given its capitalization size and the sophistication of the parties, was directly relevant to which convention should have applied.

Management Fee Treatment Above the Waterfall: The operating partner had charged a management fee equal to 3 percent of gross revenues throughout the hold period. Whether that fee was properly deducted before NOI was calculated, or whether it should have been treated as a below-the-line expense that ran after preferred return was satisfied, was a function of how the agreement defined "Net Operating Income" for waterfall purposes. We analyzed the specific definitional language and compared it against market-standard formulations in DC office JV agreements from comparable institutional transactions. This type of definitional dispute, while appearing narrow, can shift the waterfall outcome by hundreds of thousands to several million dollars over a multi-year hold.

Timing of Capital Contributions and IRR Hurdles: Several capital calls were made during the hold period for leasing costs, capital expenditures, and operating shortfalls. The operating partner calculated preferred return and IRR hurdles as if all capital contributions were made on the date the call was funded, while the limited partner argued that certain contributions were delayed by the operating partner's failure to issue timely capital call notices, and that the effective contribution dates should have been adjusted. This timing dispute affected the IRR calculation underlying the promote trigger and required us to reconstruct the full capital account history on a date-specific basis.

Recapitalization Proceeds Characterization: The core dispute involved whether proceeds from the recapitalization constituted a "capital event" triggering the waterfall or whether they were a financing transaction that should have been distributed pro rata as a return of capital without running through the promote tiers. Different JV agreements treat refinancing proceeds differently, and the language in this agreement was not explicit. We analyzed both interpretations against market convention and addressed the economic logic of each: running refinancing proceeds through a promote waterfall can create situations where the operating partner captures promoted interest before the limited partner has received a full return of capital, a result that is inconsistent with the investor-protective intent of most institutional waterfall structures.

The Washington DC Lender Market

For institutional-grade office and mixed-use JV transactions in Washington DC, the lender universe is relatively deep but has shifted significantly since 2022. Life insurance companies including those with dedicated relationship offices in DC have remained active but have tightened their credit boxes substantially, focusing almost exclusively on assets with strong in-place tenancy, weighted average lease terms of seven years or longer, and debt yields above 8 percent. They are pricing in the mid-6 to low-7 percent range for fixed-rate permanent financing and are generally not the appropriate source for transitional or lease-up assets.

CMBS conduit lenders remain active in the DC market but are effectively closed to office assets with significant vacancy or near-term lease rollover risk. CMBS executions have been used for stabilized retail, multifamily, and mixed-use assets, and can offer non-recourse terms and higher proceeds in some cases, but the underwriting constraints and ongoing covenants have made them less competitive for complex JV structures where flexibility in cash management and distribution is required.

Regional and super-regional banks, including several large institutions with significant DC presences due to the federal contracting economy, have been the most active lenders for transitional and bridge transactions. These lenders underwrite to floating-rate structures, typically SOFR plus 225 to 375 basis points depending on asset quality and sponsorship, and require personal recourse or completion guarantees in most cases. Their familiarity with the DC government contractor tenant base gives them comfort that some other lenders do not have when evaluating lease credit quality.

Debt funds and private credit platforms have filled the gap for higher-leverage or faster-close executions, but at pricing that now ranges from SOFR plus 375 to 600 basis points for transitional office assets in DC. These executions are relevant in the expert witness context because when a party argues that a particular financing was unavailable or unobtainable, understanding which lender categories were actually active at the relevant time, and at what pricing and structure, is essential to evaluating the merits of that claim.

What Attorneys Handling Washington DC CRE Disputes Need to Know

A CRE financing expert witness fills a distinct analytical role that neither a real estate appraiser nor an accountant can replicate. An appraiser determines value; an accountant traces cash flows. A CRE financing expert explains how the capital markets would have structured and priced the deal at the relevant time, whether the waterfall mechanics were consistent with market convention, and whether the financing decisions made by the parties were commercially reasonable. In JV disputes specifically, this distinction matters because the entire economic architecture of the dispute, who gets what and when, flows from the financing structure and the waterfall mechanics built around it.

Attorneys should retain a CRE financing expert early in the matter, ideally during the discovery planning phase. Understanding the financing structure, the capital stack, and the waterfall mechanics before depositions are taken allows counsel to identify the right documents to request (loan agreements, rate lock confirmations, lender credit approvals, capital account ledgers, distribution notices) and to frame the right questions for the operating partner's representatives and any lender witnesses.

The expert should be qualified by active market engagement, not just prior industry tenure. A retired banker or a former fund manager who has been out of the market for five or more years may have general knowledge but lacks the current market awareness needed to testify credibly about what lenders were requiring, how they were pricing, and what structures were achievable at the specific point in time when the disputed transaction occurred. At Commercial Lending Solutions, our qualification rests on approximately $1 billion in transaction volume across 1,000-plus lender relationships and active origination in all 50 states, including continuous engagement in the DC market.

Information counsel should gather before the expert engagement includes: the full JV agreement and all amendments; the operating agreement's definitional provisions for NOI, capital contributions, and waterfall triggers; all loan documents from the original acquisition financing and any refinancings; lender underwriting files if obtainable through discovery; capital account statements prepared by the operating partner; and any internal communications between the parties regarding distribution calculations or promote entitlement. The more complete this record is before the expert engagement begins, the more efficient and cost-effective the analysis will be.

At trial or in a mediation context, CRE financing expert testimony is most effective when it is grounded in market comparables, meaning specific evidence of what other institutional lenders and JV structures in the DC market were doing at the relevant time. Generalized testimony about how waterfall structures "typically" work is less persuasive than testimony supported by market-specific data, executed loan term sheets, and industry standard form agreements. Counsel should work with the expert to build a record that connects the specific dispute to the broader market context in a concrete, quantifiable way.

Key Takeaways

  • Waterfall disputes in DC JV structures frequently turn on definitional ambiguities in how NOI is calculated, how fees are treated, and whether refinancing proceeds constitute a capital event, issues that require forensic financial analysis grounded in market convention, not just a reading of the agreement in isolation.
  • The preferred return compounding convention matters enormously. The difference between simple and compound accrual on a multi-million dollar equity contribution over a multi-year hold can exceed seven figures and is a legitimate subject of expert testimony about market standard practice in institutional DC transactions.
  • Debt structure can be engineered to affect waterfall outcomes. Expert analysis of whether the financing obtained was consistent with market-standard lender requirements, including DSCR thresholds of 1.20x to 1.25x and LTV constraints of 55 to 70 percent depending on lender category, is essential to evaluating whether the operating partner's financing decisions were commercially reasonable or self-interested.
  • Washington DC's office market distress has made waterfall disputes more common as NOI underperformance relative to original projections collapses the gap between preferred return satisfaction and promote entitlement, creating genuine economic conflict between operating and limited partners.
  • Lender market conditions at the time of the disputed transaction must be established with specificity. Expert testimony about what was actually available in the DC market, from which lender categories, at what pricing and leverage, is more persuasive than generalized industry commentary.
  • Timing of capital contributions and IRR hurdle calculations requires date-specific reconstruction of the full capital account history. Errors or manipulations in contribution timing can shift the promote trigger by months or years, with significant economic consequences.
  • A CRE financing expert is not a substitute for an appraiser or forensic accountant but fills a distinct and non-duplicative role: explaining the capital markets logic, lender market conditions, and industry standard structuring conventions that underlie every element of a commercial real estate JV dispute.
  • Early retention of the expert produces better outcomes. Discovery strategy, deposition framing, and settlement valuation are all improved when counsel understands the financing mechanics before the litigation record is built, not after.

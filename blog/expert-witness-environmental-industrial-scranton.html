Expert Witness: Financing a Contaminated Industrial Property in Scranton, PA

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Environmental Contamination Analysis

The Scranton Market Context

Scranton, Pennsylvania sits at the center of the Lackawanna County industrial corridor, and its commercial real estate fundamentals tell a story that matters in financing disputes. The greater Scranton-Wilkes-Barre metropolitan statistical area has emerged over the past decade as a legitimate regional distribution and light industrial hub, driven by its positioning along the I-81 corridor and its proximity to major Northeastern population centers. For purposes of industrial asset financing, this geography is directly relevant: institutional lenders underwriting logistics and warehouse product in Scranton are benchmarking the market against secondary and tertiary industrial corridors in the Mid-Atlantic and Northeast, not against Class A distribution markets like the Lehigh Valley or Central New Jersey.

Industrial vacancy in the Scranton submarket has generally tracked in the 4% to 7% range in recent years, tighter than the market's historical average, reflecting both limited new supply and sustained e-commerce-driven demand for last-mile and regional distribution facilities. Asking rents for functional industrial product in the market have ranged from approximately $5.00 to $8.50 per square foot NNN depending on clear height, dock configuration, and vintage, with newer generation bulk distribution facilities commanding the upper end of that range. Legacy buildings, particularly those with environmental histories from prior manufacturing or chemical processing use, trade and finance at a meaningful discount to that range, which is a critical input in any financing analysis.

Scranton's industrial market is not dominated by a single employer or single tenant type, which from a lender's perspective creates a degree of market depth. However, the secondary market designation and the relatively thin institutional transaction volume mean that lenders applying agency-style or conduit underwriting standards will require more conservative assumptions than they would in core markets. That distinction becomes dispositive when the question before the court is whether a loan of a given size was obtainable in the market at a given point in time.

The Engagement

Counsel representing a class of secured creditors in a regulatory enforcement proceeding retained Commercial Lending Solutions to provide an independent expert opinion on the financing feasibility of a contaminated industrial warehouse property located in the Scranton submarket. The asset was a single-tenant, NNN-leased industrial building of approximately 85,000 square feet, originally constructed for manufacturing use and subsequently converted to warehouse and distribution operations. The property had been identified by Pennsylvania Department of Environmental Protection as a site requiring investigation and potential remediation in connection with chlorinated volatile organic compound contamination, specifically trichloroethylene and its degradation products including 1,4-dioxane, attributed to prior industrial operations at the site.

The property was encumbered by existing senior mortgage debt with a balance that the creditors needed to retire or refinance in order to achieve a consensual resolution of the enforcement matter. The tenant was in place under a long-term NNN lease, but the lease rate was below current market rents, having been set several years earlier without a mark-to-market provision adequate to reflect the intervening appreciation in industrial rents. Our engagement required us to opine on three specific questions: first, whether first-mortgage financing was available in the current market for a property subject to active environmental investigation and regulatory oversight; second, if so, what was the maximum supportable loan amount under prevailing market underwriting standards; and third, whether the gap between maximum supportable financing and the existing senior debt balance was attributable to market conditions, the environmental condition, the below-market lease structure, or some combination of all three.

These questions are precisely the type that arise in secured creditor disputes, bankruptcy plan confirmation proceedings, and environmental cost allocation litigation, where the answer to "what could this property support in financing" is not a matter of opinion but a matter of technical market analysis requiring direct expertise in commercial mortgage underwriting.

The Financial Analysis

The income analysis began with the actual in-place NNN lease, which was the only cash flow available to a lender underwriting this asset. The tenant was obligated to pay all operating expenses including real estate taxes, insurance, and maintenance, which is the standard NNN structure. The in-place base rent was $5.10 per square foot annually on approximately 85,000 square feet of rentable area, producing gross contract rent of approximately $433,500 per year. After a vacancy and credit loss reserve of 5%, consistent with market standard for single-tenant NNN properties where the reserve accounts for lease renewal risk rather than current vacancy, effective gross income was approximately $411,825. Management fees are typically excluded or minimized in NNN structures, yielding a net operating income for financing purposes of approximately $400,000 annually.

The critical observation here is that the market rent for this type of functional industrial building in the Scranton submarket, absent the environmental condition, would be in the range of $6.25 to $6.75 per square foot NNN. At the midpoint of $6.50, market rent for this building would produce NOI of approximately $524,000, representing a 31% premium over in-place cash flow. Lenders underwriting to in-place NOI rather than stabilized market NOI will generate a materially lower loan amount. Most conventional first-mortgage lenders in the current environment will not underwrite to speculative mark-to-market rent on a below-market lease, particularly on an environmentally impaired asset.

Applying prevailing market underwriting standards for secondary-market industrial assets with environmental complications, I used the following parameters. The debt service coverage ratio threshold was set at 1.30x, one standard deviation above the typical 1.20x to 1.25x floor, reflecting the added risk associated with environmental impairment and single-tenant concentration in a secondary market. Lenders encountered in our review of the active financing market for this property type consistently applied a minimum 1.25x DSCR, with several requiring 1.30x or 1.35x given the environmental overlay. Interest rate assumption was based on a 10-year fixed rate product, consistent with the lease term remaining, priced at approximately 7.25% over a 25-year amortization schedule, reflecting the institutional rate environment at the time of the analysis plus a spread premium for asset risk.

At a 1.30x DSCR and a 7.25% fixed rate on a 25-year amortization, the annual debt service on a $3,500,000 loan would be approximately $304,000, producing a DSCR of 1.32x against the $400,000 NOI. This is the approximate constraint point. Rounding to a cleaner analysis: maximum supportable debt service is $400,000 divided by 1.30, or approximately $307,700 per year. At 7.25% on a 25-year amortization, the loan constant is approximately 8.68% annually. Dividing $307,700 by 8.68% produces a maximum loan amount of approximately $3,545,000 on a debt service coverage basis.

The LTV constraint then becomes the binding test. For environmentally impaired industrial assets in secondary markets, lenders who will engage at all typically cap LTV at 55% to 65%, compared to the 70% to 75% available on clean product. The appraised value of the property, reflecting both the below-market lease and the environmental condition, was in the range of $4,200,000 to $4,800,000 depending on how the appraiser treated remediation cost liability. Applying a conservative 60% LTV to a $4,500,000 midpoint valuation produces a maximum loan of $2,700,000. This means the LTV constraint is more restrictive than the DSCR constraint in this case, which is the typical outcome when asset values are depressed by environmental risk and below-market leases simultaneously. The maximum supportable first-mortgage loan, after applying both constraints and taking the lower of the two, was approximately $2,700,000 to $3,000,000 depending on the lender category engaged.

The Complicating Factors

Environmental Impairment and Lender Due Diligence Requirements. The presence of 1,4-dioxane and chlorinated VOC contamination, even where a long-term NNN tenant remains in place and operations continue, is a material obstacle in the conventional lending market. The fundamental problem is that most conventional lenders, including regional banks, CMBS conduits, and insurance company lenders, require a Phase I Environmental Site Assessment at minimum and will universally order a Phase II upon any recognized environmental condition. An active PADEP enforcement action, with a required corrective action plan not yet approved, will disqualify the property from CMBS execution entirely. CMBS special servicers and B-piece buyers are categorically prohibited from accepting known environmental impairment without a remediation escrow or environmental indemnity supported by a creditworthy guarantor, and in practice they will simply pass on the deal.

Remediation Cost Uncertainty as a Loan Underwriting Variable. Lenders who will consider environmentally impaired assets, primarily certain community and regional banks, private credit funds, and hard money bridge lenders, require a quantified estimate of remediation costs as an underwriting input. Where PADEP has not yet approved a corrective action plan, that number is inherently uncertain. Lenders typically require an independent environmental consultant's estimate, and they will discount the value of the collateral by the full estimated remediation liability before calculating LTV. In this engagement, remediation cost estimates ranged from $800,000 to over $2,000,000 depending on the scope of the required investigation and the cleanup standard that PADEP ultimately accepts. That range of uncertainty directly affects the collateral value and therefore the maximum loan amount in a way that cannot be resolved until the regulatory process advances further.

Below-Market Lease and Lender Perception of Income Stability. The combination of a below-market lease and an environmental condition creates a compounding underwriting problem. Lenders will not give credit for mark-to-market rent potential when the environmental condition creates uncertainty about whether the tenant will remain in occupancy through the lease term. The tenant's NNN lease obligation to maintain the property does not insulate the landlord or the lender from the risk that the tenant exercises a lease termination right triggered by environmental conditions, if such a right exists in the lease, or simply fails to renew at expiration. A below-market lease that runs only to the end of a five-year term, for example, produces a loan duration mismatch relative to a 10-year loan term that lenders will price into their credit decisions.

PADEP Regulatory Jurisdiction and Lien Priority Concerns. Pennsylvania's Land Recycling Program and PADEP's enforcement authority create a legal overlay that affects lender collateral analysis directly. Environmental remediation liens under Pennsylvania law can achieve priority over previously recorded mortgages under certain circumstances, a consideration that institutional lenders and their counsel will scrutinize during due diligence. Any lender considering a first-mortgage position on a property subject to active PADEP enforcement must obtain a legal opinion on lien priority and typically requires an environmental indemnity agreement or pollution liability insurance policy as a condition of closing.

The Scranton Lender Market

The conventional institutional lending market, meaning CMBS conduits, life insurance companies, and agency lenders, is effectively unavailable for a property with active environmental enforcement and an open corrective action plan. These lenders have bright-line environmental due diligence requirements that this property cannot satisfy until remediation is complete and a regulatory closure letter is obtained.

The community and regional bank market in Northeastern Pennsylvania is reasonably deep for industrial product, with several institutions headquartered in Lackawanna and Luzerne Counties maintaining active commercial real estate lending programs. These lenders, including institutions with local deposit bases and direct experience with the Scranton industrial submarket, are the most likely source of conventional first-mortgage financing for this type of asset. They will typically lend at floating rates tied to the Prime rate or SOFR plus a spread of 200 to 300 basis points, with shorter loan terms of three to five years, recourse to a creditworthy guarantor, and environmental indemnity requirements. LTV for these lenders on environmentally impaired product is typically in the 55% to 60% range, and they will require escrow of a portion of estimated remediation costs as a condition of closing.

Private credit and debt fund lenders represent the next tier for this type of asset. These lenders price at significantly higher spreads, typically in the 400 to 600 basis point range over benchmark, and will accept more environmental complexity in exchange for lower LTV, stronger guaranty structures, and higher fees. Their role in this type of transaction is usually bridge financing while the environmental process concludes, after which the borrower refinances into a conventional product. For litigation purposes, the existence of private credit financing availability at a materially higher cost than conventional is itself a relevant data point when quantifying damages from an environmental condition or from a lender's alleged breach.

Hard money and asset-based lenders represent the outer boundary of the available financing market. These lenders will typically advance 40% to 50% of a heavily discounted collateral value with short terms, high origination fees, and default rates that can exceed 15%. Their existence in the market is meaningful for purposes of establishing that financing was technically available, but their terms may not produce proceeds sufficient to retire institutional senior debt, which was the central question in this engagement.

What Attorneys Handling Scranton CRE Disputes Need to Know

A CRE financing expert witness provides analysis that an appraiser is not qualified to give. Appraisers determine value. Financing experts determine what the market will lend against that value, under what conditions, at what cost, and with what structural requirements. In contested matters involving loan feasibility, refinance risk, or the question of whether a debtor's plan of reorganization is feasible, the financing expert's opinion is often the dispositive one because the court or the parties must determine not just what the property is worth but what it can actually support in debt.

Attorneys should retain a CRE financing expert in any of the following circumstances: a borrower in bankruptcy contends that a plan of reorganization is feasible based on a refinance that a creditor disputes is achievable; a secured creditor claims that but-for environmental contamination caused by a third party, the property would have supported a larger loan sufficient to repay its debt; a construction lender disputes a takeout commitment or claims that permanent financing would not have been available; or a party to a foreclosure or receivership disputes the lender's conduct in servicing a loan or making advances.

A qualified CRE financing expert must have direct, current transactional experience in the market where the subject property is located, or in directly comparable secondary markets, at the time relevant to the dispute. Retired bankers who have not originated or brokered commercial mortgage transactions in the past several years are problematic as experts because lending standards, pricing, and credit appetite change materially across market cycles. The relevant qualification is active market participation, current lender relationships, and a demonstrated track record of successfully financing comparable assets. At Commercial Lending Solutions, our approximately $1 billion in transaction volume across more than 1,000 lender relationships and active origination in all 50 states provides the foundation for market-based expert analysis that reflects actual current lender behavior rather than theoretical standards.

Before retaining a financing expert, counsel should gather the following materials: the current rent roll and all lease documents including any environmental clauses or termination rights; the Phase I and Phase II environmental reports and any PADEP correspondence; the existing loan documents including any environmental indemnity provisions; the current appraisal and any prior appraisals; and any prior financing applications or lender rejection letters. This documentary record allows the expert to reconstruct the lender's underwriting perspective and provide a well-grounded opinion rather than one dependent solely on the expert's general market knowledge.

At trial or in a plan confirmation hearing, CRE financing expert testimony typically addresses four questions: what financing was available, at what terms, from which categories of lenders, and whether those terms were sufficient to accomplish the party's stated financial objective. The expert can be used affirmatively to establish feasibility, defensively to challenge an opposing plan or valuation, or as a damages quantification witness where the difference between available financing on a clean asset and available financing on an impaired asset represents the financial harm caused by the environmental condition or by a party's conduct.

Key Takeaways

  • Environmental impairment does not categorically preclude first-mortgage financing, but it substantially reduces the universe of available lenders, increases the cost of capital, reduces maximum LTV by 10 to 15 percentage points relative to clean product, and requires specific structural accommodations including environmental escrows, indemnity agreements, and pollution liability insurance.
  • In Scranton, the most likely source of first-mortgage capital for an actively impaired industrial asset is the community and regional bank market or private credit, not CMBS or life company execution. The distinction matters because these lenders produce materially different terms, loan amounts, and structural requirements than institutional products.
  • The maximum supportable loan is constrained by both DSCR and LTV simultaneously, and for environmentally impaired, below-market-leased assets in secondary markets, the LTV constraint typically governs. In this engagement, the LTV constraint produced a maximum loan approximately $545,000 to $845,000 below the DSCR-constrained maximum.

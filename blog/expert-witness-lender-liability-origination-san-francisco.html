Expert Witness: Commercial Loan Origination Standards in San Francisco, CA

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Lender Liability and Loan Origination Standards

The San Francisco Market Context

San Francisco's commercial real estate market is among the most structurally complex and lender-sensitive in the United States, and that complexity makes it fertile ground for financing disputes. The city's post-pandemic dislocation has been severe and well-documented: office vacancy in the downtown core has exceeded 30 percent in recent quarters, with sublease availability pushing effective vacancy even higher in certain corridors. Class A rents that once commanded $80 to $100 per square foot annually have compressed significantly, and concession packages, including free rent periods of 12 to 18 months and generous tenant improvement allowances, have become standard in competitive lease negotiations. This environment creates direct underwriting challenges. When a lender or broker was originating a loan against a San Francisco office asset in 2021 or 2022, the income assumptions that drove the underwriting often did not reflect what the market was about to do. Whether those assumptions were reasonable at the time, or whether they reflected negligence, wishful thinking, or outright misrepresentation, is precisely the kind of question that requires a qualified commercial real estate financing expert to answer.

Beyond office, the city's multifamily sector has remained more resilient but is not without complexity. Rent control under the San Francisco Rent Ordinance applies broadly to pre-1979 residential units, which meaningfully affects achievable NOI growth and therefore long-term debt serviceability. Multifamily cap rates in San Francisco have generally traded in the 4.0 to 5.5 percent range depending on asset quality and rent control exposure, with rent-stabilized assets pricing tighter on a cash flow basis but carrying regulatory risk that sophisticated lenders price into their credit decisions. Mixed-use properties with retail on ground floors have faced their own headwinds, as retail vacancy in neighborhoods like Union Square and the Financial District has risen sharply. Each of these asset-specific dynamics creates a distinct underwriting environment, and disputes that arise from loans on these assets require an expert who understands not just general lending principles but the granular specifics of how San Francisco's market conditions translate into lender behavior and origination standards.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing a borrower in a lender liability matter involving a San Francisco office property. The asset was a multi-tenant mid-rise office building in the South of Market submarket, a corridor that had seen aggressive pre-pandemic investment activity and significant post-pandemic deterioration. The borrower alleged that the originating lender and the mortgage broker who placed the loan had misrepresented the terms of the financing, including the projected debt service coverage ratio at stabilization, the feasibility of the interest reserve structure, and the realistic refinance path at loan maturity. The lender, in turn, alleged that the borrower had sophisticated legal and financial counsel at closing and had signed documents containing all material terms.

Counsel retained CLS CRE to address three central questions. First, did the loan origination, as structured and as disclosed, conform to industry standards of care applicable to commercial mortgage brokers and lenders operating in the San Francisco market at the time of closing? Second, were the income projections and DSCR calculations presented to the borrower during the origination process consistent with how a reasonably competent underwriter would have modeled the asset given known market conditions? Third, what would a reasonably competent commercial mortgage broker have disclosed to a borrower in this position, and what alternatives would that broker have identified and presented? These are financing questions, not valuation questions, and they require a different analytical framework and a different expert than a licensed appraiser can provide.

The Financial Analysis

The core of our analysis required reconstructing the underwriting as it existed at origination and then testing those assumptions against both contemporaneous market data and applicable lending standards. The property was underwritten to a stabilized occupancy of 92 percent with in-place and projected rents averaging approximately $68 per square foot annually on a full-service gross basis. At the time of origination, that assumption was defensible on its face for a well-located South of Market asset, but the underwriting failed to apply meaningful haircuts for the lease-up risk on vacant suites or to account for the concession packages that were already becoming standard in competing buildings.

Stabilized effective gross income, after applying a vacancy and credit loss reserve of 8 percent, was presented in the origination package as approximately $4.1 million annually. Operating expenses, inclusive of management fees, insurance, property taxes under Proposition 13's base year constraints, and routine maintenance, were underwritten at approximately $1.55 million, producing a net operating income figure of roughly $2.55 million. The loan was a bridge product with an initial term of 36 months and two six-month extension options, structured at a floating rate of SOFR plus 325 basis points with interest-only payments throughout.

At the time of closing, the all-in rate was approximately 6.8 percent on a notional balance of $28.5 million. Annual interest expense at that rate was approximately $1.94 million, producing a trailing DSCR of approximately 1.31x on the underwritten NOI. That number appears to satisfy the standard 1.20x to 1.25x DSCR threshold that most bridge lenders and conventional commercial real estate lenders require as a minimum for income-producing assets. However, our analysis identified a critical flaw: the interest reserve built into the loan was sized for only 12 months of carry on the vacant suites, and the leasing timeline assumed new tenants would be in place and paying rent within that window. That assumption was not supported by contemporaneous market data showing average lease-up timelines in the South of Market submarket running 18 to 24 months for mid-size office suites.

When we re-ran the DSCR using conservative but market-supported assumptions, specifically a 78 percent stabilized occupancy reflecting the actual leasing velocity in the submarket, concession packages that reduced effective rent by approximately 12 percent, and an extended lease-up timeline that exhausted the interest reserve, the supportable NOI dropped to approximately $1.85 million. Against the same debt service obligation, that produced a DSCR of approximately 0.95x, a figure that would have disqualified the loan under any lender's credit policy and that would have required either a significantly lower loan amount or a materially different structure. The maximum supportable loan at a 1.20x DSCR on our conservative NOI, assuming the same rate, would have been approximately $18.3 million, roughly $10.2 million less than the loan as originated. At the time of origination, the as-is LTV was approximately 62 percent against an appraised value of $46 million; the loan-to-cost on the stabilized business plan was approximately 71 percent. These metrics appeared within standard ranges, but they were downstream of an inflated NOI assumption, meaning the LTV cushion was illusory.

The Complicating Factors

Floating Rate Exposure Without a Meaningful Cap: The loan was originated with a rate cap, as required by the lender, but the cap was struck at SOFR plus 500 basis points, meaning the borrower retained 175 basis points of floating rate exposure above the origination rate before the cap kicked in. In a rising rate environment, this structure materially increased the risk of DSCR compression. A reasonably competent mortgage broker advising a borrower with a tight leasing timeline on a mid-rise office asset would have identified this structure as carrying meaningful refinance risk at maturity and would have documented that conversation. There was no evidence in the origination file that the broker did so.

San Francisco's Office Market Trajectory: By the time this loan closed, publicly available data from major commercial real estate research providers was already showing elevated sublease availability in the South of Market submarket. The broker's underwriting package cited market rents from a period that predated a material shift in leasing fundamentals. A competent underwriter applying the standard of care for San Francisco office in that environment would have applied a forward-looking rent adjustment or at minimum disclosed the risk that rents were trending downward. The failure to do so, and the failure to present the borrower with a downside scenario, is a meaningful deviation from standard practice.

Interest Reserve Sizing: Bridge loans on transitional assets require interest reserves sized to the realistic lease-up timeline, not the optimistic one. The standard of care in the bridge lending market requires that the reserve cover the period of maximum vacancy exposure plus a buffer. In this case, the reserve was sized to the broker's projected lease-up timeline, not to a market-supported one. This is a distinction that matters legally because the reserve sizing directly affected the borrower's perception of the loan's risk profile at origination.

Refinance Feasibility at Maturity: For any bridge loan on a transitional asset, the exit strategy is the refinance into permanent agency or bank financing at maturity. A competent broker is expected to stress-test that exit against realistic maturity conditions. In this engagement, there was no evidence that a refinance feasibility analysis was performed or disclosed to the borrower. Given the trajectory of San Francisco office fundamentals and rising interest rates, a competent broker would have identified that the refinance at maturity was contingent on achieving stabilization within a compressed timeline and that failure to do so could leave the borrower facing extension fees, potential default, or a forced sale into a deteriorating market.

The San Francisco Lender Market

San Francisco's commercial mortgage market includes a layered universe of capital sources, each with distinct credit criteria and appetite for complexity. Understanding which lenders were actually available for a given asset at a given time is essential context for evaluating whether a broker's placement recommendation met the standard of care.

Life Insurance Companies: Life company lenders remain active in San Francisco for stabilized, well-leased assets with credit tenants and long weighted average lease terms. For office specifically, life companies tightened their underwriting criteria significantly beginning in late 2022, requiring higher DSCRs in the range of 1.35x to 1.40x, lower LTVs in the range of 55 to 60 percent, and in many cases imposing debt yield minimums of 9 to 10 percent. For transitional or partially vacant office assets, life companies are not a realistic lending source, and presenting one as a likely exit to a borrower would itself be a misrepresentation of market conditions.

CMBS Conduit Lenders: Conduit lenders were active in San Francisco through much of the 2020 to 2022 period for stabilized retail, multifamily, and industrial assets. For office, conduit appetite in San Francisco became highly restricted as secondary market concerns about office collateral intensified. Conduit loans require securitization-grade underwriting, meaning the income must be fully in place and the lease term must extend well beyond the loan term. They are not appropriate for transitional assets, and a competent broker would not have identified CMBS as a likely permanent financing vehicle for a partially vacant South of Market office building.

Regional and Community Banks: San Francisco is served by a substantial community and regional bank presence, including Bay Area-headquartered institutions with deep familiarity with the local market. These lenders typically require full recourse, DSCR of 1.25x on in-place income only, and LTVs in the 60 to 65 percent range for office. They are a viable source for stabilized or near-stabilized assets with strong sponsorship but are generally not structured to provide bridge financing on high-vacancy transitional properties.

Debt Funds and Bridge Lenders: The transitional asset lending space in San Francisco is dominated by institutional debt funds, including both national platforms and California-focused vehicles. These lenders underwrite to a business plan, model the lease-up, and size their loans to a combination of as-is value and stabilized value projections. They typically charge origination fees of 1.0 to 2.0 percent, floating rates of SOFR plus 275 to 425 basis points depending on leverage and asset quality, and require that the interest reserve cover a realistic lease-up timeline. This is the lender category relevant to the disputed loan, and our analysis evaluated whether the loan terms as originated were consistent with what this category of lender was actually offering in the market at that time.

What Attorneys Handling San Francisco CRE Disputes Need to Know

When a commercial real estate financing transaction is the subject of litigation, whether the claim sounds in lender liability, professional negligence, fraud, or breach of fiduciary duty, counsel needs to understand the distinction between what an appraiser can testify to and what a financing expert can address. An appraiser can opine on the value of the asset at a given point in time. A CRE financing expert addresses how lenders actually underwrite that value, what a competent broker would have disclosed, whether the loan structure was appropriate for the asset and the borrower's business plan, and whether the origination process conformed to applicable standards of care.

Attorneys should retain a CRE financing expert when the dispute involves any of the following: alleged misrepresentation of loan terms or underwriting projections during origination; claims that a broker failed to identify or disclose available financing alternatives; disputes over whether a loan was properly structured for the borrower's stated investment objective; lender liability claims based on loan acceleration, enforcement, or modification conduct; and expert testimony on whether a proposed plan of reorganization in bankruptcy is feasible given current lending market conditions.

A qualified CRE financing expert should have direct experience originating, placing, or underwriting commercial mortgages across multiple asset classes and market cycles, not merely academic familiarity with lending theory. At CLS CRE, our qualifications include approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, active relationships with more than 1,000 commercial lenders across 50 states, and direct experience in the San Francisco market across office, multifamily, mixed-use, and industrial asset types. This is a meaningful distinction from a retired banker who underwrote loans at a single institution under a single credit policy.

Before engaging a financing expert, counsel should gather the complete origination file including all term sheets, loan applications, underwriting memoranda, lender presentations, interest reserve analyses, rate cap documentation, and all written communications between the broker, borrower, and lender during the origination period. Contemporaneous market data for the submarket and asset type is also essential. The more complete the origination record, the more precise the expert's analysis of deviations from the standard of care.

At trial or in a plan confirmation hearing, a CRE financing expert provides opinion testimony grounded in the methodology of how commercial mortgage professionals actually operate. This testimony helps the fact-finder understand not just what was disclosed, but what should have been disclosed, what alternatives existed, and whether the outcome the borrower experienced was foreseeable given the origination as structured.

Key Takeaways

  • San Francisco's office market dislocation has created a generation of loans where origination-era assumptions are now provably inconsistent with the market data that was available at closing. This creates a well-defined factual basis for lender liability and professional negligence claims that a qualified expert can systematically analyze.
  • DSCR analysis must be performed on realistic, market-supported income assumptions, not on sponsor projections. A DSCR that appears compliant at 1.31x can collapse to sub-1.0x when proper haircuts for vacancy, concessions, and lease-up timing are applied. This is the analytical core of most underwriting negligence claims.
  • Interest reserve sizing is a professional obligation, not a discretionary choice. Sizing a reserve to an optimistic lease-up timeline rather than a market-supported one is a deviation from the standard of care that directly affects the borrower's risk exposure and their ability to make an informed decision at closing.
  • Refinance feasibility at maturity is a disclosure obligation in bridge loan originations. A competent broker documents and discloses the conditions under which the exit strategy is feasible and the risks if those conditions are not met. The absence of that analysis in an origination file is itself evidence of a deficient origination process.
  • The San Francisco lender market is stratified and asset-specific. Representing that a certain lender category is a realistic financing source for an asset that category will not finance is a material misrepresentation, regardless of whether it is made in writing or verbally during the origination process.
  • A CRE financing expert and an appraiser serve different functions in commercial real estate litigation. Counsel who retain only an appraiser are leaving significant expert testimony capacity on the table, particularly where the dispute centers on origination conduct, loan structure, or broker professional standards.
  • Floating rate exposure, cap structure, and maturity risk must be modeled prospectively, not just at origination. A loan that works at the day-one rate can produce a DSCR violation well within the loan term if rate cap strike prices are set too high relative to lease-up timelines and income growth assumptions.

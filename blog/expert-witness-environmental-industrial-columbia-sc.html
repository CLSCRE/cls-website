Expert Witness: Financing a Contaminated Industrial Property in Columbia, SC

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Environmental Contamination Analysis

The Columbia Market Context

Columbia, South Carolina occupies a position in the Southeast industrial market that is frequently underestimated by coastal capital. The metro sits at the confluence of I-20 and I-26, with direct highway access to the Port of Charleston roughly 115 miles to the southeast, and meaningful proximity to the Charlotte distribution corridor to the north. That geography matters for industrial underwriting: Columbia is not a tertiary backwater. It is a functional logistics node with genuine tenant demand from manufacturers, defense contractors, and distributors serving the Carolinas interior.

Industrial vacancy in the Columbia MSA has remained in the 5% to 8% range through recent reporting periods, meaningfully tighter than the national average for markets of comparable population. Net absorption has been positive in multiple consecutive quarters, driven by light manufacturing relocation from higher-cost metros and continued defense-sector activity anchored by Fort Jackson, the largest Army basic training installation in the country. Asking rents for Class B warehouse product in the range of 50,000 to 200,000 square feet have moved from the low-to-mid $4.00 per square foot NNN range toward $5.50 to $6.50 per square foot NNN in recent years, with newer vintage or recently renovated product commanding more. That rent movement is central to the financing analysis in a below-market-rent scenario, as I will explain.

Cap rate compression in Columbia industrial has lagged coastal markets but has still occurred. Stabilized industrial assets in Columbia's primary submarkets, including West Columbia, Cayce, and the I-20 corridor east of downtown, have traded in the 6.25% to 7.25% cap rate range depending on vintage, clear height, lease term, and credit quality of the tenant. Those cap rates inform both valuation and the maximum loan supportable under institutional underwriting standards. For a property encumbered by an environmental condition, the effective cap rate investors and lenders apply is higher, which compresses both value and loan proceeds, sometimes dramatically. Understanding how far that compression extends under different remediation scenarios is precisely the kind of analysis a CRE financing expert provides that an appraiser alone typically does not.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by counsel representing a class of secured creditors in a regulatory enforcement proceeding involving an industrial warehouse property in the Columbia metropolitan area. The asset was a single-tenant NNN-leased warehouse facility of approximately 120,000 square feet, operating under a long-term lease with a creditworthy industrial tenant. The lease was written at rents that had become materially below current market over time, as is common in older NNN structures with limited or no rent escalation provisions.

The property had been identified by state and federal regulators as a site of concern due to the presence of 1,4-dioxane and chlorinated volatile organic compounds, specifically trichloroethylene and its degradation byproducts, detected in soil and groundwater samples associated with prior industrial operations on the site. The current ownership had not caused the contamination; the condition predated the existing ownership structure. Nevertheless, the environmental liability attached to the real property itself, as is standard under CERCLA successor liability frameworks and corresponding state-law equivalents in South Carolina.

The core questions I was asked to address were: first, whether first-mortgage financing sufficient to retire the existing senior debt was available in the current market, notwithstanding the environmental condition; second, what the maximum supportable loan amount would be under market-standard underwriting assumptions; and third, whether the below-market lease structure created an independent constraint on loan proceeds separate from the environmental issue. My engagement did not extend to appraisal of the property or to environmental remediation cost estimation. Those opinions were provided by separately retained experts. My role was to translate the property's financial and physical characteristics into a financing analysis reflecting how institutional and non-institutional lenders actually underwrite this type of collateral in this market.

The Financial Analysis

The income analysis began with the in-place NNN lease. Because the tenant was responsible for taxes, insurance, and most operating expenses under a true triple-net structure, the effective gross income to the landlord was essentially equal to base rent. The lease provided for fixed rent of approximately $4.15 per square foot annually on the 120,000-square-foot facility, generating in-place gross revenues of approximately $498,000 per year. After a modest vacancy and credit loss reserve and minimal landlord-side management and administrative costs, the stabilized in-place NOI was approximately $475,000.

Market rents for comparable warehouse product in the relevant Columbia submarket were running in the $5.75 to $6.25 per square foot range at the time of my analysis, net of landlord obligations. The mark-to-market gap was therefore approximately $1.60 to $2.10 per square foot, or roughly $192,000 to $252,000 in annualized NOI that was not being captured under the existing lease. This matters to lenders because it cuts in two directions: the below-market rent provides some degree of tenant retention security, but it also means that if the tenant exercises any termination right or vacates at lease expiration, the owner faces a capital improvement and re-leasing timeline before the asset generates market-level income. Lenders underwrite to in-place income, not to market income, unless the lease has fewer than 12 to 18 months of remaining term, in which case they underwrite to a stressed or dark scenario. The remaining lease term in this engagement provided sufficient runway that lenders would anchor to in-place NOI, but at a meaningful discount to potential value.

On the debt service coverage ratio, the market standard for stabilized industrial assets among institutional lenders, including life companies, CMBS conduits, and larger regional banks, is a minimum 1.25x DSCR on an interest-only basis or, more commonly, on a 25-to-30-year amortization schedule depending on loan term. For environmentally impaired collateral, lenders who will lend at all typically require a higher DSCR cushion, in the range of 1.35x to 1.45x, to compensate for the additional collateral risk. I applied a 1.35x DSCR floor as a conservative but defensible standard for this engagement.

At the time of my analysis, the relevant rate index for 5-to-7-year fixed-rate industrial loans was in the 6.50% to 7.25% range for conventional lenders. For environmentally complicated collateral, pricing moves up by 50 to 150 basis points depending on lender type and the status of the remediation. I used a 7.50% all-in rate on a 25-year amortization schedule, reflecting a lender credit-adjusted pricing tier appropriate for this risk profile.

Working through the arithmetic: with an in-place NOI of $475,000 and a required DSCR of 1.35x, the maximum annual debt service the property could support was $475,000 divided by 1.35, or approximately $351,850. At a 7.50% interest rate on a 25-year amortization, a $351,850 annual debt service payment corresponds to a loan amount of approximately $4,075,000, using standard mortgage constant mathematics. That figure represents the income-constrained maximum loan, sometimes called the debt capacity figure. It is the ceiling imposed by the cash flow, independent of any value-based LTV analysis.

For LTV, lenders in this category will typically not exceed 55% to 65% LTV on environmentally impaired industrial collateral, compared to 65% to 75% for clean assets. If the appraised value was, for example, $7,500,000 reflecting a cap rate in the 6.5% range applied to in-place NOI with a modest environmental stigma adjustment, then a 60% LTV cap would yield a value-constrained maximum of approximately $4,500,000. In this scenario, the income constraint was the binding constraint, capping proceeds at approximately $4,075,000. Whether that figure was sufficient to retire the existing senior debt was a factual question specific to the debt balance, which I will not reproduce here, but the methodology and result informed the creditors' legal strategy directly.

The Complicating Factors

Environmental Condition and Lender Avoidance. The most significant complication in this engagement was also the most fundamental: the majority of the conventional lending market will not extend first-mortgage credit on a property with known, unremediated chlorinated solvent contamination. This is not a matter of pricing; it is a matter of credit policy. Life insurance company lenders, which are among the most aggressive sources of long-term fixed-rate industrial capital, exclude Phase II-identified contaminated sites from eligibility at most shops. CMBS lenders are similarly constrained by rating agency guidelines and securitization eligibility rules. Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac are not relevant to industrial product. This narrowed the practical lender universe to a subset of balance-sheet lenders, including certain regional and community banks with established environmental lending protocols, private debt funds with specific impaired-collateral mandates, and SBA 504 program lenders under limited circumstances.

Remediation Status and Lender Conditions. Lenders who will consider contaminated collateral do so under specific conditions. Most require either a No Further Action letter or equivalent regulatory closure document from the South Carolina Department of Health and Environmental Control, or alternatively, an active and monitored remediation plan with a defined cost envelope and a funded remediation escrow or bonded remediation obligation. Without one of those conditions in place, even balance-sheet lenders who consider impaired collateral will require that a material portion of loan proceeds be held in an environmental reserve account controlled by the lender, reducing net borrower proceeds and potentially making the financing economically unworkable relative to the existing debt balance.

Below-Market Lease and Mark-to-Market Risk. As noted, the lease was written at rents approximately 35% to 40% below current market. This creates what underwriters call mark-to-market risk: the risk that a sophisticated tenant, knowing it has a bargained-for below-market lease, has a reduced incentive to invest in the space and may be willing to walk away at lease expiration rather than renew at market rates. For a property also carrying an environmental condition, that walk-away risk is higher because the tenant may use the environmental issue as leverage to negotiate early termination or rental abatement. Lenders price this risk through a combination of higher DSCR requirements, lower LTV limits, and shorter loan terms aligned with remaining lease duration.

CERCLA Successor Liability and Lender Concerns. Lenders taking a first mortgage on a contaminated site must navigate their own potential liability exposure. Under CERCLA's secured creditor exemption, a lender who holds a security interest without participating in management of the facility is generally protected from USEPA cost recovery actions. However, that protection has limits, and lenders are acutely aware that exercising remedies upon default, including foreclosure, could expose them to owner or operator liability if the remediation is still active. This is why many conventional lenders will not lend at all regardless of pricing: the risk is not just financial underperformance but direct regulatory liability upon foreclosure.

The Columbia Lender Market

For a clean, stabilized industrial asset in Columbia, the lender market is reasonably deep. Regional banks headquartered in Charlotte, Atlanta, and Raleigh actively cover the Columbia market and have established industrial lending programs. South Carolina-chartered community banks, including several with meaningful commercial real estate portfolios and Columbia presence, compete aggressively for stabilized industrial credits. Life companies access Columbia through correspondent relationships and have funded industrial assets in the metro, typically at larger balances and longer terms. CMBS executes in Columbia on appropriately sized deals with strong in-place cash flow and creditworthy tenants.

For environmentally impaired industrial collateral, that universe compresses substantially. In my experience across CLS CRE's lender network of more than 1,000 relationships, the realistic lender categories for this type of collateral in a mid-South market like Columbia include: balance-sheet community banks and regional banks with dedicated environmental risk departments and established ASTM-compliant Phase I and Phase II review protocols; private credit funds and debt funds specializing in special-situation commercial real estate, typically pricing in the SOFR plus 450 to 650 basis point range depending on leverage and remediation status; and SBA 7(a) or 504 lenders where the borrower meets small-business eligibility requirements and the environmental condition is documented with an active DHEC-supervised remediation plan. Bridge lenders and hard money sources also exist in this space, but their typical terms, including 12-to-24-month loan periods, high upfront fees, and prepayment constraints, make them relevant as gap capital or repositioning capital rather than as a mechanism to retire long-term senior debt at sustainable economics.

Critically, the Columbia market benefits from the presence of several South Carolina community banks that have developed internal expertise in environmental lending, often because they serve agricultural and light-industrial borrowers across the state's rural corridors where legacy contamination from textile and chemical industries is not uncommon. That institutional knowledge within the community banking sector is a market-specific characteristic that improves the realistic availability of financing in Columbia relative to some comparably sized markets elsewhere in the South.

What Attorneys Handling Columbia CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to Retain a CRE Financing Expert. An attorney handling a secured creditor dispute, a contested plan of reorganization, a lender liability case, or a regulatory enforcement proceeding with real property at its center should consider retaining a CRE financing expert whenever the central question involves the availability, cost, or sufficiency of debt capital on a specific asset. This is distinct from the question an appraiser answers. An appraiser determines what a property is worth. A CRE financing expert determines what a lender will actually do with that property as collateral, how much they will lend, on what terms, and what conditions they will impose. Those are not the same question, and courts have recognized the distinction.

What a CRE Financing Expert Can Address That an Appraiser Cannot. Appraisers work within the USPAP framework and produce value opinions. A CRE financing expert opines on lender underwriting standards, market credit conditions, DSCR and LTV thresholds, the composition and depth of the active lender universe for a specific collateral type, and whether a specific financing structure is commercially reasonable under market standards. In an environmental impairment case, the expert can opine on how contamination affects not just value but lender eligibility, pricing, loan structure, and remediation escrow requirements. That testimony is frequently decisive in creditor disputes where the question is whether financing was available, not just what the property was worth.

Qualifications That Matter. The relevant expertise is active, current market knowledge: who is actually lending, at what terms, and for what collateral types. A retired banker with no current market engagement may have historical knowledge but will struggle to provide reliable testimony about present-day lender credit boxes, SOFR-based pricing, current DSCR thresholds, or environmental underwriting standards that have evolved considerably since the GFC. A qualified CRE financing expert should be able to identify specific lender categories and their underwriting requirements, demonstrate active transactional engagement in the relevant market, and provide a methodology that can be tested through cross-examination. Our firm's approximately $1 billion in transaction volume and active engagement across all 50 states provides the current market context that retrospective knowledge alone cannot.

Information Counsel Should Gather Before the Engagement. At minimum, the expert needs: the current rent roll with lease expiration dates, renewal options, and rent escalation provisions; the existing loan documents including the note, deed of trust, and any environmental indemnity agreements; all Phase I and Phase II environmental site assessment reports; any DHEC correspondence, consent orders, or NFA letters; the operating history of the property including any available financial statements; and any existing appraisals or broker opinions of value. The expert cannot opine on financing availability without understanding the income stream and cannot opine on lender conditions without reviewing the environmental documentation. Gaps in that documentation will produce gaps in the opinion, which opposing counsel will exploit.

How This Testimony Is Used. In a plan confirmation hearing, financing expert testimony supports feasibility arguments under Section 1129(a)(11) of the Bankruptcy Code. In a secured creditor dispute, it supports or undermines claims about whether alternative financing was available to retire a disputed debt. In a lender liability case, it establishes the standard of care for commercial mortgage underwriting. In an environmental enforcement context, it quantifies the financing impact of regulatory conditions on the property's ability to carry debt, which directly bears on damages and remediation cost allocation.

Key Takeaways

  • Environmental contamination does not automatically preclude financing, but it dramatically narrows the lender universe and imposes structural conditions, including remediation escrows, that reduce net loan proceeds and may make the financing insufficient to retire existing senior debt.
  • The income constraint, not the LTV constraint, was the binding factor in this engagement. Below-market rents suppressed NOI to a level that capped debt capacity below what the property's physical value would otherwise support, independent of the environmental discount.

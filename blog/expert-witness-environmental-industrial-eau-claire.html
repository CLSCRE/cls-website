Expert Witness: Financing a Contaminated Industrial Property in Eau Claire, WI

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Environmental Contamination Analysis

The Eau Claire Market Context

Eau Claire, Wisconsin sits at the confluence of the Chippewa and Eau Claire rivers in the west-central part of the state, roughly 90 miles east of Minneapolis-St. Paul. That proximity to a major metro matters enormously in commercial real estate litigation. Eau Claire functions as a regional hub for healthcare, light manufacturing, distribution, and professional services, and its industrial corridor has absorbed meaningful capital from operators relocating or expanding out of the Twin Cities market due to land costs and labor availability. For a secured creditor dispute involving an industrial warehouse asset, that geographic positioning is directly relevant: Eau Claire industrial product is not a secondary backwater, it is an extension of a functioning supply-chain ecosystem.

Industrial vacancy in the Eau Claire market has generally tracked in the 4% to 7% range in recent years, below the national average for comparable secondary markets. Functional Class B and Class C warehouse space with clear heights of 18 to 24 feet in the city's established industrial corridors along Highway 12, the Clairemont industrial district, and parcels near the Eau Claire Regional Airport commands triple-net rents broadly in the $4.50 to $7.50 per square foot range depending on bay configuration, dock count, and vintage. Newer construction, which has been limited, pushes toward the higher end. That rent ceiling is a critical underwriting constraint: it disciplines how aggressively any lender can underwrite NOI, even when a property is 100% occupied.

Cap rates for stabilized, NNN-leased industrial assets in Eau Claire typically land in the 7.0% to 8.5% range, occasionally compressing toward 6.75% for long-term, creditworthy tenants with no environmental clouds. In litigation contexts, where financing feasibility and collateral value are contested, that cap rate spread carries material consequences for loan sizing. A 75 basis point movement in exit cap rate can swing a concluded value by several hundred thousand dollars on a mid-size warehouse, which in turn determines whether a first-mortgage loan is sufficient to retire existing senior debt or whether the secured creditor is undersecured.

The Engagement

Counsel for the secured creditor in a regulatory enforcement proceeding retained Commercial Lending Solutions to provide a financing feasibility opinion on a single-tenant industrial warehouse property in the Eau Claire market. The property was 100% occupied under a triple-net lease structure, but the lease rates were negotiated several years prior and reflected below-market terms relative to current asking rents. The regulatory matter involved alleged contamination from prior industrial operations at the site, specifically the presence of 1,4-dioxane and chlorinated volatile organic compounds, or VOCs, in soil and potentially groundwater. A state environmental agency had issued enforcement correspondence, and the extent of remediation obligations had not yet been fully quantified.

The core legal questions our firm was asked to address were two-fold. First, does the existence of an unresolved environmental contamination condition categorically preclude first-mortgage financing from the institutional market, or is financing available under some structured set of conditions? Second, if financing is theoretically available, what is the maximum supportable loan amount under current market underwriting standards, and is that amount sufficient to retire the existing senior mortgage obligation? A secondary question, equally important to the creditor's position, was whether the below-market lease rents constrained loan sizing independent of the environmental condition, such that the debt retirement shortfall could be attributed to income deficiency rather than solely to environmental impairment.

Our engagement produced a written financing feasibility opinion consistent with the standards applicable to Rule 26 expert reports, drawing on CLS CRE's active lender relationships across the Wisconsin and Midwest institutional, regional bank, credit union, and debt fund markets.

The Financial Analysis

The income analysis began with the in-place lease. The tenant was paying gross NNN rents that, when annualized, produced a contract base rent below the current market range for comparable industrial product in Eau Claire. For the purposes of this illustration, assume a 40,000 square foot warehouse generating $5.00 per square foot in annual NNN rent, producing gross contract rent of $200,000. After deducting a structural vacancy and credit loss reserve of 5%, consistent with lender underwriting norms for single-tenant NNN assets, and accounting for a nominal management fee of 3% applied to effective gross income despite the NNN structure, the underwritten NOI arrived at approximately $183,000.

This is a point of frequent misunderstanding in litigation. Even on a triple-net lease where the tenant pays operating expenses, lenders still apply a management fee reserve and a vacancy haircut. No institutional lender underwrites 100% of contract rent as NOI on a single-tenant building, regardless of lease structure. The rationale is straightforward: if the tenant vacates, re-leasing a contaminated industrial building in a secondary Wisconsin market carries real cost and time risk.

The DSCR threshold applied was 1.25x, which is the standard minimum for conventional commercial real estate loans on industrial collateral in secondary markets. Some lenders, particularly community banks and credit unions lending in Eau Claire, apply 1.20x for owner-occupied transactions or for borrowers with strong global cash flow, but on an investment-grade, non-owner-occupied industrial asset with an environmental cloud, 1.25x is the floor. Several lenders in the current environment are requiring 1.30x on environmentally impaired collateral.

At the time of the analysis, a reasonable market rate for a 5-year fixed, 25-year amortizing commercial mortgage on industrial collateral in this complexity tier was in the 7.25% to 7.75% range. Using a 7.50% note rate and a 25-year amortization schedule, the annual debt service constant on a per-dollar-of-loan-amount basis is approximately 0.08748. Dividing the underwritten NOI of $183,000 by the 1.25x DSCR requirement yields a maximum supportable annual debt service of $146,400. Dividing that debt service figure by the annual constant of 0.08748 produces a maximum supportable loan based on income of approximately $1,673,000, rounded conservatively to $1,650,000.

The LTV constraint then operates as an independent governor. For industrial collateral with unresolved environmental conditions, lenders in the current market are generally limiting LTV to 55% to 65% of an as-is appraised value that itself reflects the environmental impairment. If the as-is appraised value of the property, post-environmental-discount, is in the $2,500,000 to $3,000,000 range, the LTV-constrained loan ceiling is roughly $1,375,000 to $1,950,000. The income-based maximum and the LTV-based maximum converge in a narrow range, and the final maximum supportable loan is the lower of the two. In this scenario, that ceiling lands in the $1,600,000 to $1,650,000 range.

If the existing senior debt obligation exceeds that figure, the secured creditor is quantifiably undersecured at current market financing capacity, and the gap between the supportable first-mortgage loan and the outstanding debt balance represents the financing shortfall that the expert opinion must document and explain.

The Complicating Factors

1,4-Dioxane and Chlorinated VOC Contamination

1,4-dioxane is a synthetic industrial solvent stabilizer historically used in chlorinated solvent manufacturing processes. It is highly water-soluble, does not readily biodegrade in the subsurface, and is not effectively captured by standard groundwater remediation technologies such as pump-and-treat systems designed for traditional chlorinated VOCs like trichloroethylene or perchloroethylene. The U.S. EPA lowered its health advisory level for 1,4-dioxane in drinking water to 0.35 micrograms per liter in 2022, and Wisconsin's own regulatory framework under the Wisconsin Department of Natural Resources addresses soil and groundwater standards that, if exceeded, trigger mandatory remediation obligations and ongoing monitoring requirements.

From a lender's perspective, 1,4-dioxane contamination presents three compounding problems. First, the remediation timeline is genuinely uncertain; conventional technologies may fail, and emerging in-situ chemical oxidation or advanced oxidation processes are expensive and not uniformly effective. Second, the total remediation cost may be open-ended at the time of loan origination, creating an unliquidated contingent liability that encumbers the asset. Third, lenders cannot obtain title insurance over an active enforcement matter without specific endorsements, and those endorsements are either unavailable or priced at levels that change the effective loan economics.

The Below-Market Lease Constraint

The NNN lease structure is favorable in isolation: a creditworthy tenant paying all operating expenses with a long-term commitment is precisely the profile institutional lenders prefer. The problem in this engagement is that the below-market rent locks the income stream at a level that constrains loan sizing below what the physical asset might otherwise support. If current market NNN rents for comparable Eau Claire industrial space are $6.50 per square foot but the subject lease runs at $5.00 per square foot with three years remaining on the primary term, the lender is underwriting the in-place income, not market rent. The result is that the maximum supportable loan reflects the below-market lease, not the asset's stabilized potential. This is a distinct and separable cause of financing constraint that must be identified independently from the environmental impairment.

Single-Tenant Concentration Risk

A 100% single-tenant building means that any credit event affecting that tenant, including a decision not to renew in the context of an environmentally impaired property, results in immediate 100% vacancy. Lenders in secondary markets like Eau Claire apply a concentration risk premium: higher DSCR requirements, lower LTV limits, and shorter loan terms on single-tenant assets relative to multi-tenant industrial parks. The combination of single-tenant concentration and environmental impairment is treated as a compounding risk factor, not an additive one.

Remediation Cost Quantification

Lenders will not close on environmentally impaired collateral without a Phase II Environmental Site Assessment and typically require a cost-to-remediate estimate from a qualified environmental professional. If the remediation cost estimate is unresolved, most lenders require an environmental escrow at closing equal to 125% to 150% of the high-end remediation cost estimate. That escrow reduces the net loan proceeds available to retire senior debt, meaning the headline loan amount and the effective net proceeds to the borrower are not the same number. Attorneys handling secured creditor disputes must understand this distinction.

The Eau Claire Lender Market

The institutional CMBS market and life insurance company lenders are effectively unavailable for this transaction. CMBS conduit lenders require environmental clearance, typically Phase I and Phase II with no recognized environmental conditions of consequence, as a condition of loan commitment. Life company lenders are even more restrictive, limiting their Wisconsin industrial exposure to clean, stabilized product in stronger submarkets. Neither category is a realistic financing source for an industrial asset under active environmental enforcement.

The most active and realistic lender category for this type of Eau Claire industrial transaction is the regional bank and community bank tier. Wisconsin has a deep community banking infrastructure, and Eau Claire specifically is served by regional institutions headquartered in Eau Claire, Chippewa Falls, Wausau, and the broader western Wisconsin market, as well as by Twin Cities-based regional banks with Wisconsin commercial real estate lending teams. These lenders will engage on environmentally impaired collateral, but they require full environmental documentation, a defined remediation path with cost estimates, evidence of regulatory cooperation, and in most cases, a personal guarantee from a creditworthy principal. Pricing on this type of loan typically carries a 50 to 125 basis point premium over the rate applicable to clean industrial collateral.

Credit unions with commercial real estate lending programs operate in the Eau Claire market as well, but their appetite for environmentally impaired collateral is limited. They are better suited to clean industrial transactions where a member-relationship or owner-occupant dynamic is present. Debt funds and bridge lenders represent a third category. Several national bridge lenders will provide short-term financing on environmentally impaired industrial collateral at higher LTV points, but at rates in the 9.50% to 11.50% range and with origination fees of 1.5% to 2.5%. The higher debt service on bridge financing further compresses the maximum supportable loan under DSCR analysis, often to a level below what a conventional bank loan would produce.

What Attorneys Handling Eau Claire CRE Disputes Need to Know

A commercial real estate appraiser and a commercial real estate financing expert are not interchangeable. An appraiser concludes value; a financing expert concludes financing feasibility, maximum supportable debt, and lender market conditions. In a secured creditor enforcement matter or a plan of reorganization hearing, the question before the court is often not purely what the property is worth, but whether a refinancing transaction could actually be completed in the current market, at what loan amount, and under what conditions. That is a financing question, not a valuation question, and it requires a different kind of expertise.

A qualified CRE financing expert must have active, current experience placing commercial mortgage transactions in the relevant market and asset type. Familiarity with how lenders are actually pricing and structuring deals today, not how they were priced five years ago, is the baseline requirement. At CLS CRE, our approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, more than 1,000 active lender relationships, and operations across all 50 states mean that our opinions are grounded in live market intelligence, not historical memory or academic theory.

Counsel should gather the following before the expert engagement begins: the full rent roll and lease abstracts including any below-market provisions or renewal options, all existing environmental reports (Phase I, Phase II, and any regulatory correspondence), the current outstanding senior debt balance and loan documents, the as-is appraisal if one exists, and any prior financing history on the asset. The expert will also need sufficient time to canvass the lender market, which means counsel should not wait until two weeks before a hearing to initiate the engagement.

In plan confirmation hearings, the financing feasibility opinion directly addresses the "feasibility" element of plan confirmation under Section 1129 of the Bankruptcy Code. In secured creditor litigation, it speaks to the adequacy of collateral coverage and the appropriate amount of any deficiency. In environmental enforcement proceedings, it can establish whether the contamination condition itself, versus income deficiency from below-market leases, is the operative cause of any financing shortfall, which is a distinction with significant remedial and liability implications.

Key Takeaways

  • Environmental contamination does not categorically foreclose financing, but it materially narrows the lender universe to community banks and selected regional institutions, excludes CMBS and life company capital entirely, and requires specific remediation documentation before any lender will commit.
  • The maximum supportable loan on this Eau Claire industrial scenario, based on in-place below-market NNN income at current market DSCR requirements of 1.25x and a 7.50% note rate on 25-year amortization, lands in the $1,600,000 to $1,650,000 range before LTV and environmental escrow adjustments further constrain net proceeds.
  • Below-market lease rents and environmental impairment are independent and separable causes of financing constraint. Identifying and quantifying each separately is essential to a well-supported expert opinion, particularly in contested matters where opposing counsel may argue that environmental conditions alone drive any shortfall.
  • Environmental remediation cost escrows reduce net loan proceeds at closing, meaning the headline loan amount approved by a lender is not necessarily the amount available to retire senior debt. Attorneys must account for this distinction in their analysis of whether a financing transaction can accomplish the desired result.
  • 1,4-dioxane contamination is treated more severely by lenders than many other environmental conditions because of its long remediation timelines, uncertain costs, and groundwater mobility. Properties with active enforcement correspondence involving this compound face heightened lender scrutiny and conservative underwriting adjustments.
  • The Eau Claire industrial market has genuine depth in the community bank tier, which means financing is not impossible on impaired collateral but requires experienced broker-of-record guidance to identify willing lenders and structure transactions that satisfy their specific credit requirements.
  • A CRE financing expert provides opinions that appraisers cannot: specifically, whether a loan of a given size can actually be placed in the current market, which lender categories will engage, and what structural requirements will govern the transaction. These opinions are directly relevant to plan feasibility, adequacy of collateral, and damages quantification.

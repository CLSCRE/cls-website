Expert Witness: CRE Financing Feasibility in Chapter 11 Proceedings in Cedar Rapids, IA

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Bankruptcy Exit Financing

The Cedar Rapids Market Context

Cedar Rapids is Iowa's second-largest city and one of the most economically resilient mid-size markets in the Midwest. For commercial real estate financing purposes, that distinction matters. The metro's industrial base anchors on food processing, advanced manufacturing, and logistics, with major distribution infrastructure tied to I-380 and the Eastern Iowa Airport corridor. Industrial vacancy in Cedar Rapids has remained consistently below 5% in recent cycles, and asking rents for Class B warehouse and flex-industrial product have trended upward modestly, reflecting national demand for Midwest logistics assets without the overbuilding pressure seen in gateway markets like Chicago or Indianapolis. For a bankruptcy estate asset, that supply-demand discipline is directly relevant: a well-located industrial or flex property in Cedar Rapids is more financeable in reorganization than comparable product in oversupplied secondary markets.

The office market tells a more complicated story. Cedar Rapids office fundamentals have softened since 2020, consistent with national trends, but the downtown core and southwest suburban submarkets retain meaningful occupancy among regional employers, professional services firms, and healthcare-adjacent tenants. Vacancy in Class B suburban office has drifted into the 15% to 20% range in certain pockets, which is a material underwriting variable when lenders are stress-testing a Chapter 11 asset's income stability. Retail in Cedar Rapids is bifurcated: grocery-anchored and necessity-based strip retail continues to perform, while secondary inline retail and power center anchors face the same structural headwinds seen nationally. Multifamily remains the most liquid asset class in the Cedar Rapids capital markets, with cap rates in the 6.0% to 6.75% range for stabilized properties, and lender appetite at its deepest for this product type. Understanding these asset-class-specific fundamentals is the starting point for any financing feasibility analysis in a Cedar Rapids reorganization proceeding.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by counsel for a plan proponent in a Chapter 11 reorganization proceeding involving a commercial income-producing property located in the Cedar Rapids metropolitan area. The asset was a multi-tenant light industrial and flex-office property, a property type that sits at the intersection of two of the market's stronger demand drivers: warehouse-distribution tenancy and small-business professional space. The property had been in the bankruptcy estate for a period of time during which deferred maintenance, lease rollover uncertainty, and the administrative complexity of the Chapter 11 process had created material questions about the asset's current and stabilized financing capacity.

Counsel retained CLS CRE to produce a written expert opinion addressing two distinct but related questions for the court. First, could the property support first-mortgage financing sufficient in size to satisfy the claims of senior secured creditors under a proposed Plan of Reorganization, and if so, under what conditions and at what loan size? Second, what categories of institutional and non-institutional lenders actively originate loans on commercial real estate assets that are part of a bankruptcy estate, and what are their standard underwriting requirements for this type of collateral and transaction structure? These are questions that a real property appraiser is not equipped to answer, and they are questions that go directly to the feasibility prong of plan confirmation under Section 1129(a)(11) of the Bankruptcy Code.

The Financial Analysis

The core of any CRE financing feasibility analysis is the construction of a defensible net operating income figure, then the application of lender-standard debt service coverage and loan-to-value constraints to determine the maximum supportable first-mortgage loan. In a bankruptcy context, this analysis carries additional weight because it is being offered to a court, not to a private lender, and must be able to withstand cross-examination on every assumption.

For the subject property, in-place gross revenue was drawn from rent rolls produced in the bankruptcy proceeding, with adjustments for credit loss and vacancy based on comparable Cedar Rapids flex-industrial properties showing stabilized vacancy in the 7% to 10% range for multi-tenant product. Management fees were applied at 4% of effective gross income, consistent with market rates for third-party property management of comparable assets in Iowa. After accounting for insurance, real estate taxes at the Linn County assessed rate applicable to the asset class, and reserves for replacement at $0.15 per square foot annually, the analysis produced a stabilized NOI in the range of $320,000 to $345,000 annually. I used the lower bound of that range as the underwriting NOI, which is consistent with how a conservative institutional lender would approach a collateral asset with any lingering occupancy uncertainty.

The debt service coverage ratio threshold I applied was 1.25x, which is the widely recognized market standard for conventional first-mortgage lending on stabilized commercial income property. Some lenders in this asset class will underwrite to 1.20x, particularly for lower-leverage requests, but using 1.25x provides the court with a defensible, conservative benchmark that reflects the tighter credit boxes lenders apply to bankruptcy-exit or recently-emerged collateral. At an assumed interest rate of 7.25% on a 25-year amortization schedule, consistent with fixed-rate pricing available in the current rate environment for five-year or seven-year term commercial mortgages on this property type and market, the maximum annual debt service supportable at 1.25x DSCR on a $320,000 NOI is $256,000. That debt service figure corresponds to a loan balance of approximately $3.05 million using a standard mortgage constant calculation.

The LTV constraint is equally important and operates as a separate, independent ceiling on loan size. For light industrial and flex product in secondary Midwest markets, conventional lenders typically apply a maximum LTV of 65% to 70% of appraised value. If the subject property was appraised at $4.8 million, which reflects the stabilized value range for comparable Cedar Rapids flex-industrial assets at current cap rates, then the 65% LTV ceiling produces a maximum loan of $3.12 million. In this case, both the DSCR constraint and the LTV constraint converged at approximately $3.0 million to $3.1 million, which is analytically significant. Convergence between the income-based ceiling and the value-based ceiling tells the court that the underwriting is internally consistent and that the maximum supportable loan is not being artificially inflated by one metric at the expense of the other. That $3.0 million to $3.1 million figure then became the basis for analyzing whether the proposed plan treatment of senior secured claims was feasible from a financing market standpoint.

The Complicating Factors

Bankruptcy-Exit Stigma in Conventional Lending. The most significant complicating factor in any bankruptcy-exit financing engagement is the institutional resistance many conventional lenders have to originating loans where the collateral is being transferred out of a bankruptcy estate, even pursuant to a confirmed plan. Bank lenders subject to OCC or FDIC examination scrutiny are particularly sensitive to "criticized asset" classifications, and a loan originated in connection with a Chapter 11 plan can attract scrutiny simply because of the paper trail. This is not an absolute barrier, but it narrows the lender universe. The practical solution is to identify lenders whose credit policies explicitly accommodate post-confirmation financing and whose underwriters have experience processing loans where title chain issues, prior lien releases, and discharge injunctions are part of the transaction. CLS CRE has worked with lenders in this category nationally, and several community banks and regional institutions in Iowa have originated post-confirmation loans when the collateral quality and borrower post-emergence creditworthiness supported the credit.

Income Continuity and Lease Seasoning. Lenders underwriting to stabilized NOI on a property that has been in Chapter 11 will ask hard questions about tenant retention. In a multi-tenant flex-industrial property, the bankruptcy filing itself can trigger landlord default clauses in tenant leases, leading to early vacancies or renegotiated rents that reduce actual cash flow below the pre-petition rent roll. If any tenant's lease was assumed and cured through the bankruptcy plan, lenders need to review the cure order and understand whether any lease modifications were made as part of assumption. Lenders will typically want to see at least six to twelve months of post-confirmation income history before committing to permanent financing, which creates a timing gap that must be addressed in the plan structure, often through a DIP-to-permanent bridge facility or a short-term balance sheet loan from a non-bank lender.

Title and Lien Release Complexity. When a first-mortgage loan closes on a property exiting bankruptcy, title insurance underwriters will require the bankruptcy court's confirmation order, the discharge of any avoided liens, and in many cases a supplemental title search going back to the original petition date. Title insurers in Iowa have become more sophisticated about bankruptcy-exit transactions, but there are still situations where mechanic's lien claims filed during the bankruptcy period, or unresolved state tax authority liens, create endorsement exceptions that conventional lenders will not accept. This is not simply a legal question for debtor's counsel. It requires coordination between the financing expert, title counsel, and the lender's credit team well in advance of any plan confirmation hearing, because the court needs to know that financing is feasible before it confirms the plan, not after.

Borrower Entity Structure Post-Emergence. After confirmation, who is the borrower on the new first mortgage? If the reorganized debtor is a single-purpose entity with no post-emergence operating history, lenders will require guarantees, either from the principal equity holder or from a creditworthy co-borrower. In Iowa, personal guarantee requirements are standard for most community bank and regional bank commercial mortgage loans regardless of entity structure. Non-bank lenders and CMBS conduits are more flexible on recourse, but CMBS presents its own complexity in a bankruptcy-exit context because of the need for a clean "non-consolidation" legal opinion and the elimination of any pending claims that could cloud the securitization trust's security interest.

The Cedar Rapids Lender Market

Community and Regional Banks. The Iowa banking market is among the most community-bank-dense in the country, and Cedar Rapids benefits from that depth. Multiple Linn County-based and statewide Iowa community banks actively originate commercial mortgage loans in the $1 million to $10 million range on industrial, multifamily, retail, and mixed-use assets. These institutions know the local market, understand Linn County property tax assessment practices, and are often more willing to underwrite to local market knowledge rather than strictly algorithmic credit models. For bankruptcy-exit financing, the challenge is that community banks typically require personal guarantees, have internal legal lending limits that may constrain loan size on larger assets, and carry more conservative LTV ceilings (often 65%) than non-bank alternatives. When the post-confirmation borrower can demonstrate clean credit history and provide a meaningful guarantee, community banks in Cedar Rapids are a viable and often pricing-competitive source of exit financing.

USDA Business and Industry Loan Program. Iowa is one of the strongest states in the country for USDA Business and Industry guaranteed lending, and Cedar Rapids borrowers have access to this program for qualifying commercial real estate assets, particularly those with job retention or creation components. The USDA B&I program provides a federal guarantee on loans originated by approved commercial lenders, which can meaningfully reduce the lender's credit risk and expand financing availability to borrowers who would not otherwise qualify for conventional terms. For a bankruptcy-exit scenario, this program requires careful timing and pre-application engagement, but it can be a legitimate financing pathway that belongs in a feasibility analysis.

Debt Funds and Non-Bank Bridge Lenders. National debt funds and non-bank commercial mortgage lenders are increasingly active in Cedar Rapids for assets in the $2 million to $15 million range, particularly for value-add, transitional, or complexity-laden transactions where conventional lenders step back. These lenders price at spreads above conventional bank rates, typically in the 8.5% to 10.5% range for bridge or short-term first-mortgage facilities, but they have broader credit boxes and genuine experience with bankruptcy-exit collateral. For a plan of reorganization that requires exit financing commitment as a condition of confirmation, a debt fund term sheet may be the most achievable near-term solution, with a refinance to conventional permanent financing projected at 12 to 24 months post-emergence once operating history is established. CLS CRE maintains relationships with lenders in this category who have originated loans on Iowa commercial properties.

Life Companies and CMBS Conduits. Life insurance companies and CMBS conduits are generally not the right primary lender category for Cedar Rapids bankruptcy-exit financing in the sub-$5 million loan range. Life companies focus on larger loan sizes and prefer pristine title and borrower credit profiles. CMBS conduits can originate on Cedar Rapids industrial product, but their minimum loan sizes (typically $2 million to $3 million at the absolute floor), non-recourse structure requirements, and securitization-driven documentation standards make them unlikely sources for complexity-laden bankruptcy-exit transactions unless the asset and loan size are at the higher end of the feasibility range.

What Attorneys Handling Cedar Rapids CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to Retain a CRE Financing Expert. Counsel handling a Chapter 11 plan confirmation proceeding involving commercial real estate should retain a CRE financing expert the moment plan feasibility under Section 1129(a)(11) becomes a contested issue. If a creditor or competing plan proponent is arguing that exit financing is unavailable or insufficient to satisfy senior claims, or if the court needs an independent analysis of whether the projected refinance is realistic, that is the specific evidentiary gap that a CRE financing expert fills. An appraiser establishes value. A CRE financing expert establishes whether a lender will actually write a check, and at what amount, on those terms, in that market, for that borrower profile.

What a Financing Expert Can Testify to That an Appraiser Cannot. An appraiser is trained to opine on value. A qualified CRE financing expert can testify to lender market conditions and active lender appetite for a specific asset type and complexity level, underwriting standards including DSCR thresholds, LTV ceilings, and rate assumptions, the composition of the lender market and which categories of lenders will and will not engage with bankruptcy-exit collateral, the feasibility of a specific proposed loan amount given market conditions, and the reasonableness of a plan's projected financing timeline. These are financing opinions, not valuation opinions, and they require active market participation to deliver credibly.

What Qualifies an Expert in This Field. A retired banker who underwrote loans twenty years ago is not the same as an active commercial mortgage broker with current lender relationships and recent closing history. An expert who can testify about what lenders are actually doing today, which specific lender categories are open to bankruptcy-exit collateral, and what the current pricing and structure look like, is far more useful than someone offering historical perspective. At CLS CRE, our active origination platform spanning approximately $1 billion in transaction volume and relationships with more than 1,000 lenders in 50 states provides the market knowledge base that supports credible, current expert testimony.

Information Counsel Should Gather Before the Expert Engagement. To produce a useful financing feasibility opinion efficiently, counsel should assemble the current rent roll with lease terms and expiration dates, the trailing 12-month operating statement, any existing appraisal or broker opinion of value, the current title commitment or preliminary title report, a description of any pending or unresolved claims that could affect title, the proposed plan term sheet or draft plan showing the proposed treatment of the first-mortgage creditor, and any existing loan documents including any modifications made during the bankruptcy. The earlier CLS CRE has access to this information, the earlier we can identify complicating factors that may require additional analysis or mitigation strategy before the plan confirmation hearing.

Key Takeaways

  • Cedar Rapids is a financeable market. The metro's industrial fundamentals, community bank depth, and economic diversification make it a market where well-structured bankruptcy-exit financing is achievable for quality assets, but the analysis must be specific to asset type and submarket conditions, not generic.
  • DSCR and LTV must both support the loan. A financing feasibility analysis that satisfies only one constraint is incomplete. Courts should expect both income-based and value-based ceilings to be analyzed and for the expert to explain what happens when they diverge.
  • Conventional bank lenders are not the only option. Debt funds, USDA B&I guarantees, and non-bank bridge lenders extend the financing universe meaningfully for bankruptcy-exit transactions where conventional lenders have reduced appetite.
  • Timing matters for plan structure. If permanent financing requires post-emergence operating history, the plan must account for an interim financing period, which must itself be shown to be feasible. A plan that assumes permanent financing at confirmation may be unrealistic for collateral with unresolved occupancy or title complexity.
  • Title and lien resolution are pre-conditions to lender commitment. No institutional lender will issue a firm commitment on bankruptcy-exit collateral until title issues are resolved. Counsel should not wait until after confirmation to address title defects; they should be identified and resolved as part of plan preparation.

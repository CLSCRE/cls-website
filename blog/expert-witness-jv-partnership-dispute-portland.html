Expert Witness: CRE Partnership and Waterfall Disputes in Portland, OR

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Joint Venture and Partnership Dispute

The Portland Market Context

Portland, Oregon occupies a distinctive position in the Pacific Northwest commercial real estate landscape, one that creates fertile conditions for joint venture disputes precisely because the market has moved in multiple directions simultaneously across different asset classes. Understanding those dynamics is not background noise in a waterfall or promoted interest dispute; it is directly material to how a tribunal should evaluate whether distributions were correctly calculated and whether the financing structure performed as the parties originally anticipated.

Portland's multifamily market has been among the most closely watched in the West. Deliveries of new apartment product accelerated significantly between 2019 and 2023, pushing vacancy rates in submarkets like the Pearl District, Lloyd District, and close-in Northeast Portland above the 6 to 8 percent range at various points, softening effective rents and compressing net operating income relative to proforma projections. For a joint venture structured during a lower-vacancy environment, that NOI compression directly affects whether a preferred return was achievable from operations, and whether a promote became payable at all. Cap rate expansion in the 5.25 to 5.75 percent range for stabilized Class A multifamily in Portland by mid-2024 also affects the exit valuation assumptions embedded in most waterfall hurdles.

Industrial is the opposite story. Portland's industrial market, anchored by the Airport Way corridor, the Swan Island submarket, and proximity to Port of Portland infrastructure, has maintained vacancy rates below 4 percent across most of the post-pandemic cycle. Rents on Class B industrial space pushed from the low $0.70s per square foot per month NNN to above $0.90 in many submarkets. For a joint venture formed around an industrial acquisition or value-add repositioning, that rent growth almost certainly generated NOI above proforma, which in turn raises the question of whether the sponsor correctly calculated the preferred return hurdle and the split above it.

Office is its own category of complexity. Portland's CBD office market absorbed significant distress, with Class A direct vacancy exceeding 25 percent in some reporting periods, sublease availability compounding that figure, and effective rents declining materially from 2020 peak levels. A joint venture formed around a value-add office play would likely show NOI substantially below proforma, and the dispute may center on whether the GP triggered a capital call correctly, whether a forced refinancing or sale was justified under the operating agreement, and whether the waterfall mechanics apply when distributable cash is insufficient to satisfy the preferred return in full.

Oregon's statewide rent control framework under HB 2001 and subsequent legislation, combined with Portland's local tenant protection ordinances, creates an additional layer of NOI predictability risk in multifamily joint ventures that is specific to this market and that must be analyzed as part of any expert opinion on whether cash flows were correctly characterized and distributed.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing the limited partner in a joint venture dispute involving a value-add multifamily asset in a close-in Portland submarket. The general partner, acting as the operating member and development sponsor, had managed a repositioning program over approximately three years, refinanced the asset during the hold period, and subsequently distributed proceeds from the refinancing to the members. The limited partner contended that the refinancing proceeds were distributable cash subject to the waterfall, that the preferred return had accrued to a specific dollar amount prior to the refinancing, and that the GP's distribution calculation failed to apply the correct preferred return balance before paying itself a promoted interest.

Counsel needed expert analysis across several intersecting questions. First, was the capital stack structure at acquisition market-standard for this asset type and vintage in Portland, and did the senior loan terms affect the amount of proceeds available for distribution? Second, was the preferred return calculation methodology the GP used consistent with market-standard JV agreement drafting, or did it deviate in ways that benefited the GP at the LP's expense? Third, when the asset was refinanced, what were the realistic financing parameters available in the Portland market at that time, and did the GP maximize proceeds in a manner consistent with its fiduciary and contractual obligations? Fourth, did the cash-on-cash return calculations supporting the GP's promote claim use gross proceeds or net proceeds, and which treatment is market-standard?

CLS CRE was engaged to produce an expert report under Rule 26 addressing each of these questions with specific reference to market conditions, lender underwriting standards, and capital stack conventions applicable to Portland multifamily transactions of this scale and vintage.

The Financial Analysis

The subject property was a 72-unit multifamily asset in a Portland infill location. At acquisition, the JV structured the deal with a senior acquisition loan, a preferred equity position held by the LP, and a GP co-invest. The operating agreement specified an 8 percent cumulative preferred return on the LP's contributed capital, compounding annually, with a catch-up provision and a 70/30 LP/GP split above the preferred return hurdle before reaching the promote tier.

The first analytical task was reconstructing the NOI at the time of the refinancing and testing whether the refinancing proceeds were correctly sized. Using actual rent rolls provided in discovery, we established a stabilized gross potential rent of approximately $1.26 million annually, with a vacancy and credit loss assumption of 7 percent reflecting Portland market conditions at that time, yielding effective gross income of approximately $1.17 million. Operating expenses, including property taxes under Oregon's assessment framework, insurance, management fees, and reserves, totaled approximately $468,000, producing a stabilized NOI of approximately $702,000.

Market-standard DSCR thresholds for agency-eligible multifamily in Portland during the refinancing period ran 1.25x at most agency executions, with some bridge lenders tolerating 1.20x on stabilized assets. Applying a 1.25x DSCR constraint against the $702,000 NOI, the maximum annual debt service supportable was $561,600. At the interest rate environment prevailing at the time of the refinancing, a 30-year amortization at 5.875 percent produces a monthly constant of approximately 0.00709, or an annual constant of approximately 8.51 percent. Maximum loan supportable by NOI at 1.25x DSCR: $561,600 divided by 0.0851, yielding approximately $6.6 million.

LTV constraints for agency product on Portland multifamily in the relevant period typically capped at 75 percent. An appraised value of approximately $9.2 million at the time of refinancing would have supported a maximum loan of $6.9 million at 75 percent LTV. The binding constraint in this case was the DSCR, not LTV, indicating the refinancing was appropriately sized by the GP. However, the dispute was not about loan sizing; it was about what happened to the $2.1 million of net refinancing proceeds after payoff of the acquisition loan.

The accrued preferred return calculation was central. The LP contributed $2.4 million at closing. Over a 31-month hold to the date of refinancing, at an 8 percent cumulative compounding preferred return, the accrued preferred return balance was approximately $527,000, for a total preferred return balance of $2,927,000. The GP's distribution calculation applied only a simple (non-compounding) preferred return calculation, reducing the LP's priority claim by approximately $61,000. That $61,000 discrepancy was then swept into the promote calculation, effectively transferring value from the LP to the GP. This analysis formed the core of our expert opinion on the distribution error.

The Complicating Factors

Ambiguity in the definition of "distributable cash" following a refinancing event. Many JV operating agreements distinguish between operating distributions and capital event distributions but use imprecise language about whether refinancing proceeds constitute a capital event triggering the full waterfall or merely a return of capital subject to a different priority structure. In Portland transactions structured before 2020, this ambiguity was common because many sponsors used template operating agreements that were not specifically tailored to refinancing scenarios. Expert analysis of what "market-standard" means in this context is not a legal opinion; it is a factual opinion about how comparable JV agreements in the Pacific Northwest CRE market are typically drafted and what the financing industry expects when a sponsor refinances an asset during a hold period.

Oregon-specific tax and assessment impacts on NOI calculations. Oregon's property tax system imposes Measure 50 limitations on annual assessed value growth, which can create a significant divergence between assessed value and market value, particularly on repositioned assets. When a property undergoes renovation and triggers reassessment, the NOI impact of a step-up in property taxes is material. In a JV dispute, if the GP failed to account for a post-renovation reassessment in its NOI projections used to calculate the promote, that error cascades through the waterfall. Expert analysis must trace the actual tax assessment history and reconcile it against what was projected in the underwriting model.

The timing of the preferred return accrual relative to capital contributions. If the LP contributed capital in tranches, as is common in value-add deals where equity is drawn for renovation costs, the preferred return accrues only from the date each tranche is funded. We have seen GP-prepared waterfall calculations that incorrectly applied the full preferred return rate to the full LP commitment from day one rather than to each tranche from its actual funding date, which overstates the LP's preferred return. The reverse error, understating the preferred return by applying it only to the initial tranche and not to subsequent draws, is equally common. Reconstructing the actual contribution timeline from bank records and capital call notices is foundational work in this type of engagement.

Bridge-to-permanent financing structures and their effect on promote timing. Many Portland value-add deals are structured with a bridge loan at acquisition and a permanent takeout at stabilization. The bridge loan covenants may restrict distributions during the bridge period, which affects when the preferred return clock runs and whether compounding was actually possible given the cash flow restrictions. If the GP argues that the preferred return should not compound because distributions were contractually restricted by the lender, that argument must be tested against the actual loan documents, which CLS CRE reviews as part of the engagement.

The Portland Lender Market

Portland's lender universe for multifamily and mixed-use commercial product is reasonably deep but has shifted meaningfully since 2022. Understanding which lenders were active, at what terms, and with what constraints during the specific period at issue in a dispute is essential expert work because the lender market conditions define the feasibility of alternative financing structures the parties may have had available to them.

Agency lenders (Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, FHA/HUD). Both GSE programs maintain active DUS lender relationships in Portland. Fannie's conventional multifamily program and Freddie's Optigo platform are the primary permanent financing vehicles for stabilized multifamily above 50 units. Typical agency parameters in Portland during the 2022 to 2024 period: LTV up to 75 percent, DSCR floor of 1.25x, 30-year amortization, 7 to 10-year fixed term, prepayment via yield maintenance or defeasance. Agency pricing in Portland has generally tracked 150 to 200 basis points over the corresponding Treasury, which in 2023 to 2024 produced all-in rates in the 5.75 to 6.50 percent range depending on sponsorship quality and asset class.

Life insurance companies. Several life company correspondents are active in Portland for core and core-plus multifamily and industrial. Life company executions typically require lower LTV (65 to 70 percent), stronger sponsorship, and longer hold periods, but price tighter than agency on a spread basis. For a value-add deal in mid-repositioning, life companies are generally not available until the asset reaches stabilization thresholds of 90 percent occupancy for 90 days or longer.

Regional and community banks. Portland has historically had a strong regional bank presence, with Pacific Northwest institutions active in construction lending, bridge lending, and smaller permanent loans. Many of these lenders pulled back significantly in 2023 as CRE concentrations on bank balance sheets drew regulatory scrutiny. For deals below $5 million, community bank product remains available but with recourse requirements, shorter amortization (25 years), and more conservative DSCR floors (1.30x or higher).

Debt funds and bridge lenders. For transitional assets, value-add repositioning, or situations where agency or bank product is unavailable due to occupancy or lease-up status, debt funds fill the gap. Portland bridge lending in 2023 to 2024 generally priced at SOFR plus 300 to 450 basis points, with LTV up to 75 to 80 percent on total cost, interest-only structures, and 24 to 36-month initial terms with extension options. These lenders are more flexible on occupancy and sponsorship credit but impose extension test requirements that can create additional disputes in a JV context.

What Attorneys Handling Portland CRE Disputes Need to Know

When should you retain a CRE financing expert? The threshold question is whether the financing structure, the capital stack, or the lender market conditions are in dispute or affect the quantification of damages. If a client argues that a preferred return was miscalculated, that a refinancing was improperly executed, that a capital call was unjustified, or that a forced sale resulted from a financing failure that should not have occurred, you need an expert who can speak to what the financing market actually allowed at that time and place, not just what the operating agreement said.

What can a CRE financing expert answer that an appraiser cannot? An appraiser values the real property. A CRE financing expert analyzes the debt structure sitting on top of that property and the economics of the capital stack. These are entirely different disciplines. An appraiser cannot tell a jury what DSCR a lender would have required in Portland in Q3 2022, what loan covenants typically restrict during a bridge loan term, what market-standard promote structures look like in Pacific Northwest value-add deals, or whether the financing the GP obtained was consistent with what a reasonably competent sponsor could have obtained in the market at that time. Those are capital markets questions, not valuation questions.

What qualifies someone as a CRE financing expert as opposed to a retired banker? Qualification requires active transactional experience placing commercial real estate debt in the current market, relationships across multiple lender categories (bank, agency, life company, debt fund), demonstrated familiarity with JV capital stack structures and operating agreement economics, and the ability to reconstruct underwriting analyses using methodology consistent with current market practice. A retired banker may have historical knowledge but cannot speak to what the market would have done in a specific period if they have been out of active deal flow. At CLS CRE, our team has closed approximately $1 billion in transaction volume with over 1,000 active lender relationships across all 50 states, which means our market knowledge is current, not archival.

What should counsel gather before the expert engagement begins? At minimum: the operating agreement and all amendments; all capital call notices and LP contribution records with dates and amounts; the acquisition loan documents; any refinancing loan documents; property-level financial statements for the entire hold period; rent rolls at acquisition, at each major capital event, and at disposition or refinancing; the GP's waterfall calculation workbooks; any appraisals commissioned during the hold; and all communications between the GP and lenders. The more complete the document set, the more specific and defensible the expert opinion will be.

How is CRE financing expert testimony used at trial or in an arbitration hearing? Our opinions typically address three categories: (1) market standard, meaning what comparable deals in this market at this time looked like in terms of capital structure, loan terms, and distribution mechanics; (2) calculation methodology, meaning whether the GP applied a recognized and market-consistent method to compute preferred returns, promotes, and distributable cash; and (3) feasibility, meaning whether the GP had access to financing alternatives that would have produced a different outcome for the LP, and whether it acted consistently with market-standard sponsor conduct in structuring and executing the financing.

Key Takeaways

  • Portland's asset-class bifurcation matters to the dispute. Whether the JV asset was industrial, multifamily, or office determines the NOI trajectory during the hold period and whether the preferred return was achievable from operations, which is often the threshold question in a waterfall dispute.
  • Preferred return calculation methodology is not standardized across all JV agreements, and the difference between simple and compounding preferred return treatment, or between actual-contribution-date accrual and full-commitment accrual, can produce material dollar discrepancies that favor one party at the expense of the other.

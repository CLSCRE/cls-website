Case Study: $2.9M Industrial Refinance in Long Beach at 5.50% Fixed With 18 Months Interest Only

Trevor Damyan Founder & Principal Broker
CA DRE #02244836

Former CBRE and Marcus & Millichap Capital Corporation. $1B+ in closed commercial real estate financing across bridge, construction, permanent, and structured debt.

July 25, 2026
Quick answer: Commercial Lending Solutions refinanced a leased industrial warehouse and office building in Long Beach, California with a $2,900,000 loan from a local community bank at 5.50% fixed. The structure: a 5-year term on a 25-year amortization with the first 18 months interest only, a 0.5% loan fee, and a 3-2-0 prepayment schedule that goes fully open after year two. Tenant rollover risk was handled with a lease reserve covenant instead of a proceeds cut.

The Deal That Walked in the Door

We are Commercial Lending Solutions, a commercial mortgage brokerage based in Los Angeles, California (CA), and this client was practically a neighbor: a longtime Long Beach property owner, a family trust that had held the building for years, refinancing an industrial warehouse and office property leased to two established commercial tenants, an engineering firm and a specialty manufacturer. The asset was a classic Long Beach infill industrial story: functional building, land-constrained submarket twenty minutes from the ports of Los Angeles and Long Beach, tenants who had been in place for years. The eventual takeout came from a local community bank.

Commercial Lending Solutions specializes in industrial refinancing and community bank placements for owners across Los Angeles County and Long Beach, and this deal was a direct fit.

The goal was straightforward, replace the maturing debt with the sharpest fixed rate available and keep monthly payments as light as possible. Straightforward goals still deserve a real process. We built a formal side-by-side comparison across the lender field in mid-2025, kept it live as quotes were refreshed, and the spreads that emerged were meaningful, in rate, in structure, and above all in how each lender treated the one soft spot in the deal: both tenant leases rolled during the proposed loan term.

What Made It Hard

  • Lease rollover inside the loan term. Both tenants' leases expired before the 5-year maturity. Many lenders respond to rollover by cutting proceeds or shortening the term.
  • Payment sensitivity. The owner wanted the low fixed rate of a bank loan but also wanted early-years cash flow closer to what an interest-only structure provides.
  • Rate environment. With most banks quoting 6%+ for industrial refis, getting into the 5s required a lender that knew the submarket and wanted the relationship.

How We Ran the Process

Trevor Damyan, a California-licensed real estate broker (DRE #02244836), ran the finalists down to a community bank headquartered in the property's own city, an institution that has been lending on Long Beach real estate for over a century, against a New York-headquartered bank's term sheet. The out-of-market bank quoted wider and treated the lease rollover as a proceeds problem. The hometown bank treated it as a covenant problem, which is a much cheaper place to solve it. Their proposal, first issued in late March 2026 and executed in revised form on April 7, won on every axis that mattered:

  • $2,900,000 at 5.50% fixed for the full 5-year term, with a 0.5% loan fee ($14,500)
  • 18 months of interest-only payments, then principal and interest of roughly $17,950 per month on a 25-year amortization
  • Conservative sizing at the lesser of 55% of appraised value or a 1.45x debt service coverage test, the trade that buys a 5-handle rate
  • 3-2-0 prepayment, completely open after year two, preserving a sale or refinance exit for most of the term
  • Rollover handled with a reserve covenant, not a proceeds cut: if either tenant's lease is not renewed by three months before expiration, the borrower funds a reserve account equal to six months of that tenant's rent, released once a renewal or replacement lease is signed. Full proceeds at closing, protection for the bank only if the risk actually materializes.
  • Relationship banking: the borrower moved the property's operating accounts to the bank, standard for community bank pricing at this level

The loan closed in Q2 2026, secured by a first deed of trust on the property.

"The lease rollover was the whole negotiation. Instead of letting a lender cut proceeds for a risk that had not happened yet, we structured a reserve that only comes into play if a tenant actually walks."
, Trevor Damyan, Commercial Lending Solutions

Why We Won This One

The lease-rollover reserve is the detail worth stealing. When a lender sees tenant expirations inside the loan term, the lazy answers are lower proceeds, a shorter term, or a higher rate, all of which make the borrower pay today for a risk that may never show up. A springing reserve flips that: the borrower keeps full proceeds and the sharp rate, and only posts collateral if a lease actually goes unrenewed. If your lender responds to rollover risk by cutting your loan, ask about a reserve structure instead, or find a lender who will.

The other lesson is that community banks beat money-center pricing on their home turf. A bank that has financed buildings on the same streets for decades does not need a risk premium to get comfortable with Long Beach infill industrial. The out-of-market competitor quoted the same deal wider.

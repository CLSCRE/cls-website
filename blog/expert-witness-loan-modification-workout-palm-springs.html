Expert Witness: Commercial Loan Modification Standards in Palm Springs, CA

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Loan Modification and Workout Dispute

The Palm Springs Market Context

Palm Springs and the broader Coachella Valley present a commercial real estate landscape that is genuinely unlike most California submarkets. The market is driven by a hospitality-first economy, heavy seasonal demand cycles, a concentrated high-net-worth residential base, and a commercial inventory that skews toward boutique hospitality assets, mixed-use retail, and smaller multifamily projects rather than the institutional-grade office and industrial product that defines Los Angeles or the Inland Empire. When a commercial loan modification or workout dispute arises here, the market context is not incidental to the analysis. It is central to it.

Retail vacancy in the Palm Springs urban core and nearby Cathedral City and Palm Desert submarkets has remained relatively stable in recent years, running in the 6 to 9 percent range depending on corridor, with strong demand for food-and-beverage and lifestyle-oriented tenancy. However, that stability masks a critical vulnerability: tenant sales volumes are intensely seasonal, with Q1 (January through March) representing the peak revenue period and summer months routinely producing materially lower foot traffic and receipts. This seasonality does not appear on a trailing twelve-month rent roll in a way that is immediately obvious, but it directly affects a borrower's ability to service debt obligations uniformly across the calendar year, and it is precisely the kind of factor that a lender's workout team should have modeled before imposing modification terms.

Multifamily cap rates in the Coachella Valley have historically traded in the 5.0 to 6.5 percent range for stabilized assets, with some compression occurring during the 2021 to 2022 interest rate trough. More recently, as the rate environment shifted, owners of smaller apartment and condominium-style rental properties found their debt service coverage ratios deteriorating rapidly, a dynamic that produced a meaningful uptick in workout and modification requests to local and regional lenders beginning in 2022 and continuing into 2024. That is the environment in which many of the disputes now working their way through litigation were born.

Hospitality-adjacent commercial assets, including retail on hotel corridors, mixed-use projects with short-term rental components, and wellness or spa-oriented commercial spaces, carry additional complexity because their income streams are neither purely hospitality nor purely retail in how underwriters evaluate them. Lenders who failed to understand this hybrid nature when structuring or later modifying loans created documentation gaps and analytical errors that form the factual foundation of many of the disputes I have been retained to analyze.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing the borrower in a commercial loan modification dispute involving a mixed-use retail and office asset in the Palm Springs urban core. The property had been performing within acceptable parameters prior to the COVID-19 disruption period, after which the borrower entered into workout discussions with the lender. The core of the dispute was whether the lender's conduct throughout the modification process, including the terms ultimately imposed and the timeline over which the lender acted, met industry standards of care for commercial mortgage workouts.

Counsel needed answers to three distinct but interconnected questions. First, were the modification terms the lender offered commercially reasonable given the property's actual cash flow and the market conditions that existed at the relevant time? Second, did the lender follow standard commercial real estate workout practices in terms of process, documentation, and responsiveness, or did its conduct deviate from what a reasonable commercial lender would do in analogous circumstances? Third, could the borrower, given the property's income characteristics and the lender market conditions at the time, have realistically accessed alternative financing to retire the loan, and did the lender's delay in processing the workout request meaningfully impair that access?

CLS CRE's role was to provide a Rule 26-compliant expert report analyzing each of these questions with specific financial modeling, market data, and comparisons to industry-standard workout practice. We were not retained to opine on legal conclusions. Our scope was the financing and credit analysis that underpins those legal conclusions.

The Financial Analysis

The foundational task in any loan modification dispute is reconstructing what the property's economics actually showed at the time the modification was requested, not at the time of litigation. Lenders and borrowers frequently dispute this baseline, because it determines everything: whether the property was viable enough to warrant a modification, what terms were supportable, and whether the lender's proposed modification was calibrated to the actual situation or to something else entirely.

We began with a trailing twelve-month and trailing twenty-four-month income and expense analysis drawn from the borrower's actual operating statements, the lender's own internal credit files (obtained through discovery), and independent market data for comparable Palm Springs retail and office assets. The subject property generated gross rental income of approximately $680,000 annually at the time of the modification request, with documented vacancy of approximately 18 percent by square footage, concentrated in the second-floor office component. After operating expenses, the net operating income available to service debt was approximately $398,000.

Applying the DSCR threshold that governs conventional commercial real estate lending for this asset type, which is 1.25x for mixed-use commercial assets in most institutional and bank credit boxes, the maximum supportable annual debt service on a stabilized basis was approximately $318,400. Expressed as a monthly payment, that is approximately $26,533. At a 7.25 percent interest rate on a 25-year amortization schedule, consistent with market pricing for this asset type and credit quality during the relevant period, the maximum supportable loan balance was approximately $3,540,000.

The existing loan balance at the time of the modification request was approximately $4,100,000. This created an immediate structural problem: the property, as-is with existing vacancy, could not support the existing debt at market interest rates under any standard underwriting framework. The property was approximately $560,000 over-leveraged relative to its current income capacity. This is not an unusual situation in workout practice, and experienced commercial lenders have well-established protocols for handling it. The question in this dispute was whether the lender applied those protocols appropriately.

The as-stabilized analysis told a different story. If the second-floor office vacancy, which was the primary driver of income impairment, was absorbed at market rents for comparable Palm Springs mixed-use office space (approximately $28 to $32 per square foot annually on a modified gross basis), the projected stabilized NOI increased to approximately $512,000. At the same 1.25x DSCR and 7.25 percent rate, the stabilized supportable loan balance increased to approximately $4,550,000, which exceeded the outstanding balance. The property had a viable path to debt coverage. It simply required time to execute on leasing and, critically, a lender willing to provide a structured forbearance or interest reserve to bridge the stabilization period.

The lender's proposed modification terms required full debt service payments beginning within 60 days, which the financial analysis demonstrated was impossible to meet given the actual cash flow. That mismatch between what the property could support and what the lender demanded is a central issue in the litigation.

The Complicating Factors

Several factors specific to the Palm Springs market and this asset type complicated both the workout analysis and the assessment of the lender's conduct.

Seasonal income volatility: As noted above, Palm Springs commercial properties frequently produce materially uneven monthly income. A borrower's ability to service a flat monthly debt obligation is genuinely more constrained in summer months than in peak season. Standard commercial workout practice for this market should involve averaging income analysis across a full calendar year, not penalizing a borrower for a cash flow dip that is predictable and market-consistent. The lender in this matter applied a single-month snapshot taken in July to characterize the property's performance, a methodologically indefensible approach that any competent commercial lender in a seasonally driven market should know is unreliable.

Mixed-use underwriting complexity: Properties that combine retail and office components in a single structure present underwriting challenges because different lender categories apply different credit standards to each component. Retail cash flows are capitalized differently than office cash flows, and a blended NOI that does not segregate the two can be misevaluated by lenders who specialize in one category but not the other. In this dispute, the lender's internal credit file applied a uniform retail capitalization framework to what was partly an office income stream, understating the stabilized value and mischaracterizing the leasing risk profile.

Market disruption documentation: The COVID-19 disruption period created documented, involuntary income impairment across virtually every Palm Springs commercial property. Industry-standard workout practice during that period recognized force majeure-adjacent circumstances and required lenders to evaluate income impairment in context. Failure to contextualize the income disruption within the documented market environment is a deviation from reasonable workout practice that I documented in my expert report with reference to guidance issued by federal banking regulators encouraging flexible loan accommodations during the disruption period.

Lender delay and its consequences: The lender took approximately nine months to respond substantively to the borrower's modification request. During that period, the borrower incurred additional default interest, legal fees, and reputational harm with existing tenants who were receiving demand letters from the lender's counsel. The delay also narrowed the borrower's refinancing options: a lender willing to provide takeout financing on a distressed-but-stabilizing asset in 2022 was far more accessible than one in 2023 as the rate environment deteriorated. Delays in workout processing that impair a borrower's ability to cure are a recognized form of lender misconduct in commercial real estate practice, and quantifying the economic harm of that delay is a core function of the expert financial analysis.

The Palm Springs Lender Market

Understanding which lenders are actually active in Palm Springs for commercial assets of this type and complexity is essential context for any modification dispute analysis. The lender market here is narrower than in Los Angeles or San Diego, and that narrowness has direct implications for both the reasonableness of the lender's conduct and the availability of alternatives.

Community and regional banks: Several regional banks with California desert presence have been the primary source of commercial real estate lending for Palm Springs mixed-use assets in the $2 million to $10 million range. These institutions typically require 1.20x to 1.25x DSCR, 65 to 70 percent LTV, and personal recourse or partial recourse. Their credit boxes are conservative, and their workout departments are modestly staffed, which helps explain why modification timelines in this market tend to run longer than in major metros. These lenders generally maintain a relationship-driven approach, which creates both opportunities for negotiated workouts and risks of inconsistent documentation.

Credit unions with CRE capabilities: Several larger California credit unions have expanded their commercial real estate programs into the Coachella Valley and represent a meaningful alternative source for mixed-use assets in the $1.5 million to $6 million range. Their pricing tends to be slightly more aggressive than community banks, and their modification processes, while less formalized, can sometimes be more flexible.

Debt funds and bridge lenders: For stabilizing assets or borrowers who have exhausted community bank options, private debt funds represent the next tier of capital. Palm Springs bridge lending typically prices at SOFR plus 400 to 600 basis points for mixed-use commercial assets, with origination fees of 1.5 to 2.5 points and 12 to 24 month terms. These lenders will advance to 65 to 70 percent of stabilized value on a business plan basis and are explicitly comfortable with the lease-up risk that conventional lenders avoid. Understanding that this market existed at the time of the dispute is relevant to whether the borrower had realistic refinancing alternatives and what they would have cost.

CMBS and agency lenders: CMBS execution is generally not available for Palm Springs mixed-use retail and office assets of the size involved in most local disputes. The minimum loan sizes, property type constraints, and market depth requirements of CMBS conduit programs effectively exclude most Coachella Valley commercial assets below $10 million. Any lender or expert who argues that CMBS was a realistic alternative for a sub-$5 million Palm Springs mixed-use asset is not applying credible market knowledge.

What Attorneys Handling Palm Springs CRE Disputes Need to Know

Attorneys engaged in commercial real estate litigation, whether in state court, federal court, or a bankruptcy proceeding, frequently rely on appraisers to establish value. That is appropriate and necessary. However, an appraiser's role ends at value. A CRE financing expert's role begins where the appraiser's ends: we analyze what the financing markets would have done with that value, and whether the parties in the dispute acted consistently with how sophisticated commercial real estate lenders and borrowers actually behave.

When to retain a CRE financing expert: In any commercial loan modification or workout dispute, counsel should consider retaining a financing expert when the central factual questions involve underwriting methodology, the commercial reasonableness of loan terms, lender conduct during the workout process, borrower access to alternative capital, or the calculation of damages resulting from lender delay or overreach. These are not questions an appraiser is trained to answer, and a retired banker without current active market experience may not have the market intelligence to credibly address them either.

What distinguishes a qualified CRE financing expert: The expert should have current, active deal experience, not just historical credentials. The commercial real estate lending market changes materially across rate cycles, and an expert whose last deal was closed five years ago may be applying stale underwriting assumptions. The expert should also have documented experience across multiple lender categories, because the question in many disputes is not just what one lender did, but what the broader market would have done in analogous circumstances. At CLS CRE, we maintain active relationships with more than 1,000 lenders across all categories and are currently active in 50 states, with approximately $1 billion in transaction volume providing the market breadth that credible expert testimony requires.

Information counsel should gather before the expert engagement: The most useful expert reports are built on complete discovery. Counsel should prioritize obtaining the lender's internal credit files, including all underwriting memoranda, credit approval documents, internal correspondence about the modification request, and any internal valuation or appraisal work. The borrower's complete operating statements for at least 24 months prior to and through the default period are equally important. Loan documents, all correspondence between the parties regarding the modification, and any third-party market data the lender relied upon should all be in hand before the expert begins substantive analysis.

How expert testimony is used: In a trial context, a CRE financing expert typically testifies to establish the industry standard of care for lender conduct in workout situations, to demonstrate whether specific loan modification terms were commercially reasonable, and to quantify damages arising from the lender's deviation from that standard. In a bankruptcy plan confirmation hearing, financing expert testimony is frequently used to establish feasibility of a proposed plan, to challenge or support a lender's credit bid, or to demonstrate that a plan's exit financing assumptions are achievable in the actual lender market. In mediation or arbitration, a credible expert report often drives settlement because it demonstrates to the opposing side that the financial analysis will withstand scrutiny.

Key Takeaways

  • Palm Springs commercial real estate income is inherently seasonal. Any lender underwriting or workout analysis that does not account for seasonal income variability is methodologically deficient, and that deficiency can and should be documented and challenged in litigation.
  • DSCR analysis must be applied to the correct income period and with appropriate market context. A single-month snapshot of cash flow, particularly from a summer month in a desert resort market, does not constitute a commercially reasonable basis for evaluating a borrower's ability to service debt or qualify for a modification.
  • Lender delay in the workout process causes quantifiable economic harm. Refinancing options that existed at one point in the rate cycle may not exist nine months later. A lender's failure to process a modification request within a reasonable timeframe is not a neutral act, and the resulting harm can be modeled and presented at trial.
  • Mixed-use assets require disaggregated income analysis. Applying a retail capitalization framework to office income, or vice versa, is a material underwriting error that understates stabilized value and distorts the coverage analysis. Identifying this error is one of the most common and impactful contributions a CRE financing expert makes in these disputes.
  • The Palm Springs lender market is narrower than major metro markets. This narrowness is relevant both to the assessment of alternative financing availability and to the standard of care applicable to a local or regional lender who operates in this market and should know its characteristics.
  • As-stabilized analysis is the appropriate framework when vacancy is the primary driver of income impairment. Lenders who refuse to engage with a stabilization business plan and insist only on as-is coverage may be applying an unreasonably rigid standard that deviates from common workout practice.

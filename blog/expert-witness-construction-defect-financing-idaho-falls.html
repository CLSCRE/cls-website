Expert Witness: How Construction Defects Affect CRE Financing in Idaho Falls, ID

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Construction Defect Impact on Financing

The Idaho Falls Market Context

Idaho Falls sits at a commercial real estate crossroads that most outside observers underestimate. The market has absorbed significant industrial and mixed-use development pressure over the past several years, driven by eastern Idaho's position as a regional logistics hub, the proximity to the Idaho National Laboratory complex, and sustained population growth in Bonneville County that has outpaced many comparable Intermountain West secondary markets. That growth creates real commercial demand, and with real demand comes real development, and with real development comes real construction defect exposure.

On the industrial side, Idaho Falls vacancy rates have remained compressed, generally tracking below 5% in functional warehouse and flex-industrial product, reflecting the market's role as a distribution and service hub for a broad geographic catchment area stretching into Wyoming and Montana. That tight vacancy has driven speculative development, and speculative development built quickly in a constrained labor and materials environment creates exactly the conditions where construction defect claims emerge. When a newly constructed industrial or flex facility carries a pending defect claim, the financing implications are immediate and severe, because the lenders who would otherwise be most competitive in that space, specifically regional banks and life company correspondents, have almost no tolerance for undisclosed or unresolved construction litigation.

Multifamily in Idaho Falls has seen meaningful cap rate compression from the high-6s into the low-to-mid-6s over the past three years, though 2023 and 2024 interest rate conditions created some softening in values for leveraged buyers. New multifamily construction has been active, with several garden-style and podium projects delivered in the west side and Ammon submarkets. Those projects are financed at ground-up construction loan proceeds and, once stabilized, are refinanced into permanent debt. When a construction defect claim surfaces during that stabilization-to-refinance window, the financing timeline and loan proceeds available to the borrower are directly and materially impacted. That impact is the core of what a CRE financing expert analyzes.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by counsel representing a defendant in a construction defect matter involving a newly constructed commercial property in the Idaho Falls market. The asset was a stabilized multifamily project that had recently completed its lease-up phase and was being positioned for a permanent takeout refinance from its original construction financing. CLS CRE was engaged to provide a Rule 26 expert report addressing three specific financial questions that had become central to the damages analysis in the litigation.

First, counsel needed an analysis of whether and to what extent the alleged construction defects, which included structural concerns affecting certain building envelope systems and deferred maintenance obligations arising from substandard work on common area mechanical systems, impaired the property's ability to qualify for institutional permanent financing. Second, counsel required a quantitative analysis comparing the maximum supportable loan the property could carry as a defect-free asset against the maximum supportable loan available given the disclosed defect conditions and the remediation cost uncertainty they created. Third, counsel needed an expert opinion on what categories of lenders, if any, would fund a loan on a property with active or recently resolved construction defect litigation, and at what pricing and structural terms, so that the financing cost differential could be expressed as a measurable damages component.

These are not questions an appraisal expert or a general contractor's damage estimator can answer. They require someone who actively places commercial mortgage debt in secondary and tertiary markets, understands how underwriters think, and can speak to lender credit policy with current, transaction-level authority.

The Financial Analysis

The methodology began with a stabilized net operating income analysis. The property's trailing 12-month operating statements were reviewed alongside the current rent roll, vacancy assumptions, and verified operating expenses. For a garden-style multifamily asset of this type and scale in Idaho Falls, effective gross income was established using market rents consistent with comparable projects in the Ammon and west Idaho Falls submarkets, applying a stabilized vacancy assumption of approximately 6%, which reflects both historical performance and current market conditions for comparable product. After subtracting verified operating expenses including management fees, maintenance reserves, insurance, property taxes, and a replacement reserve consistent with agency and bank underwriting standards (typically $250 to $350 per unit annually for properties of this vintage and construction class), the stabilized NOI used in the base-case analysis was established at approximately $620,000 annually.

The DSCR standard applied was 1.25x, which is the market-standard minimum for conventional bank permanent financing and agency-eligible multifamily product. Some community banks in secondary Idaho markets will underwrite to a 1.20x floor on stronger deals, but 1.25x is the appropriate standard for a property with any complexity or risk layering, and this property clearly carried risk layering given the pending defect claim. At 1.25x DSCR and using a 30-year amortization schedule with an assumed note rate of 6.75%, which reflects the approximate pricing available from regional and national bank lenders for Idaho Falls multifamily assets at the time of the analysis, the maximum annual debt service supportable by the property's NOI was $496,000. That debt service figure supports a maximum loan balance of approximately $6.55 million.

On the LTV side, the property's as-is appraised value for a defect-free asset of this type was established at approximately $9.8 million, implying a maximum LTV-constrained loan of $7.35 million at 75% LTV, which is the conventional maximum for this asset class with conventional bank financing. Under a defect-free scenario, the DSCR constraint of $6.55 million was the binding constraint, not the LTV ceiling. That is an important underwriting observation: it tells you the property is not over-leveraged on a value basis but is moderately constrained on a cash flow basis, which is typical for recently constructed Idaho Falls multifamily at current interest rates.

The construction defect scenario required a parallel analysis. The defects introduced two categories of financial impairment. The first was a remediation cost estimate, provided by the plaintiff's construction expert, of approximately $850,000 to address the structural envelope and mechanical deficiencies. Under lender underwriting standards, any known remediation obligation is treated as a reduction in collateral value and, where applicable, as a reserves holdback that reduces net loan proceeds. The second impairment was an income-side effect: the defects affecting common area mechanical systems were creating tenant retention risk and had already resulted in one unit becoming unleasable pending repairs, compressing effective occupancy and reducing the property's demonstrable NOI. Adjusting for both the reserves holdback that any responsible lender would require and the income impairment, the defect-scenario NOI dropped to approximately $574,000, and the lender-required remediation holdback of $900,000 (reflecting a standard 6% contingency load on the $850,000 estimate) was deducted from gross loan proceeds.

Under the defect scenario, the maximum supportable loan before the holdback was approximately $6.07 million. After the holdback, net loan proceeds to the borrower were approximately $5.17 million, compared to $6.55 million in the defect-free scenario. That is a financing gap of approximately $1.38 million, which directly quantifies the capital impairment caused by the defect condition independent of any appraisal-level value opinion. This is the kind of analysis that an appraiser cannot perform, because appraised value is a point-in-time market value conclusion, not a lender-proceeds analysis tied to underwriting criteria.

The Complicating Factors

Active Litigation as a Lender Disqualifier. The single most significant financing complication is not the defect itself but the existence of active litigation. Virtually every institutional lender, including agency lenders such as Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, life insurance companies, CMBS conduits, and most regional banks, will decline to originate a loan on a property that is the subject of pending construction defect litigation, regardless of the merits. This is not a credit quality judgment; it is a collateral eligibility judgment. Lenders do not want to hold a first lien on an asset whose title chain, physical condition, and future liability exposure are all in legal flux. In Idaho Falls, where the institutional lender presence is thinner than in Boise, this disqualification has outsized impact because the borrower cannot easily pivot to alternative capital sources without accepting materially worse terms.

Remediation Cost Uncertainty and Reserve Requirements. Even after litigation resolves, lenders require certainty about the scope and cost of remediation before they will fund permanent financing. If the remediation work is incomplete at the time of loan closing, lenders will require a reserves holdback equal to 100% to 110% of the estimated remediation cost, sometimes more if the scope is uncertain. That holdback reduces net proceeds to the borrower dollar for dollar, and it earns no meaningful interest return during the holdback period. If the remediation scope is contested, which it often is in active litigation, the lender may decline to underwrite at all until a court or settlement agreement establishes a binding remediation obligation with a defined scope and budget.

Insurance and Warranty Gaps. Construction defect claims frequently create gaps in the property's insurance and warranty coverage history that lenders scrutinize carefully. If the builder's risk policy has expired and the defects are of a type that general liability or completed operations coverage may not fully address, lenders will require confirmation of insurance adequacy before funding. In Idaho Falls, where smaller regional contractors are common on multifamily projects, the insurance depth on the responsible contractor may be limited, creating residual risk that lenders will either price into the spread or treat as a disqualifying condition.

Appraisal Complications and "As Repaired" Value Uncertainty. Lenders typically require an "as-is" appraisal and, where defects are present, an "as-repaired" appraisal reflecting the property's value upon completion of remediation. The spread between those two values establishes the lender's collateral ceiling during the remediation period. If the as-repaired value is still below the level necessary to support the borrower's loan request at an acceptable LTV, the borrower faces a structural equity gap that must be filled with additional cash, mezzanine financing, or a discounted loan payoff on the existing construction debt.

The Idaho Falls Lender Market

Idaho Falls does not have the deep institutional capital market presence of a Boise or a Salt Lake City. The lender universe for commercial assets in this market is dominated by regional and community banks, credit unions with commercial real estate programs, and occasionally USDA Business and Industry loan program lenders for qualifying asset types. For a stabilized multifamily asset of moderate size, agency executions through Fannie Mae's small loan program or Freddie Mac's small balance program are theoretically available, but in practice, those executions require clean title, no pending litigation, and a property condition report with no deferred maintenance flags, all of which are compromised by an active construction defect matter.

The community bank lender tier, which includes several Idaho-chartered banks with meaningful Idaho Falls commercial loan portfolios, can be more flexible on collateral complexity but typically price that flexibility into a higher spread over their cost of funds. These lenders may be willing to engage on a post-resolution defect situation, meaning after litigation has settled and remediation is either complete or bonded, but they will underwrite conservatively, require personal guarantees, limit LTV to 65% to 70%, and require DSCR coverage of at least 1.25x on the post-remediation income stream. Their loan committees are relationship-driven but not risk-blind.

Bridge lenders and private debt funds represent the last available financing tier for a property with unresolved defect exposure. These lenders will price the risk aggressively, typically at 300 to 500 basis points over SOFR with origination fees of 1 to 2 points, and they will impose strict remediation milestones as funding conditions. While bridge financing can preserve a borrower's ability to stabilize and eventually refinance, the all-in cost differential between a bridge execution and a conventional bank permanent loan on a defect-free property in Idaho Falls represents a quantifiable and ongoing damages component that expert analysis can express with precision.

What Attorneys Handling Idaho Falls CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to Retain a CRE Financing Expert. Counsel should retain a financing expert early, ideally before expert disclosure deadlines and certainly before any appraisal expert has issued a report that does not account for financing impairment. Appraisers establish market value. They do not establish what a lender will actually fund, on what terms, and under what conditions. Those are separate, technically distinct questions that require a different expert with a different methodology.

Questions Only a Financing Expert Can Answer. A CRE financing expert can quantify the actual loan proceeds lost due to a defect condition, establish the rate differential between conventional and bridge financing and express that differential as a present-value damages figure, opine on lender credit policy and whether a specific lender would have funded a specific loan on a specific asset in its defect-free condition, and explain why a borrower had no reasonable financing alternative to an expensive bridge execution given the state of the lender market at the relevant time.

What Makes a Financing Expert Qualified. A retired community banker from southeastern Idaho is not the same thing as a qualified CRE financing expert witness. Qualification requires current, active participation in the commercial mortgage market, documented transaction volume and lender relationships, experience with the specific asset class and market at issue, and the ability to present a methodology that survives Daubert scrutiny. At CLS CRE, our approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, 1,000-plus active lender relationships across 50 states, and ongoing active deal flow in secondary and tertiary markets including Idaho Falls ensure that our opinions reflect current, transaction-level market reality rather than historical general knowledge.

What to Gather Before the Expert Engagement. Counsel should collect the property's operating statements for the trailing 24 to 36 months, the current rent roll with lease expiration dates, any existing appraisals or broker opinions of value, the construction defect expert's scope-of-work and cost-of-remediation reports, any correspondence between the borrower and existing or prospective lenders that documents financing rejections or modified term offers, the existing loan documents including the maturity date and any extension conditions, and any property condition reports or engineering assessments prepared in connection with the defect claim.

How Financing Expert Testimony Is Used. At trial, financing expert testimony is used to establish the causal link between the defect and the measurable economic harm to the borrower, expressed in financing terms rather than appraised value terms. In settlement negotiations, a well-prepared financing expert report quantifying the loan proceeds gap, the financing cost differential, and the duration of the impairment period often drives resolution because it converts an abstract liability argument into a specific dollar amount that both sides can evaluate. In Plan confirmation hearings in bankruptcy contexts, financing expert testimony establishes whether a proposed plan is feasible based on whether the reorganized debtor can actually obtain the financing the Plan assumes.

Key Takeaways

  • Construction defect litigation does not just impair appraised value; it impairs financing eligibility entirely for institutional lenders until the litigation is resolved and remediation is complete or bonded. These are separate and additive damages components that must be analyzed independently.
  • The DSCR and LTV analysis in a defect scenario requires adjusting both the income and the collateral value simultaneously, because defects reduce demonstrable NOI through income impairment while also triggering remediation holdbacks that reduce net loan proceeds, producing a compounded impact on available financing.
  • In Idaho Falls specifically, the shallow institutional lender pool means that lender disqualification from a defect condition has a larger practical impact than in larger markets, because the borrower's alternatives are limited to more expensive bridge or private capital rather than competing institutional bidders.
  • The financing cost differential between a conventional permanent loan and a bridge execution is a quantifiable, ongoing damages figure that expert analysis can express as an annual carrying cost and, where the impairment period is estimable, as a present-value lump sum.
  • Remediation holdbacks imposed by lenders are a dollar-for-dollar reduction in net loan proceeds and represent a direct capital impairment to the borrower that is independent of the cost of the remediation work itself.
  • Attorneys should not rely solely on an appraiser to establish the financial impact of construction defects on a commercial property. Appraisal methodology does not capture lender credit policy, underwriting constraints, or the financing market conditions that determine what a borrower can actually borrow.
  • Expert witness qualification in CRE financing requires current, active deal flow and documented lender relationships, not just academic credentials or prior banking experience.

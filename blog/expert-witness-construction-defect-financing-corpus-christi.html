Expert Witness: How Construction Defects Affect CRE Financing in Corpus Christi, TX

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Construction Defect Impact on Financing

The Corpus Christi Market Context

Corpus Christi sits at a genuinely interesting intersection of energy infrastructure, port logistics, and coastal real estate dynamics that makes it a distinct and sometimes underestimated market for commercial real estate disputes. The Port of Corpus Christi is the largest crude oil export terminal in the United States, and that single fact reshapes how lenders underwrite nearly every asset class in the metro. Industrial, flex, and warehouse properties within the port corridor and the Northside industrial submarkets have seen sustained demand from petrochemical, liquefied natural gas, and logistics operators. As of recent market data, industrial vacancy in the broader Corpus Christi metro has remained in the 5 to 8 percent range, well below national averages for secondary markets, and net absorption has been positive in consecutive years driven by energy sector expansion and distribution activity tied to the refinery complex along the Ship Channel.

Multifamily fundamentals tell a more nuanced story. Corpus Christi's apartment market has experienced above-average rent growth relative to other Texas secondary cities, but that growth has been uneven across submarkets. Properties in Flour Bluff, Portland, and Calallen have performed differently than urban core and South Side assets, and lenders are acutely aware of this submarket divergence when underwriting stabilized or value-add multifamily. Cap rates for multifamily in the Corpus Christi metro have generally ranged from the high 5s to the mid 6s, reflecting the market's secondary status relative to Austin, Dallas, and Houston but also the resilience of the energy employment base. Retail has been more challenged, with older strip center inventory facing leasing headwinds as the market absorbs new mixed-use product near SPID and the Southside growth corridor. Office fundamentals remain soft city-wide, consistent with national trends but compounded locally by the reliance on energy sector tenants who have rationalized their footprints significantly since the 2014 to 2016 oil price correction.

For construction defect litigation purposes, one characteristic of the Corpus Christi market deserves particular emphasis: the coastal environment materially affects what constitutes a defect, how lenders treat disclosed defects, and what remediation costs are reasonable. Properties in the Corpus Christi metro face wind-driven rain exposure, salt air corrosion, expansive clay soil conditions, and the ever-present threat of tropical weather events. FEMA flood zone designations affect a meaningful portion of the commercial inventory. All of these factors shape the baseline underwriting assumptions a lender applies to any asset in this market, and they compound the financial consequences of unresolved construction defects in ways that would not apply in an inland market.

The Engagement

Counsel for the property owner retained Commercial Lending Solutions to provide expert analysis in a construction defect dispute involving a recently completed mid-size commercial property in the Corpus Christi metro. The asset was a multitenant light industrial and flex facility, a property type that has been actively transacting in Corpus Christi given the sustained logistics and energy-adjacent demand described above. The alleged defects involved the building envelope, specifically the exterior cladding system, the roofing assembly, and associated waterproofing details at penetrations and transitions. Secondary defect claims involved the concrete slab and foundation stemwall, where differential settlement and cracking had been observed and documented by a licensed structural engineer retained by the plaintiff.

The core legal and financial questions put to our firm were threefold. First, assuming the alleged defects were proven as described in the engineering report, could the property be financed on commercially reasonable terms in the current market, and if so, under what conditions? Second, how did the disclosed existence of pending construction defect litigation affect the underwriting of the property independent of whether the defects were ultimately proven or remediated? Third, what was the quantified difference in maximum supportable loan amount between this property in its current condition and a defect-free comparable asset of the same type in the same submarket? These questions required a financing feasibility analysis grounded in current lender requirements, market-specific underwriting criteria, and detailed income and valuation modeling, not a retrospective appraisal exercise.

CLS CRE was not retained to appraise the property, assess construction quality, or opine on causation. Our role was specific: to explain what the capital markets would do with this asset given the facts as presented, how lenders actually process and price disclosed construction defect exposure, and what the financial consequence of the defect condition was when measured against the financing a comparable unencumbered asset would support.

The Financial Analysis

The income analysis began with a review of the rent roll, the leases in place, and the operating expense history. For a Corpus Christi light industrial and flex asset of this type and vintage, market rents for occupied suites in the submarket ranged from approximately $8.00 to $11.50 per square foot per year on a net basis, depending on suite configuration, clear height, and dock versus grade-level access. The subject property contained approximately 48,000 square feet of leasable area, with occupancy at approximately 78 percent at the time of the analysis, partially reflecting the uncertainty created by the pending litigation and the lender market's response to it.

Gross potential rent at full occupancy, using market rents at the lower end of the range to reflect the property's condition uncertainty, produced a potential gross income of approximately $432,000 annually. Applying a 10 percent vacancy and credit loss allowance, consistent with what conservative lenders apply to flex industrial assets in secondary markets, produced an effective gross income of approximately $389,000. Operating expenses for a net-leased flex industrial asset of this type run in the range of 15 to 20 percent of effective gross income when management, insurance, reserves, and landlord-retained maintenance obligations are included. Using 18 percent produced operating expenses of approximately $70,000, yielding a stabilized net operating income of approximately $319,000.

The critical question in any financing feasibility analysis is what DSCR threshold applies. For conventional permanent debt on a light industrial or flex asset in a secondary Texas market, the standard minimum DSCR is 1.25x. Some bank lenders apply 1.20x for well-located stabilized assets with strong sponsorship, but agency-backed lending and CMBS execution consistently require 1.25x or better on this asset type. I used 1.25x as the controlling threshold.

Assuming a 7.25 percent all-in interest rate (appropriate for permanent bank financing on this asset type in the current rate environment, factoring in a spread over the applicable index and typical origination costs) and a 25-year amortization schedule, the maximum annual debt service supportable at 1.25x DSCR on $319,000 of NOI was approximately $255,000. At a 7.25 percent rate with 25-year amortization, the loan constant is approximately 8.55 percent. Dividing the supportable annual debt service of $255,000 by the loan constant of 8.55 percent produced a maximum supportable loan of approximately $2,982,000, call it $2.98 million.

For the defect-free comparable scenario, I modeled the same property at full market occupancy (95 percent), using mid-range market rents of $10.00 per square foot, with operating expenses held constant. This produced a stabilized NOI of approximately $437,000. Applying the same DSCR, rate, and amortization assumptions, the maximum supportable loan on the defect-free comparable was approximately $4,086,000, call it $4.09 million. The financing gap attributable to the defect condition was therefore approximately $1.11 million at the income level alone, before addressing the LTV and appraisal complications described below.

On the LTV side, most permanent lenders in this market apply a 65 to 70 percent LTV ceiling on flex industrial assets. If the property's as-is value, reflecting the defect condition and associated uncertainty, was appraised at $4.5 million rather than the $6.2 million value indicated for a defect-free comparable, the LTV constraint would cap the loan at $2.925 million to $3.15 million on the defect condition property versus $4.03 million to $4.34 million on the comparable. The DSCR and LTV analyses were therefore consistent and reinforcing: both methodologies produced a financing gap of approximately $1.1 million to $1.2 million directly attributable to the defect-affected condition of the asset.

The Complicating Factors

Active litigation as an independent underwriting barrier. Even setting aside the physical condition of the property, the existence of pending construction defect litigation creates an independent financing obstacle that most attorneys and borrowers underestimate. Virtually every commercial loan agreement contains a representation and warranty that there is no pending or threatened litigation that could materially affect the collateral or the borrower's financial condition. A property with active construction defect litigation fails this representation on day one. Lenders who discover litigation at the due diligence stage typically respond in one of three ways: they decline the loan outright, they condition approval on a litigation hold reserve equal to the claimed defect amount, or they require indemnification agreements that most borrowers and their counsel find commercially unacceptable. In the Corpus Christi market, where the community bank and regional bank lenders have direct relationships with local counsel and are acutely aware of active disputes in the market, a filed construction defect suit is not easily concealed and travels fast through the underwriting pipeline.

Environmental and coastal compounding. In Corpus Christi specifically, envelope failures and foundation defects carry a layered consequence that inland market defects do not. A failed cladding system in a coastal Texas environment allows salt-laden moisture infiltration that can rapidly accelerate corrosion of embedded steel, HVAC components, and interior buildout. Lenders in this market understand that a defect that might be a $200,000 remediation problem in Dallas becomes a $400,000 to $600,000 problem in Corpus Christi if left unaddressed for two or three years while litigation proceeds. This escalating remediation cost trajectory affects lender confidence in any reserve analysis and pushes many institutional lenders toward requiring completed remediation as a condition of closing rather than accepting a holdback.

Insurance complications. Lenders require property and casualty insurance as a condition of any commercial loan. When construction defects are known and disclosed, underwriters for commercial property policies frequently exclude coverage for losses arising from or related to the defect condition. A property operating under a policy with a construction defect exclusion creates a coverage gap that a lender's counsel will identify and that will, at minimum, increase the required insurance reserve and, at maximum, trigger a denial of the loan application entirely until the defect is remediated and the exclusion removed.

Appraiser behavior under defect disclosure obligations. Texas-licensed commercial appraisers are required to report known adverse conditions that affect value, and the existence of pending litigation is itself a condition that must be disclosed in the appraisal report. Appraisers responding to a lender's engagement letter in this scenario will typically apply a distressed condition adjustment, reducing comparable sale adjustments to account for the cost to cure the defect, the litigation uncertainty, and the marketability discount associated with the pending claim. This appraisal haircut flows directly into the LTV calculation and compounds the DSCR-driven loan reduction described above.

The Corpus Christi Lender Market

Corpus Christi's commercial lending market reflects the city's size and economic profile. It is not an institutional CMBS or life company market for most transactions below $20 million, but it has meaningful depth in community and regional bank lending, and it is within reach of Texas-based and regional credit unions, USDA Business and Industry programs for rural-adjacent assets, and SBA 504 and 7(a) execution for owner-occupied properties. Understanding which of these lender categories will and will not engage with a defect-affected asset is essential to the financing feasibility analysis.

Regional and national banks with Corpus Christi commercial real estate departments generally have the most sophisticated credit processes and the most formal legal review procedures. These lenders will identify pending litigation immediately through title searches, borrower disclosures, and public record review. Their credit approval processes require sign-off from in-house or outside counsel on any material litigation, and approval of a loan on a property with unresolved construction defect litigation requires senior credit officer or loan committee approval with specific conditions. These lenders are not categorically unwilling to close on defect-affected properties, but the conditions they impose (holdbacks, litigation reserves, personal guarantees, completion bonds) substantially alter the economics of the transaction.

Community banks with local Corpus Christi presence, including those with long-standing relationships with local developers and owners, have more flexibility in their credit processes but also have more concentrated exposure to the local market. A community bank that has already underwritten other assets for the same borrower may extend more latitude on a litigation hold structure, but they are equally bound by their regulators' expectations regarding documentation of known adverse conditions in loan files. Post-2010 regulatory guidance from the OCC and FDIC requires banks to document how known litigation risk was evaluated and mitigated, and this documentation requirement has substantially reduced the informal accommodations that were more common in prior decades.

For truly distressed or litigation-clouded assets in the Corpus Christi market, private bridge lenders and hard money execution are available but at materially different pricing: rates in the 10.5 to 13 percent range with 12 to 24 month terms, origination fees of 1.5 to 3 points, and cross-collateralization requirements are common. This pricing differential is itself relevant to damages analysis: if a borrower who would otherwise qualify for permanent bank financing at 7.25 percent is forced into bridge execution at 11.5 percent while litigation resolves, the carry cost differential over the litigation period is a quantifiable economic harm.

What Attorneys Handling Corpus Christi CRE Disputes Need to Know

The decision to retain a CRE financing expert should be made early in the litigation, not at the eve of trial. The most common error I see in construction defect matters involving commercial properties is that counsel retains an appraiser and assumes the appraiser's value conclusion captures the full financial impact of the defect. It does not. An appraiser can tell you what the property is worth in its current condition. A CRE financing expert can tell you what financing the property can support, what lender categories will engage with it, what the conditions and costs of that financing are, and how all of those factors translate into concrete financial harm to the owner or the project.

The questions a CRE financing expert can answer that an appraiser typically cannot include: Would a specific lender have funded this project at the proposed loan amount if the defects had been disclosed at underwriting? What would the lender have required as conditions to closing given the defect disclosure? What is the difference in maximum supportable debt between the defect-affected and defect-free scenario? What is the carry cost impact of delayed or denied financing? What does the lender market's response to pending construction defect litigation mean for the borrower's practical ability to refinance or sell the property during the litigation period?

Qualification matters significantly in this field. A retired banker with residential lending experience is not an adequate substitute for an expert with active, current commercial mortgage brokerage practice. The lender market changes rapidly, credit standards shift with rate cycles and regulatory environments, and the ability to speak credibly about what lenders are actually doing today requires active engagement with those lenders on current transactions. At CLS CRE, we maintain active relationships with over 1,000 lenders across all capital stack categories, we have closed approximately $1 billion in commercial transaction volume, and we operate in all 50 states. That active market presence is what allows us to testify credibly about current lender behavior rather than historical practice.

Before retaining a CRE financing expert, counsel should gather the following: the complete rent roll and leases; two to three years of operating statements; all appraisal reports, including any lender-ordered appraisals; the construction defect engineering report; all correspondence between the borrower and any lender during the relevant period; any loan applications, term sheets, or commitment letters that were issued or withdrawn; the property's insurance policies and any coverage denials or exclusions; and the property's title commitment with all exception documents. The expert will need all of this to construct a credible financing feasibility analysis that can withstand cross-examination.

At trial or in an arbitration proceeding, CRE financing expert testimony is typically used in one of two ways: as affirmative damages evidence showing the quantified financing gap between defect and defect-free scenarios, or as rebuttal testimony challenging an opposing party's claim that financing was or would have been available on commercially reasonable terms. Both uses require the expert to be current, specific, and grounded in documented lender market practice rather than generalized assertions about what lenders "typically" do.

Key Takeaways

  • Construction defects create a dual financing impairment: both the physical condition of the asset and the existence of pending litigation independently reduce the pool of available lenders and the maximum supportable loan amount, and these effects compound rather than simply add.

Expert Witness: Financing a Contaminated Industrial Property in Burlington, VT

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Environmental Contamination Analysis

The Burlington Market Context

Burlington, Vermont occupies a narrow but strategically important niche in the northeastern commercial real estate landscape. With a metropolitan statistical area population of roughly 230,000 and anchored by the University of Vermont, UVM Medical Center, and a cluster of technology and healthcare employers, Burlington punches well above its weight in terms of institutional real estate activity relative to its market size. That dynamic matters in a financing dispute because it means a legitimate pool of institutional capital does flow into this market, but that pool is materially shallower and more selective than what you would find in Boston, Hartford, or Albany.

Industrial real estate in the greater Burlington area, encompassing the Chittenden County submarkets from South Burlington through Williston and out to Essex Junction, has experienced meaningful compression in vacancy over the past several years. Functional industrial and flex-industrial product in these corridors has traded at vacancy rates consistently below 5%, driven by distribution demand, light manufacturing, and the supply constraints inherent to a geographically compressed market bounded by Lake Champlain to the west and the Green Mountains to the east. That supply constraint is a double-edged sword in a contamination dispute: it supports value arguments, but it also means the pool of comparable unencumbered transactions from which to derive market rents and capitalization rates is thin.

Industrial rents in Chittenden County for functional warehouse and distribution product have generally ranged from the mid-$7.00 to low-$11.00 per-square-foot range on a triple-net basis depending on clear height, dock configuration, and location within the county. Below-market leases on NNN industrial assets in this market are a recurring issue in financing because the rent differential can be significant relative to replacement cost and market rent, and most institutional lenders will underwrite to in-place rent rather than mark-to-market projections absent a lease restructure. That distinction is central to any maximum-loan analysis in a dispute context.

Capitalization rates for stabilized, unencumbered industrial assets in the Burlington area have generally ranged from approximately 6.25% to 7.50% for institutional-quality product, with the higher end of that range applying to assets with below-market leases, shorter lease term, functional obsolescence, or environmental complexity. For a contaminated asset with a below-market lease, a lender's stabilized value assumption will diverge substantially from the appraised fee-simple value, and that divergence is where much of the expert financing analysis lives.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by counsel representing secured creditors in a regulatory enforcement proceeding involving a single-tenant industrial warehouse property in the Chittenden County submarket of greater Burlington. The property was encumbered by first-mortgage debt that the secured creditor sought to retire through refinancing, and the core question before the court was whether that refinancing was achievable under current market conditions given the asset's environmental condition.

The property had been used for industrial purposes by a prior owner and had documented groundwater and soil contamination consistent with chlorinated volatile organic compound, specifically 1,4-dioxane, releases from historical operations. At the time of the engagement, the property was subject to an active Vermont Agency of Natural Resources enforcement order requiring ongoing investigation and remediation. The asset was 100% occupied under a single NNN lease, but that lease was executing at rents materially below current market levels for comparable Burlington-area industrial product, with several years remaining on the base term and no percentage rent or CPI adjustment provisions of consequence.

Opposing counsel had argued that the environmental condition categorically precluded first-mortgage financing, and therefore that the secured creditor's position was impaired beyond any prospect of payoff through a market refinance. Our engagement was to assess that claim rigorously: to determine whether financing was available in the current market for a contaminated NNN industrial asset of this type, what the maximum supportable loan would be at market underwriting standards, and whether that loan would be sufficient to retire the existing senior debt in full or in part.

The engagement required producing a written opinion under the standards applicable to Rule 26 expert reports, with a methodology section, data sources, assumptions, and conclusions supported by market evidence rather than conjecture. This is a materially different analytical task than an appraisal, and the distinction between the two disciplines matters to attorneys preparing for these hearings.

The Financial Analysis

The income analysis began with the in-place NNN lease. Because the property was triple-net, the landlord's operating expense exposure was limited, and the effective gross income approximated the base rent with modest adjustments for structural reserves and vacancy credit risk. For a single-tenant NNN asset, most lenders apply a vacancy credit of 5% to effective gross income even when the property is 100% occupied, reflecting the binary occupancy risk inherent to a single-tenant structure. That credit was applied here.

For purposes of illustration consistent with Burlington-area industrial properties of the relevant size and vintage, assume in-place gross rent of approximately $610,000 annually on a NNN basis. After applying the 5% single-tenant vacancy credit and a structural reserve of approximately $0.15 per square foot consistent with the property's age and condition, the underwritten net operating income arrived at approximately $570,000. This figure, not the market NOI, is what lenders will use unless the lease restructure is complete and documented prior to closing.

Market-standard DSCR thresholds for industrial assets in secondary and tertiary markets, which Burlington qualifies as from an institutional lender's perspective, generally run from 1.20x at the aggressive end to 1.30x for standard institutional underwriting. For a contaminated asset with environmental contingency, most lenders in our conversations with their credit teams applied a floor of 1.25x DSCR, and several required 1.30x as a condition of credit approval. CLS CRE used 1.25x as the operative threshold for the base case, with sensitivity analysis at 1.30x for conservative presentation.

Rate assumptions reflected the financing environment applicable to the engagement period. For a 5-year fixed-rate, non-recourse first mortgage on a contaminated industrial asset in a secondary New England market, lenders were pricing at spreads ranging from 250 to 350 basis points over the corresponding Treasury index, reflecting contamination risk, market illiquidity, and single-tenant concentration. At a blended all-in rate of approximately 7.50% and a 25-year amortization schedule, the annual debt service on a per-dollar-of-loan basis was approximately $0.0884 per dollar of principal.

Applying the DSCR constraint: maximum annual debt service equals NOI divided by the DSCR threshold, which is $570,000 divided by 1.25, producing a maximum supportable debt service of approximately $456,000. Dividing that debt service by the annual constant of 0.0884 yields a maximum supportable loan of approximately $5,160,000, rounded to $5.2 million for the base case.

LTV constraints provide the secondary binding constraint. For contaminated industrial assets, most lenders in this market applied LTV caps ranging from 55% to 65% of the as-is appraised value, net of any environmental remediation reserve holdback. Assuming an as-is appraised value of approximately $7.8 million reflecting the below-market lease and contamination discount, the 65% LTV ceiling produces a loan ceiling of approximately $5.07 million, and the 55% LTV ceiling produces approximately $4.29 million. The DSCR-derived maximum of $5.2 million was the binding constraint at the 65% LTV level, but the 55% LTV applied by more conservative lenders became binding and reduced the maximum loan to the $4.3 million range.

The conclusion of the base case analysis was that first-mortgage financing in a range of approximately $4.3 million to $5.2 million was supportable under current market conditions, depending on lender category and risk tolerance, and that the environmental condition did not categorically preclude financing. Whether that range was sufficient to retire the existing senior debt was a factual question determined by the outstanding balance, which was provided by counsel and compared against the financing range in the final opinion.

The Complicating Factors

1,4-Dioxane and Chlorinated VOC Contamination. These compounds represent some of the most technically and financially complex environmental conditions a lender encounters because of their migration characteristics, regulatory trajectory, and remediation cost uncertainty. Unlike petroleum hydrocarbon contamination, which has a well-developed regulatory framework and established remediation cost ranges, 1,4-dioxane does not adsorb to soil particles and migrates rapidly in groundwater, making plume delineation and containment more expensive and less predictable. Vermont ANR has been increasingly aggressive in its enforcement posture on chlorinated solvent and co-contaminant sites, which adds regulatory cost risk that lenders must factor into their underwriting. Most lenders require a Phase II environmental site assessment conducted by a licensed Vermont environmental professional, a remediation cost estimate with a clear contingency range, and a defined remediation end-state before they will underwrite a maximum loan. If those documents do not exist or are contested, the financing universe narrows materially.

The Environmental Reserve Holdback. Even when a lender is willing to close on a contaminated asset, they will typically require an environmental remediation reserve holdback of 125% to 150% of the estimated remaining remediation cost, funded from loan proceeds or borrower equity. That holdback reduces the net loan proceeds available to retire existing debt, and it must be modeled in any maximum-loan analysis presented in a dispute context. Opposing counsel who argue that a $5 million loan retires existing debt without accounting for a $600,000 or $800,000 holdback are presenting an incomplete picture of net proceeds.

Below-Market Leases and Lender Underwriting. Burlington industrial rents have risen meaningfully over the past five years, and a below-market lease locks in a NOI figure that understates the property's market income potential. Lenders are contractually prohibited from underwriting to projected market rents unless there is a documented lease amendment or new lease at market rates. This creates a gap between the appraised value using a discounted cash flow that captures lease-up at market and the lender's underwritten value based on in-place income. That gap is real and material in any financing feasibility opinion, and it is frequently misunderstood by parties who conflate appraised value with financeable value.

Vermont ANR Enforcement Order. An active enforcement order triggers lender due diligence requirements that go beyond standard Phase II assessment. Most institutional lenders and many regional banks will require a comfort letter or no-further-action letter from ANR as a condition of closing, or at minimum a representation from the environmental consultant that the remediation path is defined and the cost estimate is reliable within a stated confidence interval. In the absence of that clarity, some lenders in the Vermont market will decline to quote entirely, which narrows the competitive lender pool and affects pricing.

Single-Tenant Concentration Risk. A 100% NNN leased industrial property sounds like an ideal financing candidate, but single-tenant concentration is treated as a risk amplifier by most institutional underwriters. If the tenant vacates, the property's cash flow drops to zero. In a contaminated property scenario, re-leasing is additionally complicated by disclosure requirements and potential tenant resistance, which reduces the property's lease-up velocity assumption in lender stress testing.

The Burlington Lender Market

Understanding which lenders will actually quote a contaminated NNN industrial asset in Burlington requires an accurate picture of the active capital in this submarket. CLS CRE maintains active relationships with more than 1,000 institutional and private lenders across 50 states, and our Vermont market coverage reflects the specific characteristics of this capital environment.

Vermont Community and Regional Banks. The most likely source of financing for a contaminated industrial asset in Burlington is a Vermont-chartered community bank or a New England regional bank with a Vermont lending team. These institutions have experience underwriting Vermont ANR enforcement matters, understand the regulatory timeline, and have the balance-sheet flexibility to hold environmental risk that CMBS or agency lenders cannot. Institutions in this category typically require full recourse, personal guarantees from creditworthy sponsors, and enhanced due diligence including a lender-commissioned Phase II and remediation cost review. Pricing in this category generally runs at spreads of 225 to 325 basis points over corresponding Treasury or SOFR indices, with terms of 3 to 5 years and amortization of 20 to 25 years. LTV caps of 55% to 65% are standard for contaminated assets. This category produces the most likely path to closing but may not produce the highest loan proceeds.

Debt Funds and Private Bridge Lenders. For an asset in an enforcement posture where institutional lenders require a comfort letter as a condition of closing, private bridge capital can sometimes close without that condition in exchange for higher pricing and a shorter term. Bridge lenders active in the New England market have priced contaminated industrial bridge loans in the range of 9% to 11.50% all-in for 12 to 24 month terms, with origination fees of 1.00% to 2.00%, and typically require a defined remediation plan and cost estimate even if they do not require a comfort letter. This category is relevant for a dispute context because it demonstrates that even in a worst-case regulatory posture, financing options exist, which directly rebuts a categorical preclusion argument.

CMBS and Agency Lenders. These sources are not viable for a contaminated asset with an active enforcement order. CMBS credit guidelines universally exclude properties with identified, unremediated environmental contamination from eligibility. Agency programs, including those administered through life insurance company correspondents, have equivalent restrictions. Presenting CMBS or agency debt as a viable option for this type of asset would be an error in any expert financing opinion.

Life Company and CMBS Correspondent Lenders. These lenders could become relevant post-remediation and post-NFA letter, and noting that pathway in a financing opinion helps quantify the value trajectory of the asset as an environmental clearance is achieved. That analysis can support damages or valuation arguments in the litigation context.

What Attorneys Handling Burlington CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to Retain a CRE Financing Expert. If your case turns on any of the following questions, you need a CRE financing expert, not just an appraiser: whether financing sufficient to retire existing debt is available in the current market; what maximum loan proceeds a market-rate lender would provide on a specific asset; whether a debtor's refinancing plan in a Chapter 11 plan of reorganization is feasible; whether a lender's failure to close was commercially reasonable given market conditions; or whether a borrower could have refinanced out of a defaulted loan at a specific point in time. Appraisers opine on value. Financing experts opine on debt market capacity, lender standards, and capital markets execution. These are different disciplines and different testimony.

What a CRE Financing Expert Provides That an Appraiser Cannot. An appraisal determines the fee-simple or leased-fee market value of real property using income, sales comparison, and cost approaches. It does not determine how much a specific pool of lenders will lend against that value, at what pricing, subject to what conditions, and through what execution pathway. A financing expert analyzes the lender market directly, applies current underwriting criteria, and produces a maximum-loan determination grounded in actual lender behavior rather than appraised value assumptions. In contamination disputes, the gap between appraised value and financeable value is frequently the pivotal number in the case.

What Qualifies an Expert in This Discipline. A retired banker who made loans 15 years ago is not the same as an active commercial mortgage broker with current lender relationships and transaction data. The qualifying criteria should include active origination volume in the relevant asset class, current lender relationships across the relevant capital stack categories, demonstrated experience with distressed and contaminated asset financing, and ideally prior Rule 26 expert report experience reviewed by courts. CLS CRE has approximately $1 billion in transaction volume and more than 1,000 active lender relationships, with direct experience in contaminated asset financing across multiple states including Vermont.

Information to Gather Before the Engagement. Provide the expert with: the most recent Phase I and Phase II environmental site assessments; any remediation cost estimates and regulatory correspondence from ANR; the current rent roll including lease commencement, expiration, base rent, rent escalations, and expense structure; the existing loan documents including principal balance, maturity date, and default provisions; the most recent property financial statements; and any existing appraisals or broker opinions of value. The earlier in the matter you engage the expert and provide this data, the more defensible and complete the financing opinion will be.

Expert Witness: How Construction Defects Affect CRE Financing in Raleigh, NC

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Construction Defect Impact on Financing

The Raleigh Market Context

Raleigh and the broader Research Triangle market have experienced one of the most sustained commercial real estate expansion cycles in the southeastern United States over the past decade. That growth has created a large and active pipeline of ground-up construction across every major asset class, which in turn has generated a corresponding increase in construction defect claims. Understanding how those claims intersect with financing is critical, because the financial damage flowing from a construction defect is rarely limited to the cost of remediation. In a market like Raleigh, the indirect financing consequences frequently exceed the direct repair cost by a significant margin.

Raleigh's commercial fundamentals remain strong by most measures, but they are not uniform. Multifamily is the dominant story: the Triangle added approximately 6,000 to 7,000 apartment units annually over the recent high-cycle years, and cap rates for stabilized Class A multifamily assets in submarkets like North Hills, downtown Raleigh, and Cary have compressed into the 4.25% to 5.00% range. Industrial, driven by e-commerce fulfillment and the advanced manufacturing ecosystem surrounding the Research Triangle Park, has seen vacancy rates drop below 5% in several corridors, with asking rents pushing above $9.00 to $11.00 per square foot NNN for newer product in the I-40 and I-540 corridors. Office is more complicated: suburban Class B office has softened materially since 2020, while life science and lab-enabled office near RTP commands a premium and remains lender-friendly. Retail continues its bifurcation between grocery-anchored neighborhood centers that lenders actively pursue and unanchored inline product that most institutional lenders will not touch.

For a construction defect matter, the specific asset class matters enormously in the financing analysis, because the lender universe, the underwriting standards, and the sensitivity to deferred maintenance or latent defects vary significantly across asset types. A multifamily development with alleged waterproofing failures near downtown Raleigh is a fundamentally different financing challenge than an industrial building with structural defects in a Wake County logistics corridor. Both cases require precise, market-specific financial analysis to quantify the financing impact, and that is exactly the type of analysis my firm, Commercial Lending Solutions, provides as part of expert engagements in construction defect matters.

The Engagement

Counsel representing the property owner retained CLS CRE to provide expert analysis in a construction defect dispute involving a recently completed mid-rise multifamily development located in a core Raleigh submarket. The property had reached substantial completion and was in the early stages of lease-up when defects were identified involving the building envelope, including alleged failures in the exterior cladding system, window flashing, and membrane waterproofing at balcony decks. The owner had placed a construction loan on the property during development, and the original business plan contemplated refinancing into permanent agency financing upon stabilization at or above a defined occupancy threshold.

The core legal and financial questions put to us were as follows. First, did the alleged defects, as described in the engineering reports and consultant findings already in the record, materially affect the property's ability to qualify for the permanent financing that would have been available to a defect-free asset of equivalent type in the same submarket? Second, how would a lender's underwriter actually treat those defects in sizing the loan, and what specific underwriting adjustments would result? Third, what was the maximum supportable loan amount on the property as it sat with known defects versus what that loan amount would have been absent the defects? And fourth, what categories of lenders, if any, would still consider the asset, and at what pricing and structural premium?

These are not questions an appraiser can answer completely. An appraiser can estimate as-is versus as-repaired value, but that analysis does not capture how lenders actually underwrite, how their credit committees respond to known litigation, or what structural requirements lenders impose on properties with disclosed defect claims. That gap between appraised value and financing feasibility is precisely where CLS CRE expert analysis provides independent and material value to counsel.

The Financial Analysis

Our analysis began with a reconstruction of the property's stabilized net operating income under two scenarios: a defect-free baseline and a defect-burdened current state. The property contained 210 units across a mix of one-bedroom and two-bedroom floor plans, with a projected stabilized average monthly rent of approximately $1,850 per unit, consistent with the competitive set in that submarket at the time of the dispute. Stabilized gross potential rent at 100% occupancy was therefore approximately $4,662,000 annually. Applying a market vacancy and credit loss of 5.0%, consistent with current Raleigh multifamily performance in this submarket, yielded effective gross income of approximately $4,429,000. With other income (parking, pet fees, amenity revenue) of approximately $126,000, total effective gross income reached approximately $4,555,000.

Operating expenses for a property of this type and vintage in Raleigh, including management fee at 4.5% of EGI, real estate taxes, insurance, utilities, repairs and maintenance, payroll, and reserves for replacement at $350 per unit, were underwritten at approximately $1,740,000 annually, yielding a stabilized NOI of approximately $2,815,000 under the defect-free baseline scenario.

Under the defect-burdened scenario, the analysis required adjustments across three separate lines. First, insurance. Once the defect claims were disclosed to the property's insurer and became a matter of record, the owner's property and casualty carrier issued a coverage exclusion for water intrusion claims related to the identified systems. A replacement policy with equivalent coverage, accounting for the disclosed defect history, carried an annual premium approximately $68,000 higher than the baseline policy. Second, reserves for replacement. Lender underwriters, when confronted with known building envelope defects, consistently increase the reserve requirement above the market standard $250 to $350 per unit range. In this case, a lender would require an escrow or underwritten reserve of approximately $600 to $700 per unit to account for accelerated deterioration and remediation risk, increasing the reserve line by approximately $95,000 annually. Third, and most significantly, stabilized occupancy. Properties with disclosed construction defect litigation experience demonstrable leasing friction: prospective tenants who conduct due diligence, particularly institutional or corporate relocation tenants who consult counsel, frequently pass on the asset. We modeled a stabilized occupancy haircut to 91% from the baseline 95%, representing a loss of effective gross income of approximately $186,000 annually.

The aggregate NOI impact of these three adjustments was approximately $349,000, reducing the defect-burdened NOI to approximately $2,466,000.

For loan sizing, we applied agency-consistent underwriting, specifically Freddie Mac Multifamily SBL and conventional agency execution standards, as those represented the most likely permanent financing path for this asset class and Raleigh market position. The applicable DSCR minimum for a standard agency execution at the time was 1.25x, consistent with the market standard range of 1.20x to 1.25x for conventional multifamily. We used a note rate of 6.50%, reflecting the prevailing all-in rate for 10-year fixed agency execution, on a 30-year amortization schedule. The annual debt service per $1,000,000 of loan balance at those terms is approximately $75,939.

Maximum supportable loan under the baseline NOI of $2,815,000 at 1.25x DSCR: $2,815,000 divided by 1.25 equals maximum annual debt service of $2,252,000, which supports a loan of approximately $29,655,000. At the assumed appraised value of $52,500,000 for a defect-free asset at a 5.00% cap rate, the LTV on that loan was approximately 56.5%, well within agency LTV limits of 65% to 80% depending on execution tier.

Maximum supportable loan under the defect-burdened NOI of $2,466,000 at 1.25x DSCR: $2,466,000 divided by 1.25 equals maximum annual debt service of $1,972,800, which supports a loan of approximately $25,978,000. The financing gap attributable directly to the defects was therefore approximately $3,677,000. That figure represents the quantifiable financing damage independent of and in addition to the cost of remediation itself.

The Complicating Factors

Known litigation as a lender disqualifier. The single most significant complicating factor is not the physical condition of the building. It is the existence of filed or threatened litigation. Most institutional lenders, including all agency executions through Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, will not fund a permanent loan on a property that is the subject of active construction defect litigation, full stop. The prohibition is not merely a pricing adjustment. It is a hard stop in the credit approval process. Lenders cannot take a first lien on a property where there is an unresolved legal claim that may result in a mechanic's lien, a remediation order, or an insurance coverage dispute that could impair the collateral value. In Raleigh, where the community bank market is more flexible than the institutional market, there are lenders who will consider the asset, but they will do so only with a specific structure: full cash escrow or holdback equal to 125% to 150% of the engineer-estimated remediation cost, plus a legal reserve, plus a rate premium of 75 to 125 basis points over a clean-asset execution. That structural requirement is not a negotiating position. It is a credit requirement imposed by the lender's regulatory environment.

Agency execution is eliminated during litigation. For a Raleigh multifamily asset that would otherwise be a natural fit for agency permanent financing, the loss of agency execution access is a discrete and quantifiable damage. Agency rates are consistently 50 to 100 basis points inside comparable bank or CMBS execution. Over a 10-year loan term on a loan in the $25,000,000 to $30,000,000 range, that rate differential represents a present value cost of $1,500,000 to $2,500,000 in additional interest expense, entirely attributable to the defect-driven agency lockout.

Appraisal complications and as-is versus as-stabilized value divergence. Lenders underwrite to the lesser of as-is value or the loan amount supported by income. For a property in active litigation with occupancy headwinds, the as-is value may be substantially below the as-stabilized value. In our engagement, the appraiser retained by the potential lender applied a discount of approximately 8% to as-is value relative to as-stabilized, reflecting litigation risk, leasing friction, and remediation uncertainty. That discount compressed the LTV-constrained loan ceiling independently of the DSCR analysis, creating a second limiting factor that produced an even lower maximum loan in some lender scenarios.

Construction loan extension risk. Many construction defect disputes arise precisely when the developer is trying to refinance out of a construction loan approaching maturity. Construction lenders typically do not extend loans on properties with unresolved defect claims without significant fee and reserve requirements. The cost of those extensions, including extension fees, increased interest carry, and additional required reserves, represents a separate category of financial damage that must be modeled and presented with precision.

The Raleigh Lender Market

Raleigh enjoys an unusually deep lender market relative to its population and transaction volume, a product of its sustained growth trajectory and its position as a target market for both southeastern regional banks and national institutional capital. For a clean, stabilized multifamily asset in a core Raleigh submarket, the financing universe is genuinely broad: agency lenders, life insurance companies, CMBS conduits, regional banks, community banks, and debt funds all compete actively for quality product. That depth is relevant to our analysis because it establishes what financing was available and foregone as a result of the defects.

For a defect-burdened asset in active litigation, the universe narrows sharply. Agency lenders are out entirely during litigation, as noted above. Life company lenders, who are among the most conservative capital sources in commercial real estate, will not consider an asset with disclosed defect claims under any circumstances. CMBS conduits face the same limitation because their pooling and servicing agreements require representations and warranties about known litigation that a property in active suit cannot satisfy.

That leaves three categories of lenders who will consider the asset. First, local and regional community banks headquartered or with significant presence in the Triangle, including institutions based in Raleigh, Durham, and Charlotte. These lenders have familiarity with the market, more flexible credit committee processes, and a history of structuring around known issues with appropriate reserves and pricing premiums. They will typically lend at 55% to 60% LTV on a defect-burdened asset, at a rate 75 to 150 basis points above the prevailing agency benchmark, with a full remediation holdback. Second, bridge lenders and debt funds, who will accept higher risk in exchange for higher yield. These lenders price at 300 to 500 basis points over SOFR, require short loan terms of 12 to 24 months, and impose origination fees of 1.00% to 2.00%. They are not permanent financing. They are an expensive bridge to a resolution. Third, in limited cases, a mezz or preferred equity structure layered over a conservative senior loan, though this structure is difficult to execute on litigation-burdened assets because the senior lender will typically restrict subordinate financing.

What Attorneys Handling Raleigh CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to retain a CRE financing expert. If your case involves a commercial property where the plaintiff or defendant claims financial harm that flows through a financing event, whether a failed refinance, a construction loan default, a loss of proceeds, or a delayed capital event, you need a CRE financing expert. The question is not whether the property was damaged. The question is how the damage translated into specific, quantifiable financial loss through the debt markets. That is not a question an appraiser, an accountant, or a general real estate broker can answer with the technical precision required for expert testimony.

What a CRE financing expert answers that an appraiser cannot. An appraiser opines on value. A CRE financing expert opines on financing feasibility, loan sizing, lender behavior, underwriting standards, and the structure and pricing of debt. In a construction defect case, the most significant financial damages frequently do not flow from appraised value loss alone. They flow from loan amount reduction, rate premium, loss of agency execution, increased reserve requirements, and construction loan extension costs. None of those items appear in a standard appraisal. All of them require a financing expert with current, active transaction experience in the relevant market.

Qualifications that matter. A qualified CRE financing expert is not a retired banker who underwrote loans 15 years ago. The lending market changes rapidly. Rate environments, lender credit boxes, agency guideline updates, and reserve requirement standards shift materially from cycle to cycle. The expert must be currently active in the market, with recent closed transactions across multiple lender types, demonstrable lender relationships, and a portfolio of current engagements that validates their market knowledge. CLS CRE has approximately $1 billion in transaction volume across more than 1,000 lender relationships, operating in all 50 states, which provides the breadth and depth to opine on what a specific lender type in a specific market would actually do with a specific fact pattern.

Information to gather before the expert engagement. Counsel should gather the following before retaining and briefing the financing expert: the original loan commitment or term sheet on the construction loan; any permanent financing application, commitment, or correspondence; the engineering or consultant reports describing the alleged defects; any insurance coverage correspondence, including reservation of rights letters or exclusion notices; lease-up data showing actual versus projected occupancy; the property's actual operating statements for all periods available; and any correspondence between the borrower and lender regarding loan extensions, defaults, or maturity issues. The more complete this record, the more precise the expert's analysis will be.

How expert testimony is used. In construction defect litigation, CRE financing expert testimony is used to establish the financing damages component of the plaintiff's economic harm, to rebut a defense claim that alternative financing was readily available, or to establish that the defects would not have materially affected a reasonable lender's credit decision. At trial, the testimony typically addresses methodology, market standards, and the specific calculations underlying the damages opinion. In mediation, a well-prepared financing expert report is frequently the most persuasive tool for bridging the gap between repair cost estimates and total economic loss, because it quantifies damages that the other side's appraiser has not addressed.

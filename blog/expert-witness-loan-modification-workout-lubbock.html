Expert Witness: Commercial Loan Modification Standards in Lubbock, TX

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Loan Modification and Workout Dispute

The Lubbock Market Context

Lubbock, Texas occupies a distinct position in the Texas commercial real estate landscape. It is not Austin or Dallas, but it is not a tertiary market in the sense that word implies fragility. Lubbock is a supply-constrained, institutional anchor-driven market built around Texas Tech University, a regional medical complex centered on University Medical Center and the Texas Tech University Health Sciences Center, and a deep agricultural and energy-related industrial base. These anchors create stable occupancy demand across multifamily, retail, medical office, and industrial asset classes, but they also create pricing dynamics and lender expectations that differ meaningfully from major Texas metros.

On the multifamily side, Lubbock has historically demonstrated cap rates in the 6.0 to 7.5 percent range depending on asset quality, vintage, and proximity to the Texas Tech corridor. Student-adjacent properties carry both premium occupancy and elevated lender scrutiny due to seasonal cash flow patterns. Market rents in stabilized non-student multifamily have trended upward modestly, averaging $950 to $1,200 per unit per month for Class B product as of recent reporting periods, with newer Class A projects pushing above $1,400. Vacancy in well-located multifamily has generally held in the 5 to 8 percent range, though oversupply risk near campus can push that higher when new deliveries hit the market in concentrated periods.

Retail fundamentals in Lubbock are bifurcated. Anchored neighborhood and community retail serving the medical district and established residential corridors has remained resilient. Strip retail and smaller unanchored centers in secondary locations have faced the same headwinds seen nationally, with vacancy rates drifting toward 10 to 14 percent in weaker corridors. Industrial in Lubbock benefits from the region's agricultural processing, logistics, and energy-sector support functions. Vacancy in functional industrial has remained below 6 percent, and lease rates for basic warehouse and flex product have been in the $6.00 to $9.00 per square foot range, NNN. Office is the most challenged asset class, as it is across most mid-size markets, with Class B and C office experiencing vacancy north of 15 percent in some submarkets.

These market fundamentals matter enormously in a loan modification or workout dispute. A lender evaluating whether to grant forbearance or restructure debt on a Lubbock commercial property in 2023 or 2024 was operating in a market where rising interest rates were compressing valuations, refinance proceeds were shrinking relative to outstanding balances, and certain asset classes were under genuine operational stress. The question of whether a lender's conduct was commercially reasonable cannot be answered without grounding the analysis in these specific conditions.

The Engagement

In a recent matter, we were retained by counsel representing a commercial borrower in a dispute arising from a failed loan workout negotiation. The asset at issue was a retail strip center in Lubbock, approximately 28,000 square feet, with a mix of local service tenants and one regional anchor occupying roughly 40 percent of the gross leasable area. The loan had been originated several years prior at a fixed rate, and it matured into a rising interest rate environment that made conventional refinancing at comparable proceeds impossible without the borrower injecting substantial new equity.

The borrower had approached the lender proactively, approximately eight months before maturity, requesting a modification that would extend the loan term by 24 months while a lease renewal was finalized with a major tenant and market conditions were monitored for a potential refinance window. The lender acknowledged the request, conducted an internal review process that extended over five months, and ultimately declined to offer a modification, instead issuing a notice of default and initiating foreclosure proceedings when the loan matured without payoff.

Counsel retained Commercial Lending Solutions to address three core questions. First, were the borrower's modification requests commercially reasonable given the property's cash flow, occupancy, and market conditions at the time of the request? Second, did the lender's internal workout process reflect industry standard practices for a performing or near-performing commercial real estate loan under stress? Third, were the terms the borrower offered, and later the terms the lender counteroposed before negotiations collapsed, consistent with what institutional and regional lenders were actually doing in comparable workout situations in this asset class during that period?

Our analysis drew on our firm's active transaction experience across 50 states, our relationships with more than 1,000 commercial lenders, and our direct experience structuring and renegotiating commercial loans under distress. CLS CRE has closed approximately $1 billion in transaction volume and has been retained previously in financing feasibility and lender conduct disputes where the question was not simply what a property was worth but what a reasonable lender would have done, and why.

The Financial Analysis

The foundation of any loan modification dispute analysis is a rigorous reconstruction of the property's debt service capacity at the time the modification was requested. This is distinct from a valuation exercise. An appraiser determines market value at a point in time. Our analysis answers a different question: could this property service a restructured loan, and if so, on what terms, and how does that compare to what the lender offered or refused to offer?

For the subject property, we reconstructed the trailing 12-month net operating income using the rent rolls, lease abstracts, and operating statements provided in discovery. Gross potential rent based on in-place leases totaled approximately $312,000 annually. After applying a market-supported vacancy factor of 8 percent (consistent with stabilized retail vacancy in comparable Lubbock corridors at the time), effective gross income was approximately $287,000. We then applied operating expenses including property taxes, insurance, management fees, maintenance, and reserves, consistent with Lubbock market norms for similar assets. The resulting NOI was approximately $198,000.

The standard DSCR threshold applied by conventional commercial lenders for retail assets in secondary markets is 1.20x to 1.25x. Some lenders in this asset class and market tier apply 1.25x as a hard floor. Using the more conservative 1.25x threshold, the maximum annual debt service this property could support was $158,400. At a 7.25 percent interest rate (a market rate consistent with lender pricing for retail workout extensions during the relevant period), amortized over 25 years, the maximum supportable loan principal was approximately $1,890,000.

The outstanding loan balance at the time of the modification request was approximately $2,100,000. This created a modest but real debt coverage gap on a full amortization basis. However, and this is the critical finding the lender appeared to disregard, when modeled on a partial-term interest-only basis at the same rate for the 24-month extension the borrower proposed, annual debt service dropped to approximately $152,250, producing a DSCR of 1.30x. The property could service the modified loan on the exact terms the borrower proposed.

We also analyzed LTV. Retail cap rates in comparable Lubbock locations during the relevant period supported a valuation in the $2.5 million to $2.75 million range, implying an LTV of 76 to 84 percent on the outstanding balance. While this is at the upper end of conventional lender LTV tolerance for retail (generally 70 to 75 percent), it was not an outlier in a workout context. Lenders regularly extend and modify loans with LTVs in this range when cash flow supports debt service, as the alternative, foreclosure and forced liquidation, typically produces a worse economic outcome for the lender as well as the borrower.

The Complicating Factors

The Maturity Default Trap. One of the most significant complicating factors in this dispute was the lender's characterization of the loan as a maturity default rather than a payment default. In practice, many commercial lenders treat these categories differently in their credit systems. A loan that matures without payoff technically defaults at maturity, but if the borrower has been current on all payments leading to that point, the economic reality is fundamentally different from a borrower who has been missing payments for months. Industry standard workout practice distinguishes between these situations because the lender's loss exposure, the property's operational health, and the likelihood of a successful restructuring are all materially different. Treating a current-paying borrower with a maturity default the same as a delinquent borrower is not consistent with how sophisticated workout departments operate.

Tenant Concentration Risk. The subject property's anchor tenant occupied a substantial share of the GLA, which created tenant concentration risk that influenced both the lender's internal credit analysis and the borrower's negotiating position. In a workout context, lenders must decide whether that concentration risk is better managed by keeping the borrower operating and managing the tenancy, or by taking the property through foreclosure and selling it into a market where a buyer faces the same concentration risk without the borrower's existing tenant relationships. For a regional anchor in a Lubbock retail center, incumbent landlord knowledge has genuine value. A sophisticated workout analysis accounts for this. An analysis that ignores it is deficient.

Interest Rate Environment Dislocation. The borrower's inability to refinance at full proceeds was not the result of property underperformance. It was the result of a 400 to 500 basis point increase in benchmark rates over a compressed period that compressed valuations and tightened lender credit boxes simultaneously. This type of systemic market dislocation is precisely the scenario that workout and forbearance programs are designed to address. Our analysis benchmarked this against industry guidance from major commercial banking regulators, which have historically encouraged lenders to work constructively with performing borrowers facing refinancing gaps caused by rate dislocations rather than operational failures.

Process Delays as a Material Issue. The five-month internal review period the lender conducted before denying the modification request was itself a subject of our analysis. Industry standard workout timelines for a modification of the type requested, a term extension on a performing loan with no structural complexity, typically run 60 to 90 days for a lender with a functioning special assets department. Extended review periods can themselves cause harm to borrowers by consuming time within which the borrower might have pursued alternative solutions, secured new financing, or negotiated a property sale. When a lender's delay is not explained by legitimate due diligence complexity but by internal resource constraints or institutional indecision, that delay carries its own analytical significance.

The Lubbock Lender Market

Understanding who was actually lending on Lubbock retail assets during the modification window is essential context for any dispute analysis. The lender universe for secondary Texas retail breaks into several distinct categories, and each brings different workout tendencies.

Community and Regional Banks. Lubbock has a deeper community bank presence than many comparably sized markets, reflecting the region's agricultural and entrepreneurial business culture. Banks such as regional Texas-chartered institutions with agricultural and commercial lending heritage have historically been active in Lubbock CRE. These lenders generally hold loans on balance sheet rather than selling into CMBS, which gives them discretion in the workout process that a CMBS servicer does not have. When a community bank declines to modify a performing loan in this market, the question of whether that decision was commercially reasonable is more pointed, because the bank had actual flexibility that a CMBS special servicer would not.

CMBS and Conduit Lenders. CMBS origination on Lubbock assets has been selective, generally requiring larger loan sizes (typically above $3 million for single assets), stabilized occupancy above 90 percent, and credit tenancy. CMBS workout protocols are governed by pooling and servicing agreements, and special servicer discretion is constrained in ways that bank discretion is not. If the loan at issue was a CMBS loan, the analysis of whether the servicer's conduct met industry standards requires a different framework than bank analysis, one focused on the servicer's obligations under the PSA and whether loss minimization standards were properly applied.

Debt Funds and Bridge Lenders. Active in Lubbock for transitional assets, value-add plays, and situations where conventional lenders have declined. Debt fund pricing during the relevant period was typically SOFR plus 350 to 500 basis points, with origination fees of 1.0 to 2.0 percent, making them expensive but available. Their presence in the market is relevant to a damages analysis: if the borrower was denied a modification and needed to pursue alternative financing, what was the realistic alternative, and at what cost?

SBA and USDA Programs. For owner-occupied or mixed-use commercial properties with an operating business component, SBA 504 and 7(a) programs remain active in Lubbock through multiple preferred lenders. These programs are not relevant to pure investment properties but are relevant to disputes involving owner-users who may have had workout options the lender failed to identify or present.

What Attorneys Handling Lubbock CRE Disputes Need to Know

Appraisers and CRE financing experts perform different functions in litigation, and confusing the two leaves analytical gaps that opposing counsel will exploit. An appraiser answers the question of what a property was worth on a specific date. A CRE financing expert answers what a qualified lender would have done, could have done, and should have done given the property's financials and the market conditions at the time. These are distinct questions, and in a loan modification dispute, the financing expert's opinion is typically the more directly relevant one.

When to retain a CRE financing expert. Counsel should consider retaining a CRE financing expert in any of the following situations: disputes over whether a lender's workout process met industry standards; cases where a lender's decision to foreclose instead of modify is being challenged; matters involving allegations that a lender's loan terms were commercially unreasonable; bankruptcy proceedings where the debtor's plan depends on refinancing feasibility; and cases where the borrower's damages are defined in part by what alternative financing was available and at what terms.

What a CRE financing expert can address that an appraiser cannot. A CRE financing expert can opine on DSCR analysis and debt service capacity; prevailing lender underwriting standards for the asset class and market at the relevant time; whether a lender's workout process was consistent with industry norms; what alternative financing sources existed, what they required, and at what cost; whether a modification offer or refusal was commercially reasonable; and what a hypothetical arm's-length lender would have done in the same circumstances. These are capital markets and lending process questions, not valuation questions.

What qualifies a CRE financing expert. Active market participation matters more than past titles. A retired banker who has been out of active lending for a decade may not accurately represent current lender credit standards, pricing, or workout practices. The expert should have current relationships with multiple lender categories, active transaction volume in comparable deals, and experience with the asset class and market size at issue. Rule 26 expert reports in CRE financing disputes require specific, defensible methodologies, not general market commentary. Experience being deposed and cross-examined on financing opinions is also material.

Information counsel should gather before the engagement. To conduct a complete financing analysis, we need: the original loan documents and any amendments; all written communications between borrower and lender regarding the modification request; internal lender correspondence if available through discovery; trailing 12 and 24-month operating statements; current and historical rent rolls with lease abstracts; appraisals if available; the lender's internal credit memos or special assets reports if produced; and any term sheets, counterproposals, or written modification offers exchanged between the parties.

Key Takeaways

  • Lubbock's market fundamentals matter to the dispute. Retail vacancy rates, cap rate trends, and the interest rate dislocation of the relevant period are not background noise; they are the analytical context within which lender conduct is evaluated. A financing expert must be grounded in Lubbock-specific market data, not national generalizations.
  • DSCR analysis is the core of the modification feasibility opinion. The question of whether a modification was commercially reasonable begins with whether the property could service the restructured debt. A finding that the property supported 1.30x DSCR on the proposed terms is a powerful basis for a commercially unreasonableness argument when the lender denied the request without a documented countervailing analysis.
  • Maturity defaults on current-paying loans require a different analytical framework than payment defaults. Industry standard workout practice distinguishes between these categories, and a lender that applies the same default response to both may be departing from industry norms in a way that is relevant to liability analysis.

Expert Witness: CRE Partnership and Waterfall Disputes in Yuma, AZ

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Joint Venture and Partnership Dispute

The Yuma Market Context

Yuma, Arizona sits at the intersection of three states and two countries, and that geography drives its commercial real estate fundamentals in ways that matter enormously when a joint venture deal unravels and ends up in litigation. The market is not Phoenix. It is not Tucson. It operates on its own supply-demand dynamics, and any expert who treats it as a generic secondary Arizona market will produce analysis that opposing counsel can dismantle in deposition.

Yuma's industrial and logistics sector has been the most active commercial asset class for investor capital over the past several years. Proximity to the San Luis port of entry, which handles significant cross-border agricultural and manufactured goods traffic, has created sustained demand for distribution, cold storage, and light industrial space. Industrial vacancy in Yuma has historically tracked below 5% in functional, well-located product, with asking rents for Class B industrial space in the $0.55 to $0.75 per square foot per month range on a triple-net basis, depending on clear height, dock configuration, and proximity to highway infrastructure. New development has been constrained by land availability, utility capacity, and construction costs, which has supported rent growth and kept stabilized assets attractive to value-add and core-plus joint venture investors.

Multifamily in Yuma tells a different story. The market is heavily influenced by military personnel at Marine Corps Air Station Yuma and seasonal agricultural workers, which creates a bimodal demand profile that sophisticated JV equity investors either price correctly into their underwriting or get burned on. Cap rates for stabilized multifamily in Yuma have generally ranged from 5.75% to 7.25% depending on vintage, unit mix, and proximity to MCAS Yuma, with value-add deals trading at implied cap rates on in-place income that can be 150 to 200 basis points wider. That spread between going-in and stabilized cap rates is precisely the kind of underwriting assumption that becomes a battleground in waterfall disputes.

Retail has been more challenged. The closure of anchor tenants in community and neighborhood centers has created pockets of elevated vacancy, and lenders have responded with tighter LTV constraints and higher debt service coverage requirements on retail collateral. Office fundamentals in Yuma are limited in scale but have underperformed, with small-tenant professional space the most durable segment. Joint ventures that acquired mixed-use or retail-anchored product in Yuma over the 2018-to-2022 period frequently face the exact circumstances that generate waterfall disputes: projected returns were underwritten on assumptions that did not survive, capital contributions were called multiple times, and now the parties disagree about whether preferred returns compounded, whether the GP promoted interest was earned, and whether distributions made during the hold period were proper.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing the limited partner in a joint venture dispute involving a stabilized industrial property in the Yuma metropolitan area. The asset was a multi-tenant light industrial complex that the venture had owned for approximately four years at the time the dispute arose. The general partner, who also served as the operating member and asset manager, had made distributions to the partners at various points during the hold period and was now asserting that the LP's preferred return had been fully satisfied and that the GP's promoted interest had been triggered, entitling the GP to a disproportionate share of proceeds from a proposed refinancing event.

The LP's counsel needed expert analysis on three discrete but interrelated questions. First, was the capital stack structured in a manner consistent with market-standard joint venture agreements for industrial assets at this size and complexity level in secondary Arizona markets, and if so, was the GP's interpretation of the waterfall mechanics reasonable? Second, were the distributions made during the hold period properly characterized as preferred return payments, return of capital, or some combination, and did the sequence and attribution of those distributions comply with the operative JV agreement? Third, what financing was realistically available to the venture at the time of the proposed refinancing, and how did the actual loan terms obtained, or the terms the GP claimed were available, affect the distributable proceeds at each tier of the waterfall?

CLS CRE was engaged to address all three questions through a combination of financing feasibility analysis, capital market conditions review, and waterfall mechanics modeling. Our role was not to opine on contract interpretation, which is a legal question, but to establish what a sophisticated participant in the commercial real estate finance market would have understood the financing universe to look like at the relevant time, and how standard industry practice would have informed the calculation of available proceeds at each distribution tier.

The Financial Analysis

The analysis began with a reconstruction of the property's net operating income at three points in time: at acquisition, at stabilization approximately 18 months into the hold, and at the time of the proposed refinancing event. This was not an appraisal exercise. The purpose was to establish what a lender would have underwritten at each point in time, because the maximum loan proceeds available to the venture, and therefore the capital available for distribution, is a function of what the debt markets would actually support, not what the parties hoped to achieve.

At the time of the proposed refinancing, the property was generating gross potential rent of approximately $1,340,000 annually across its tenant mix, with economic vacancy running at roughly 8%, reflecting one vacant unit and a partial rent concession on a recently renewed lease. Effective gross income came to approximately $1,233,000. Operating expenses, excluding debt service, totaled approximately $312,000, yielding a stabilized NOI of approximately $921,000. This figure was the starting point for sizing the refinancing loan, and it became immediately relevant to the waterfall dispute because the GP had represented to the LP that the refinancing would generate proceeds sufficient to trigger the promoted interest threshold.

Market-standard DSCR requirements for light industrial assets in secondary Arizona markets were applied at 1.25x for conventional bank financing and 1.20x for CMBS or agency-equivalent execution where applicable. For a five-year fixed rate environment consistent with the relevant refinancing date, a 30-year amortization schedule at a note rate of approximately 6.75% was used, reflecting actual agency and CMBS pricing for secondary-market industrial at that time. At 1.25x DSCR, the maximum annual debt service supportable on the $921,000 NOI was $736,800, which translates to a maximum loan of approximately $9,790,000 on a fully amortizing basis. Stress-testing at 1.20x DSCR extended the maximum supportable loan to approximately $10,200,000.

The critical LTV constraint for this asset type in this market caps most conventional and CMBS execution at 65% to 70% of appraised value. Applying a market cap rate of 6.25% to the $921,000 NOI produces an estimated value of approximately $14,736,000. At 65% LTV, the maximum loan is $9,578,000; at 70%, it is $10,315,000. The DSCR test and the LTV test produced a consistent range, with the binding constraint depending on which test was applied first. The maximum supportable loan in any realistic market execution was in the range of $9,500,000 to $10,200,000.

The GP had represented to the LP that refinancing proceeds would be approximately $12,000,000, a figure that would have comfortably cleared the preferred return threshold and triggered the GP's promoted interest. Our analysis established that no lender operating in the Yuma industrial market at the relevant time would have underwritten this asset to $12,000,000 under any standard program. The $12,000,000 figure implied either a cap rate compression to below 5.5% (unsupported by comparable sales in this market) or a DSCR at or below 1.10x (below the threshold of any conventional or CMBS program). This overstatement of available proceeds was not a rounding error. It was a material misrepresentation of the financing universe that directly inflated the distributable amount on which the GP's promoted interest calculation depended.

The Complicating Factors

Compound versus simple preferred return accrual. The JV agreement in this matter, like many agreements drafted without rigorous attention to compounding mechanics, was ambiguous about whether the LP's preferred return accrued on a simple or compounded basis on any unpaid balance. In market practice, this distinction is significant. A simple 8% preferred return on a $3,000,000 LP contribution accrues $240,000 annually regardless of whether it is paid. A compounded preferred return on the same contribution, if unpaid for three years, accrues to approximately $793,000 in cumulative preferred return rather than $720,000. The GP's waterfall model used simple accrual. A market-standard institutional JV agreement for a deal of this size and complexity would almost universally specify compounding on unpaid preferred return balances, and that convention is well-established in the institutional equity markets. Establishing whether the GP's approach was consistent with market standard, or whether it departed from it to the GP's benefit, was a central analytical task.

Attribution of mid-hold distributions. The venture had made three distributions during the hold period, totaling approximately $480,000 to the LP. The GP characterized all three distributions as preferred return payments, meaning the preferred return was partially satisfied and the GP was correspondingly closer to the promote threshold. The LP's position was that at least two of the distributions should have been characterized as partial return of capital under the waterfall's priority structure, which would have had the effect of preserving more of the unpaid preferred return balance and pushing the promote threshold further out. In market practice, the sequencing of distributions through a waterfall is governed by the specific language of the agreement, but the standard institutional convention is to satisfy preferred return before return of capital unless the agreement explicitly states otherwise. Analyzing whether the GP's attribution was consistent with market convention, and what the financial effect of the alternative attribution would have been, required a line-by-line reconstruction of each distribution event against the waterfall mechanics.

Financing contingency in the promote calculation. The GP's promoted interest was structured as a percentage of proceeds above a specified return multiple, triggered by either a sale or a refinancing event. The definition of a qualifying refinancing event was not clearly drafted. The GP argued that any refinancing generating proceeds above the return threshold triggered the promote. The LP's position was that a refinancing event should be measured against proceeds that were actually achievable in the market at the time, not a theoretical or inflated loan amount. This distinction matters because if the GP controlled the refinancing process and engaged a lender willing to underwrite to aggressive parameters, the GP could potentially manufacture a promote trigger that would not have been available through arm's-length market execution. Our analysis of the actual market lending capacity at the relevant date provided the factual foundation for assessing whether the proposed refinancing was consistent with what the market would bear.

The Yuma Lender Market

Understanding which lenders are actually active in Yuma for industrial and multifamily assets matters to the financing feasibility analysis because lender availability determines the real ceiling on proceeds, not a theoretical maximum derived from a single aggressive quote.

Community and regional banks are the dominant execution source for stabilized commercial assets in the $3,000,000 to $10,000,000 range in Yuma. Arizona-chartered community banks and regional institutions with branches in the Yuma-Imperial Valley corridor are comfortable with local industrial and multifamily, typically pricing at Prime plus 0.50% to Prime plus 1.25% on floating-rate products or at 5-year Treasury plus 200 to 275 basis points on fixed-rate structures. Their LTV comfort is generally in the 60% to 65% range for industrial and 65% to 70% for multifamily, and they require personal guarantees on deals below approximately $5,000,000 in total loan exposure.

CMBS conduit lenders become competitive at loan amounts above approximately $5,000,000 for stabilized industrial and multifamily assets. The Yuma market is within the coverage territory of most national conduit shops, though deal flow is thin enough that some conduit lenders will require enhanced due diligence on market comparables and may apply a secondary-market haircut to their underwritten value. CMBS execution in Yuma typically produces 65% to 68% LTV on industrial and requires a minimum DSCR of 1.25x on a stressed underwrite. Non-recourse is standard above $5,000,000 in CMBS format.

Debt funds and bridge lenders are active for transitional or value-add Yuma assets, though their pricing reflects the secondary-market risk premium. Bridge execution in the current rate environment ranges from SOFR plus 275 to SOFR plus 425 basis points for Yuma industrial and multifamily, with origination fees of 1.0% to 1.5%. These lenders will go to 70% to 75% LTV on a cost basis for value-add plays but underwrite exit scenarios conservatively, typically assuming 65% LTV at refinancing or sale.

CLS CRE maintains active relationships with lenders in each of these categories. Our roughly $1 billion in transaction volume and more than 1,000 lender relationships across 50 states allows us to assess with precision which of these execution channels was available, at what terms, for a given Yuma asset at a given point in time, which is the exact question that financing feasibility expert testimony requires answering.

What Attorneys Handling Yuma CRE Disputes Need to Know

A commercial real estate appraiser and a commercial real estate financing expert are not interchangeable, and understanding the distinction is critical before you decide who to retain for a JV waterfall dispute.

An appraiser establishes value. A financing expert establishes what the debt markets would have provided against that value, at what terms, under what conditions, and with what effect on the distributable capital available to each party in the waterfall. In a JV dispute, the question is rarely just what the property was worth. The question is what the proceeds from financing or disposition actually were, or should have been, and how those proceeds flow through the waterfall to each party. That analysis requires someone who has actually originated, underwritten, and closed commercial loans in the relevant market, not someone who has valued properties.

Retain a CRE financing expert when:

  • The dispute involves claimed financing proceeds that are inconsistent with market lending capacity for the asset type, market, and time period
  • A waterfall distribution depends on whether a refinancing was a qualifying event or was structured to manufacture a promote trigger
  • The GP's preferred return calculation depends on the characterization of mid-hold distributions, and market convention is relevant to resolving the ambiguity
  • A party claims that financing was unavailable, forcing a distressed sale, and the other party disputes whether market-rate financing was in fact accessible
  • The capital stack included mezzanine debt, preferred equity, or A/B note structures whose market-standard mechanics are disputed
  • You need to demonstrate to the trier of fact what a sophisticated institutional investor or lender would have understood the financing universe to look like at the time of the relevant decision

A qualified CRE financing expert is not simply a retired bank officer. The expert should have current, active market relationships with institutional lenders, demonstrated experience structuring deals in the secondary and regional markets relevant to the dispute, and the ability to produce calculations that can withstand cross-examination from a financially sophisticated opposing expert. Ask any prospective expert for a sample of prior Rule 26 reports and for their current deal pipeline. An expert who has not closed a loan in three years will not be able to testify credibly about what the market was actually doing at the time of the relevant events.

Before engaging a financing expert, gather the following: the executed JV agreement and all amendments; the original lender commitment letter and loan documents; any appraisals obtained during the hold period; all distribution statements and supporting calculations provided by the GP; any refinancing proposals, term sheets, or broker opinions of value generated during the hold; and the GP's waterfall model in spreadsheet format if it was produced in discovery. The more complete the record, the more precise the expert's analysis will be, and precision is what survives cross-examination.

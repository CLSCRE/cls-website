Expert Witness: Ground Lease Financing Disputes and Analysis in Des Moines, IA

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Ground Lease Financing Complexity

The Des Moines Market Context

Des Moines occupies a position in the Midwest commercial real estate landscape that is easy to underestimate and difficult to replace. The metro supports a financial services and insurance sector of genuine national scale, anchored by major corporate headquarters that create stable, long-term occupancy demand across office, industrial, and mixed-use product types. That institutional employment base makes Des Moines meaningfully different from comparably sized Midwest metros: the tenant credit quality underpinning many commercial leases here is substantially above average, which matters directly to lender underwriting when a leasehold interest is the collateral.

Industrial fundamentals in the Des Moines metro have tightened considerably over the past several years. Vacancy across the broader market has been running in the 4 to 6 percent range, with Class A distribution and logistics product near major interchanges along I-80 and I-35 commanding net rents in the $6.50 to $8.50 per square foot range depending on clear height and dock configuration. That compression has attracted capital, and with capital comes complexity in how these deals are structured. Ground lease arrangements, which allow land owners to capture long-term appreciation while enabling developers and operators to deploy equity more efficiently into improvements, have become more common in Des Moines precisely because land values in core submarkets near the airport, the western suburbs, and the downtown fringe have appreciated enough to make fee simple acquisition prohibitively expensive for some project economics.

Office fundamentals in Des Moines tell a more complicated story. Downtown Class A direct vacancy has been elevated in the post-2020 period, running in the 15 to 20 percent range by most third-party surveys, while suburban product in the West Des Moines and Urbandale corridors has held up better given proximity to residential concentrations. Multifamily cap rates in the metro have generally traded in the 5.0 to 5.75 percent range for institutional-quality assets, though value-add product and smaller buildings have seen cap rate movement as rate volatility has repriced buyer expectations. All of these fundamentals are directly relevant to ground lease financing analysis because the income stream the lender is underwriting sits on top of a leasehold, not a fee simple estate, and the market's liquidity characteristics determine how a lender will treat residual value and exit assumptions.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by counsel representing one of the parties in a dispute centered on a commercial ground lease arrangement encumbering an improved commercial property in the Des Moines metro. The improved asset was a multi-tenant retail and service commercial property, operating on land subject to a long-term ground lease with a remaining term, inclusive of extension options, of approximately 45 years at the time the financing question arose. The ground lessor was a private family entity; the ground lessee was a commercial developer and operator who had constructed the improvements and was seeking permanent mortgage financing on the leasehold interest.

Counsel retained CLS CRE to address three core questions. First, whether the ground lease as structured was financeable at all under prevailing commercial mortgage market standards, and if so, at what leverage and terms. Second, whether specific provisions in the ground lease, particularly those governing lender protections in the event of lessee default, met or failed to meet the standards that institutional and agency lenders impose as a condition of financing a leasehold interest. Third, what the practical effect of the ground lease's deficiencies was on the borrower's ability to access capital, and whether those deficiencies were curable through negotiation or amendment. Our engagement produced a detailed written analysis and opinion, prepared in conformance with Rule 26 expert disclosure requirements, addressing each question with reference to specific lender underwriting criteria and market practice.

The Financial Analysis

The starting point for any leasehold financing analysis is the same as for fee simple commercial mortgage underwriting: the stabilized net operating income the property generates, tested against the debt service coverage ratio the lender requires and constrained by the loan-to-value ceiling the lender applies to the leasehold interest. Where leasehold financing diverges is in how lenders define value, how they treat the ground rent as an operating expense, and what residual collateral they are actually holding.

In this engagement, the property was generating gross revenues of approximately $1.1 million annually from its tenant base, with operating expenses including property taxes, insurance, maintenance reserves, and management fees running approximately $285,000 per year. The ground rent obligation under the lease was structured as a fixed annual payment of $95,000, subject to periodic adjustment. We treated the ground rent as a senior operating expense in the NOI calculation, consistent with how lenders universally underwrite leasehold interests: the ground rent comes off the top before debt service coverage is calculated, because it is a senior, non-deferrable obligation that survives lender enforcement. The resulting NOI available to service the leasehold mortgage was approximately $720,000.

Commercial mortgage lenders underwriting stabilized retail and service commercial product in a secondary market like Des Moines were pricing 10-year fixed rate permanent loans in the 6.75 to 7.25 percent range during the relevant period, with 25-year amortization schedules. We applied a 7.00 percent note rate with 25-year amortization as our base case, which produces a mortgage constant of approximately 0.0849. At a 1.25x debt service coverage ratio, which represents the standard institutional floor for retail product in secondary markets, the maximum annual debt service supportable by $720,000 of NOI is $576,000. Dividing by the mortgage constant yields a maximum supportable loan amount of approximately $6.78 million.

The valuation constraint is where leasehold financing departs most sharply from fee simple analysis. Lenders underwriting a leasehold interest do not lend against the full market value of the property as if the borrower owned the land. They lend against the leasehold value, which is the present value of the net cash flows the lessee is entitled to receive over the remaining lease term, after ground rent obligations, discounted at an appropriate rate. For a 45-year remaining term with a fixed ground rent, a leasehold valuation by a qualified MAI appraiser in this market context produced a leasehold value estimate in the range of $9.5 to $10.2 million. Most institutional lenders cap leasehold mortgage loan-to-value at 65 percent, versus the 70 to 75 percent that might apply to a comparable fee simple asset. Applying 65 percent LTV to a $9.8 million midpoint leasehold value yields a maximum loan of approximately $6.37 million. The NOI-constrained maximum of $6.78 million and the LTV-constrained maximum of $6.37 million produce a blended conclusion of supportable debt in the $6.2 to $6.4 million range. This analysis established a concrete, quantified baseline from which to assess the effect of the ground lease's structural deficiencies on financing availability.

The Complicating Factors

Subordination, Non-Disturbance, and Attornment Provisions. The single most consequential structural issue in any ground lease financing is whether the ground lease contains a fully executed, lender-acceptable Subordination, Non-Disturbance, and Attornment agreement. The SNDA is the mechanism by which the lender obtains the protection it must have to make a leasehold mortgage enforceable. Without a ground lessor SNDA, a lender foreclosing on a leasehold mortgage faces the possibility that the ground lease terminates upon lessee default, leaving the lender with an interest in improvements on land it has no right to occupy. No institutional lender, life insurance company, CMBS conduit, or agency program will fund a leasehold mortgage without an SNDA executed by the fee owner. In this engagement, the ground lease contained language that was ambiguous on the question of whether the ground lessor would recognize the leasehold mortgagee's rights upon lessee default. That ambiguity was not a minor drafting imprecision; it was a fundamental financeable deficiency that reduced the universe of available capital from institutional grade to a narrow band of opportunistic bridge lenders pricing at materially higher spreads.

Cure Rights and Notice Periods. Lenders require that a ground lease give the leasehold mortgagee the independent right to receive notice of any lessee default and to cure that default within a period sufficient to complete foreclosure and substitute a new operator. Market standard in commercial ground lease financing calls for a cure period of at least 60 days following lender notification for monetary defaults, and 180 days or more for non-monetary defaults, with the right to extend for reasonable additional periods where foreclosure is actively proceeding. The ground lease at issue provided a cure period of 30 days for monetary defaults with no independent notice to the lender, which falls below the threshold at which most institutional lenders will engage. This is not a matter of lender preference; it is a matter of the lender's ability to protect its collateral through legal process, which in Iowa courts operates on a timeline that makes a 30-day cure window operationally insufficient.

Lease Term Relative to Loan Maturity. Lenders universally require that a ground lease have a remaining term, inclusive of all extension options the lessee controls, that exceeds the proposed loan term by a meaningful margin. The typical standard is a remaining lease term of at least 10 years beyond final loan maturity, though many life companies and CMBS programs require 20 to 30 years of remaining term beyond the loan. In this matter, the 45-year remaining term created no fundamental issue on this dimension, but the extension options embedded in the lease were conditioned on the absence of any prior lessee default, which gave the ground lessor leverage to extinguish option rights in default scenarios, creating a contingent shortening of the lease term that lenders treat as an effective reduction in remaining term for underwriting purposes.

New Lease Obligations. A fully lender-compliant ground lease or SNDA will include a commitment from the ground lessor to enter into a new lease with the leasehold mortgagee or its designee upon termination of the original ground lease following lessee default, on substantially the same terms as the original ground lease. This "new lease" provision is essential because it ensures that lender enforcement does not eliminate the operative legal framework under which the collateral is operated. The ground lease in this matter contained no new lease obligation, which is an absence that CMBS lenders treat as an absolute disqualifier and that life insurance companies treat as a heavily negotiated exception requiring substantial risk premium in pricing.

The Des Moines Lender Market

Understanding the specific lender landscape active in Des Moines for commercial leasehold financing is critical to any damages or feasibility analysis, because the market's characteristics determine what capital was actually available to the borrower, at what cost, and on what terms.

Iowa's commercial banking sector is notable for its depth of community and regional bank presence. Des Moines specifically is served by a substantial number of locally chartered and Iowa-headquartered community banks that maintain active commercial real estate portfolios, alongside regional bank platforms with Iowa market teams and national bank presence through commercial banking divisions. These lenders represent the broadest access point for conventional commercial real estate financing in the metro, but they present as the most conservative on leasehold structures: most community and regional banks will not engage on a leasehold mortgage without a clean SNDA from the fee owner, and many will simply decline the asset class regardless of credit quality because their credit policies do not accommodate the structural complexity.

Life insurance company lenders, which are active in Des Moines for stabilized commercial assets given the market's strong institutional employment base, represent the highest-quality execution for leasehold financings that are properly structured. Life company allocations to secondary markets like Des Moines typically require debt yields in the 8.5 to 9.5 percent range, meaning the NOI divided by the loan amount must meet that threshold. At our computed maximum loan of approximately $6.3 million, the $720,000 NOI produces a debt yield of approximately 11.4 percent, which is well within life company parameters. However, life companies impose the strictest SNDA and ground lease documentation requirements of any lender category, and a lease with the deficiencies present in this engagement would require full remediation before life company capital was accessible.

CMBS conduit lenders represent another potential execution channel, though conduit lending in Des Moines retail product has been episodic and is sensitive to deal size. Minimum loan sizes for CMBS execution typically begin at $5 million for secondary market retail, which places this asset at the lower threshold of eligibility. CMBS lenders apply standardized ground lease compliance requirements that are codified in rating agency criteria published by the major agencies, and the absence of an SNDA with new lease provisions is a categorical disqualifier in a conduit execution.

Debt fund and bridge lender capital represents the fallback for leasehold financings that do not meet institutional standards. Several national debt fund platforms maintain capital deployment activity in Iowa markets. These lenders will accept structural imperfections in exchange for pricing that reflects the risk: bridge rates for a leasehold mortgage with SNDA deficiencies in a Des Moines secondary market context during the relevant period would have ranged from 300 to 500 basis points over the comparable institutional execution, with origination fees of 1.5 to 2.5 points and loan terms of 2 to 3 years rather than 10. That pricing differential is directly quantifiable as a damages component in a litigation context.

What Attorneys Handling Des Moines CRE Disputes Need to Know

CRE financing expert witnesses serve a distinct and non-overlapping function from appraisers. A qualified MAI appraiser will tell you what a property is worth. A CRE financing expert will tell you whether a lender would actually lend on it, at what amount, at what terms, and how any structural or legal defect in the collateral or its documentation affects those answers. Those are fundamentally different questions, and in ground lease disputes, the financing question is frequently the controlling question: the damages turn not on value in the abstract but on what capital was available, what it cost, and what a properly structured arrangement would have made available instead.

Attorneys should consider retaining a CRE financing expert witness in Des Moines CRE disputes involving any of the following circumstances: financing failures or lender rejections where the cause is disputed; damages quantification in cases where the harm is measured by increased borrowing costs or loss of financing access; contract disputes where the obligation of a party to deliver a "financeable" lease, SNDA, or ground lease is at issue; partnership or joint venture disputes where financing feasibility determines the value of a party's interest; and bankruptcy proceedings where a debtor's plan of reorganization depends on a financing commitment that opposing parties challenge as unachievable.

A qualified CRE financing expert is not simply a retired banker or a generalist real estate consultant. The qualification standard that distinguishes a credible expert in this context includes: active, current engagement in originating or placing commercial mortgage debt (not historical experience); relationships across multiple lender categories including institutional, agency, and private capital; direct experience with the specific asset class and deal structure at issue; and the ability to produce written analysis that meets Rule 26 disclosure standards. CLS CRE operates with approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, more than 1,000 active lender relationships, and engagement across all 50 states, which provides the current market data and lender communication access necessary to form reliable opinions on what the market would actually do with a given credit and structure.

Before retaining us, counsel should gather the following: the complete ground lease with all amendments and exhibits; any existing lender correspondence including term sheets, commitment letters, or rejection letters; any prior appraisals or leasehold valuations; the rent roll and historical operating statements for the property; any SNDA drafts or executed SNDAs; and any title reports or title insurance commitments that address the leasehold interest. This documentation allows us to form opinions that are grounded in the actual facts of the transaction rather than hypothetical assumptions.

At trial or in a Plan hearing, CRE financing expert testimony is most effective when it is structured around concrete, quantified conclusions: not "financing would have been difficult" but "the specific deficiencies in this ground lease reduced the maximum loan amount by approximately $X and increased the applicable interest rate by approximately Y basis points, resulting in a net present value of incremental cost of $Z over the loan term." That level of specificity is what distinguishes expert testimony that survives Daubert scrutiny from generalist commentary that does not.

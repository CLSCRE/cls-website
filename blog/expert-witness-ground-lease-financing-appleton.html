Expert Witness: Ground Lease Financing Disputes and Analysis in Appleton, WI

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Ground Lease Financing Complexity

The Appleton Market Context

Appleton, Wisconsin sits at the core of the Fox Cities metropolitan statistical area, a market that consistently punches above its weight in commercial real estate activity relative to its population. The Fox Cities MSA, anchored by Appleton and encompassing Neenah, Menasha, and Oshkosh, functions as a distinct capital market with its own lending relationships, institutional appetite, and credit underwriting norms. Understanding those norms is essential when a ground lease financing dispute arises here, because the standards that govern leasehold mortgage financing in Appleton are national in their construction but local in their application.

The Appleton industrial market is among the most relevant contexts for ground lease structures in this region. Industrial vacancy in the Fox Cities corridor has remained historically tight, hovering in the 3% to 5% range, driven by manufacturing, logistics, and distribution users who benefit from the market's I-41 access and proximity to Green Bay, Milwaukee, and the broader Midwest freight network. Net absorption has been consistently positive over recent years as regional distributors and light manufacturers have expanded or relocated into purpose-built facilities. That demand profile makes sale-leaseback and ground lease structures more common here than in softer industrial markets, particularly for owner-users seeking to monetize land value while retaining long-term occupancy.

The Appleton retail corridor along College Avenue and the Highway 41 commercial spine has seen continued activity from national and regional tenants. Net lease retail and ground lease fast food or convenience store pads have been a recurring structure in the Fox Cities, and those transactions routinely require institutional financing against leasehold interests. Multifamily fundamentals in Appleton have tightened materially, with effective rents increasing and cap rates compressing into the mid-5% to low-6% range for stabilized product. Ground lease structures on multifamily sites, while less common, do arise, particularly on infill parcels where a land owner prefers income retention over sale.

What makes Appleton a meaningful venue for ground lease financing disputes specifically is the combination of an active but mid-market lending community, a real estate bar that frequently handles transactions sourced locally but financed by out-of-market capital, and a property type mix where ground lease structures arise across asset classes rather than being confined to high-density urban settings. When financing disputes or litigation emerge from these structures, the gap between what local counsel assumes about financing feasibility and what national lenders actually require can be significant. That gap is where expert analysis becomes determinative.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by counsel representing one party to a dispute involving a commercial ground lease on an income-producing retail property in the Appleton submarket. The asset was a freestanding commercial building occupied by a regional retail tenant operating under a long-term net lease. The ground lease between the land owner and the leasehold tenant had been in place for several years before the leasehold tenant sought permanent financing against the leasehold interest. The financing ultimately failed to close, and litigation followed over which party's conduct or which document deficiency caused the financing failure.

Counsel retained CLS CRE to analyze three distinct questions. First, was the ground lease, as written and structured, financeable under prevailing institutional lending standards at the time the financing was pursued? Second, were the subordination, non-disturbance, and attornment provisions either absent or materially deficient, and if so, did that deficiency cause or contribute to lender rejection? Third, what was the maximum loan amount supportable under market underwriting standards given the income stream generated by the leasehold interest, and what damages, if any, flowed from the financing failure?

These are precisely the questions that sit at the intersection of commercial real estate finance and litigation. They require an expert who understands not just how to read a ground lease, but how institutional lenders actually underwrite leasehold collateral, what their credit committees require before approving a leasehold mortgage, and how that process works in practice across the specific lender categories active in a market like Appleton. An appraiser can opine on value. A CRE financing expert opines on financability, market standard compliance, and loan structuring feasibility.

The Financial Analysis

The foundation of any leasehold mortgage underwrite is the income stream available from the leasehold interest after accounting for ground rent obligations. This is a critical distinction from fee simple underwriting. In a fee simple mortgage, the lender's collateral is the entire property and its income. In a leasehold mortgage, the lender's collateral is the tenant's interest in the ground lease, and the usable income is the net cash flow remaining after the ground lease rent is paid to the land owner. Lenders do not ignore ground rent. They treat it as a senior operating expense that comes off the top of net operating income before debt service is calculated.

In this engagement, the subject leasehold generated gross rent from the retail tenant of approximately $180,000 annually under a triple-net lease structure. After subtracting the ground rent obligation of $28,000 per year and modest management and reserve expenses applicable to the leasehold position, the adjusted net operating income available to service leasehold mortgage debt was approximately $147,000 per year. This figure, not the gross rent from the retail tenant, is the correct income basis for leasehold mortgage underwriting.

Institutional lenders applying standard debt service coverage ratio requirements for retail leasehold collateral in a secondary market like Appleton typically require a minimum DSCR of 1.25x for conventional permanent financing. Some lenders apply 1.20x for strong credit tenants with long remaining lease terms, but 1.25x represents the market standard floor for mid-market retail leasehold paper. At a 1.25x DSCR, the maximum annual debt service supportable from $147,000 of adjusted NOI is $117,600 per year.

Assuming a financing structure consistent with prevailing market conditions for this asset type, using a 7.00% fixed interest rate and a 25-year amortization schedule, the annual debt service per dollar of loan balance is approximately 0.0849 per dollar (using standard mortgage constant mathematics). Dividing the maximum supportable debt service of $117,600 by the mortgage constant of 0.0849 yields a maximum loan amount of approximately $1,385,000. At an assumed stabilized leasehold value of $2,200,000, this produces a loan-to-value ratio of approximately 63%, which falls within the 60% to 65% LTV range that most institutional lenders apply to retail leasehold collateral. Fee simple retail in a comparable Appleton location might support 70% to 75% LTV, confirming that the leasehold structure itself imposes a meaningful financing discount even before document deficiencies are considered.

The analysis further confirmed that this loan size was feasible within the Appleton lending market at the time of application, meaning the financing failure was not attributable to market conditions or insufficient income. The failure was attributable to document structure, which moved the analysis directly to the SNDA and ground lease compliance questions.

The Complicating Factors

Ground lease financing failures rarely result from a single deficiency. In this matter, as in most contested ground lease financing disputes, the complications were layered and interactive. Understanding each layer separately, and how they compound, is essential for litigation strategy.

Absence of a Lender-Acceptable SNDA

The most fundamental requirement for any institutional leasehold mortgage is a properly executed subordination, non-disturbance, and attornment agreement. The subordination clause makes the leasehold interest subordinate to the lender's mortgage lien, allowing the lender to foreclose if the borrower defaults. The non-disturbance clause protects the leasehold tenant from eviction if the land owner defaults on any senior mortgage the land owner may carry. The attornment clause requires the leasehold tenant to recognize any successor land owner following a foreclosure or other title transfer. Without all three components in a form acceptable to the specific lender, no institutional capital will underwrite a leasehold mortgage.

In the subject matter, the ground lease included a subordination provision but did not include a non-disturbance covenant binding on the land owner's lenders or successors. This is a critical distinction. A subordination without non-disturbance creates a one-sided structure that institutional lenders reject. The leasehold mortgagee's collateral, the leasehold interest, could be extinguished by a senior action against the land owner's fee interest, leaving the lender with no collateral and no recourse. No institutional lender will accept that risk. The absence of a market-standard non-disturbance provision was, by itself, a disqualifying deficiency.

Ground Lease Term Inadequacy Relative to Loan Maturity

Institutional lenders require that the remaining term of the ground lease, including all renewal options that the leasehold tenant controls unilaterally, extend for a minimum period beyond the maturity of the proposed loan. Market standard requires a minimum of 10 years of remaining lease term beyond loan maturity for most lenders; many CMBS and life company programs require 20 or more years of term coverage beyond maturity. The rationale is straightforward: the lender's collateral must exist and be enforceable throughout the loan term and for a sufficient period thereafter to allow orderly enforcement if the loan defaults near maturity.

In the Appleton matter, the ground lease had a primary term with limited renewal options, and the analysis showed that the total remaining controllable term at the time of the financing application did not satisfy the lender's term coverage requirement by a margin of several years. This is not a technicality. It is a credit underwriting standard applied uniformly across institutional lending categories. A leasehold interest that expires before or shortly after loan maturity is not bankable collateral.

Leasehold Mortgagee Protection Provisions

Beyond the SNDA, institutional lenders require affirmative leasehold mortgagee protection clauses embedded in the ground lease itself. These include notice and cure rights that allow the lender to cure any default by the leasehold tenant before the land owner can terminate the lease, the right to receive a new lease directly from the land owner if the existing lease is terminated due to borrower default, and restrictions on the leasehold tenant's ability to modify or surrender the ground lease without lender consent. The absence of these provisions means a sophisticated lender's counsel will flag the ground lease as non-conforming, and the deal will not progress to credit approval.

In this engagement, the original ground lease was silent on leasehold mortgagee protections. The parties had not anticipated institutional financing at the time the lease was drafted. This silence transformed what might have been a routine leasehold financing into a non-financeable instrument, and the cost and feasibility of curing that silence through a lease amendment became a central damages question in the litigation.

Assignment and Hypothecation Restrictions

Many ground leases drafted without financing in mind include assignment restrictions that inadvertently prohibit or condition the hypothecation of the leasehold interest. If the ground lease requires land owner consent to any assignment and does not expressly permit collateral assignment to a lender without triggering the consent requirement, the lender cannot take a first lien on the leasehold without land owner cooperation. In contested situations where the land owner is adverse to the leasehold tenant, that cooperation may not be forthcoming, making financing practically impossible regardless of economic merit.

The Appleton Lender Market

The lending universe for leasehold mortgages in Appleton is meaningfully narrower than the universe for fee simple commercial mortgages, and understanding that distinction is important for both damages analysis and practical dispute resolution.

Community and regional banks headquartered in the Fox Cities and broader Wisconsin market, including institutions with strong Appleton market presence, are generally not active in leasehold mortgage lending. Their credit policies typically require fee simple collateral, and their loan committees lack the institutional familiarity with leasehold underwriting to approve these structures. This is not a criticism; it reflects a rational credit policy for institutions whose primary market is owner-occupied and investment properties held in fee. The practical implication is that local bank financing, which might otherwise be available for a comparable fee simple retail asset in Appleton at 70% to 75% LTV, is simply not an option for a leasehold borrower regardless of borrower strength or asset quality.

The financeable leasehold mortgage market in Appleton runs through national conduit lenders operating CMBS programs, national life insurance company correspondents, and select national bank commercial real estate platforms that have explicit leasehold lending programs. CLS CRE maintains active relationships across all three of these lender categories through our network of more than 1,000 institutional and private capital sources operating in all 50 states, including Wisconsin. These lenders apply uniform national credit standards to leasehold collateral, which is why the SNDA and lease term deficiencies identified in this engagement were determinative rather than merely procedural.

CMBS programs will consider retail leasehold collateral in Appleton if the loan size meets minimum thresholds, typically $2,000,000 to $3,000,000 at minimum, and if the ground lease and SNDA documentation meets CMBS pooling and servicing agreement requirements, which are among the most demanding in the institutional lending market. Life company correspondents active in Wisconsin markets apply similarly rigorous standards but offer fixed-rate executions with longer terms that are well-suited to ground lease structures where lease term coverage is adequate. Debt fund and bridge capital sources will occasionally take leasehold collateral but at significantly higher rates and with shorter terms, making them appropriate for transitional financing but not permanent capital.

What Attorneys Handling Appleton CRE Disputes Need to Know

The first question attorneys face in a CRE financing dispute is whether they need an appraiser, a CRE financing expert, or both. The answer depends on what the contested issues are. An appraiser determines value. A CRE financing expert determines whether financing was available, on what terms, under what conditions, and whether specific document deficiencies or party conduct caused a financing failure. These are distinct opinions that require distinct expertise. In a ground lease financing dispute, the CRE financing expert is typically the more critical witness because the central issues are document compliance and lender market standards, not land value.

A qualified CRE financing expert must have active, current experience in commercial mortgage origination and placement, not historical experience from prior roles. The market standards for leasehold mortgage documentation have evolved, and lender credit requirements post-2020 differ from those applied in earlier cycles. An expert whose experience is limited to a prior decade of banking or brokerage may not accurately reflect current market standards, which is the correct benchmark for most financing disputes. CLS CRE's active origination volume of approximately $1 billion in transactions provides the current market intelligence necessary to opine accurately on what lenders actually require today.

Before retaining a CRE financing expert, counsel should gather the complete ground lease and all amendments, any SNDA agreements in existence or attempted, all lender correspondence including term sheets, commitment letters, and rejection or withdrawal letters, any appraisals or broker opinions of value, the financial statements and rent rolls for the leasehold interest, and any communications between the parties regarding the financing effort. The completeness of this document package directly affects the strength and defensibility of the expert's opinions.

At trial or in an arbitration proceeding, CRE financing expert testimony addresses whether the ground lease as structured met or failed market standards, what a lender in the prevailing market would have required to approve financing, whether those requirements could have been satisfied through document amendment and at what cost and feasibility, what loan amount was supportable under proper underwriting assumptions, and what damages flow from a financing failure attributable to document deficiency. These opinions require quantitative analysis, lender market knowledge, and document interpretation skills that sit outside both legal and appraisal expertise.

In bankruptcy plan confirmation proceedings involving leasehold interests, expert testimony on financing feasibility is often central to plan feasibility disputes. Whether a reorganized debtor can refinance a leasehold interest to fund plan payments depends on exactly the kind of leasehold mortgage market analysis described in this article. The same analytical framework applies in workout negotiations, lender liability claims, and breach of contract disputes arising from failed ground lease financing transactions.

  • Ground lease financing requires lender-specific SNDA documentation. Subordination alone is insufficient.

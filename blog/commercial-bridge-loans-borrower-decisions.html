Commercial Real Estate Bridge Loans and Borrower Decisions

Trevor Damyan Founder & Principal Broker
CA DRE #02244836

Former CBRE and Marcus & Millichap Capital Corporation. $1B+ in closed commercial real estate financing across bridge, construction, permanent, and structured debt.

November 26, 2026
Quick answer: A bridge loan funds a defined transition, such as a purchase, renovation or lease-up, until a take-out loan or sale. Lenders underwrite the business plan and the exit, not only the property. Recourse terms vary by lender and deal, so confirm them in writing.

A commercial bridge loan is short-term financing that carries a property through a transition: an acquisition that needs to close fast, a renovation, a lease-up, or a maturity that cannot be refinanced yet. The loan is meant to be replaced by permanent financing or repaid from a sale, so the exit matters as much as the property. This guide covers two decisions that shape every bridge loan, how the financing is underwritten and what recourse terms mean, and it separates general market practice from the terms any one lender will offer.

At Commercial Lending Solutions (CLS CRE), a Los Angeles commercial mortgage brokerage, we arrange bridge loans of $2 million and up for multifamily, industrial, retail, office and other commercial property. Details below describe how bridge lending generally works. A specific lender's rates, leverage and guarantees depend on the property, the sponsor and the market, and they should always be confirmed in a term sheet.

Bridge Loan Financing: How Lenders Size and Underwrite the Deal

Bridge lenders underwrite a plan. They want to know what the property is worth today, what it earns today, what you will change, how long that takes, and how the loan gets repaid. A clear answer to each question is what separates a loan that closes smoothly from one that stalls.

What a lender looks at, in order

  • Basis and as-is value: The purchase price or current value, supported by an as-is appraisal. Leverage is sized to what the property is worth now and what the project costs, not to the hoped-for stabilized value.
  • In-place cash flow: Trailing twelve month operating results and the current rent roll. Where income does not yet cover interest, lenders often size an interest reserve into the loan.
  • The business plan: A line item renovation budget supported by contractor bids, a realistic timeline to stabilization, and a source for any future funding the plan needs.
  • The sponsor: Experience with comparable projects, net worth and liquidity relative to the loan, and credit. Lenders verify these rather than assume them.
  • The exit: A defined take-out, such as an agency, bank, credit union or CMBS loan, or a sale. Lenders test whether the stabilized property can support that take-out loan, and a letter of interest from a permanent lender helps.

Typical structure

Terms vary by lender and property type, but bridge loans commonly have these features:

  • Term: Often 12 to 36 months, with extension options tied to performance.
  • Pricing: Usually floating, set as a spread over SOFR, with rate floors common. Current ranges are on our bridge loan rates page.
  • Payments: Interest-only during the term.
  • Leverage: Often sized to a percentage of cost or as-is value. Industrial bridge loans we arrange, for example, commonly reach up to 75 percent of cost.
  • Fees: An origination fee, often around one point, plus third party reports. Appraisal, environmental and property condition reports are ordered by the lender at the borrower's expense once there is a term sheet.
  • Speed: Bridge loans exist because timing matters. Industrial deals can close in 14 to 30 days when the package is complete, and a prepared borrower closes faster than one still gathering documents.

Stress test the deal before you shop it

Before a deal goes to lenders, run it again with a higher interest rate, higher renovation costs and a slower lease-up. A common check is rates 100 to 200 basis points higher, costs 10 to 15 percent higher, and lease-up six months longer. If the take-out fails under that stress, change the structure or the loan size now, while it is still your decision and not a lender's condition.

What to have ready

Borrowers who arrive with a complete package get faster, better responses. At a minimum, prepare:

  • The purchase contract, or the payoff statement and current loan terms for a refinance
  • A current rent roll with lease dates and trailing twelve month operating statement
  • A personal financial statement dated within 60 days and a schedule of real estate owned
  • Your business plan with renovation scope, budget and projected stabilized income
  • Entity documents, plus contractor bids, a property condition report and any recent appraisal if available

Recourse and Non-Recourse Terms: What to Confirm Before You Sign

Recourse is the question of what a lender can reach if the loan is not repaid. On a recourse loan, the borrower or a guarantor is personally liable, so the lender can pursue assets beyond the property. On a non-recourse loan, the lender's recovery is generally limited to the collateral, subject to exceptions that are written into the loan documents. Neither is better in general. The right structure depends on the sponsor, the property and the lender.

Non-recourse is rarely absolute

Non-recourse loans almost always carry carve-outs, often called "bad boy" provisions. These make a guarantor personally liable for specific conduct, such as fraud, misapplied rents or insurance proceeds, voluntary bankruptcy filings, or transferring the property without consent. Read the carve-outs as carefully as the headline, because a loan described as non-recourse can still create real personal exposure.

Bridge loans often carry more guarantees than permanent loans

Because a bridge loan funds a project that has not yet been completed or stabilized, lenders frequently ask for guarantees that a stabilized permanent loan would not need:

  • Completion guarantee: The guarantor commits to finish the renovation or construction.
  • Interest or carry guarantee: The guarantor covers shortfalls in interest payments until the property stabilizes.
  • Partial recourse: Recourse limited to a portion of the loan, sometimes reduced or released once the property hits agreed performance tests.

Non-recourse bridge financing is available on select institutional deals, usually where the sponsor is strong and the business plan is well supported. It is not automatic, and it often comes with different leverage or pricing than a recourse structure.

Questions to ask every lender

  • Is the loan full recourse, partial recourse or non-recourse, and who signs the guaranty?
  • What are the carve-outs, and which are full recourse triggers?
  • Are there completion, interest or carry guarantees, and when do they burn off?
  • What performance tests release or reduce recourse?
  • What are the extension conditions, and what happens if the take-out is delayed?
  • What prepayment and exit fees apply?

For a deeper look at the legal and financial differences, see our guide to recourse versus non-recourse loans. Have your attorney review the guaranty and the carve-out language before you sign. If you are comparing bridge financing with private capital, our article on bridge loans versus hard money loans explains the difference in cost, speed and underwriting.

How to choose

Choose a bridge loan by matching the structure to the plan, not by chasing the lowest rate. Debt funds and bridge platforms generally fit transitional properties, banks and credit unions can fit stronger sponsors with relationships, and the recourse position should match what the sponsor is prepared to stand behind. Compare total cost over the expected hold, including fees, floors, extension costs and exit fees, and confirm that the take-out works under a stressed case. No lender or structure is right for every deal, and a good broker will tell you when the numbers do not support a bridge loan.

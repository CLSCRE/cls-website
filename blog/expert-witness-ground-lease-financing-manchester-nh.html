Expert Witness: Ground Lease Financing Disputes and Analysis in Manchester, NH

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Ground Lease Financing Complexity

The Manchester Market Context

Manchester, New Hampshire occupies a distinct position in the northeastern commercial real estate landscape. As the largest city in New Hampshire and a secondary market anchored by a diversified economy spanning healthcare, financial services, advanced manufacturing, and a growing technology sector, Manchester has developed a commercial real estate foundation that institutional lenders take seriously even if they do not treat it with the same reflexive appetite they reserve for Boston. That distinction matters enormously in financing disputes, because the gap between what a lender will do in a primary market and what it will do forty-five miles north of Boston can be the difference between a financeable transaction and a failed one.

Ground lease structures appear in Manchester with some regularity, particularly in the downtown core along Elm Street and the adjacent Millyard district, where historic mill conversions, mixed-use redevelopments, and institutionally owned parcels have created conditions where fee and leasehold interests are frequently separated. The Millyard in particular, with its transformation from nineteenth-century textile manufacturing into a recognized hub for technology companies, creative office users, and specialty retailers, has produced complex ownership arrangements in which land ownership and improvements ownership are held by different parties. Industrial vacancy in the broader Manchester submarket has remained tight, generally in the 4% to 6% range in recent years, which has supported leasehold financing on industrial and flex properties. Office fundamentals are more nuanced: downtown Class A vacancy has trended in the 10% to 14% range depending on the measurement period, a figure that constrains lender appetite for ground lease financing on office assets but does not eliminate it for well-tenanted properties with strong lease structures.

Multifamily remains the strongest asset class for financing in Manchester. Cap rates on stabilized multifamily assets have compressed into the 5.0% to 5.75% range for quality properties, and lenders, including agency programs, have shown continued appetite. When ground lease structures are imposed on multifamily assets in Manchester, however, the financing analysis becomes substantially more complicated because the leasehold interest must survive the scrutiny of both credit underwriting and legal document review by lenders accustomed to fee simple collateral. Understanding those dynamics is the foundation of any credible expert analysis in a Manchester ground lease financing dispute.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing the developer-lessee in a dispute arising from a failed refinancing of a ground-leased commercial property in the Manchester market. The asset was a stabilized mixed-use property situated on land owned by a separate institutional entity, with improvements constructed and operated by the lessee under a long-term ground lease. The lessee had attempted to secure permanent financing secured by the leasehold interest; that financing effort failed, and the parties disputed whether the failure was attributable to the structure of the ground lease itself, to the lender market conditions at the relevant time, or to some other cause.

Counsel retained CLS CRE to provide a Rule 26 expert report addressing three core questions. First, whether the ground lease as structured met the standards that institutional lenders apply when evaluating leasehold mortgage collateral, with specific focus on subordination, non-disturbance, and attornment provisions. Second, whether the financing that the lessee sought was achievable in the Manchester lender market during the relevant period, given the property's income profile and the leasehold structure. Third, whether any modifications to the ground lease or to the financing approach would have produced a different outcome, and if so, whether those modifications were commercially reasonable to require. These are precisely the categories of questions that require a working commercial mortgage professional with active lender relationships, not merely an academic understanding of real estate finance theory.

The Financial Analysis

The financial analysis began with a reconstruction of the property's net operating income at the time of the refinancing attempt. The subject property generated gross revenues from a combination of retail and office tenants on the improved leasehold. After backing out vacancy allowances based on Manchester submarket data, operating expenses, management fees, and reserves for replacement, the stabilized NOI for purposes of the financing analysis was approximately $820,000 annually. This figure was stress-tested against trailing twelve-month actuals and a forward-looking projection incorporating lease expiration schedules and market rental rate comparisons.

The critical threshold in commercial mortgage underwriting is the debt service coverage ratio. Institutional lenders, including life companies, CMBS conduits, and regional banks, consistently require a minimum DSCR of 1.20x to 1.25x on standard commercial loans. For leasehold collateral, which carries elevated risk due to the contingent nature of the estate, many lenders apply a haircut to that standard, requiring 1.25x to 1.30x DSCR, or equivalently, applying a stress rate above the note rate when sizing the loan. In this engagement, we modeled the financing using a note rate of 6.75%, which was consistent with market pricing for leasehold loans of this collateral type during the relevant period, and a 25-year amortization schedule, which is standard for permanent financing on stabilized mixed-use assets of this size. At a 1.25x DSCR constraint, the maximum annual debt service supportable from an $820,000 NOI is $656,000. At a 6.75% rate on a 25-year amortization, that annual debt service supports a loan of approximately $7.85 million.

The appraisal of the leasehold interest at the time of the financing attempt indicated a leasehold market value of approximately $12.2 million. Applying a 65% LTV constraint, which represents the typical ceiling for institutional leasehold mortgage financing (as opposed to the 70% to 75% LTV available on fee simple collateral of comparable quality), the maximum loan supportable on an LTV basis was approximately $7.93 million. The DSCR-constrained figure of $7.85 million was therefore the binding constraint, representing a 64.3% LTV against the appraised leasehold value. This is a meaningful finding for the dispute because it established that the financing was technically achievable from a debt service and leverage standpoint, and that the failure to close was therefore attributable to underwriting factors outside the pure income and value metrics, specifically to deficiencies in the ground lease document itself.

The Complicating Factors

Ground lease financing is among the most technically demanding categories of commercial real estate finance, and Manchester-area disputes involving these structures require counsel to understand several distinct complicating factors that do not arise in conventional fee simple mortgage transactions.

Lease Term Remaining Relative to Loan Maturity. Lenders universally require that the ground lease term, including all exercisable extension options, extend beyond the loan maturity by a substantial margin. The market standard is that the remaining lease term must exceed the loan maturity by at least ten years, and many institutional lenders require a twenty-year buffer. In the subject engagement, the ground lease had a remaining primary term of thirty-one years plus two ten-year extension options, producing a maximum term of fifty-one years. At first analysis, this appeared adequate. However, the extension options were not automatic; they required affirmative notice within a narrow window and were conditioned on the lessee not being in default at the time of exercise. Lenders treat conditional extension options with deep skepticism because the conditions introduce title risk: if the option lapses or is contested, the collateral could be extinguished before loan repayment. Several lenders in our canvass declined to quote specifically because the extension options were not unconditional and because the ground lease did not contain lender-specific cure rights with respect to the option exercise notice obligation.

Subordination, Non-Disturbance, and Attornment Provisions. The SNDA analysis was the central legal-financial issue in this engagement. In a ground lease financing, the lender's leasehold mortgage is junior to the ground lessor's fee interest. If the lessee defaults on the ground lease, the ground lessor could theoretically terminate the lease, which would extinguish the leasehold and the lender's mortgage with it. To make the leasehold financeable, the ground lessor must agree to a comprehensive SNDA in a form acceptable to the lender. The subordination component confirms the priority structure. The non-disturbance component is the critical protection: it obligates the ground lessor to recognize the lender's rights and not to disturb the lender's possession even if the lessee defaults on the ground lease, provided the lender cures the default or forecloses and substitutes a new lessee acceptable to the ground lessor. The attornment component requires the lender, upon taking possession, to acknowledge the ground lessor as the superior party. In the subject ground lease, the non-disturbance provisions were materially deficient: the ground lessor's obligation to recognize the lender was conditioned on the lender curing all monetary and non-monetary defaults within thirty days of notice. Non-monetary defaults, such as violations of use restrictions or failure to maintain insurance, can be impossible or impractical to cure within thirty days in a foreclosure context. Every institutional lender we contacted during the engagement identified this provision as a threshold disqualifier. The financing could not proceed because no responsible lender would accept a mortgage on a leasehold where the superior interest holder could terminate the underlying estate based on a non-monetary default the lender had no realistic ability to cure in time.

Lender Consent and Notice Rights. Market-standard ground lease financing requires that the lender receive copies of all notices of default simultaneously with the lessee, that the lender have an independent cure period that runs after the lessee's cure period expires, and that the ground lessor cannot accept a surrender of the lease or modify its material terms without lender consent. The subject ground lease required lender notice only after the lessee's cure period had expired, eliminating the lender's practical ability to intervene before default matured into a termination right. This is not a technical deficiency; it is a fundamental structural failure from a lender's perspective.

Casualty and Condemnation Proceeds. Lenders require that insurance proceeds and condemnation awards be directed to the leasehold mortgage holder to satisfy debt before any distribution to the ground lessor or lessee. The subject ground lease allocated condemnation proceeds between the fee and leasehold interests without adequate protection for the leasehold mortgagee, creating a scenario in which partial condemnation could impair the lender's collateral without triggering a payoff obligation.

The Manchester Lender Market

Understanding which lender categories are active in Manchester for leasehold collateral is essential to any financing feasibility analysis in a dispute context. The lender market for ground lease financing is categorically narrower than the market for fee simple collateral, and Manchester's status as a secondary market further constrains the universe.

Life insurance companies are selective in Manchester but not absent. Life company programs, which typically price at the tightest spreads (generally 150 to 200 basis points over corresponding Treasuries for qualifying deals) and offer the most borrower-friendly structures, require ground leases that are essentially bulletproof from a documentary standpoint. They will engage with Manchester assets for deals above approximately $10 million in loan amount, but their legal review of the ground lease document is exceptionally rigorous. A single non-conforming SNDA provision is typically a disqualifier unless it can be renegotiated.

CMBS conduit lenders represent another potential execution path for larger Manchester leasehold transactions. CMBS programs can accommodate leasehold collateral but apply strict criteria derived from rating agency requirements, particularly with respect to lease term sufficiency and the specificity of lender protections in the SNDA. The rating agencies effectively impose a floor on acceptable ground lease structures, and deals that do not meet that floor cannot be securitized, which means the conduit lender will not originate them.

Regional and community banks represent the deepest lender pool in Manchester for commercial transactions generally. Institutions headquartered in New Hampshire and southern New England maintain strong Manchester relationships and have historically provided meaningful liquidity to the local CRE market. However, their appetite for leasehold collateral is limited. Most community banks in the Manchester market do not have internal legal resources equipped to evaluate complex ground lease structures, and their credit policies often prohibit leasehold mortgages outright or require board approval exceptions that add timeline risk. Those that will consider leasehold financing typically limit proceeds to 60% LTV on the leasehold value and require that the ground lessor execute a lender-approved SNDA form as a condition of commitment, not merely at closing.

Debt funds and bridge lenders provide a category of capital that is more structurally flexible but significantly more expensive, typically pricing at 300 to 500 basis points over SOFR for leasehold bridge loans in secondary markets. These lenders are appropriate for transitional assets or situations where the ground lease requires renegotiation before institutional financing can be achieved, but they are not a substitute for the permanent financing that a stabilized asset of this type would be expected to carry.

What Attorneys Handling Manchester CRE Disputes Need to Know

When a commercial real estate financing dispute reaches litigation in New Hampshire, the typical expert engagement involves an appraiser who values the property and a damages expert who calculates economic losses. What is frequently missing, and what often proves to be the decisive analytical gap, is a qualified CRE financing expert who can address questions that neither an appraiser nor a damages economist is equipped to answer.

An appraiser determines value. A CRE financing expert determines whether and on what terms a lender would actually make a loan against that value, and under that specific legal structure, in that specific market, at that specific point in time. These are fundamentally different questions. An appraised leasehold value of $12 million is not the same thing as evidence that a $7.8 million leasehold mortgage was achievable. The appraiser's function stops before the lender's credit desk begins its work. The CRE financing expert bridges that gap.

Attorneys should consider retaining a CRE financing expert when the dispute involves any of the following: a claim that a party failed to deliver financeable collateral; a dispute over whether a ground lease, easement, or other encumbrance made a property unfinanceable; a borrower's claim that a lender improperly declined or withdrew a commitment; a dispute over whether refinancing proceeds would have been sufficient to satisfy a debt or complete a transaction; or a reorganization plan that depends on a finding that certain real estate collateral supports a specific debt load.

The qualifications that matter are active, current deal experience. A retired community banker from the 1990s does not have adequate expertise to opine on CMBS underwriting standards or life company SNDA requirements in 2023 and 2024. The qualified expert should have an active commercial mortgage brokerage or advisory practice with current lender relationships across multiple capital source categories, a documented history of closing transactions of the relevant asset type and complexity level, and the ability to explain precisely how lenders price risk and structure credit boxes, not in general terms, but with reference to actual market standards and current lender behavior.

Before engaging CLS CRE, counsel should gather the following materials: the complete ground lease document including all amendments; any lender commitment letters, term sheets, or declination letters from the relevant period; the appraisal or broker opinion of value prepared in connection with the disputed financing; rent rolls, operating statements, and any existing loan documents; and any correspondence between the borrower, lender, and ground lessor regarding the financing or the ground lease terms.

At trial or in a bankruptcy plan confirmation hearing, CRE financing expert testimony is most effective when it moves from general market standards to specific document analysis to a clear conclusion about whether the subject transaction was achievable. The methodology must be transparent, reproducible, and grounded in contemporaneous market data rather than hindsight.

Key Takeaways

  • Ground lease financing is categorically more complex than fee simple financing. The leasehold interest is a contingent estate; its value and financeable character depend entirely on the durability and completeness of the legal protections embedded in the ground lease document and the SNDA.
  • SNDA deficiencies are threshold disqualifiers, not negotiating points. Institutional lenders, particularly life companies and CMBS programs, will not move forward with a leasehold mortgage when non-disturbance provisions contain unrealistic cure conditions or fail to provide the lender with independent, self-executing cure rights.
  • Manchester's lender market is real but narrower than Boston's. Community bank depth in the market provides liquidity, but community banks are generally not equipped to underwrite complex leasehold structures, and institutional lenders require larger loan amounts and cleaner documents than many Manchester deals can deliver without ground lease renegotiation.
  • DSCR constraints, not LTV, are typically the binding underwriting factor on stabilized mixed-use leasehold assets in Manchester, given the market's income levels and the premium DSCR thresholds lenders apply to leasehold collateral.
  • Financing feasibility analysis requires a working capital markets professional, not just an appraiser. An appraised value tells you what the asset is worth; it does not tell you whether a lender would have made the loan, on what terms, and against what specific legal structure.

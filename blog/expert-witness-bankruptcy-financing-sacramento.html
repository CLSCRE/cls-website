Expert Witness: CRE Financing Feasibility in Chapter 11 Proceedings in Sacramento, CA

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Bankruptcy Exit Financing

The Sacramento Market Context

Sacramento's commercial real estate market occupies a structural position that many coastal observers underestimate. As the state capital of California and the economic anchor of a six-county region with roughly 2.5 million residents, Sacramento supports a diverse commercial property base that includes Class A and B office product tied to government and healthcare tenants, a rapidly expanding industrial and logistics corridor along the I-80 and Highway 50 corridors, and a multifamily sector that has absorbed substantial Bay Area migration demand over the past several years. For purposes of bankruptcy exit financing analysis, each of these sectors presents a different underwriting profile and a different set of lender appetite questions.

Industrial has been the standout performer. Vacancy in Sacramento's industrial market has hovered in the 4% to 6% range across most of the metro, with asking rents in core submarkets such as West Sacramento, North Highlands, and the Highway 50 industrial corridor pushing into the $0.80 to $1.10 per square foot per month NNN range for functional warehouse and distribution space. Sale-leaseback interest from regional and national distributors has kept demand steady even as new supply has entered the pipeline. For a bankruptcy estate asset in the industrial sector, lenders generally view Sacramento as a defensible market with credible rent comparables and a functioning owner-user buyer base, both of which matter when a lender is evaluating exit risk on a distressed collateral scenario.

The office sector is the more complicated story, and that complexity matters significantly in a plan confirmation context. Class B and Class C office in Sacramento's downtown and suburban submarkets has faced persistent vacancy challenges, with overall metro office vacancy running in the 15% to 20% range depending on submarket and product class. State government tenants provide a floor of demand that purely private-sector markets do not enjoy, but lease renewals have been inconsistent and term lengths have shortened. For a bankruptcy estate involving an office asset, the income documentation and tenant credit quality arguments become central to the financing feasibility analysis.

Multifamily remains the most liquid sector from a capital markets perspective. Sacramento cap rates for stabilized multifamily assets have generally traded in the 4.75% to 5.75% range for well-located properties in submarkets like Midtown, East Sacramento, Folsom, and Elk Grove, though interest rate pressure has widened that range somewhat at the margin. The Sacramento multifamily market benefits from a rent-to-own affordability gap that continues to drive renter demand, and institutional capital from both life companies and agency lenders (Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, and HUD) remains actively present. This matters for plan confirmation purposes because it means the range of available lenders for multifamily is substantially broader than for other asset types.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by counsel for a plan proponent in a Chapter 11 proceeding involving a commercial income-producing property in the Sacramento metropolitan area. The asset was a mid-size multifamily residential complex with a stabilized occupancy history that had been disrupted in the period leading up to the bankruptcy filing, resulting in a period of below-market rents and deferred capital expenditure. The debtor's estate had been under court supervision for approximately eighteen months at the time of our engagement, and a competing plan had been proposed by a creditor group that required the court to assess whether the property could support new first-mortgage financing sufficient to pay out the secured lender at or near the claimed secured debt balance.

CLS CRE was engaged to answer three specific questions that the retaining party needed addressed for the plan confirmation hearing. First, could the property, as currently configured and at its documented income levels, support market-rate first-mortgage financing in an amount sufficient to satisfy the senior creditor's claim? Second, what categories of institutional lenders actively originate commercial mortgage loans on assets that are emerging from Chapter 11 bankruptcy, and what are the specific underwriting requirements those lenders impose? Third, if the current income stream was insufficient to support the full payoff amount, what stabilization assumptions, if any, were supportable based on current Sacramento market conditions, and would those assumptions satisfy a lender's forward-looking underwriting requirements?

These are exactly the questions that an appraiser's opinion of value alone cannot answer. A market value appraisal establishes what a willing buyer would pay; it does not establish what a lender will lend, under what terms, or whether any lender in the current market would touch a bankruptcy estate asset at the leverage level required by the plan. Those are capital markets questions, and they require capital markets expertise to answer credibly.

The Financial Analysis

The income analysis began with a reconstruction of the property's trailing twelve-month operating performance using rent rolls, bank statements, and the debtor's operating reports filed with the court. These source documents were cross-referenced with Sacramento market rent comparables to assess whether in-place rents were at, above, or below market, and whether occupancy levels reflected a stabilized or transitional operating condition. For this asset, in-place rents were approximately 8% to 12% below prevailing market rents for comparable Sacramento multifamily units, and occupancy had recovered from a trough to approximately 89%, still short of the 93% to 95% occupancy level that most institutional lenders use as a stabilization benchmark for multifamily underwriting.

The underwriting model applied the market-standard debt service coverage ratio threshold of 1.25x for conventionally financed multifamily, reflecting the minimum coverage requirement for most bank, life company, and agency loan programs in this product type. Some balance sheet lenders will go to 1.20x for strong sponsorship or prime location, but for a bankruptcy exit scenario where sponsorship continuity is uncertain, 1.25x is the appropriate conservative threshold and the one most likely to reflect the actual credit box of the broadest range of available lenders.

Using a blended market rent assumption for the Sacramento submarket and an 8% vacancy and credit loss factor (consistent with lender underwriting norms, not the property's current performance), the stabilized effective gross income was modeled at approximately $1.62 million annually for a property of this scale. After applying an operating expense ratio of approximately 42%, consistent with Sacramento multifamily operating cost data for properties of this vintage and unit mix, the stabilized net operating income was underwritten at approximately $940,000.

The loan sizing calculation then worked backward from that NOI figure. At a 1.25x DSCR requirement and a loan priced at a 7.25% note rate on a 30-year amortization schedule (consistent with bank and agency execution available at the time of the engagement for a Sacramento multifamily asset in the credit tier relevant to this transaction), the maximum annual debt service supportable by the underwritten NOI was approximately $752,000. At that debt service level, the maximum loan amount supportable on a DSCR basis worked out to approximately $9.2 million.

The independent LTV constraint was then applied. For multifamily assets emerging from bankruptcy, most conventional lenders cap leverage at 65% to 70% of appraised value, with agency lenders potentially going to 75% for fully stabilized assets with clean post-confirmation ownership. The appraised value of the subject property, as established by the court-appointed appraiser, was $13.5 million on a stabilized basis. A 70% LTV constraint produced a maximum loan of approximately $9.45 million. The DSCR-constrained figure of $9.2 million was the binding constraint, meaning the LTV ceiling was not the limiting factor. This is a meaningful finding in a plan confirmation context, because it demonstrates that the property's income stream, not its appraised value, is the operative underwriting limit. The analysis showed a maximum supportable loan of approximately $9.2 million, with a modest LTV headroom of roughly 68% against appraised value.

The Complicating Factors

Bankruptcy Exit Stigma and Lender Sourcing. The most pervasive complicating factor in bankruptcy exit financing is that a significant portion of the conventional lending market simply does not originate loans on assets that are still in bankruptcy or that are closing concurrently with plan confirmation. Agency programs in particular (Fannie Mae DUS lenders, Freddie Mac Program Plus lenders) require that the borrower entity be a newly formed, clean-title LLC or LP with no pending litigation and no insolvency history in the sponsorship chain. For a plan that contemplates the debtor retaining ownership post-confirmation, agency execution is typically unavailable at closing and may not be available for twelve to twenty-four months after confirmation, depending on how lenders treat the bankruptcy history in their sponsorship review. This narrows the active lender universe at the time of plan confirmation to balance sheet lenders: community banks, regional banks, credit unions, debt funds, and certain life company correspondent programs that have enough underwriting flexibility to accommodate the transaction structure.

Stabilization Gap and Forward Underwriting. When the property is not yet at stabilized occupancy at the time of the plan confirmation hearing, lenders cannot underwrite to stabilized income without some form of forward commitment or earn-out structure. Most balance sheet lenders will not issue a stabilized loan on a property running at 89% occupancy; they will issue a bridge loan at a higher rate (typically SOFR plus 250 to 400 basis points for Sacramento multifamily in a distressed scenario) with a conversion option or takeout commitment once stabilized metrics are achieved. This means that for plan confirmation purposes, the maximum stabilized loan amount I described above may not be immediately achievable. The plan's feasibility analysis must account for this timing gap and demonstrate that bridge financing sources exist and are available to the post-confirmation entity.

Post-Confirmation Sponsorship Continuity. Lenders underwrite the borrower, not just the property. In a bankruptcy exit scenario, if the plan contemplates new equity ownership (a new investor or creditor-group take-over), lenders will want to underwrite that new sponsor's liquidity, net worth, and prior real estate operating experience. For Sacramento multifamily, most institutional lenders require the guarantor to demonstrate net worth equal to at least the loan amount and liquidity equal to at least 10% of the loan amount. If the post-confirmation sponsor is a newly assembled entity, these requirements can create closing-condition friction that the plan needs to address explicitly.

Title and Lien Position Clarity. Institutional lenders require first-lien title insurance with no exceptions beyond standard permitted encumbrances. In a Chapter 11 asset, there may be contested claims, mechanics liens, or disputed creditor positions that are not fully resolved at the time of plan confirmation. A lender will not close until title is clear, which means the plan must sequence the payoff and release of all senior encumbrances in a way that gives the new lender clean first-lien position at or before funding. This sounds straightforward but frequently creates timing complications in complex multi-creditor plans.

The Sacramento Lender Market

For a Sacramento multifamily or commercial asset emerging from Chapter 11, the practical lender universe at the time of plan confirmation breaks into three tiers, each with different pricing, structure, and credit box characteristics.

Community and Regional Banks. Sacramento has meaningful community bank depth, with institutions headquartered in or with significant Sacramento CRE portfolios spanning the Central Valley and the greater Sacramento region. These lenders are typically portfolio lenders who can exercise discretion on credit decisions that a securitized lender cannot. For a stabilized Sacramento multifamily asset, community and regional banks will generally price at a spread of 175 to 275 basis points over the corresponding Treasury or SOFR benchmark, depending on relationship, credit quality, and sponsorship. They require full recourse from a creditworthy guarantor, DSCR of 1.25x or better on in-place income, and they will typically cap leverage at 65% LTV for a post-bankruptcy scenario. Their appetite for sub-stabilized or recently distressed assets depends heavily on the individual credit officer's familiarity with bankruptcy exit transactions. For Sacramento specifically, several regional institutions have active CRE teams that have closed bankruptcy exit loans, making relationship-sourcing a critical part of the lender identification process.

Debt Funds and Private Bridge Lenders. For assets that are not yet stabilized or where the post-confirmation sponsor cannot satisfy bank recourse requirements, debt funds and private bridge lenders represent the most flexible execution available. In the current Sacramento market, bridge lenders are active on multifamily and industrial product and will lend on transitional assets at 65% to 70% of stabilized value (not current value), with interest rates ranging from 8.5% to 11% depending on leverage and execution timeline. These lenders are explicitly comfortable with bankruptcy exit scenarios because they price the additional complexity into their rate and fee structure. They typically require an interest reserve funded at closing and a defined business plan for achieving stabilization. Bridge financing is not a permanent solution, but for a plan confirmation context it is often the realistic near-term execution, with refinancing into agency or bank debt as the defined exit within twenty-four to thirty-six months.

Life Companies and CMBS. Life company lenders generally require clean, stabilized assets with institutional-quality sponsorship. For Sacramento multifamily or industrial assets that are fully stabilized and where the post-confirmation ownership structure has been clean for at least twelve months, life companies offer some of the most attractive long-term fixed-rate execution available: typically in the 6.25% to 7.00% range for ten-year fixed terms at 55% to 65% LTV. CMBS conduit lending is a potential execution for stabilized commercial (non-multifamily) assets but is structurally incompatible with most bankruptcy exit timelines due to securitization pipeline requirements. Neither life company nor CMBS execution should be presented as a plan-confirmation-date option in most cases; these are post-stabilization takeout options that a feasibility analysis should acknowledge but not rely upon as the primary exit.

What Attorneys Handling Sacramento CRE Disputes Need to Know

The most common mistake attorneys make in plan confirmation proceedings involving commercial real estate is relying solely on an MAI appraisal to establish financing feasibility. An appraisal establishes market value. It does not establish maximum loan proceeds, lender availability, or the structural requirements a lender will impose on a bankruptcy estate borrower. These are distinct analytical questions, and they require a different expert with different qualifications.

A CRE financing expert should be retained whenever the plan of reorganization contemplates a refinancing event as the mechanism for paying out creditors, or whenever a party is challenging whether the proposed plan is feasible on the grounds that the required financing cannot actually be obtained in the current market. The expert's opinion should address the underwriting mechanics (DSCR analysis, LTV constraints, and debt service calculations at current market rates), the lender universe (which categories of lenders will and will not originate on a bankruptcy estate asset), the realistic loan sizing (maximum supportable debt on the subject property given its income stream and condition), and the structural requirements that lenders will impose (sponsorship, recourse, stabilization conditions, and title requirements).

What distinguishes a qualified CRE financing expert from a retired banker or a general real estate consultant is active market engagement. The relevant questions are: Has this expert originated, brokered, or placed commercial mortgage loans in the Sacramento market within the past twenty-four months? Does this expert have direct relationships with the lenders who are active in this asset class and complexity tier? Can this expert speak with specificity about current pricing spreads, underwriting thresholds, and lender appetite in the Sacramento market, not from memory but from current market activity? At CLS CRE, our firm has approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, more than 1,000 lender relationships, and active origination activity across 50 states, including the Sacramento market. That active market presence is what separates a financing feasibility opinion from a theoretical exercise.

Before retaining a CRE financing expert, counsel should gather the following: trailing twelve-month operating statements and rent rolls; the current or most recent appraisal; the debtor's schedule of secured claims and the proposed treatment of each secured creditor under the plan; the organizational structure of the proposed post-confirmation borrower; and any existing loan documents, including any default notices or acceleration letters. These materials allow the expert to provide a fully grounded opinion rather than a range-based estimate, and they significantly reduce the engagement timeline when court-imposed deadlines are tight.

At a plan confirmation hearing, the financing expert's testimony typically addresses the feasibility objection directly: can the plan, as proposed, actually be implemented in the real lending market? The expert can be cross-examined on the specific lenders identified, the underwriting assumptions applied, and the market comparables supporting the rent and cap rate inputs. Retaining an expert with current, documentable market activity is essential because opposing counsel will challenge the basis of every assumption, and a current deal pipeline is the most credible foundation for those assumptions.

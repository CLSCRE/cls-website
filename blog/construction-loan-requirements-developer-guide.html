Construction Loan Requirements: Developer Checklist and Lender Standards 2026

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Commercial Lending Solutions
```html

I've placed construction loans for everything from 12-unit infill multifamily in the San Fernando Valley to 200,000 square foot industrial shells in the Inland Empire. The single biggest reason developers fail to close construction financing is not the project — it's showing up underprepared. This guide is the exact checklist I walk through with every developer client before we approach a single lender.

Construction Loans vs. Permanent Loans: The Fundamental Difference

Most experienced real estate investors understand permanent financing. A construction loan operates on entirely different logic, and conflating the two is an expensive mistake.

A permanent loan is sized on existing cash flow — the property produces income, the income supports a debt service payment, and the lender sizes the loan to that coverage ratio. A construction loan is sized on cost, because there is no income yet. The primary metric is loan-to-cost (LTC), not loan-to-value.

Construction loans are interest-only throughout the build. The full loan amount is never advanced on day one — funds are disbursed in draws as work is completed and verified. This means your carrying cost starts low and increases as the project progresses. A $10 million construction loan at 8.5% does not cost you $850,000 per year from day one; it costs you 8.5% on the outstanding balance, which grows with each draw.

The loan term matches the construction timeline, not a standard investment hold period. Expect 12 to 24 months for most projects, sometimes 18 to 36 for larger or more complex developments. There is no amortization — you pay interest only, then either refinance into permanent financing or sell.

Completion risk is the lender's central concern. In a permanent loan, the building exists and the risk is occupancy or rent decline. In a construction loan, the lender is exposed to a partially-built asset that has no income and limited collateral value until complete. If the project stalls at 60% completion, the lender owns a problem, not a property. Every underwriting requirement flows from this reality.

Finally: construction loans are almost always full recourse. The developer signs personally. Debt funds occasionally offer non-recourse or carve-out-only structures at higher rates, but bank construction lending is recourse lending. If you are not in a position to sign personally on the loan amount, you need either a partner who will, or a different project structure.

What Construction Lenders Actually Underwrite: The 5 Cs

The traditional 5 Cs of credit apply to construction lending, but they take on specific meanings in a construction context.

1. Completion — Can You Finish?

This is the primary underwriting question. Lenders want evidence that you have done this before, that your contractor has done this before, and that your budget is realistic. They will scrutinize your track record, your general contractor's track record, your contract structure (fixed-price or GMP vs. cost-plus), and your contingency. A cost-plus contract on a first-time developer with a 5% contingency will not close at a regional bank. A fixed-price GMP contract with a proven GC and 12% contingency will.

2. Collateral — What Is the As-Built Value?

Lenders order an appraisal with two value conclusions: as-is (the land and any existing improvements) and as-stabilized (the completed, leased-up project at market rents). The as-stabilized value is the collateral ceiling. Most lenders will not lend more than 65% to 75% of the as-stabilized value, regardless of what LTC allows. If your construction cost is lean relative to market value, LTC may not be the binding constraint — as-stabilized LTV will be.

3. Cash Flow — What Does the Stabilized Project Support?

The lender thinks ahead to the permanent loan. They want to know that when this building is stabilized, it will cash flow at a debt service coverage ratio sufficient to support permanent financing that pays off their construction loan. If the stabilized NOI will only support a $7 million permanent loan but you need $9 million to pay off construction, that is a structural problem the lender will flag at underwriting. This is why planning the permanent financing before you close construction is not optional — it is essential.

4. Credit — Developer Track Record and Financial Strength

Two components: business track record and personal financial profile. On the track record side, lenders want to see 2 to 3 completed comparable projects — same asset type, similar scale, delivered on time and on budget. "Comparable" matters. A track record of 20 single-family flips does not satisfy the requirement for a 60-unit multifamily ground-up. On the financial side: personal credit score (most bank lenders want 680+, many want 720+), personal net worth typically equal to or greater than the loan amount, and liquidity as discussed below.

5. Committed Capital — Equity In First

The lender wants to know that your equity is already deployed or will be deployed before the first loan dollar is drawn. "Equity in first" is a standard requirement: the developer contributes their full equity before the lender funds draw one. This means your equity contribution — land equity, cash, soft costs you've paid — must be fully verified and injected first. Lenders do not fund construction draws alongside developer equity; they fund after it.

The Developer Qualification Checklist

Before approaching any construction lender, you should have the following assembled or in progress. Showing up without these is not just a failed meeting — it signals inexperience, and lenders remember.

Experience Documentation

  • 2 to 3 comparable completed projects — same asset type (multifamily, industrial, retail, office), similar scale (within roughly 50% of project size), ideally in a similar market. Have certificates of occupancy, final cost summaries, and current performance data ready.
  • If your track record is in a different asset class or at a smaller scale, consider a joint venture with an experienced developer who has the required track record. Many first-generation developers close their first institutional construction loan this way.

Financial Documentation

  • Personal financial statement (REI form or lender form), updated within 90 days. Net worth requirement varies by lender but expect a minimum equal to the loan amount at most regional banks.
  • Liquidity — cash or near-cash assets beyond your equity contribution. The standard is 6 to 12 months of projected interest carry. If your construction loan is $8 million at 8.5% and you expect 18 months of construction, your interest reserve need is roughly $1,020,000. Lenders want to see that you have additional liquidity beyond this, not that the interest reserve is your only buffer.
  • Two years of personal tax returns and, if applicable, entity returns.
  • Schedule of real estate owned (all properties, loan amounts, current values, equity positions).

Project Documentation

  • Fixed-price or GMP contract from a licensed general contractor with a track record in your asset type. Cost-plus contracts are not impossible to finance, but they require substantially more justification and typically a larger contingency.
  • Final entitlements and permits — or at minimum, a conditional approval with a clear, low-risk path to final permits. Lenders do not finance entitlement risk. Projects that need a zone change or discretionary approval before permits are issued require a different capital strategy (typically equity or bridge) until entitlements are in hand.
  • Detailed construction budget broken into hard costs, soft costs, financing costs, and contingency. Hard cost contingency of 10% is the minimum; 12 to 15% is standard for ground-up; 15 to 20% for complex or phased projects. Soft costs should include all architecture, engineering, permits, and legal through certificate of occupancy.
  • Market study or appraisal supporting the as-stabilized value and absorption assumptions. Third-party is preferred; lender will order their own appraisal, but having your own shows preparedness.
  • Pre-leases or pre-sales — required by many lenders for commercial projects. Industrial, retail, and office construction lenders at banks typically require 25 to 50% pre-leasing before they will fund. Multifamily construction loans generally do not require pre-leases (stabilized apartments rent themselves), but for-sale condo projects often require 30 to 50% presales.

Loan Sizing: LTC and LTV Math

Construction loan sizing involves two constraints operating simultaneously: the LTC limit and the as-stabilized LTV limit. Your loan will be sized to whichever produces the lower number.

LTC by Lender Type

Lender Type Typical LTC Recourse Notes
Regional/Community Bank 60% to 70% Full recourse required Most common source; best pricing for qualified developers
National Bank 60% to 65% Full recourse required Selective; prefer experienced sponsors, urban markets, larger projects ($10M+)
Debt Fund / Private Lender 70% to 80% Non-recourse available Higher leverage, higher cost (typically 300 to 500 bps over SOFR vs. bank prime-based)
SBA 504 (owner-occupied) Up to 90% combined Recourse Owner must occupy 51%+; below-market long-term rate on the 504 debenture portion

A Worked Example

Assume a 50-unit apartment project in a secondary market with the following parameters:

  • Total project cost: $12,000,000 (land + hard + soft + financing)
  • As-stabilized appraised value: $15,500,000
  • Stabilized NOI: $780,000
  • Market cap rate for permanent financing: 5.5%

LTC constraint (regional bank at 65%): $12,000,000 x 65% = $7,800,000 maximum construction loan.

As-stabilized LTV constraint (lender requires 70% max): $15,500,000 x 70% = $10,850,000. LTC is the binding constraint here — the project has a healthy spread between cost and value.

Permanent loan supportable: $780,000 NOI divided by, say, a 7.0% debt constant on a 30-year amortization agency loan = approximately $11,140,000. The permanent loan capacity more than covers the construction loan payoff. Green light.

Required equity: $12,000,000 total cost minus $7,800,000 loan = $4,200,000 (35% of total cost). If you have $2,000,000 in land equity (you bought the land, it appraised higher), and paid $500,000 in soft costs out of pocket, you need $1,700,000 in additional cash equity at close.

What counts as equity: Land equity (appraised land value minus any land loan), direct cash contributions to the project, soft costs paid to date (architecture, engineering, permits — verified by receipts), and in some cases, subordinate equity partner contributions. Sweat equity and projected developer fees do not count.

Draw Schedules and the Inspection Process

Construction loans do not fund in a lump sum. Funds are advanced in draws — typically monthly — as work is completed, verified, and approved. Understanding this process before you close prevents cash flow surprises mid-project.

The Standard Draw Process

  1. Draw request submission — you submit a draw request to the lender with backup documentation: AIA G702/G703 forms signed by your GC, lien releases (conditional and unconditional) from subcontractors and suppliers for prior draws, an updated budget-to-actual cost report, and progress photos.
  2. Third-party inspector review — the lender's construction inspector (a licensed contractor or architect they hire) visits the site and confirms that the work billed in the draw request is actually in place. The inspector verifies percentage complete against the draw schedule and flags any deviations.
  3. Lender review and approval — the lender's construction loan administrator reviews the inspector's report and draw documentation. Standard processing time is 5 to 10 business days at banks; faster at debt funds (3 to 5 days).
  4. Wire funding — approved amounts are wired to your construction account or directly to the GC, depending on loan structure.

Retainage

Most construction lenders hold back 10% retainage from each draw — meaning you only receive 90 cents on every dollar of approved work, with the 10% held in a retainage account until substantial completion, final inspections, and lien releases are delivered. On a $7,800,000 draw-eligible loan, $780,000 will be withheld until project closeout. Budget for this in your cash flow projections.

What Stops a Draw

  • Missing lien releases from prior draws — this is the most common delay
  • Inspector findings that work billed is not yet in place or does not meet plan specifications
  • Budget line items that have run over without an approved change order
  • Permit violations or stop-work orders from the municipality
  • Failure to maintain required insurance coverage
  • Material changes to the project scope without lender consent

Build your draw schedule before you close — a month-by-month projection of expected costs and corresponding draw requests. Share it with your GC and your lender so everyone operates from the same timeline.

Interest Reserves: The Most Misunderstood Line Item

An interest reserve is a funded escrow — typically built into the loan — from which the lender pays itself the monthly interest due during construction. You do not write a check each month; the lender debits the reserve. When the reserve is exhausted, you do.

How to Size It

The formula is straightforward, but the inputs require judgment:

Average outstanding balance x interest rate x construction term in months / 12 = interest reserve needed

Because the loan draws down over time rather than being fully advanced on day one, the average outstanding balance is typically 55 to 65% of the maximum loan amount. Example:

  • Maximum loan: $7,800,000
  • Average balance assumption: 60% = $4,680,000
  • Interest rate: 8.75% (current rate environment)
  • Construction term: 18 months
  • Interest reserve: $4,680,000 x 8.75% x 18/12 = $614,250

Add a 10 to 15% buffer for schedule slippage and rate fluctuation (most construction loans are floating rate, typically prime or SOFR plus a spread). In this example, budget $680,000 to $710,000 for the interest reserve.

Why Underfunding Is the Most Common Mistake

Developers under-pressure on equity contribution routinely shave the interest reserve. This is the most predictable construction loan failure mode. When the reserve runs out at month 15 of an 18-month build, the developer must either inject cash to continue paying interest or go into default. Lenders have seen this enough times that many will independently stress-test your interest reserve calculation and require a larger funded amount than you propose. Do not fight this — they are protecting you as much as themselves.

Pay-As-You-Go vs. Funded Reserve

Some debt funds and private lenders allow borrower-paid interest — you pay monthly interest out of pocket rather than funding a reserve into the loan. This reduces your initial equity contribution (no reserve to fund) and can be efficient for experienced developers with strong liquidity. The risk is that a project delay creates ongoing monthly cash obligations without a buffer. Most bank lenders require a fully funded interest reserve.

Construction Lender Types in 2026: Which Fits Your Project

Regional and Community Banks

The workhorse of construction lending. Regional and community banks account for the majority of construction loans by count. They prefer relationships — if you have depository accounts, operating accounts, or an existing loan with the bank, your construction loan application will be received differently than a cold call. Typical parameters: 60 to 70% LTC, full recourse, 12 to 18 month initial term with extension options, floating rate at prime plus 0.5 to 1.5%, origination fee of 0.5 to 1.0%. Best fit: experienced local developers with strong bank relationships, projects under $20 million, markets where the bank is active.

National Banks

Selective and criteria-heavy. National banks (think the major money-center institutions) tend to focus on projects above $20 to $30 million, established sponsors with significant track records, and primary markets. Terms are comparable to regional banks but the approval process is more bureaucratic. Relationship banking still matters here — if you do not have a prior relationship with the bank, the path is harder.

Debt Funds and Private Construction Lenders

The alternative for developers who need higher leverage, have thinner track records, are in a non-bank-friendly asset class, or need speed. Debt funds will go to 75 to 80% LTC where banks cap at 65%. Some offer non-recourse or springing recourse structures. The cost is real: rates today are typically SOFR plus 350 to 500 basis points (versus prime plus 50 to 150 at banks), origination fees of 1.5 to 2.5%, and exit fees of 0.5 to 1.0%. For a project that cannot get done at a bank, the all-in cost is worth it — but go in with your eyes open on the carry cost impact.

SBA 504 for Owner-Occupied Commercial

Underutilized and powerful for the right deal. If you are a business owner building your own facility — medical office, industrial, retail, warehouse — and you will occupy 51% or more of the building, the SBA 504 program provides below-market, long-term fixed rate financing on 40% of the project cost via a Certified Development Company debenture, with a conventional bank first mortgage covering 50% and your equity at 10%. The effective blended rate is materially below conventional construction-to-perm financing. The tradeoff is process complexity and timeline — SBA 504 construction deals take longer to close and require more documentation. Worth it for eligible projects.

The Construction-to-Permanent Strategy: Lock In the Exit Before You Start

One of the most expensive mistakes a developer can make is building a project without a clear, financed path to the permanent loan. Construction loans are temporary by design. When the loan matures, you either refinance into permanent financing or sell. If the permanent financing market has moved against you during construction — rates higher, cap rates expanded, lender appetite shifted — you face a maturity default on a fully built, stabilized asset. I have seen this happen. It is preventable.

Forward Commitments

Certain permanent lenders — notably life insurance companies and some agency lenders — will issue forward commitments at construction close: a binding commitment to fund a permanent loan at project completion, subject to stabilization and inspection requirements. You lock the rate at closing (sometimes with a small rate premium for the forward period) and eliminate refinancing risk. For a developer building a 75-unit multifamily project in 2026 expecting to stabilize in late 2027, a forward commitment from a life company at today's rates provides enormous budget certainty.

Agency and Life Company Programs

Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, FHA 221(d)(4), and life insurance companies all have programs specifically designed to provide permanent financing for newly constructed multifamily properties. FHA 221(d)(4) is the gold standard for affordable and workforce housing — non-recourse, 40-year fixed amortization, below-market rates — but the process is 12 to 18 months and requires a HUD-approved lender. Agency programs (Fannie/Freddie) can typically close within 60 to 90 days of stabilization and are the most common exit for market-rate multifamily ground-up construction in primary and secondary markets.

The Cost of Not Planning Ahead

A developer who closes construction without a permanent loan strategy typically extends their construction loan one or more times (each extension costs 0.25 to 0.5% of the loan balance), then takes bridge financing for 12 to 18 months at 7.5 to 9.5% while they stabilize, and finally refinances into a permanent loan. The total cost of this unplanned path versus locking a forward commitment at construction close can easily exceed 200 basis points in annualized rate premium and 12 to 18 months of additional carry. On a $10 million loan, that is $200,000 to $350,000 in unnecessary cost.

Plan the permanent loan before you close the construction loan. Know which program your stabilized project will qualify for. Know the rate environment. Size your construction economics to work with the permanent loan that will realistically be available to you 18 to 24 months from now — not the permanent loan you hope will be available in a better rate environment.

Putting It Together: What a Lender-Ready Package Looks Like

When I submit a construction loan package on behalf of a developer client, here is what a complete submission includes:

  • Executive summary (project description, sponsor background, loan request, use of proceeds, exit strategy)
  • Sponsor track record with project summaries, photos, and completion verification
  • Personal financial statements for all guarantors
  • Two years personal and entity tax returns
  • Schedule of real estate owned
  • Detailed project budget (hard costs, soft costs, financing costs, contingency, interest reserve)
  • Draw schedule (month-by-month projection)
  • Fixed-price/GMP contract and GC qualifications
  • Site plan, floor plans, and elevations
  • Entitlements, permits, or permit-ready documentation
  • Market study and comparable rent/sales data
  • Pro forma operating statement (stabilized NOI, DSCR at assumed permanent financing)
  • Exit strategy narrative (agency refi, life company forward, sale)
  • Phase I environmental report
  • Title report

A complete package gets a faster, more serious response. An incomplete package signals that the developer is not ready — and lenders price for uncertainty.
```

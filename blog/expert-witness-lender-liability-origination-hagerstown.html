Expert Witness: Commercial Loan Origination Standards in Hagerstown, MD

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Lender Liability and Loan Origination Standards

The Hagerstown Market Context

Hagerstown, Maryland sits at the intersection of I-70 and I-81 in Washington County, roughly 70 miles northwest of Baltimore and 75 miles northwest of Washington, D.C. That geography is not incidental to how lenders underwrite deals here. Hagerstown has quietly become one of the Mid-Atlantic's more active secondary logistics and light industrial corridors, drawing interest from regional distributors, cold storage operators, and last-mile fulfillment users who cannot afford Tier 1 industrial rents in Northern Virginia or Baltimore's I-95 corridor but need highway access to both metro areas. Industrial vacancy in the Hagerstown submarket has generally tracked in the low-to-mid single digits over the past several years, with asking rents on newer warehouse and flex-industrial product ranging from roughly $7.00 to $10.00 per square foot NNN, depending on clear height, dock configuration, and vintage. Those are not trophy industrial numbers, but they are numbers that pencil for institutional and regional lenders underwriting at appropriate secondary-market leverage.

Multifamily fundamentals in Hagerstown tell a different story, and one equally relevant to lender liability disputes. Washington County has a renter household base that skews toward workforce and affordable product. Class B and C apartment communities dominate the inventory. Market-rate Class A development has been limited, constrained by land costs that are attractive relative to the DMV metro but by construction costs that are not. Cap rates for stabilized multifamily in the Hagerstown area have historically run in the 6.0% to 7.5% range, materially wider than suburban Maryland cap rates closer to the Beltway. For a lender liability case, those cap rate benchmarks matter enormously: a lender who underwrote a Hagerstown multifamily acquisition using a 5.0% cap rate would have made a fundamental market knowledge error, and that error is documentable through comparable transaction evidence.

Office and retail in Hagerstown reflect trends seen in most secondary markets: suburban office has faced structural headwinds, with vacancy in some corridors exceeding 15%, and retail fundamentals are bifurcated between grocery-anchored necessity retail, which remains serviceable, and unanchored inline retail, which has been challenged. Any lender liability or professional negligence claim involving an office or retail asset in Hagerstown requires expert analysis that accounts for these submarket-specific conditions, not national averages.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing the defendant in a lender liability action arising from the origination of a commercial real estate loan on an industrial flex property in the Hagerstown market. The plaintiff, the borrower, alleged that the originating mortgage broker acted negligently in structuring and placing the loan, specifically that the broker failed to disclose material differences between the loan terms ultimately delivered at closing and the terms reflected in the initial term sheet, that the broker failed to adequately advise the borrower on prepayment penalty exposure, and that the loan placed was not consistent with what a reasonably competent commercial mortgage broker would have sourced given the borrower's stated investment objectives and the property's income profile. Counsel retained CLS CRE to provide a Rule 26 expert report addressing three specific questions: whether the loan origination process met applicable industry standards of care, whether the disclosed and delivered loan terms were consistent with market practice for this property type and loan size in the Hagerstown submarket, and what a reasonably competent commercial mortgage broker would have done differently at each stage of the origination process.

The asset was a multi-tenant industrial flex building constructed in the early 2000s, approximately 60,000 square feet, with a mix of office buildout and warehouse space on a single story. The property was operating at approximately 78% occupancy at the time of loan origination, with leases of varying remaining term. The loan was a bridge-to-permanent structure with a stated intention to stabilize the asset and refinance into long-term fixed-rate debt. The transaction involved a third-party mortgage broker intermediary between the borrower and the lender, which is central to the professional negligence claim.

The Financial Analysis

My first task in any expert engagement of this type is to reconstruct the underwriting independently, using the data that was available to the broker at origination. This is not a hindsight exercise. The standard of care is evaluated at the time of origination, based on what a competent professional should have known and done given the information then available.

The property's trailing 12-month gross rental income at origination was approximately $480,000, consisting of base rents from occupied units. A competent broker analyzing this property would have applied a vacancy and credit loss allowance consistent with Hagerstown industrial flex market conditions. Given the property's actual 78% occupancy and submarket vacancy trends, a prudent underwriting assumption would not have applied less than 10% to 12% economic vacancy against gross potential rents, reflecting both physical vacancy and lease-up risk during the bridge loan period. Gross potential rent at full occupancy, based on in-place lease rates, was approximately $615,000 annually. Applying a 10% vacancy factor to gross potential rent yields effective gross income of approximately $553,500.

Operating expenses on a property of this type in the Hagerstown market, including real estate taxes, insurance, management fees, maintenance reserves, and common area expenses, would reasonably total between $3.50 and $4.50 per square foot annually, or approximately $210,000 to $270,000. I used $240,000 as the midpoint, producing a stabilized Net Operating Income estimate of approximately $313,500 for fully stabilized underwriting, and an as-is NOI based on actual occupancy and in-place rents of approximately $247,000.

Industry standard DSCR thresholds for bridge loans on industrial flex assets in secondary markets like Hagerstown range from 1.15x to 1.25x on a current or as-is basis, with the expectation that stabilized DSCR will reach 1.25x or better before any refinance takeout. For permanent financing, most institutional lenders and CMBS programs require 1.25x DSCR at underwritten NOI. Community banks in Washington County may flex to 1.20x for relationships with strong sponsorship.

At the loan amount actually originated, approximately $3,200,000, with an interest rate of 7.25% (a reasonable bridge rate for this vintage and market) and a 25-year amortization schedule, annual debt service was approximately $262,500. Dividing as-is NOI of $247,000 by that debt service produces a DSCR of 0.94x at origination. A DSCR below 1.0x on an industrial flex property with only 78% occupancy is not a bridge loan with a credible stabilization path; it is a loan that begins underwater and depends entirely on lease-up assumptions that were not adequately stress-tested in the broker's origination materials. A competent broker should have identified this gap, disclosed it explicitly to the borrower, and either structured the loan differently, negotiated a lower loan amount, or advised the borrower that the loan economics were inconsistent with the stated goal of a clean refinance exit within 24 to 36 months.

From an LTV standpoint, a contemporaneous appraisal valued the property at $4,100,000 as-is. The originated loan of $3,200,000 represents approximately 78% LTV. Bridge lenders in this product category and market will generally advance to 70% to 75% LTV on partially stabilized industrial flex. An 78% LTV loan on a sub-1.0x DSCR asset falls outside the credit parameters of virtually every institutional bridge program active in the Hagerstown corridor, raising the question of what lender accepted this loan and on what disclosed terms.

The Complicating Factors

Lease rollover risk and weighted average lease term. Industrial flex properties with multiple tenants on short-term leases present compounded rollover risk that many borrowers do not fully appreciate and that negligent brokers sometimes do not disclose. At origination, the subject property's weighted average lease term across occupied suites was approximately 18 months. A competent broker would have modeled lease-up and rollover scenarios, presented those to the borrower, and discussed how the lender would handle lease renewals and lease-up milestones during the bridge period. There was no evidence in the origination file that any such analysis was prepared or shared.

Prepayment penalty structure mismatch. The loan as placed carried a step-down prepayment penalty of 5-4-3-2-1 over a five-year term. The borrower's stated investment objective was a 24 to 36-month hold followed by a refinance into permanent fixed-rate financing once the property stabilized. A five-year prepayment structure is fundamentally inconsistent with a 24 to 36-month business plan. A reasonably competent broker would have negotiated a shorter lockout or step-down aligned with the borrower's exit timeline, or at minimum documented in writing that the borrower understood and accepted the mismatch. Industry practice requires brokers to confirm alignment between loan structure and sponsor exit strategy. That alignment was absent here.

Term sheet to closing variance. One of the most litigated issues in lender liability cases involving mortgage brokers is the gap between initial term sheet economics and final closing terms. In this case, the initial term sheet reflected an interest rate of 6.75% and a 70% LTV. The loan closed at 7.25% and 78% LTV. The rate increase of 50 basis points on a $3,200,000 loan increases annual interest expense by $16,000, which on a tight DSCR situation is material. Industry standards require brokers to promptly notify borrowers of material changes to offered terms and obtain written acknowledgment before proceeding. A 50 basis point rate move is material. The broker's failure to document that disclosure is a significant departure from standard practice.

Secondary market lender depth. Hagerstown is a secondary market. The universe of lenders willing to originate bridge loans on partially stabilized industrial flex at this loan size is more limited than in Baltimore or Northern Virginia. A broker who represented to the borrower that the terms obtained were competitive without demonstrating that meaningful alternative lender quotes were solicited failed a basic standard of care. Best execution in a secondary market requires active canvassing of the lender market, not a placement with a single relationship lender.

The Hagerstown Lender Market

Understanding the actual lender market active in Hagerstown for industrial and flex product is essential context for any financing expert opinion. The lender landscape here breaks into several distinct categories, each with different credit parameters, pricing expectations, and relationship dynamics.

Regional and community banks. Washington County and the adjacent Cumberland Valley region are served by a meaningful network of community and regional banks with active CRE lending programs. These institutions will lend on industrial, multifamily, and owner-occupied commercial assets in the Hagerstown market, typically up to 75% LTV, at DSCR minimums of 1.20x to 1.25x, with personal guarantees required and local market knowledge factored into credit decisions. Rates for these lenders have historically run at spreads over benchmark indices appropriate to term. For a partially stabilized asset, community banks may require a deposit relationship or other ancillary business as a condition of approval.

CMBS and conduit lenders. CMBS programs will consider stabilized industrial and multifamily product in the Hagerstown market, typically requiring 90-day average occupancy above 85%, DSCR of 1.25x or better at underwritten NOI, LTV not exceeding 70% to 75%, and minimum loan sizes typically starting at $2,000,000 to $3,000,000. Hagerstown industrial properties with strong in-place cash flow and tenancy from credit-rated or institutional tenants are CMBS-eligible; partially stabilized multi-tenant flex with short lease terms is generally not.

Debt funds and bridge lenders. For transitional assets like the subject property, debt funds operating in the Mid-Atlantic corridor are the appropriate capital source. These lenders will advance on properties in lease-up or repositioning, typically at 65% to 75% of stabilized value, at floating rates over SOFR with origination fees of 1% to 2%. They require credible business plans, experienced sponsorship, and completion guarantees or carry reserves. CLS CRE maintains active relationships with more than 1,000 lenders nationally, including debt funds with specific Mid-Atlantic secondary market programs.

Life companies and agency lenders. Life insurance company programs and agency lenders such as Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac are relevant for stabilized multifamily in the Hagerstown market but not for transitional industrial flex. A broker who suggested agency financing as a takeout option for a property at 78% occupancy either lacked market knowledge or was not being straight with the borrower.

What Attorneys Handling Hagerstown CRE Disputes Need to Know

Retaining a CRE financing expert is not the same as retaining an appraiser, and the distinction matters. An appraiser determines value. A CRE financing expert determines whether the process and conduct surrounding a loan origination, loan modification, or financing decision met applicable industry standards. Those are different questions, and they require different expertise.

When to retain a CRE financing expert. Counsel should consider retaining a CRE financing expert when the litigation involves allegations of broker negligence or misrepresentation in loan placement, lender liability for improper loan modification or acceleration, disputes over whether a borrower received competitive financing terms, claims that a lender or broker failed to disclose material loan terms, foreclosure defense strategies involving the adequacy of the original underwriting, and bankruptcy plan feasibility disputes where the debtor claims a loan was structured improperly from origination.

What a CRE financing expert can answer that an appraiser cannot. An appraiser will tell you what the property was worth. A CRE financing expert will tell you whether the loan should have been made on those terms, whether the broker exercised reasonable care in placing the loan, what alternative lender programs were available and on what terms, whether the disclosed terms were consistent with market practice, and what the underwriting file should have contained but did not. These opinions go to conduct, not just value.

Qualifications matter. A retired banker who spent a career approving loans at a single institution is not the same as an active commercial mortgage broker with broad market exposure. Expert qualifications should include active transaction experience across multiple lender types, demonstrated knowledge of the specific market and asset class at issue, experience preparing and submitting loan packages to multiple lender categories, and ideally prior Rule 26 expert engagement. At CLS CRE, I have that combination: approximately $1 billion in transaction volume across 50 states, relationships with more than 1,000 lenders, and prior expert engagement experience in financing disputes.

Information counsel should gather before the expert engagement. Before retaining a CRE financing expert, counsel should gather the complete origination file including all term sheets and commitment letters, the appraisal and any lender review appraisals, all written and email communications between broker, borrower, and lender, the final loan documents, any marketing materials or lender presentations prepared by the broker, evidence of what other lenders were contacted or considered, and the borrower's stated investment objectives from any engagement letter or correspondence.

How expert testimony is used. In trial, CRE financing expert testimony typically establishes the applicable standard of care, documents the specific departures from that standard, and quantifies damages by showing what loan terms a competent broker would have obtained and how those better terms would have changed the borrower's economic position. In bankruptcy plan hearings, financing expert testimony is often used to establish that a proposed exit financing or takeout loan is feasible given market conditions, or conversely that a lender's claim that no refinancing was possible is not supported by the actual lender market.

Key Takeaways

  • DSCR below 1.0x at origination is a red flag, not a starting point. Any broker who originates a loan on a partially stabilized property without disclosing that as-is DSCR is below coverage thresholds has failed a basic standard of care, regardless of what the stabilized projections show.
  • Loan structure must align with sponsor exit strategy. A five-year prepayment penalty on a 24-month business plan is not a broker error; it is a negligent mismatch that exposes the borrower to material unquantified cost. Documenting borrower acknowledgment of that mismatch is minimum practice.
  • Material term sheet variances require written disclosure. A 50 basis point rate move between term sheet and closing is material on any commercial loan. Industry standards require brokers to notify borrowers promptly and in writing, obtain acknowledgment, and give the borrower an opportunity to evaluate alternatives before closing.

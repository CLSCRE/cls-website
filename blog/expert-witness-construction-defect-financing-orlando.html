Expert Witness: How Construction Defects Affect CRE Financing in Orlando, FL

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Construction Defect Impact on Financing

The Orlando Market Context

Orlando's commercial real estate market carries more complexity than its tourism-driven reputation suggests. The metro has emerged as one of the most dynamic secondary markets in the Southeast, with a diversified economic base spanning healthcare, aerospace and defense, technology, higher education, and logistics. That diversification matters in a construction defect financing dispute because lenders underwriting stabilized or value-add assets in Orlando are not applying the same risk lens as they would in a single-industry market. The story the asset can tell a lender depends heavily on which submarket it sits in and what demand drivers are actually at work.

On the multifamily side, Orlando posted some of the sharpest rent growth in the country between 2021 and 2023, driven by significant in-migration from higher-cost Northeast and Midwest markets. Cap rates for stabilized apartment assets in submarkets like Lake Nona, Winter Park, and the I-Drive corridor have compressed into the low-to-mid 5% range for Class A product, with Class B value-add assets trading in the 6% to 6.75% range depending on condition and vintage. That vintage qualifier becomes legally significant when a property is subject to construction defect claims: a lender's perception of a 2019 build versus a 2007 build is entirely different, and an expert needs to articulate that distinction with precision.

Industrial is the other standout. Orange and Osceola Counties have seen industrial vacancy compress below 5% in recent quarters, supported by last-mile distribution demand tied to the region's tourism and population base, as well as broader e-commerce tailwinds. New construction deliveries have been absorbed at a pace that kept asking rents for Class A industrial in the $10 to $13 NNN per square foot range. When construction defect litigation involves an industrial asset in this environment, the financing feasibility question is not academic. A compromised building in a tight market represents a quantifiable economic injury precisely because the demand to lease a fully functional asset exists; the defects are what prevent the borrower from capturing that value.

Office fundamentals in Orlando remain challenged, with downtown and suburban Class B vacancy elevated above 18% in several nodes, a condition lenders are acutely aware of. Any construction defect dispute involving office product must account for a financing environment where even a defect-free asset faces significant institutional lender resistance, which means a defect-laden office asset occupies a particularly difficult financing position.

The Engagement

In this matter, our firm, Commercial Lending Solutions, was retained by litigation counsel representing the property owner in a construction defect action involving a newly developed mid-rise multifamily asset in the greater Orlando metropolitan area. The property had been completed and was in lease-up when the alleged defects were identified, creating a situation where the owner was simultaneously managing litigation exposure, attempting to stabilize occupancy, and confronting a refinance requirement as the construction loan maturity approached.

Counsel needed expert analysis addressing three distinct but interrelated financial questions. First, what was the financing feasibility of the as-defective asset in the current lender market, and which lender categories would even consider a loan under those circumstances? Second, how did the alleged defects affect the property's supportable loan amount, specifically through their impact on underwritten net operating income, achievable debt service coverage ratios, and maximum loan-to-value constraints? Third, what would the financing landscape look like for a defect-free asset of equivalent type, location, and vintage, so that the delta between the two scenarios could be quantified and presented as a measure of financing-related damages?

These are not questions an appraiser is positioned to answer from first principles. An appraiser can establish market value. A CRE financing expert establishes what a lender will actually do with a given asset under given conditions, which is a function of credit policy, capital markets conditions, debt yield requirements, reserve holdbacks, and lender-specific construction defect protocols. CLS CRE was engaged to provide a Rule 26 expert report covering all three questions, supported by quantitative analysis and our direct knowledge of lender requirements in the Florida multifamily market.

The Financial Analysis

The starting point for any financing feasibility analysis is the income stream. For this asset, we built a stabilized net operating income model based on actual in-place leases, market rent comparables drawn from a defined competitive set within the submarket, and an underwritten vacancy and credit loss assumption benchmarked to both current market conditions and the specific operational disruption caused by the alleged defects.

In the defect-free scenario, the property's achievable stabilized gross potential rent was modeled at approximately $3.6 million annually for the unit mix in question. Applying a market-standard vacancy and credit loss factor of 7%, adding ancillary income from parking and fees, and deducting stabilized operating expenses including management fees, insurance, real estate taxes, maintenance, and capital reserves, we arrived at a stabilized NOI of approximately $2.05 million. That figure represented the income a fully functional, defect-free version of this asset could credibly support for underwriting purposes.

In the as-defective scenario, the calculus changed materially. The alleged defects, which included moisture intrusion, compromised envelope systems, and HVAC deficiencies affecting a meaningful percentage of units, created three distinct income impairments: rent concessions required to lease affected units, increased unit turnover reducing effective occupancy, and elevated operating expenses from temporary remediation measures and insurance claim activity. After modeling those impairments against the known defect scope, the underwritten NOI in the as-defective scenario fell to approximately $1.61 million, a reduction of roughly $440,000 annually.

DSCR thresholds for agency-eligible multifamily in Florida have generally been benchmarked at 1.25x on a stressed basis, with Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac applying debt service calculations at note rate on a 30-year amortization schedule for permanent financing. For conventional bank and debt fund bridge lending, the threshold is typically 1.20x at the note rate, though many bridge lenders also apply a global cash flow test and a minimum debt yield floor in the 8% to 9% range for Class A multifamily. Using a current note rate assumption of 6.75% on a 30-year amortization, the maximum supportable loan in the defect-free scenario at 1.25x DSCR was approximately $24.1 million. At a typical 70% LTV ceiling for agency multifamily, that loan amount implied a required asset value of approximately $34.4 million, which aligned with the appraiser's defect-free value conclusion and confirmed internal consistency in our analysis.

Running the same calculation on the as-defective NOI of $1.61 million produced a maximum supportable loan of approximately $18.9 million at 1.25x DSCR, a reduction of roughly $5.2 million in debt capacity. But the loan amount constraint was not the only financing impairment. In the as-defective scenario, lenders applying a debt yield floor of 8.5% would cap the loan at $18.9 million regardless of appraised value, and many would further impose a construction defect reserve holdback of 100% to 125% of the estimated remediation cost, reducing the net loan proceeds actually available to the borrower at closing. On a remediation estimate of $3.1 million, that holdback could reduce available proceeds by an additional $3.1 million to $3.875 million, compressing the effective loan to approximately $15 million to $15.8 million. The financing-related damages, measured as the reduction in net available loan proceeds between the defect-free and as-defective scenarios, fell in the range of $8.3 million to $9.1 million, exclusive of the cost of remediation itself.

The Complicating Factors

Active Litigation as a Lender Disqualifier. The most immediate financing obstacle a property owner faces when construction defect litigation is pending is not the defects themselves. It is the existence of the lawsuit. Most institutional lenders, including agency sellers and servicers, life companies, CMBS conduits, and regional banks, will not originate a new loan on a property where the borrower is a named party in active litigation involving the collateral. This is a standard credit policy restriction, not a negotiated term. The concern is twofold: the litigation outcome is uncertain and could impair title, value, or operability, and the legal fees and potential liability represent contingent obligations that could affect the borrower's capacity to service debt. For counsel, this means that the financing impairment caused by construction defects is not simply a matter of lower NOI and reduced loan proceeds. The litigation itself, irrespective of its merits, triggers a separate financing market exclusion that must be analyzed independently.

Construction Defect Reserves and Holdbacks. For bridge lenders and debt funds willing to lend on properties with disclosed defects or pending remediation, the standard market practice is to impose a reserve holdback equal to at least 100% of the independent engineer's remediation estimate, often 125% given cost uncertainty. These holdbacks are funded from loan proceeds, meaning the borrower receives less net capital at closing than the face amount of the loan. If the property is in lease-up and the borrower needs proceeds for carry costs or operating shortfalls, the effective capital available may be far below what the nominal loan amount suggests. Presenting this holdback mechanism clearly in expert testimony allows counsel to frame the financing impairment in terms that are concrete and comprehensible to a fact finder.

Insurance Complications. Commercial property insurance carriers track construction defect claims, and a property with an open defect claim frequently faces non-renewal of its existing policy, coverage exclusions for defect-related perils, or material premium increases at renewal. Lenders require evidence of replacement cost coverage as a condition of any loan. A property that cannot obtain standard insurance at reasonable cost creates an independent financing obstacle layered on top of the income and DSCR issues. In our analysis, we documented the insurance market response and its role in lender decision-making, because an attorney arguing financing impairment needs to account for every layer through which the defects affect capital access.

Lender Recourse Requirements. Most non-agency lenders presented with a construction defect situation will require full personal recourse or a carve-out guaranty specifically covering defect remediation obligations, even if the same loan would otherwise be structured non-recourse. This is a material change in deal terms that imposes personal liability on the principal, which has its own economic value and damages implications.

The Orlando Lender Market

Orlando is well served by institutional capital for clean, stabilized multifamily assets. Agency lenders through Fannie Mae's Delegated Underwriting and Servicing program and Freddie Mac's Optigo network are active in the market, and several of the largest agency origination shops have Southeast regional offices that cover Orlando. However, as described above, agency lenders will not engage on a property with pending construction defect litigation, and their underwriting guidelines require representations at closing that no material defects exist. That door is closed until litigation is resolved and remediation is complete.

For bridge and transitional financing on a defect-affected asset, the relevant lender universe shifts to debt funds and private credit platforms. Several large national debt funds are active in the Florida market and have experience structuring loans with construction defect holdbacks and remediation completion requirements. These lenders price at spreads of 300 to 500 basis points over the Secured Overnight Financing Rate depending on leverage, defect severity, and remediation timeline certainty. They typically require engagement of an independent construction manager to oversee remediation, draw controls on the holdback reserve, and a completion guaranty from the principal sponsor. Loan terms are typically two to three years with extension options tied to leasing and remediation milestones.

Community banks and regional banks headquartered in Florida, including several active in the Central Florida market, represent another potential source, but their credit policies vary significantly. Some Florida-chartered community banks will lend on assets with disclosed but contained defects if the loan-to-value is sufficiently conservative, typically 55% to 60% on an as-is basis, the principal has strong liquidity, and a funded remediation reserve is established at closing. Their pricing is generally more favorable than debt funds, but their approval processes are less standardized and their capacity to handle complex construction defect structures is inconsistent.

Life insurance companies and CMBS conduits are not relevant capital sources in an active defect scenario. Both require clean environmental and property condition reports, clear title, and no pending litigation affecting the collateral. CLS CRE maintains active relationships across all these lender categories, and our ability to canvas the actual lender market in real time, rather than relying on historical generalizations, is a core component of what makes our expert analysis credible and current.

What Attorneys Handling Orlando CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to retain a CRE financing expert. If your case involves a commercial real estate asset where the alleged wrong, whether a construction defect, a seller's failure to disclose, a breach of contract, a lender's failure to fund, or a fiduciary breach, had any effect on the property's ability to obtain financing, refinancing, or exit through sale, you need a CRE financing expert. An appraiser establishes value. A CRE financing expert establishes what a borrower can actually do with a given asset in the real capital markets, which is where economic harm becomes concrete.

What a CRE financing expert can answer that an appraiser cannot. Appraisers apply standardized methodologies to arrive at a market value conclusion. They are not generally trained in or focused on lender credit policy, DSCR underwriting mechanics, debt yield floors, reserve holdback practices, recourse structures, or the specific eligibility requirements of agency, CMBS, life company, and debt fund capital sources. When the legal question is not "what is this property worth" but rather "what financing could this borrower have obtained and on what terms, and how does that compare to a defect-free scenario," an appraiser is the wrong expert. The financing expert answers questions that exist in capital markets, not in the Uniform Standards of Professional Appraisal Practice.

What qualifies a CRE financing expert. Relevant qualifications include direct, current transactional experience originating, placing, or structuring commercial loans across multiple asset types and capital sources. A retired banker who spent a career at one institution has deep knowledge of one credit box; they may not understand how debt funds price, how agency sellers underwrite, or how CMBS conduits handle deferred maintenance. A mortgage broker or capital advisor with active relationships across hundreds of lenders, who is placing loans in the current market, can credibly testify about what lenders actually do, not what they did in a prior credit cycle.

Information counsel should gather before the expert engagement. The expert will need the rent roll, trailing 12-month operating statements, the property condition and environmental reports, the existing loan documents including any guaranties, the construction contract and any pay application records, the independent engineer's defect assessment and remediation cost estimate, and any lender correspondence reflecting the borrower's efforts to refinance or restructure the loan during the defect period. The more complete this record, the more defensible the expert's quantitative conclusions.

How expert testimony is used. In trial, CRE financing expert testimony typically addresses damages, specifically the quantification of financing-related harm as a component of the total economic injury claimed. In plan confirmation hearings, expert testimony may address feasibility, specifically whether a reorganized debtor can actually obtain the financing the plan assumes. In mediation, a well-documented expert report often provides the analytical foundation around which a settlement range is defined.

Key Takeaways

  • Active construction defect litigation is itself a lender disqualifier, separate from the income and value impairments caused by the defects. This dual impairment must be analyzed and quantified independently in any damages framework.
  • The DSCR and debt yield mechanics are where financing impairment becomes quantifiable. A reduction in underwritten NOI caused by defect-related rent concessions, elevated expenses, or occupancy disruption flows directly and arithmetically into a reduced maximum supportable loan, and that delta is a defensible measure of financing-related damages.
  • Reserve holdbacks imposed by lenders on defect-affected assets reduce net available loan proceeds well below the nominal loan amount, creating a layer of financing impairment that is separate from, and additive to, the DSCR-driven loan reduction.
  • The lender universe contracts materially when construction defects are present and litigation is pending. Agency, life company, and CMBS sources are effectively eliminated, and the remaining bridge and bank options price at significantly higher rates with more restrictive terms, all of which represent quantifiable economic harm to the borrower.
  • Orlando's strong multifamily and industrial fundamentals mean that defect-free assets of equivalent type can access a deep, competitive financing market. That market depth is what makes the financing impairment in a defect scenario so pronounced: the borrower is excluded from a favorable market, not a distressed one.

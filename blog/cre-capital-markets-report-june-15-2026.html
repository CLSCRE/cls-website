Commercial Real Estate Capital Markets Report: June 15, 2026

By Trevor Damyan June 15, 2026

Every two weeks we pull together the rate environment, yield curve movements, and deal-level observations that matter most to CRE borrowers and investors. Here is what we are watching as of June 15, 2026.

US Treasury Yield Curve: Month-Over-Month Shift

The yield curve remained the dominant conversation in CRE lending circles heading into mid-June. Month over month, the curve continued its gradual re-steepening as short-term yields held near recent ranges while the long end absorbed ongoing supply pressure from Treasury issuance. For CRE borrowers, this dynamic matters because most permanent loan pricing anchors to the 5-year or 10-year Treasury, and incremental movement at those maturities flows directly into fixed-rate spreads quoted by life companies, CMBS conduits, and agency lenders.

When the long end moves, even modestly, borrowers who delayed rate locks feel it. A 10 to 15 basis point move on the 10-year Treasury translates to a real change in debt service coverage on a levered asset. That is the mechanical reason we track this data every issue: not to forecast rates, but to give our clients an honest read on where the cost of capital sits today versus 30 days ago.

What the Curve Shape Means for Loan Structuring

A steeper curve creates a meaningful spread between short-term floating-rate debt and long-term fixed-rate debt. In practical terms, bridge loans priced off SOFR are currently cheaper in coupon than fixed-rate permanent financing, which has extended the appetite for floating-rate structures on transitional assets. Sponsors who would have historically refinanced into a 10-year fixed execution are instead holding bridge loans while they wait for the long end to settle or their business plans to mature.

This is not a risk-free posture. Floating-rate borrowers carry rate cap costs, extension fee exposure, and lender re-underwriting risk at each extension. When we structure bridge financing for clients today, we are explicit about building in rate cap coverage through the full term, including extensions, and stress-testing exit assumptions against a range of permanent loan rates rather than a single point estimate.

Lender Appetite Across Property Types

Across our active lender relationships, the clearest theme in June is selectivity by property type rather than a broad pullback in capital availability. Here is how the landscape looks from our desk:

  • Multifamily: Agency executions through Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac remain the tightest spreads available in the market for stabilized assets. A regional bank active in our network recently tightened its spread on 5-year multifamily term loans, reflecting renewed confidence in rent fundamentals in primary markets. Competition among lenders for quality multifamily paper is real.
  • Industrial: Life insurance companies continue to favor long-lease industrial. We are seeing spreads on 10-year fixed-rate industrial loans hold at levels that are attractive relative to history, driven by strong appetite from institutional fixed-income allocators who view the asset class as a credit-quality play.
  • Retail: Grocery-anchored and necessity-based retail with long WALT is financeable, and in some cases lenders are competing aggressively for it. Unanchored strip and fashion-dependent retail continues to face meaningful haircuts on proceeds and higher spreads. Know your subtype before approaching lenders.
  • Office: The bifurcation between trophy Class A urban product and suburban Class B/C remains stark. A private bridge lender we work with recently closed on a suburban office repositioning at a loan-to-cost that would have been unthinkable 18 months ago, reflecting the risk premium lenders demand for office exposure. Long-term fixed-rate office financing outside of high-quality assets is largely unavailable from traditional sources.
  • Hospitality: Select-service hotels in drive-to leisure markets continue to attract lender interest. Debt funds and a handful of regional banks are active here. Full-service urban hotels require either a patient debt fund or an SBA component to get deals across the finish line.

Bridge Lending Activity: What We Are Closing

Our transaction pipeline in May and early June reflected the broader theme of transitional asset financing. A private bridge lender provided proceeds on a value-add multifamily acquisition where the business plan required unit interior renovation across a significant percentage of the rent roll. The structure included full-term rate cap coverage, a funded interest reserve, and a renovation holdback with a draw schedule tied to unit completion and re-leasing milestones. Getting the draw mechanics right on a bridge loan of this type is as important as the headline rate, and that is where deal experience on the broker side matters.

On the permanent side, a life insurance company closed a 10-year fixed-rate loan on a grocery-anchored retail center. Proceeds were sized to a debt yield floor that the lender held firm on throughout the process. The lesson here is that in the current environment, lenders are underwriting to metrics they will defend, and borrowers who enter the process with realistic expectations close faster and on better terms than those who push for maximum leverage early and concede ground late.

Rate Lock Timing and Execution Risk

One topic that comes up repeatedly with clients right now is when to pull the trigger on a rate lock. With the yield curve showing month-over-month movement, the temptation to wait for a better entry point is understandable. Our view is straightforward: we do not try to time the Treasury market for our clients, and we counsel against it. What we do is make sure every borrower understands the cost of a 30-day delay in concrete terms, including the rate differential, any lock extension fees, and the business plan impact of a delayed close. That math usually makes the decision clear.

If you have a transaction in process and are weighing rate lock timing, that is exactly the conversation we want to have before you make the call.

Looking Ahead to July

The second half of June brings Fed meeting commentary, fresh CPI data, and the typical mid-year portfolio rebalancing by institutional lenders that can shift spread levels in either direction with limited notice. We will be watching the 10-year Treasury closely and updating our lender conversations in real time. As always, the borrowers who stay close to their brokers during periods of rate volatility are the ones who close on the terms they underwrote.

Frequently Asked Questions

Most permanent CRE loans price off the 5-year or 10-year US Treasury yield. When the long end of the curve rises, fixed-rate spreads quoted by life companies, CMBS conduits, and agency lenders translate that movement into higher all-in rates for borrowers. Tracking the yield curve month over month helps borrowers understand whether the cost of capital has shifted since they last underwrote their deal.
Bridge loans are typically floating-rate instruments priced off SOFR and are used for transitional or value-add assets. Permanent loans are fixed-rate and priced off Treasury benchmarks. In a steeper yield curve environment, the short end can be cheaper in coupon, making bridge financing attractive, but borrowers must account for rate cap costs, extension fees, and exit financing risk when comparing the two structures.
Stabilized multifamily and long-lease industrial are attracting the most competitive lender interest and the tightest spreads. Grocery-anchored retail with strong anchor credit and long weighted average lease terms is also well-received. Office and unanchored retail face more constrained capital availability and require a more targeted lender approach.
Rate lock timing is one of the most consequential decisions in a CRE financing. Rather than attempting to time the Treasury market, borrowers should quantify the cost of waiting in concrete terms: the rate differential, any lock extension fees, and the business plan impact of a delayed close. A commercial mortgage broker can help you run that analysis before you commit.
A funded interest reserve is a portion of the loan proceeds held by the lender and used to make monthly debt service payments during the period when the property is not yet generating stabilized cash flow. It protects both the borrower and the lender during the value-add business plan execution period and is a standard structural feature in well-underwritten bridge loans.

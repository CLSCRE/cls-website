Expert Witness: Commercial Loan Modification Standards in Atlantic City, NJ

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Loan Modification and Workout Dispute

The Atlantic City Market Context

Atlantic City is not a monolithic commercial real estate market, and attorneys handling CRE disputes here need to understand exactly why that matters when evaluating a loan modification or workout. The market operates across two fundamentally different economic layers: the gaming and hospitality corridor anchored along the Boardwalk and Marina District, and the broader Atlantic County commercial base that includes retail, multifamily, mixed-use, and industrial product spread across submarkets like the Chelsea neighborhood, the Inlet, and adjacent municipalities such as Absecon, Egg Harbor Township, and Pleasantville. A workout dispute involving a Boardwalk-adjacent hotel is a categorically different underwriting problem than one involving a strip retail center in the Chelsea neighborhood or an apartment complex in the Inlet area, even though all three might carry the same zip code.

The gaming sector's structural contraction since 2013 and 2014, when several major casino properties closed within months of each other, permanently reset investor and lender expectations for Atlantic City commercial real estate. Lenders who had previously underwritten to gaming-driven hospitality demand retreated dramatically, and the institutional capital that remained required substantially more conservative debt coverage ratios and lower loan-to-value thresholds than they applied in comparable New Jersey coastal markets. By the late 2010s, the market was beginning to stabilize as new non-gaming hospitality concepts, state-supported infrastructure investment, and a modest residential conversion pipeline started attracting fresh capital. Then the 2020 disruption hit a market that was still in the middle of its recovery arc, compounding existing stress on properties that had never fully normalized their income streams.

As of recent years, Atlantic City's multifamily fundamentals have been among the more attractive components of the local market, with occupancies in stabilized properties running in the high 80s to low 90s in better-located submarkets, and effective rents showing modest but consistent upward movement as workforce housing demand has firmed. Retail vacancy remains elevated across the market, particularly in dependent or secondary trade areas, with vacancy rates in non-Boardwalk retail corridors exceeding 15% in many blocks. Office product in Atlantic City proper remains challenged; the market never developed a deep corporate office base, and what exists tends to be government, healthcare, or legal services occupancy. Industrial product in the broader Atlantic County area, particularly near the Atlantic City International Airport and the Egg Harbor Township logistics corridors, has tightened meaningfully, benefiting from the same regional distribution demand that has compressed vacancy across South Jersey. Understanding which segment of this market a disputed loan touches is the threshold question for any financing expert engagement here.

The Engagement

In this type of matter, Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing a commercial borrower in a dispute arising from a loan modification and forbearance process that the borrower contended was conducted in bad faith and on commercially unreasonable terms. The asset at issue was a mixed-use commercial property in the Atlantic City market combining ground-floor retail space with upper-floor multifamily units, a property type that became particularly complicated during the pandemic-era disruption given the divergent performance of its two income components. The borrower had approached the lender seeking a modification after the retail component experienced significant rent interruption, while the residential component continued generating income, though at reduced effective rents due to concessions offered to retain tenants.

Counsel retained CLS CRE to address four core questions. First, whether the lender's offered modification terms, including a proposed interest rate increase, a shortened amortization schedule, and a required principal paydown, were consistent with what a commercially reasonable lender operating in the Atlantic City market would have offered under the documented conditions. Second, whether the lender's internal analysis of the property's income stream used methodology consistent with industry standards for mixed-use CRE workouts. Third, whether an alternative modification structure existed that would have been commercially feasible and adequately protective of the lender's security interest without the punitive economic terms the borrower alleged were imposed. Fourth, what the financing market for this asset type actually looked like during the relevant forbearance period, meaning whether the borrower's implicit threat of refinancing away was credible or illusory, a fact with direct relevance to the good faith question.

The Financial Analysis

The first analytical task was reconstructing the property's actual net operating income during the period immediately preceding and during the forbearance negotiation. For mixed-use product in the Atlantic City market, this requires treating the retail and residential components separately before consolidating, because the applicable market assumptions, vacancy rates, expense ratios, and capitalization rates differ meaningfully between the two uses.

On the residential side, the subject property contained approximately 18 market-rate residential units. At average in-place rents of roughly $1,150 per month and a stabilized occupancy of 88%, the gross potential residential income ran approximately $248,000 annually, with effective gross income after vacancy and collection loss landing near $218,000. Operating expenses on the residential component, including management, maintenance, insurance, real estate taxes, and reserves, ran approximately 42% of effective gross income, yielding residential NOI of approximately $126,000.

The retail component presented a more complicated picture. The ground-floor retail space totaling approximately 4,200 square feet had a single anchor tenant whose lease included a co-tenancy clause that was triggered by vacancy in the surrounding block, resulting in a rent reduction that the tenant exercised during the relevant period. In-place retail rent dropped from a contract rate of approximately $22 per square foot to an effective rate closer to $14 per square foot during the forbearance period, producing retail gross income of approximately $59,000 against which management and net lease pass-through reconciliation adjustments reduced NOI contribution to roughly $41,000.

Consolidated property NOI during the forbearance period was therefore approximately $167,000. The original loan carried a balance of approximately $1.85 million at a fixed rate of 5.25% with a 25-year amortization, producing annual debt service of approximately $136,700 and a DSCR of approximately 1.22x, which placed the loan precisely at the lower threshold of what most commercial lenders treat as acceptable coverage. The industry standard DSCR floor for mixed-use product in secondary markets like Atlantic City runs 1.20x to 1.25x for conventional portfolio lenders, with agency-eligible multifamily product requiring a minimum 1.25x and CMBS execution historically requiring a minimum 1.20x on stressed underwriting. The loan was technically meeting its coverage minimum, but barely, and the lender's modification proposal reflected an apparent determination that the property should be underwritten on a stressed forward-looking basis rather than on trailing actual performance.

The lender's proposed modification increased the rate to 6.75%, extended the remaining amortization term to 20 years rather than resetting it, and required a $150,000 principal curtailment as a condition of the modification. Running those proposed terms against the documented NOI produced a modified debt service of approximately $154,800, yielding a DSCR of 1.08x, which is below the lender's own stated underwriting floor of 1.20x as set forth in the loan documents. This analytical finding was central to the engagement: the lender's modification proposal, as structured, would have produced a loan that failed the lender's own minimum underwriting threshold by a material margin, which is inconsistent with standard commercial workout practice and raises the question of whether the modification was designed to be accepted or designed to create conditions for a default.

Our analysis demonstrated that an alternative structure, specifically a rate modification to 5.75% with a 25-year re-amortization and no principal curtailment, would have produced annual debt service of approximately $130,500, a DSCR of 1.28x, and an LTV consistent with the property's current market value of approximately $2.1 million. That LTV of approximately 88% is above conventional threshold, but the modification would have been supported by additional collateral already held by the lender, making the curtailment requirement analytically unjustifiable on security grounds.

The Complicating Factors

Mixed-use income volatility and retail co-tenancy risk. Lenders underwriting or restructuring loans on mixed-use assets in Atlantic City face a genuine analytical challenge when the retail component carries co-tenancy or kick-out provisions. These clauses, common in leases executed during the 2005 to 2015 period, can trigger rent reductions or early termination rights when vacancy thresholds in adjacent space are exceeded. Atlantic City's retail corridors outside the Boardwalk entertainment zone have experienced persistent vacancy, meaning co-tenancy triggers are not theoretical risks but recurring operational realities for mixed-use owners. A competent lender workout officer should underwrite the retail component at the triggered rent level rather than the contract rate when market conditions make trigger activation probable. Failure to do so overstates NOI in the lender's internal analysis, which can then produce modification terms that appear adequately covered on paper but are actually predicated on income that will not materialize.

The Atlantic City market discount applied by institutional lenders. Institutional lenders, including regional banks, insurance companies, and CMBS programs, apply a market-specific risk premium to Atlantic City commercial assets that does not apply equally across New Jersey. This premium reflects the market's gaming dependency history, the demonstrated illiquidity of certain asset classes during stress periods, and concerns about long-term population and income trends in the core city. In practice, this discount manifests as lower LTV limits, higher DSCR requirements, and reduced loan proceeds relative to appraised value. During a workout negotiation, this market discount affects both parties: the borrower cannot easily refinance out of a stressed modification because the replacement financing market is constrained, and the lender cannot easily dispose of the collateral at appraised value if foreclosure becomes the alternative. An expert witness who does not understand this market-specific dynamic will mischaracterize both the borrower's leverage and the lender's realistic downside.

Documentation of good faith and the forbearance timeline. In workout disputes, the sequence and timing of lender communications, financial information requests, and modification proposals matters as much as the economic terms. Atlantic City CRE lenders, like those elsewhere, are expected under industry standard practice to engage substantively with a borrower's forbearance request within a reasonable timeframe, to provide written responses to financial submissions, and to base modification proposals on the submitted financial information rather than on assumptions that contradict the borrower's documented performance. In this engagement, the lender's initial modification proposal was transmitted approximately 11 months after the borrower's initial forbearance request, during which time default interest had been accreting, and the proposal made no reference to the borrower's submitted rent rolls, leases, or operating statements. That pattern of conduct is inconsistent with standard commercial workout practice, where responsive engagement with submitted financial data is expected within 60 to 90 days of a complete submission package.

The Atlantic City Lender Market

Understanding which lenders are actually active in Atlantic City for a given asset type is essential both to the substantive analysis and to evaluating the borrower's realistic alternatives during the workout period. The lender universe for Atlantic City CRE is narrower than many borrowers and their counsel assume.

Community and regional banks. The most active conventional lenders in the Atlantic City market are South Jersey-focused community banks and mid-sized regional institutions with established Atlantic County relationships. These lenders typically offer portfolio loans at rates priced to the five-year or seven-year Treasury plus a spread of 225 to 325 basis points depending on asset quality and borrower strength, with LTVs capped at 70% to 75% for mixed-use product and DSCR minimums of 1.20x to 1.25x. Their credit underwriting is relationship-driven, and they are more willing than larger institutions to underwrite to normalized income projections if the borrower can document a credible recovery path. However, their loan size limits generally cap out below $5 million for Atlantic City assets outside the hospitality sector.

Debt funds and bridge lenders. For assets with transitional income or borrowers who cannot qualify for conventional financing, private debt funds and bridge lenders fill a portion of the Atlantic City market. These lenders price at significantly higher rates, typically in the 9% to 12% range on an all-in basis, with origination fees of 1% to 2%, and they underwrite to as-stabilized values rather than current NOI. They are not a realistic refinance alternative for a stabilized mixed-use asset in a workout context because the economics do not support the debt service at those rates without significant additional equity.

SBA programs. For owner-occupied commercial properties in the Atlantic City market, SBA 504 and 7(a) programs remain active through authorized CDC lenders operating in New Jersey. These programs are relevant to certain workout disputes because an SBA loan modification involves a federal guarantor and introduces procedural requirements that differ materially from conventional bank workout practice, including required SBA authorization for material modifications.

What Attorneys Handling Atlantic City CRE Disputes Need to Know

A CRE financing expert witness is not an appraiser, and the distinction is legally significant. An appraiser determines value; a financing expert determines whether a given loan, modification, or workout was economically rational, structurally feasible, and consistent with how commercial lenders actually operate. In a loan modification dispute, the central questions, whether the proposed terms were commercially reasonable, whether the lender's underwriting methodology was standard, and whether the borrower had realistic refinancing alternatives, require expertise in loan structuring, lender underwriting criteria, and capital market conditions, not in property valuation methodology. Retaining only an appraiser in this type of matter leaves critical questions unanswered.

Attorneys should retain a CRE financing expert when the dispute turns on any of the following questions: whether a lender's modification terms were commercially reasonable relative to market conditions; whether a lender's internal underwriting of the property's income stream used appropriate methodology; whether a borrower had a realistic ability to refinance or recapitalize during the relevant period; whether a lender failed to engage in good faith modification discussions consistent with industry practice; or whether the terms of a workout agreement, viewed in the context of the lender market conditions at the time, were the product of economic coercion rather than legitimate credit risk management.

A qualified CRE financing expert is not simply a retired banker. The relevant qualification is active, current engagement in commercial mortgage origination and structuring across multiple lender types and markets, combined with a documented history of analyzing financing feasibility for contested or complex transactions. An expert who has not been active in the lending market for five or more years will lack current knowledge of lender appetite, pricing benchmarks, and underwriting standards, all of which change materially with interest rate cycles and credit conditions.

Before engaging an expert, counsel should gather the complete loan file including the original underwriting, all credit memoranda, the modification request package submitted by the borrower, all lender communications and internal memoranda produced in discovery, the property's rent rolls and operating statements for the relevant period, any appraisals obtained by either party, and any third-party reports on the property's market or condition. The expert's analysis is only as good as the documentary record underlying it.

Key Takeaways

  • Atlantic City's market-specific lender discount is real and quantifiable. Any analysis of whether modification terms were commercially reasonable must account for the constrained financing universe that applies to this market, which affects both what replacement financing the borrower could realistically obtain and what recovery the lender could expect from foreclosure as an alternative.
  • A modification that produces a DSCR below the lender's own stated underwriting floor is analytically indefensible. When a lender's proposed modification terms result in projected debt service coverage that fails the lender's own minimum threshold, that is material evidence that the modification was not designed to succeed and raises legitimate questions about the lender's good faith in the workout process.
  • Mixed-use income analysis requires component-level underwriting. Consolidating retail and residential income streams without separately analyzing vacancy risk, lease structure, and applicable market conditions produces unreliable NOI estimates, and lenders who do so in a workout context may be applying an inflated income figure that does not reflect the property's realistic performance.
  • Timeline and responsiveness matter in establishing industry standard of care. Commercial workout practice requires timely engagement with borrower financial submissions, and extended periods of non-response or modification proposals that ignore submitted financial data are departures from standard practice that a financing expert can document and quantify.
  • The borrower's refinancing alternatives during the workout period are an objective fact, not an assertion. A CRE financing expert can model what replacement financing was realistically available during the forbearance period, which directly bears on whether the borrower had genuine economic alternatives to accepting the lender's modification terms.
  • SBA-guaranteed loans introduce procedural standards that differ from conventional workout practice.

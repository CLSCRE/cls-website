Expert Witness: Financing a Contaminated Industrial Property in Missoula, MT

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Environmental Contamination Analysis

The Missoula Market Context

Missoula is not a secondary market in the pejorative sense that institutional capital sometimes uses that phrase. It is a supply-constrained, university-anchored, lifestyle-driven market with a diversified employment base that includes healthcare, education, technology, and outdoor recreation industries. The University of Montana enrolls roughly 10,000 students and employs several thousand full-time staff, providing a stable demand floor that insulates the local economy from the volatility seen in single-industry markets. Missoula's population has grown consistently over the past decade, and inbound migration from higher-cost Western metros has accelerated since 2020, compressing cap rates on stabilized income property and tightening vacancy across most commercial asset classes.

For industrial property specifically, Missoula operates under severe physical supply constraints. The Missoula Valley is ringed by mountains, the Clark Fork River corridor limits developable land, and the cost of extending municipal infrastructure into viable flat parcels is prohibitive. Industrial vacancy in the Missoula market runs consistently below 4%, a figure that would draw institutional capital in any comparable market. Functional warehouse and flex-industrial product is genuinely scarce. Rents for Class B industrial space in the South Reserve Street and Mullan Road corridors have moved upward meaningfully over the past three years, with asking rates for NNN warehouse space now ranging from approximately $8.00 to $11.00 per square foot depending on clear height, dock access, and power. Class A product with modern specifications commands a premium above that range, and there is essentially no speculative pipeline being delivered in the near term given entitlement complexity and construction cost inflation.

The financing market for Missoula industrial assets is active but shallow. Community banks with regional footprints dominate the origination activity. National conduit lenders will look at Missoula industrial deals with the right credit profile, but they require stronger debt service coverage and apply more conservative LTV discipline than they would in a primary market. Credit unions, life companies, and SBA 504 programs each play a role in specific deal structures. The scarcity of industrial product means that when a well-leased building comes to market or requires recapitalization, lenders do not have deep local comparable sets to draw from, which introduces appraisal risk and underwriting conservatism that attorneys and borrowers both need to understand.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by counsel representing secured creditors in a regulatory enforcement proceeding involving an industrial warehouse property in the Missoula Valley. The property is a single-tenant, triple-net-leased industrial warehouse of approximately 40,000 square feet, constructed in the mid-1980s on a site with a documented history of industrial chemical use. Prior operations on the site involved solvent-based manufacturing processes that resulted in subsurface contamination, specifically the presence of 1,4-dioxane and chlorinated volatile organic compounds, including trichloroethylene, in soil and groundwater. The Montana Department of Environmental Quality had issued a formal enforcement notice, and a remediation action plan was under review at the time of our engagement.

The current owner had financed the acquisition with first-mortgage debt that was now approaching maturity. The lease in place was a long-term NNN instrument with a creditworthy single tenant, but the lease rate reflected market conditions at origination several years prior and was meaningfully below current market rents given how much industrial rents in the Missoula Valley had appreciated. The core legal questions presented to CLS CRE were as follows: first, does the documented environmental contamination categorically preclude first-mortgage financing in the current lending market, or is financing potentially available subject to conditions; second, if financing is available, what is the maximum supportable loan amount under current market underwriting standards; and third, does the below-market lease rate constrain the supportable loan to a level insufficient to retire the existing senior debt at maturity. The answers to these questions had direct implications for creditor recovery, plan feasibility, and the resolution of competing claims in the enforcement proceeding.

The Financial Analysis

The income analysis began with the in-place lease. The tenant was paying a base rent of $7.25 per square foot NNN on approximately 40,000 square feet, producing gross base rent of $290,000 annually. Under a true NNN structure, the tenant bears real estate taxes, insurance, and maintenance, so the effective net operating income to the landlord equals base rent less any non-reimbursed management and administrative costs. We applied a 3% management and administrative load, yielding a stabilized NOI of approximately $281,300.

This was the first constraining variable. Market rents for comparable industrial space in the Missoula Valley at the time of our analysis were running $9.00 to $10.50 per square foot NNN for functional single-tenant warehouse with similar specifications. The in-place rent at $7.25 per square foot represented a discount of roughly 20% to 30% below market. An appraiser would capture this in a market value analysis by distinguishing contract rent from market rent and potentially applying a leased-fee discount. For financing purposes, the relevant figure is the in-place NOI, not market NOI, because lenders underwrite to the cash flow they can actually collect, not what might be available at lease expiration.

We applied a debt service coverage ratio threshold of 1.25x, which is the standard minimum for stabilized industrial assets among conventional commercial mortgage lenders in secondary and tertiary markets. Some community banks will go to 1.20x for a well-known borrower on a straightforward deal, but for an asset with environmental complications, 1.25x is the floor, and many lenders would require 1.30x or higher as a risk premium. Using the 1.25x DSCR threshold against an NOI of $281,300, the maximum supportable annual debt service is $225,040.

We modeled the loan at a 6.85% interest rate, reflecting the prevailing pricing for a 5-year fixed-rate commercial mortgage on a secondary-market industrial asset with environmental complications at the time of our analysis. This rate reflects a spread of approximately 250 to 275 basis points over the 5-year Treasury, which is consistent with the risk premium lenders apply to contaminated properties even when financing is available in principle. On a 25-year amortization schedule, which is standard for industrial assets in this size range, a $225,040 annual debt service supports a loan of approximately $2,730,000. We also modeled a 20-year amortization, which several lenders in the Missoula market prefer for older vintage industrial product, and that reduced the supportable loan to approximately $2,510,000.

The second constraining variable was LTV. Environmental contamination introduces a significant valuation discount in the appraisal process. The unimpaired value of a 40,000-square-foot NNN-leased industrial building in the Missoula Valley at a market cap rate of approximately 6.25% to 6.75% applied to market NOI of roughly $360,000 would indicate a value in the range of $5.3 million to $5.75 million. However, the leased-fee value based on in-place contract rent at that same cap rate range is approximately $4.2 million to $4.5 million. The environmental impairment discount applied by appraisers in contaminated-site valuations typically ranges from 10% to 35% depending on the severity, remediation status, and regulatory posture of the contamination. For a site with active DEQ enforcement and an unresolved remediation plan, we applied a conservative impairment range of 20% to 25%, producing an appraised value estimate for financing purposes of approximately $3.15 million to $3.36 million.

At a 65% LTV constraint, which is the standard maximum for environmentally impaired industrial assets among lenders willing to finance them at all, the LTV-constrained loan ceiling is approximately $2.05 million to $2.18 million. This LTV ceiling is lower than the DSCR-constrained maximum of $2.73 million, which means LTV was the binding constraint in this deal. The maximum supportable first-mortgage loan, incorporating both the DSCR test and the LTV ceiling, was in the range of $2.05 million to $2.18 million under our analysis.

The existing senior debt balance at maturity exceeded this figure by a material amount, which directly supported our opinion that the environmental condition, combined with the below-market lease rate, constrained the refinancing capacity of the asset below the level required to retire the existing debt in full from conventional first-mortgage proceeds alone.

The Complicating Factors

1,4-Dioxane and Chlorinated VOC Contamination. These two contaminant classes create different but compounding problems for lenders. Chlorinated VOCs, including trichloroethylene and its breakdown products, are well-understood by environmental engineers and regulators, and their fate-and-transport behavior is relatively predictable. Lenders have developed at least some institutional familiarity with financing properties with documented chlorinated solvent plumes, particularly when remediation is active and regulatory communication is constructive. 1,4-Dioxane is a more difficult contaminant from a financing perspective because it is highly mobile in groundwater, does not readily biodegrade under most natural attenuation conditions, and does not adsorb well to soil particles, meaning it travels further and faster than co-occurring chlorinated solvents. The EPA's health advisory for 1,4-dioxane in drinking water has been tightened substantially, and Montana DEQ applies aggressive regulatory standards. Most conventional lenders, including community banks, become significantly more cautious when they see 1,4-dioxane as a listed contaminant, even when chlorinated VOC contamination alone might have been manageable.

Active Regulatory Enforcement. The presence of a DEQ enforcement notice, rather than a completed remediation with a No Further Action letter, is a near-absolute financing barrier with most lenders who would otherwise consider an impaired property. The distinction matters enormously: a property with a closed regulatory file and recorded institutional controls is financeable for a meaningful population of lenders. A property under active enforcement, with a remediation action plan still pending regulatory approval, creates open-ended liability that most lenders cannot underwrite. Lenders cannot model the cost or duration of remediation with sufficient certainty to price the risk, and environmental liens that arise under CERCLA or state analog statutes can theoretically prime a first mortgage, creating title concerns that render the property unacceptable as collateral.

Below-Market Lease Rate. The below-market rent compounds the financing problem by suppressing the NOI that supports the loan. In a different scenario, a property with strong in-place rents at or above market might generate enough NOI to support a loan large enough to retire the senior debt even with an environmental discount to value. In this deal, the in-place rent was already a ceiling on debt service capacity, and the environmental discount compressed the LTV ceiling simultaneously, creating a two-sided squeeze on the maximum supportable loan.

Remediation Cost Uncertainty. Lenders who are willing to finance contaminated properties at all will typically require a Phase II environmental site assessment, a remediation cost estimate from a licensed environmental professional, and confirmation that the remediation cost is either funded into escrow or covered by an environmental insurance policy with adequate limits. In active enforcement scenarios, the remediation cost estimate is often disputed between the owner and the regulatory agency, and insurers are reluctant to issue new policies covering already-known conditions. This creates a practical impasse that limits the financing universe to lenders with specific environmental lending programs or a high degree of institutional risk tolerance.

The Missoula Lender Market

The lender universe for industrial assets in Missoula under normal conditions includes local and regional community banks, credit unions, national community banks with Western regional footprints, CMBS conduit lenders, life insurance companies for larger stabilized assets, and SBA 504 for owner-occupied transactions. For this asset, with active environmental enforcement and a complex contamination profile, the effective lender universe contracts sharply.

Local and regional community banks, including institutions headquartered in Missoula and Billings with commercial real estate portfolios, are the most likely source of financing for contaminated industrial assets in this market, but only under specific conditions. They require a clear remediation path, confirmation from DEQ that the enforcement posture is cooperative rather than adversarial, environmental cost-to-complete funding, and often a personal guarantee from a creditworthy sponsor. Their credit box for contaminated assets typically requires 1.25x to 1.30x DSCR, maximum 60% to 65% LTV on the impaired appraised value, and a loan term that does not extend beyond the reasonably estimated remediation timeline. Pricing will be 50 to 100 basis points wider than a clean asset.

CMBS conduit lenders will not finance properties under active regulatory enforcement. This is categorical, not situational. Conduit lenders need to securitize loans into pools sold to institutional investors, and environmental contingency of any kind creates representation and warranty risk that the conduit cannot accept. Once a No Further Action letter is in hand and the regulatory file is closed, certain conduit lenders will reconsider, but active enforcement is an absolute disqualifier.

Specialty environmental lenders, including certain non-bank lenders and bridge lending programs that focus specifically on contaminated or transitional industrial assets, represent a meaningful part of the solution set for deals like this one. They are more expensive, typically pricing at 200 to 350 basis points over conventional lending rates, and they apply more conservative LTV constraints, usually 55% to 60% of impaired value. However, they bring institutional expertise in environmental credit underwriting that community banks lack, and they can structure around active enforcement scenarios more effectively. Their presence in the Missoula market is limited, meaning deals typically need to be brokered to these lenders through a national platform rather than sourced locally.

What Attorneys Handling Missoula CRE Disputes Need to Know

A CRE financing expert witness answers a category of questions that an appraiser, an environmental consultant, and a fact witness collectively cannot. The appraiser tells you what the property is worth. The environmental consultant tells you what the contamination is and what it will cost to remediate. The CRE financing expert tells you whether, and on what terms, a secured creditor can expect to be repaid through refinancing proceeds, what loan amount the market will actually support for this specific asset with this specific contamination profile, and whether a proposed plan of reorganization or settlement reflects commercially reasonable financing assumptions.

Attorneys should retain a CRE financing expert in any matter where financing feasibility, refinancing capacity, or the adequacy of a proposed payoff amount is in dispute. In secured creditor enforcement actions, plan confirmation proceedings, receivership disputes, and lender liability cases, the question of what a reasonable lender would do with this specific asset is a factual question that requires expert testimony grounded in active market knowledge, not academic theory.

A qualified CRE financing expert is not a retired banker or a former appraiser who has pivoted to consulting. The relevant qualification is active, current involvement in originating and placing commercial mortgage debt across multiple lender types, in markets similar to the subject, on asset types comparable to the subject. CLS CRE has approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, over 1,000 active lender relationships, and is active in all 50 states. That market access is what makes the expert opinion credible under Daubert scrutiny: it is based on actual conversations with actual lenders about actual deals, not on textbooks or historical experience.

Before engaging a CRE financing expert, counsel should gather the following: the current rent roll and lease abstracts; the most recent Phase I and Phase II environmental reports; any regulatory correspondence from DEQ including enforcement notices, orders, and remediation plans; the existing loan documents including the promissory note, mortgage, and any forbearance or modification agreements; the most recent appraisal if available; and any financial statements or rent payment history for the tenant. The earlier in the litigation timeline this information is assembled, the more useful the expert can be in shaping the theory of the case.

At trial or in a plan confirmation hearing, CRE financing expert testimony is typically used to establish the ceiling on refinancing proceeds available from the market, to rebut an opposing expert's overly optimistic financing assumption, or to demonstrate that a proposed plan of reorganization is not feasible because the assumed refinancing cannot be consummated on the proposed terms. In cramdown proceedings, the expert's opinion on market interest rates and loan terms directly informs the fair and equitable standard.

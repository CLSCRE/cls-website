What Is a Rule 26 Expert Report and Why Courts Need Financing Experts

Rule 26 of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure governs expert witness disclosures. When a party in federal litigation wants to use an expert, they have to produce a written report signed by that expert that contains their complete opinions and the basis for them. It has to be thorough enough that the other side can actually challenge it, depose the expert, and potentially cross-examine at trial.

The reason courts need financing experts specifically, separate from appraisers, is that an appraisal tells you what a property is worth. A financing expert tells you what a lender will actually do with it. Those are very different questions.

An appraiser might value a contaminated industrial property at $12 million. That is their job. But the follow-up question, the one that actually matters in a Plan of Reorganization or a dispute over collateral, is: can the borrower refinance the existing debt at that value? What rate? What LTV? What conditions? Which lenders will even look at it? That is where a financing expert comes in.

The Three Factors That Determine Financing Feasibility for Contaminated Properties

Factor one is LTV headroom. Loan-to-value ratio. Lenders look at whether there is enough equity cushion above the loan balance to absorb the environmental liability, even if the full remediation cost hits the value as a deduction. If the LTV is already at 75% and the remediation estimate is $3 million on a $10 million property, you have a problem. If the LTV is approximately 45% and the remediation estimate represents a fraction of the equity cushion, lenders can work with that.

Factor two is remediation quantification. Lenders do not like unknowns. If you have a Phase I environmental site assessment that says contamination may be present, that is a red flag. If you have a Phase II with a fully scoped remediation estimate from a licensed environmental engineer, a defined cost, a defined timeline, an active regulatory file, that is something a lender can underwrite around. The contamination is known. The liability is quantified. It is not an open-ended black hole.

Factor three is insurance coverage. Environmental liability insurance, also called pollution legal liability coverage, is widely available for commercial properties and can be structured to cover both cleanup costs and third-party claims. Lenders that deal with contaminated properties regularly will often require this as a loan condition, and it closes the risk loop for them.

Lender Categories: Who Actually Lends on Contaminated Properties

Not every lender will look at contaminated properties. But more do than you might think, and they fall into distinct categories.

Bridge lenders and debt funds are the most flexible. They underwrite on current income and collateral, they move quickly, and many have specific environmental programs. Rates are higher, typically in the 9 to 12% range, and terms are short, usually one to three years. But they will close when banks will not.

Community banks and credit unions are the middle tier. Many will lend on contaminated properties if they have a Phase II report with a quantified estimate and if the LTV cushion is adequate. They will often require environmental liability insurance as a condition. Rates are more competitive, but underwriting takes longer and requirements are more specific.

Hard money lenders are the backstop. Higher rates, shorter terms, but maximum flexibility on collateral condition. For a property that needs to refinance quickly or bridge to a remediated future state, hard money can be a viable path.

The Environmental Stress Test and What It Actually Means

In an expert report covering a scenario like this, I run what I call an environmental stress test. I ask: what happens to the LTV if we assume the full remediation cost hits as a dollar-for-dollar deduction from the appraised value? No other assumptions, just that one scenario.

Using the illustrative numbers above, even under that stressed scenario, the LTV on the existing loan balance is still within the 65% threshold that bridge lenders in this market apply. The financing is still feasible. The existing loan is still payoffable from refinancing proceeds.

That is critical analysis for a court proceeding or a dispute between parties. It is not enough to say contamination exists. You have to model the financial impact of that contamination on actual financing capacity. That is the job of a financing expert.

The Key Point: Lenders Are Not Taking on the Cleanup

Here is something that gets missed in almost every non-lender discussion of contaminated property financing. The lender is not taking on the environmental liability. They are not responsible for cleanup. They are underwriting two things: the income stream that services the debt, and the collateral cushion that protects them if the borrower defaults.

If the income is sufficient to cover debt service and the LTV is low enough that the collateral value exceeds the loan even after environmental discounts, the lender is protected. That is how they think about it. The contamination affects value and it affects income to a degree, but it does not make the property untouchable if the math works.

What DOES Preclude Financing

To be clear about the limits: some situations genuinely do preclude financing. Unknown contamination with no Phase II and no remediation estimate is a deal-stopper for most lenders. They cannot underwrite an open-ended liability. No LTV headroom is another stopper. If the loan is already at 70% and there is a $5 million environmental liability, the math does not work. And no income, no tenant, no lease, meaning a vacant contaminated property with no cash flow, is a very difficult financing candidate.

So the issue is not contamination per se. It is the combination of contamination with insufficient cushion, unknown scope, or no income stream. Any one of those adds risk; all three together usually means no conventional financing path.

For Attorneys: Why Environmental Expert Testimony Is Not Enough

If you are an attorney handling a case where contamination is a factor, hear this clearly: the environmental expert testimony and the financing expert testimony are separate and both necessary. The environmental expert tells the court what the contamination is, how it got there, what remediation costs, and what the regulatory timeline looks like. The financing expert tells the court what impact that contamination has on the property's access to capital.

Those are different questions. A contamination expert cannot tell you whether the financing market has adequate products for this property at this LTV. A financing expert cannot tell you what the remediation cost estimate is. You need both, and they need to be coordinated so the assumptions are consistent across both reports.

What DSCR Is and Why Lenders Care About It

DSCR stands for Debt Service Coverage Ratio. It is a measure of how much income a property generates relative to what it costs to service the debt on that property.

The formula is simple: DSCR equals Net Operating Income divided by Annual Debt Service. That is it. The numerator is what the property earns, after operating expenses but before debt payments. The denominator is what the mortgage costs per year, principal and interest combined.

A DSCR of 1.0x means the property earns exactly enough to cover the mortgage payment. That is a break-even. Lenders do not want break-even. They want a cushion. The market standard for most commercial property types is 1.25x, meaning the property needs to earn at least 25% more than the annual debt service. Some lenders require 1.30x or higher for riskier assets or markets.

The reason lenders care is obvious when you think about it: if the property's income drops 20% due to a vacancy, a lease renegotiation, or a market downturn, a 1.25x DSCR gives the lender a buffer before the borrower is unable to service the debt. It is a safety margin built into the underwriting.

What Each Variable Means and Why Those Specific Values

Let me go through why each parameter is what it is, because in expert testimony, you have to defend every assumption.

The in-place NOI is based on actual current rents from actual current leases. Not projected rents. Not stabilized rents assuming full occupancy. What is actually being collected right now. That is the most conservative and defensible NOI figure for an expert report, because it eliminates projection-based assumptions that opposing counsel can attack. In a scenario where in-place rents are $9.00 per square foot against a market of $13 to $16, using in-place NOI is deliberately conservative.

The 1.25x DSCR is market standard. I can cite lender guidelines, industry publications, and my own direct experience placing loans to support this number. It is not cherry-picked high or low to favor a conclusion. It is what lenders actually require.

The 7% rate reflects current market pricing for this product. When I am writing a report, I have current term sheets from lenders in this category. This is not a textbook rate. It is a live-market rate.

The 30-year amortization is conservative. Many bridge lenders would offer interest-only, which would actually reduce the debt service and increase the maximum supportable loan. Using full amortization is the more conservative assumption, and conservative assumptions make for more defensible expert opinions.

Why DSCR and LTV Both Matter: Whichever Is More Restrictive Wins

Here is a critical nuance that non-lenders often miss. DSCR and LTV are two independent constraints, and the maximum loan is whichever is lower.

In the type of scenario I analyze: the DSCR test might show a maximum supportable loan of $7.2 million based on income. The LTV test establishes a separate maximum based on a percentage of the appraised value. Both tests apply simultaneously. The lender will not go above either limit. So if the LTV limit is lower, that is the binding constraint, regardless of how strong the income is. If the DSCR limit is lower, the lender will not lend to the LTV maximum even though the collateral would support it.

In a well-structured analysis, both constraints point in the same direction: the existing loan balance is within both limits. That is a critical finding because it means refinancing is feasible on both measures simultaneously, not just one.

Common Attorney Mistakes When Dealing With DSCR

I see certain misconceptions come up repeatedly in legal proceedings involving CRE financing. Here are the most common ones.

Mistake one: assuming appraised value equals maximum loan. It does not. The maximum loan is the lower of the LTV-based limit and the DSCR-based limit. A property worth $15 million does not automatically support a $9.75 million loan at 65% LTV if the income only supports $6 million.

Mistake two: using gross income as the DSCR numerator. The DSCR is calculated on Net Operating Income, after operating expenses. Gross rents minus vacancy, minus taxes, minus insurance, minus management and maintenance. If an expert or a party's financial analysis is using gross income in the numerator, the DSCR is being overstated.

Mistake three: not accounting for environmental discounts on value. If a contamination-related deduction reduces the appraised value, that reduction flows through to the LTV-based loan limit. An expert report that ignores this is not credible.

What Daubert and Rule 702 Actually Require

Let me start with the legal standard, because it is relevant to why this distinction matters in court, not just in theory.

Under Federal Rule of Evidence 702 and the Daubert standard, expert testimony is admissible if the expert has sufficient knowledge, skill, experience, training, or education; if the testimony is based on sufficient facts or data; if it is the product of reliable principles and methods; and if the expert reliably applied those principles and methods to the facts of the case.

Note what Rule 702 does not say. It does not say the expert has to have an academic title. It does not say the expert has to have published. It says knowledge, skill, experience. Reliable methods applied to sufficient facts. An active commercial mortgage broker who is closing deals in the current market has knowledge, skill, and experience that is directly grounded in current market conditions. That is exactly what Rule 702 is looking for.

Conversely, an expert whose methodology relies primarily on secondary sources, academic publications, and historical data is potentially more vulnerable to a Daubert challenge, not less, if the core question is what the market looks like right now.

Current Market Knowledge vs. Textbook Knowledge: What Changed Since 2022

Let me be specific about what has changed in commercial real estate lending since 2022, because the changes are substantial and they go directly to the issues that come up in litigation.

Interest rates moved from historically low levels to a substantially higher range in a very short period. That fundamentally changed the DSCR math on almost every transaction. A loan that would have been feasible in 2021 at a 3.5% rate requires dramatically different income support at a 7% rate. If your expert is using rate assumptions from 2020 or 2021 because that is the last time they were active in the market, their analysis is materially wrong.

Lender credit boxes tightened significantly. Many lenders who were active in certain product types, construction loans, bridge loans on value-add assets, loans on properties with occupancy issues, pulled back or raised their requirements. Knowing which lenders are still active, what they require today, and what rate they are pricing at requires being in the market. It is not something you can get from a journal article.

Cap rates expanded across most asset types. That affects appraised values and LTV calculations. If you do not know where cap rates are right now in a specific market for a specific asset type, you cannot accurately calculate the LTV on a property. And that has changed from what it was three years ago.

The Mechanics: What 11 U.S.C. Section 364 Means

The key statutory provision for new money in bankruptcy is 11 U.S.C. Section 364. This section governs the debtor's ability to obtain new credit during the bankruptcy case. It requires Court approval for most forms of new financing, which means any lender making a new loan to a debtor in possession is doing so with court oversight.

Here is the thing that surprises most non-bankruptcy practitioners: this creates structural certainty, not uncertainty. The Court's involvement means the lender gets a court order confirming their lien position. There is no question about whether a subsequent transfer or event will affect their security. The court has jurisdiction and has explicitly approved the financing. That is actually an advantage from a lender's perspective on the title and lien priority questions.

What this means in practice: certain lenders, primarily bridge lenders and specialty finance companies with bankruptcy lending experience, view court-supervised lending as a feature, not a bug. The court is the backstop. The process is defined. The timeline is set by the Plan. Uncertainty, which is the thing lenders hate most, is actually reduced relative to a workout outside of court.

Three Categories of Bankruptcy-Adjacent Lenders

When it comes to financing a property in or emerging from Chapter 11, there are three distinct categories of lenders, each with a different position in the process.

The first category is DIP lenders, or Debtor-in-Possession lenders. These are lenders who provide financing during the bankruptcy case itself, before confirmation of the Plan. DIP financing is typically structured to cover operating costs, professional fees, or property preservation during the reorganization. DIP loans are expensive, super-priority in right of payment, and require court approval. But they exist and they serve a real purpose for properties that need capital to survive the bankruptcy process.

The second category is exit financing lenders. These are lenders who commit to providing a new permanent or bridge loan that funds on the effective date of the confirmed Plan, the moment the debtor exits bankruptcy. Exit financing is what pays off the existing secured debt under the Plan. It requires commitment before confirmation, because the Plan typically specifies the source of the payoff. Exit financing lenders are essentially betting on the confirmed Plan and the post-bankruptcy property performance.

The third category, and the one most relevant to the cases I work on, is bridge lenders who close after confirmation but before the formal Plan effective date, or immediately after it. These are lenders who look at the confirmed Plan, assess the property and its income, and provide a loan on the strength of the Plan's structure and the property fundamentals. They are not DIP lenders. They are conventional bridge lenders who are comfortable with the bankruptcy timeline and the court-ordered structure.

The Structural Advantages of Bankruptcy Financing

Let me be specific about why bankruptcy financing is sometimes easier to structure than a troubled loan workout outside of court.

Court-ordered lien priority confirmation. When the court approves the financing under Section 364, the lender's lien priority is established and documented in a court order. There is no ambiguity about competing claims or post-closing transfers that could impair the lien. That is a significant underwriting advantage.

Clean title delivered post-confirmation. The Plan confirmation process typically includes provisions for the debtor to transfer or retain property with a court order that addresses competing claims. A lender making a post-confirmation loan is lending against a property whose title history through the bankruptcy has been court-addressed.

Defined timeline via the Plan. The Plan of Reorganization specifies what happens and when. Lenders know the timeline. They know when the existing debt is supposed to be paid off. They know what conditions the borrower must satisfy. That defined structure is actually helpful for underwriting relative to an open-ended workout.

The Challenges: What Makes Bankruptcy Financing Harder

I do not want to make this sound easy, because it is not always easy. There are real challenges.

Borrower cooperation is one of the biggest. In a contentious bankruptcy, the debtor may be adverse to the financing process. If the borrower is not cooperating with lender due diligence, not providing documents, not engaging with the process, lenders will walk away. This is a real impediment that comes up in the type of scenario I analyze.

Court timeline adds complexity. Bankruptcy proceedings are not always fast. If the Plan confirmation is delayed, the lender's commitment may expire. Managing the timing of a financing commitment alongside the court calendar requires experience on both sides.

Stacked complications are common. Many bankruptcy-adjacent properties have multiple issues simultaneously: environmental conditions, deferred maintenance, below-market leases, or management disputes. Each additional complication narrows the lender field. In a scenario like the one I outlined in Scripts 1 and 2, you have environmental contamination stacked on top of the bankruptcy, stacked on top of an adversarial borrower. You need to work through each layer systematically.

How This Is Underwritten: Same Methodology, Plus the Plan

The core underwriting for a bankruptcy-adjacent property is the same DSCR and LTV analysis I described in other videos. You need the income to support the debt service at current market rates, and the collateral to support the loan at an appropriate LTV. Those fundamentals do not change because the property is in bankruptcy.

What the lender adds is underwriting of the Plan process itself. They want to understand: what is the timeline to confirmation? What conditions remain unsatisfied? What is the borrower's track record with the court? Is there litigation risk that could delay or derail the Plan?

For a lender who specializes in this space, those are not unfamiliar questions. They have underwritten Plans before. They know what to look for. For a conventional lender without bankruptcy lending experience, these questions are often disqualifying. That is why the lender market for bankruptcy-adjacent properties is narrower, but it exists.

What Attorneys Need to Prove at a Plan Hearing

This is the part that matters most if you are an attorney handling a Plan of Reorganization that involves real property.

To confirm a Plan that proposes to refinance existing secured debt, you need to be able to demonstrate that the financing is actually available in the market at the terms proposed. Not theoretically available. Actually available, to this borrower, for this property, at these terms, in this market, right now.

That means: is there a lender who will make this loan? At what rate? At what LTV? Subject to what conditions? And importantly, is the existing secured debt payoffable from the refinancing proceeds?

A financing expert provides quantitative, replicable, current-market evidence on each of those questions. Not opinion in the sense of "I think a lender might consider this." Evidence in the sense of: here is the DSCR calculation using in-place NOI and current market parameters, here is the maximum supportable loan, here is the LTV at the appraised value, here is a survey of the lender categories that are active for this asset type and market, here is the rate range they are pricing at, and here is how the refinancing proceeds compare to the existing debt balance.

That is the structure of a credible financing feasibility opinion. It is built from the bottom up, not from the top down. And it is what courts can actually evaluate and rely on.

When I testify as an expert witness in a Plan hearing, my role is to provide that quantitative analysis on the financing question. I am not there to say whether the Plan should be confirmed. I am not there to opine on legal issues. I am there to answer the financing question: is the financing described in the Plan actually feasible in the current market?

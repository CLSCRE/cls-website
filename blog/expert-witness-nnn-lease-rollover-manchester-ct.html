Expert Witness: NNN Lease Rollover Upside and Financing Impact in Manchester, CT

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Below-Market NNN Lease and Rollover Upside Analysis

The Manchester Market Context

Manchester, Connecticut sits at the eastern edge of the Hartford metro, a mid-size New England market with a commercial real estate profile that reflects decades of industrial legacy, a modest but resilient retail base, and a healthcare and service-sector employment foundation that shapes tenant demand across all asset classes. For single-tenant NNN assets specifically, Manchester presents a market where the gap between in-place rents on legacy leases and current market rents can be substantial, because many NNN properties in the submarket were leased during development cycles that preceded significant shifts in retail traffic patterns, healthcare facility consolidation, and industrial tenant requirements.

Industrial vacancy in the Greater Hartford market, of which Manchester is a core submarket, has trended below 6% in recent reporting periods, with Manchester benefiting from its proximity to Interstate 84 and the Route 6 corridor. Warehouse and flex product in the 20,000 to 80,000 square foot range commands net rents in the $7.00 to $10.00 per square foot range depending on clear height, dock configuration, and power. For freestanding retail NNN product, market rents vary dramatically by tenancy: nationally-branded quick service restaurant pads trade at cap rates in the 5.5% to 6.5% range when fully occupied by credit tenants on long-term leases, while single-tenant general retail, urgent care, or service commercial properties in Manchester's secondary corridors trade closer to 7.0% to 8.5% going-in, with meaningful variation based on lease term remaining and tenant credit.

What this market context means for a below-market NNN lease dispute is that the spread between an in-place rent and market rent in Manchester is not hypothetical. It is a real and quantifiable number with direct implications for lender underwriting, supportable loan size, and refinancing feasibility. A tenant locked into a lease executed in 2008 or 2012 may be paying rents that are 15% to 35% below what the same space would command today, and that delta is precisely what drives the financing dispute that counsel retained us to analyze.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing the property owner in a dispute involving a freestanding single-tenant NNN commercial property in Manchester. The asset was a mid-size freestanding commercial building occupied by a single tenant under a long-term NNN lease with approximately four years remaining on the primary term, with renewal options structured at fixed rental rates that themselves reflected below-market economics at execution.

The core legal questions centered on financing feasibility: could the property, as encumbered by the existing lease, support refinancing at the loan-to-value and debt service coverage ratios that the owner had projected in business plans and financial models that were now at issue in the litigation? The opposing party contended that the property was refinanceable at levels consistent with its appraised value. Counsel retained CLS CRE to provide an independent analysis of how commercial mortgage lenders actually underwrite single-tenant NNN assets with below-market in-place rents, what the supportable loan amount was under current market lending standards, and whether rollover upside, meaning the prospective increase in income at lease expiration, constitutes underwritable value in the context of loan sizing.

These are not questions an MAI appraiser is equipped to answer definitively. An appraisal establishes market value. An expert analysis of financing feasibility establishes what a lender will actually lend, which is a function of underwriting standards, capital market conditions, lender credit policy, and debt yield requirements that operate independently of appraised value. The distinction between those two outputs was central to this engagement.

The Financial Analysis

The analysis began with the income stream actually available to a lender at the time of underwriting: the in-place rent contractually obligated under the existing NNN lease. For this property, in-place base rent was $11.50 per square foot net on a building of approximately 12,400 square feet, generating gross base rent of $142,600 annually. Under a true NNN structure, tenant reimbursements covered real estate taxes, insurance, and maintenance, so effective NOI, after deducting a modest management fee of 2% and a nominal vacancy reserve consistent with lender requirements for single-tenant assets, was approximately $136,900.

The critical methodological point here is that lenders underwriting single-tenant NNN assets do not, as a rule, underwrite to projected future rents, rollover upside, or market rent estimates. They underwrite to the contractual income stream in place at loan origination, because that is the income the asset is actually generating and the only income stream with legal certainty. Market rent analysis is relevant to the lender's assessment of re-tenanting risk at expiration, but it does not add dollars to the underwritten NOI unless the loan is structured with a holdback or earn-out mechanism, which most conventional lenders will not offer on a stabilized NNN asset.

Applying standard commercial mortgage underwriting parameters for this asset class and market, the analysis used the following inputs. A DSCR requirement of 1.25x is the floor for conventional bank and CMBS execution on single-tenant retail or commercial NNN product. Some lenders apply 1.30x when the in-place lease term is short relative to the proposed loan term, as was the case here. Using 1.25x as the market standard, the maximum annual debt service supportable by $136,900 of underwritten NOI was $109,520 ($136,900 divided by 1.25). At a market interest rate of 7.25% on a 25-year amortization schedule, a loan with annual debt service of $109,520 supports a loan balance of approximately $1,335,000. Using a 30-year amortization schedule at the same rate, the supportable loan increases to approximately $1,465,000.

The appraised value of the property was placed at approximately $1,950,000 in the appraisal that had been produced in the litigation. At the $1,335,000 debt service-constrained loan ceiling, the loan-to-value ratio is 68.5%. At $1,465,000 it is 75.1%. These figures sit at or slightly above the LTV limits most conventional lenders apply to single-tenant NNN product with short lease terms: 65% to 70% LTV for properties with less than five years of primary term remaining is a standard institutional constraint, and some CMBS conduit lenders apply 60% LTV when remaining term is under three years at maturity of the proposed loan. The analysis demonstrated that even before reaching the LTV ceiling, the DSCR constraint was the binding limitation, meaning the property could not support a loan sized to its appraised value regardless of how generous the LTV policy was. The maximum supportable loan was constrained by income, not by value.

The rollover upside argument, which contended that the property's value at lease expiration would reflect market rents of approximately $15.75 per square foot and therefore justify higher loan sizing today, was analyzed and rejected under lender underwriting standards. No conventional lender, CMBS conduit, or life company lender will size a loan today based on projected post-rollover NOI without a funded reserve, a lease-up hold-back, or a credit enhancement structure that the borrower would need to provide at origination. The upside is real in a valuation sense; it is not lendable income today.

The Complicating Factors

Short Remaining Lease Term Relative to Loan Maturity. This is the single most consequential structural problem in a below-market NNN lease financing. Most commercial mortgage lenders require that the lease expiration date extend at least 12 months, and preferably 24 to 36 months, beyond the proposed loan maturity. Where a 10-year loan is sought on a property with four years of primary term remaining, the lender faces the scenario in which the property may go dark, require re-tenanting costs, or experience rent disruption before the loan is retired. Lenders price this risk through tighter LTV requirements, shorter loan terms, and sometimes outright declinations. In this engagement, the short remaining term compressed both the available loan term and the lender universe materially.

Fixed-Rate Renewal Options at Below-Market Rents. When renewal options are structured at fixed rates that themselves reflect below-market economics, the lender recognizes that exercise of the option, while providing lease continuity, does not provide income recovery. This is a compounding problem: the property does not go dark at expiration, but income does not recover to market either. Lenders will underwrite to the renewal option rent if the tenant has exercised or is likely to exercise, which produces an income stream that may be only marginally better than the expiring term. Option structures of this type reduce the probability that rollover produces a meaningful re-pricing event.

Tenant Credit and Re-Tenanting Risk. Single-tenant NNN assets are binary in a way that multi-tenant properties are not. The entire income stream depends on one tenant. Lenders evaluating a below-market lease ask not only what the rent is, but whether the tenant is likely to remain, and if not, how liquid the re-tenanting market is for that specific building configuration, size, and location in Manchester. Freestanding commercial buildings with specialized configurations, drive-through infrastructure, or specific power requirements have a narrower pool of replacement tenants, which lenders incorporate into their credit decision through tighter leverage constraints.

CMBS Execution and Loan Minimum Size. CMBS conduit lenders, which can offer non-recourse execution and longer amortization periods that would otherwise benefit a borrower in this position, typically require minimum loan sizes of $2,000,000 to $3,000,000. At the supportable loan levels calculated in this analysis, CMBS is not an available execution path, which limits the borrower to portfolio lenders and local or regional banks, each of which applies recourse requirements and shorter amortization periods that further constrain proceeds.

The Manchester Lender Market

The lender market for single-tenant NNN commercial assets in Manchester reflects the broader Connecticut commercial mortgage landscape, which is characterized by strong community bank depth, a handful of active regional lenders, and limited institutional or agency appetite for assets below the size thresholds that attract national capital. Understanding which lender categories are genuinely active and what their credit parameters look like is essential to a financing feasibility opinion.

Connecticut community and mutual savings banks represent the most active lending category for NNN product in the $1,000,000 to $3,000,000 loan range in the Manchester submarket. Institutions headquartered in Hartford County and Tolland County maintain active commercial real estate lending programs and will consider single-tenant product with shorter lease terms, but they apply full recourse requirements, typically limit amortization to 20 or 25 years, and require DSCR of 1.20x to 1.30x on underwritten in-place cash flow. They will not credit rollover upside in their loan sizing. Their rate pricing in the current environment runs 25 to 75 basis points above corresponding treasury benchmarks or FHLB advance rates, which in mid-2024 conditions places them in the 7.00% to 7.75% range for 5-year or 7-year fixed-rate terms.

Regional banks with Connecticut footprints will look at this product but apply stricter minimum debt yield requirements, typically 8.5% to 9.5% on NNN assets with lease term concerns. Debt yield is calculated as underwritten NOI divided by the proposed loan amount. On our $136,900 NOI, an 8.5% debt yield floor produces a maximum loan of $1,611,000, which is actually slightly above the DSCR-constrained ceiling calculated at 1.25x, meaning the debt yield constraint and the DSCR constraint produce similar results and confirm the analysis from a second direction.

Debt funds and bridge lenders represent a theoretical option where a borrower anticipates near-term lease rollover and re-pricing, but the economics are punishing. Bridge lenders on transitional or short-lease NNN product in secondary markets like Manchester will price at 300 to 500 basis points over SOFR, apply origination fees of 1.0% to 2.0%, and structure the loan with a short initial term of 12 to 24 months with extension options tied to leasing benchmarks. This execution is not a refinancing; it is a capital markets triage instrument. It can be relevant to a litigation analysis as evidence of what a borrower would actually have had to accept in lieu of conventional refinancing.

Life company lenders and CMBS conduits, as noted, are effectively unavailable at Manchester NNN loan sizes and complexity levels, unless the asset is part of a portfolio financing or the loan size meets minimum thresholds through an expansion or additional collateral structure.

What Attorneys Handling Manchester CRE Disputes Need to Know

The financing expert witness function in a commercial real estate dispute is distinct from both the appraisal function and the accounting function, and attorneys who conflate them create gaps in their expert strategy that opposing counsel will exploit.

When to retain a CRE financing expert. If the dispute involves any of the following, a financing expert is warranted: alleged inability to refinance; damages tied to lost financing proceeds; breach of representations regarding financing feasibility; disputes over whether a transaction was financeable at projected terms; plan confirmation fights where feasibility of a refinancing exit is at issue; and disputes over lender conduct or underwriting practices. In the Manchester NNN context specifically, if the value of the property or the damages calculation depends on what a lender would have done or could do today with a below-market leased asset, that question requires a financing expert, not just an appraiser.

What a financing expert can address that an appraiser cannot. An appraisal establishes market value under a defined set of assumptions. It does not establish what a specific loan would have been approved for, what underwriting standards apply to a given asset class in a given market, what lender universe exists for a specific deal profile, how lenders treat lease rollover risk in their credit analysis, what DSCR floors apply, or whether a borrower's financial projections were consistent with market lending standards. These questions sit squarely within the financing expert's scope and outside the appraiser's lane.

What qualifies a CRE financing expert. Relevant qualifications include active commercial mortgage brokerage practice across multiple lender categories, documented transaction volume that demonstrates current market engagement rather than historical familiarity, relationships with lenders at the credit committee level, and a track record of structuring complex transactions in comparable markets. A retired banker who last originated loans in 2019 lacks current lender relationship insight. A broker who has closed comparable transactions in the past 12 to 24 months can speak to what lenders are actually doing today, which is what the analysis requires.

Information counsel should gather before the expert engagement. Counsel should obtain the full lease document including all amendments and option schedules, current rent rolls, the existing loan documents if any, any appraisals produced in the matter, financial statements for the property, correspondence between the borrower and any lenders regarding financing, and any term sheets or commitment letters that were issued or declined. CLS CRE can work with partial information, but a complete document set produces a more defensible and specific opinion.

How financing expert testimony is used. In commercial litigation, a financing expert's Rule 26 report establishes the factual foundation for financing feasibility opinions that can be introduced at trial, used in deposition to constrain opposing witnesses, or used in mediation to anchor settlement positions. In bankruptcy plan confirmation hearings, financing feasibility opinions are often dispositive on the question of whether a proposed plan is achievable. The expert may testify live, provide a written report, or both, depending on the forum and procedural posture of the matter.

Key Takeaways

  • Lenders underwrite to contractual in-place rent, not market rent or rollover projections. In a below-market NNN lease, the gap between in-place NOI and market NOI directly reduces the supportable loan amount, regardless of appraised value. This is the central financing reality in disputes of this type.
  • DSCR and debt yield constraints often bind before LTV constraints in below-market lease scenarios. A property may have sufficient appraised value to support a higher LTV loan on paper, but if the income cannot service the debt at the required coverage ratio, the higher loan is not available. Both constraints must be analyzed independently and together.
  • Rollover upside is a real economic concept but is not lendable income. Lenders will not size a loan today against projected post-lease-expiration rents. The upside affects exit value and long-term IRR analysis, but it does not increase the loan available at origination on a stabilized NNN asset.

Expert Witness: Commercial Loan Modification Standards in Modesto, CA

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Loan Modification and Workout Dispute

The Modesto Market Context

Modesto sits at the economic heart of Stanislaus County, and its commercial real estate market reflects a city that has been systematically underestimated by institutional capital for decades. That dynamic matters enormously in loan modification and workout disputes, because lender conduct and the reasonableness of workout terms cannot be evaluated in a vacuum. They must be evaluated against what the Modesto market actually looks like, what comparable lenders were doing in this specific submarket, and what a commercially sophisticated lender with full market knowledge should have known at the time the modification was negotiated.

Modesto's industrial sector has emerged as one of the stronger performing segments in the Central Valley. Vacancy rates in the Modesto industrial submarket have ranged between 4% and 7% over the past several years, supported by food processing, agricultural distribution, logistics, and cold storage users who need to be geographically proximate to the San Joaquin Valley's agricultural production base. Net asking rents for industrial product in the Modesto area have ranged from approximately $0.60 to $0.90 per square foot per month for functional distribution and warehouse space, with newer or temperature-controlled facilities commanding premiums above that range. These fundamentals matter in a workout dispute because they directly inform whether a lender's assessment of stabilized cash flow was reasonable or whether the lender applied assumptions that were inconsistent with demonstrable market conditions.

The multifamily market in Modesto has been characterized by rising rents driven by housing affordability pressure, with average effective rents for Class B garden-style apartments generally in the range of $1,400 to $1,800 per month depending on unit mix and submarket positioning. Cap rates for stabilized multifamily assets in Modesto have historically traded in the 5.25% to 6.50% range, compressing during low-rate environments and softening as rates increased. Office fundamentals are considerably weaker, with downtown Modesto office vacancy remaining elevated and suburban office facing ongoing pressure from remote and hybrid work patterns. Any workout analysis involving Modesto office assets must grapple honestly with structural demand headwinds that a lender relying solely on pre-pandemic underwriting norms would have failed to account for.

Understanding these specifics is not background noise. When I am retained as an expert witness in a Modesto CRE workout dispute, the first question I answer is whether the lender's conduct was measured against an accurate picture of this market or against a generic assumption set that bore no relationship to what Stanislaus County commercial real estate was actually doing at the relevant time.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing the borrower in a dispute arising from a contested loan modification and forbearance process involving a stabilized commercial income property in the Modesto metropolitan area. The asset was a multi-tenant retail and light industrial mixed-use property, owner-operated in part and partially leased to third-party tenants under short and medium-term leases. The loan had originally been originated by a regional lender and had gone through one prior modification before the events at issue in the litigation.

Retaining counsel needed answers to a specific set of financial and industry standard questions that an appraiser is not positioned to answer. The core issues were: Did the lender follow commercially accepted workout practices during the forbearance negotiation period? Were the modification terms the lender ultimately proposed or imposed consistent with what a commercially reasonable lender, acting in good faith in this market, would have offered given the property's actual cash flow and the prevailing lending environment? Did the lender misapply DSCR calculations or impose LTV haircuts that were inconsistent with market norms? And did the lender's handling of the borrower's financial disclosures during the workout process meet the standard of care applicable to sophisticated commercial mortgage lenders?

CLS CRE was engaged to produce a Rule 26-compliant expert report addressing those questions, supported by a full independent underwriting analysis of the subject property's supportable debt capacity and a review of comparable workout structures executed in the Central Valley market during the same time period.

The Financial Analysis

The foundation of any workout dispute analysis is an independent reconstruction of the property's actual cash flow at the time the modification was negotiated, compared against what the lender's internal underwriting reflected. These two numbers are frequently different, and the gap between them is often where the dispute lives.

For the subject engagement, I analyzed the property's trailing twelve-month income and expense statements, rent rolls, lease abstracts, and operating history going back three years prior to the modification request. Gross potential rental income across the tenancy was approximately $480,000 annually. After applying a market-supported vacancy and credit loss allowance of 8%, consistent with Modesto mixed-use market conditions at the relevant time, effective gross income was approximately $441,600. Stabilized operating expenses, inclusive of property taxes, insurance, management, maintenance, and reserves for replacement, totaled approximately $148,000, producing a stabilized net operating income of approximately $293,600.

The industry standard DSCR threshold for commercial mortgage loans on assets of this type, in this market, during the relevant period, was 1.20x to 1.25x. This range is not arbitrary. It reflects the minimum coverage ratio that a prudent lender requires to have meaningful protection against income volatility, and it is consistently applied across the conventional commercial bank and CMBS lending markets. I applied a 1.25x DSCR threshold as the more conservative and lender-appropriate benchmark for stress-testing purposes.

Using a blended interest rate of 6.75%, which was consistent with prevailing commercial mortgage rates for this loan type and credit profile during the relevant modification period, and a 25-year amortization schedule (the standard for mixed-use commercial properties at this loan size), the maximum supportable debt service at 1.25x DSCR was derived as follows: $293,600 divided by 1.25 equals $234,880 in allowable annual debt service. At a 6.75% rate on a 25-year amortization, the mortgage constant is approximately 0.0869. Dividing $234,880 by 0.0869 produces a maximum supportable loan of approximately $2,702,875, which I rounded conservatively to $2,700,000 for analytical purposes.

The lender's modification proposal was structured around a loan balance that, after capitalized default interest and fees, had grown to approximately $3,200,000. At the same 6.75% rate and 25-year amortization, debt service on that balance would have been approximately $278,080 annually, producing a DSCR of only 1.056x against stabilized NOI. That is materially below the 1.25x standard the lender's own credit policy required, and it is below the 1.20x floor that any reasonable commercial lender would accept as a viable modification structure. The modification, as proposed, was not financially supportable on the cash flow the property could actually generate. This finding was central to the expert analysis.

On the LTV side, a contemporaneous appraisal placed the property's as-stabilized value at approximately $4,100,000 at the time of the modification negotiation. The $3,200,000 loan balance represented a loan-to-value ratio of approximately 78%, well above the 65% to 70% LTV ceiling that conventional commercial lenders apply to mixed-use assets in secondary markets. A commercially reasonable lender, applying market-standard LTV constraints, would have recognized that the loan was not only over-leveraged relative to cash flow but also meaningfully over-leveraged relative to collateral value. The appropriate workout response was either a principal write-down to bring LTV into compliance, an equity infusion requirement, or a phased modification structure tied to demonstrated leasing milestones. The lender pursued none of these alternatives and instead proposed terms that compounded the borrower's inability to service the debt.

The Complicating Factors

Capitalized Default Interest and Fee Accumulation. One of the most consequential complicating factors in Modesto workout disputes is the accumulation of default interest, late charges, and legal fees during the forbearance period. When a lender allows a property to remain in forbearance for an extended period without advancing the workout toward resolution, the accruing default interest at penalty rates can push the loan balance to a level where no commercially viable modification structure is mathematically achievable. In this engagement, default interest at 5% above the note rate had accrued for approximately 18 months during what the lender characterized as a "good faith workout process." That accrual added over $200,000 to the balance, which was precisely the gap that rendered the modification unworkable on the property's actual cash flow. Whether or not the lender was legally entitled to accrue that interest is a question for the court. Whether the resulting loan balance was consistent with a commercially reasonable workout structure is a question I can answer, and the answer here was no.

Short Remaining Lease Terms and Rollover Risk. Mixed-use properties in Modesto's secondary submarket often carry tenants on shorter lease terms, which creates rollover risk that lenders must evaluate differently than stabilized long-term-tenanted assets. In this case, two of the three income-producing tenancies had 12 to 18 months remaining at the time of the modification request. A commercially reasonable lender would have analyzed the renewal probability for each tenancy, obtained estoppels, and structured any modification with covenants tied to lease renewals rather than treating the as-is rent roll as indefinitely stable. The lender's failure to conduct that analysis, and its reliance on a rent roll that was about to turn over, reflected an underwriting deficiency that also supported the conclusion that the lender's modification terms were not commercially grounded.

Interest Rate Environment and Refinance Feasibility. Workout viability is always tested against the question of whether the borrower can exit the modification through a refinance within the modification term. In the elevated rate environment of the relevant period, refinancing a Modesto mixed-use asset at a loan balance of $3,200,000 was not achievable on market terms. My analysis identified that a market-rate takeout loan was supportable only at approximately $2,700,000, meaning any modification that did not address principal reduction was structuring the borrower into a dead end with no viable exit. Commercially reasonable lenders in workout situations are expected to conduct that exit analysis. Here, the record reflected no evidence that the lender had done so.

The Modesto Lender Market

Modesto is served by a reasonably deep community bank and regional bank market, which is the primary lending universe for commercial properties in the $1,000,000 to $5,000,000 range. Lenders with meaningful Stanislaus County commercial mortgage portfolios include California-chartered community banks, Central Valley-focused agricultural and commercial lenders, and the Modesto and Fresno branches of several regional Western bank platforms. These lenders generally apply DSCR floors of 1.20x to 1.25x, LTV limits of 65% to 75% depending on asset type, and amortization schedules of 20 to 25 years. Their credit appetite for mixed-use assets reflects familiarity with the local market but also conservatism born from Central Valley market cycles.

SBA 504 and 7(a) lending plays a meaningful role in Modesto commercial real estate financing, particularly for owner-user properties. Several CDC partners and SBA-preferred lenders are active in the Central Valley, and their participation can expand leverage for qualifying owner-occupied transactions. Life company and CMBS capital is present but generally reserved for stabilized assets above $5,000,000 to $7,500,000 with institutional-quality tenancy and longer weighted average lease terms than most Modesto mixed-use deals can demonstrate.

Credit unions operating in the Central Valley have also become meaningful participants in the Modesto commercial mortgage market, particularly for smaller transactions and borrowers with established deposit relationships. Their pricing is often competitive with community bank rates, but their workout policies and credit oversight practices are materially different from regulated bank standards, which matters when evaluating whether a credit union lender's workout conduct met the same standard of care applicable to a regulated commercial bank.

What Attorneys Handling Modesto CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to retain a CRE financing expert. If the dispute involves any question about whether a loan modification was commercially reasonable, whether a lender's workout conduct met industry standards, whether the proposed terms could have been refinanced, or whether the lender properly evaluated the borrower's financial disclosures, you need a CRE financing expert. An appraiser establishes value. A CRE financing expert establishes what a knowledgeable lender should have done with that value, what debt the property could support, and whether the lender's actual conduct conformed to market practice.

Questions the expert can answer that others cannot. A CRE financing expert can testify on what the active lending market looked like in Modesto at the time of the modification, what DSCR and LTV standards applied, whether the lender's modification terms were achievable given prevailing capital market conditions, whether a refinance exit was feasible, what the standard of care requires during a commercial workout process, and whether the lender's conduct was consistent with how comparable lenders handle workout situations for comparable assets. None of those questions are within the scope of an appraisal or an accounting analysis.

What qualifies an expert vs. what does not. A retired banker who underwrote one category of loans in one institution is not a CRE financing expert in the sense required for broad market standard testimony. The qualified expert has active, current experience across multiple lender categories and asset types, a demonstrable track record of structuring and closing commercial transactions in the relevant market, and the ability to articulate what the market, not one institution, required. At CLS CRE, our approximately $1,000,000,000 in transaction volume and relationships with more than 1,000 institutional and private lenders active in 50 states give me the platform to testify on market-wide standards, not just one lender's internal practices.

What to gather before the engagement. Retaining counsel should compile: all loan documents including the original note, deed of trust, and all modifications; all internal lender communications and credit memoranda related to the workout; the borrower's financial submissions to the lender during the workout period; the property's rent rolls, operating statements, and any appraisals obtained during the workout; and any written workout or forbearance proposals exchanged between the parties. The earlier this package is assembled, the faster the expert can identify the dispositive issues and scope the necessary analysis.

Key Takeaways

  • Stabilized NOI is the anchor of every workout analysis. A lender's modification terms that require debt service coverage below 1.20x are presumptively inconsistent with market standards and must be justified by extraordinary collateral or credit support. In this engagement, the proposed terms produced a 1.056x DSCR, which fell well below any commercially defensible threshold.
  • Capitalized default interest can render a workout mathematically impossible. Extended forbearance periods during which penalty interest accrues without advancement toward resolution are not commercially neutral. When that accrual pushes the balance beyond what supportable cash flow can service, the lender's conduct becomes a direct cause of the modification's failure.
  • LTV compliance is as important as DSCR compliance. Mixed-use and secondary market assets in Modesto carry LTV ceilings of 65% to 75% from conventional lenders. A modification that restores a loan to performing status on paper but leaves the borrower at 78% LTV has not created a viable exit and should be evaluated accordingly.
  • A commercially reasonable lender conducts refinance exit analysis. Workout terms that cannot result in a market-rate takeout loan within the modification period are not commercially reasonable, regardless of whether they technically satisfy contractual formulas. Lenders are expected to know what the takeout market looks like and to structure modifications against that reality.
  • Modesto market specifics are not interchangeable with general Central Valley assumptions. Vacancy rates, rent levels, cap rates, and lender appetite in Modesto are specific and measurable. Expert testimony that applies generic assumptions without Modesto-specific calibration will not withstand cross-examination from a properly prepared expert on the other side.
  • The lender's own credit policy is the first standard against which its conduct is measured. If the lender's internal workout policy required minimum DSCR compliance, contemporaneous LTV verification, and borrower financial disclosure review, deviations from that policy are directly relevant to whether the standard of care was met.
  • CRE financing expert testimony is distinct from appraisal testimony and should be retained separately and early. The expert witness issues in a Modesto workout dispute require market knowledge, underwriting expertise, and active lender relationship context that appraisers are not trained to provide and should not be asked to provide.

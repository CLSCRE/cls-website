Expert Witness: How Construction Defects Affect CRE Financing in Rock Hill, SC

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Construction Defect Impact on Financing

The Rock Hill Market Context

Rock Hill, South Carolina sits at the northern edge of York County, directly south of the Charlotte metropolitan boundary along the I-77 corridor. That positioning is not incidental to commercial real estate finance. The market has absorbed significant industrial and logistics demand spilling out of Charlotte's constrained land supply, and institutional capital has followed. For the past several years, Rock Hill and the broader York County submarket have drawn attention from regional and national lenders who treat it as an extension of the Charlotte industrial and multifamily investment thesis, with the benefit of lower land basis, favorable South Carolina property tax structures under the fee-in-lieu program, and a municipal government that has actively pursued economic development infrastructure including the now-suspended Rock Hill Amphitheater project, which itself became a case study in construction risk and public-private financing complexity.

Industrial vacancy in the Rock Hill-to-Fort Mill corridor has remained compressed, generally tracking below 5% for bulk distribution product, with asking rents on new Class A industrial space moving from the low-$6.00 range per square foot NNN toward the $7.50 to $8.50 range over recent years as Charlotte-area occupiers seek cross-dock and last-mile facilities with direct I-77 access. Multifamily fundamentals in Rock Hill have followed a similar trajectory, with new construction lease-up periods extending modestly as additional units delivered, but effective rents on market-rate product in the Winthrop University corridor and the downtown revitalization zone holding relatively firm in the $1,350 to $1,650 per unit range for one-bedroom and two-bedroom configurations. Retail along Celanese Road and the Dave Lyle Boulevard nodes remains neighborhood-anchored and grocery-driven, which is the most defensible retail format in the current lending environment. Office, by contrast, remains functionally challenged as it is in every secondary market, and lenders have broadly retracted from speculative office exposure in Rock Hill.

For purposes of construction defect litigation, this market context matters because lender underwriting benchmarks, appraised value ranges, and financing feasibility determinations are all market-calibrated. A defect that reduces net operating income by a specific dollar amount produces a different financing impact on a $4 million asset in Rock Hill than it would on a comparable asset in a primary market where cap rate compression provides more cushion. In secondary markets like Rock Hill, lenders price to risk with less tolerance for ambiguity, and the margin between a fundable loan and an unfundable one is often narrow.

The Engagement

Counsel representing a party in a commercial construction defect dispute retained Commercial Lending Solutions to provide an expert analysis and Rule 26 report addressing the financing implications of alleged structural and mechanical system defects in a newly completed mixed-use commercial property in Rock Hill. The asset consisted of ground-floor retail or commercial space with upper-floor income-producing units, a format common in Rock Hill's downtown core redevelopment zone. The property had reached substantial completion and was in early lease-up when the alleged defects were identified, triggering litigation between the property owner and the general contractor.

The core questions presented to CLS CRE were as follows: first, whether the alleged defects, if proven as described in the engineering and inspection reports provided to us, rendered the property unfinanceable in the conventional or commercial lending market at the time of the alleged harm; second, how a lender would quantify the income impact of the defects in its underwriting model and what that impact meant for the maximum supportable loan amount; third, how the property's loan-to-value headroom and debt service coverage ratio compared between a defect-free scenario and the defect-impacted scenario; and fourth, what remediation steps, cost estimates, or conditions would be necessary for the property to re-enter the financeable universe and at what cost to the borrower's equity position.

These are financing feasibility questions, not appraisal questions. They require an expert who understands how lenders actually build their credit models, what their internal credit policy constraints are, how they treat properties with unresolved litigation or known physical deficiencies, and what the realistic financing market looks like for a specific asset type in a specific submarket at a specific point in time. Our firm brings approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, relationships with more than 1,000 lenders, and active placement experience across all 50 states to that analysis.

The Financial Analysis

The methodology applied in this engagement followed the same framework any institutional lender would apply at loan origination, with the critical difference that we ran parallel analyses: one modeling the property as if the alleged defects did not exist (the "stabilized defect-free scenario"), and one modeling the property as impacted by the defects as described in the technical inspection reports (the "defect-impacted scenario"). The gap between those two scenarios represents the financing harm caused by the alleged defects.

Income Analysis: The property's potential gross income was derived from the executed and pending leases at the time of the alleged harm, supplemented by market comparables in the Rock Hill downtown submarket. For the ground-floor commercial component, in-place rents were compared against the Celanese Road and Main Street retail corridors. For the upper-floor units, rent comparables were drawn from similar mixed-use and multifamily product within a one-mile radius. A market-standard vacancy and credit loss factor of 7% to 10% was applied depending on the component, consistent with what regional lenders underwrite for this asset type in this market. Operating expenses were analyzed against actual figures and benchmarked against stabilized comparable properties.

In the defect-free scenario, the stabilized net operating income for a property of this size and configuration in Rock Hill supported an NOI in the range of approximately $220,000 to $260,000 annually, a range we use here for illustration that is consistent with the scale of mixed-use assets typical in Rock Hill's downtown redevelopment corridor. In the defect-impacted scenario, the alleged defects produced two categories of income reduction: direct income loss from units taken offline for remediation or deemed untenantable by the inspection reports, and a market rent discount applied to units where the defects were disclosed to prospective tenants but remediation had not been completed. The combined income reduction documented in that scenario reduced stabilized NOI by approximately 18% to 24%, bringing it into the range of $170,000 to $210,000 annually.

DSCR and Rate Assumptions: For a mixed-use commercial property in Rock Hill at the time period relevant to this engagement, a conventional commercial lender or regional bank would apply a minimum debt service coverage ratio of 1.25x, which is the standard floor for most bank portfolio lenders and CMBS underwriters on this property type. Some community banks active in the York County market will lend at 1.20x for well-located, stabilized assets with strong sponsorship, but 1.25x represents the market standard floor that governs maximum loan sizing in our analysis. A 30-year amortization schedule is standard for this asset type, and we applied an interest rate assumption consistent with the prevailing rate environment at the time of harm, which for a 5-year fixed term on a commercial property of this type would fall in the range of 6.75% to 7.25%, producing a loan constant in the range of 7.78% to 8.06% annually on a 30-year amortization.

Supportable Loan Calculations: Using the 1.25x DSCR floor and a loan constant of approximately 7.90% (the midpoint of the range), the maximum annual debt service supportable by the defect-free NOI of $240,000 (midpoint of the defect-free range) is $192,000, which supports a maximum loan of approximately $2,430,000. Applying an LTV constraint of 70% to 75%, typical for this asset type with a regional bank or life company lender in Rock Hill, and assuming a stabilized value of approximately $3,100,000 to $3,400,000 (consistent with a 7.25% to 8.00% cap rate range for mixed-use product in this submarket), the LTV-constrained loan ceiling falls in the range of $2,170,000 to $2,550,000. In the defect-free scenario, the DSCR-constrained loan and the LTV-constrained loan are broadly consistent with one another, meaning the property has normal, underwritable financing capacity.

In the defect-impacted scenario, the reduced NOI of approximately $190,000 (midpoint of the impacted range) supports maximum annual debt service of $152,000 at the 1.25x threshold, which translates to a maximum DSCR-supported loan of approximately $1,924,000. More critically, the presence of unresolved construction defect litigation causes most lenders to apply an additional risk discount to the as-is value and to require either a holdback reserve or a complete payoff of the litigation contingency before funding, which further reduces net loan proceeds available to the borrower. The financing gap between the two scenarios, representing the economic harm attributable to the alleged defects in financing terms, is approximately $500,000 to $600,000 in reduced loan capacity, plus the cost of any additional equity or reserves the borrower must post to satisfy lender conditions in the defect-impacted state.

The Complicating Factors

Unresolved Litigation as a Lender Disqualifier: The single most significant complicating factor in any construction defect financing engagement is that most institutional lenders will not close a loan on a property that is the named subject of unresolved construction defect litigation. This is not a matter of pricing or risk premium. It is a categorical exclusion that appears in the credit policy of virtually every CMBS conduit, life insurance company, and agency lender, and in the underwriting guidelines of the majority of regional banks. The reason is straightforward: unresolved litigation creates a contingent liability that could attach to the collateral, impair the lender's lien position, or require remediation expenditures that reduce the net value of the lender's security. In Rock Hill, where the lender market is dominated by regional banks and community institutions that hold loans in portfolio, the practical effect is that a property under active construction defect litigation is either unfundable or fundable only through bridge or hard money capital at materially higher cost.

Deferred Maintenance and Reserve Requirements: Even where a lender is willing to engage on a property with known defects that are not yet in litigation, the lender's engineering inspection will identify the defects and the lender will require either a remediation escrow funded at closing, a holdback of loan proceeds released upon verified remediation, or a reduction in the loan amount sufficient to protect the lender's LTV position. In practice, this means the borrower bears the remediation cost twice: once as a reduction in loan proceeds and once as actual cash spent on repairs. The financing impact of even a moderately complex defect can therefore significantly exceed the direct cost of the remediation itself when measured against the borrower's capital stack.

Lease-Up Period Disruption: In a newly completed property still in early lease-up, construction defects impose a particularly severe financing impact because the property has not yet demonstrated stabilized income. Lenders underwriting lease-up assets apply a "burn-off" analysis that projects the timeline to stabilization, and any defect that extends that timeline directly extends the period during which the loan is either unsupported or requires additional interest reserves. For a mixed-use property in Rock Hill attempting to stabilize during an active remediation period, lender appetite is further constrained by the uncertainty around lease-up velocity.

Secondary Market Pricing Sensitivity: Rock Hill's position as a secondary market means that lenders applying risk-based pricing have less room to absorb income volatility before the loan becomes uncompetitive with the borrower's equity return requirements. In primary markets, compression of cap rates provides a buffer between income disruption and value impairment. In Rock Hill, at cap rates of 7.25% to 8.25% for mixed-use product, a 20% NOI reduction translates directly and almost proportionally into a 20% value reduction, with limited offset from the cap rate environment. This makes the financing impact of construction defects proportionally more severe in Rock Hill than in Charlotte proper or other primary market contexts.

The Rock Hill Lender Market

The Rock Hill commercial lending market is served by a layered set of capital sources, each with distinct underwriting parameters and relevance to a defect-impacted asset.

Regional and Community Banks: This is the dominant capital source for commercial loans on mixed-use, retail, and smaller industrial assets in Rock Hill. Institutions headquartered in Charlotte, Greenville, Columbia, and within York County itself maintain active commercial real estate lending programs in this submarket. These lenders generally require 1.20x to 1.25x DSCR, 70% to 75% LTV, and full recourse from the sponsoring entity. They hold loans in portfolio, which gives them flexibility on structure but also makes them highly sensitive to asset quality and litigation risk. A known construction defect or pending litigation will trigger their legal and engineering review process, and most will require resolution of the defect issue as a condition of closing.

CMBS and Conduit Lenders: Applicable for larger commercial assets in Rock Hill, generally above $3 million to $5 million in loan size. CMBS lenders underwrite to securitization standards, which means they apply rigorous income documentation requirements and categorically exclude properties with material unresolved litigation or known physical deficiencies that have not been remediated and re-inspected. For a defect-impacted property, CMBS execution is effectively unavailable until the defect is cured, the litigation is resolved, and the property demonstrates at least 90 days of post-remediation stabilized income.

Debt Funds and Bridge Lenders: For properties that cannot qualify for conventional or CMBS financing due to defect-related complications, bridge lenders and private debt funds represent the available alternative. These lenders will typically advance at 65% of as-stabilized value, price at 300 to 500 basis points over the applicable index, and impose origination fees of 1% to 2%. They will lend on the defect-impacted property but will underwrite based on the remediated value and will require the borrower to demonstrate a clear remediation path and timeline. The cost differential between conventional financing and bridge financing, when calculated over the expected loan term, is itself a quantifiable component of the financing harm in a defect litigation context.

SBA 504 and USDA Programs: For owner-occupied commercial properties in Rock Hill, SBA 504 financing through a Certified Development Company remains available and active. These programs require the property to be free of known unresolved defects and clear of litigation at the time of application, but they can be relevant to the damages analysis as a measure of the financing opportunity cost when a defect prevents their use.

What Attorneys Handling Rock Hill CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to Retain a CRE Financing Expert: Any time the alleged harm in a commercial real estate dispute includes a claim that the plaintiff was prevented from financing, refinancing, selling at full value, or leasing a commercial asset, a CRE financing expert is appropriate. This includes construction defect cases where the defect impairs the property's lendability, breach of contract cases involving failed loan commitments, lender liability cases, and disputes where the parties disagree about whether a transaction was financially feasible at a specific point in time. The earlier in the litigation the expert is retained, the more useful the analysis will be in shaping discovery and damages theory.

What a CRE Financing Expert Does That an Appraiser Cannot: An appraiser establishes value. A CRE financing expert establishes financibility and financing capacity. These are related but distinct questions. An appraiser will tell you what the property is worth. A CRE financing expert will tell you what a lender would actually do with that property: what loan amount they would approve, what conditions they would impose, what the maximum DSCR-supportable debt is, whether the property falls within or outside the credit policy of the relevant lender universe, and what the cost differential is between conventional and alternative financing sources. These are the questions that quantify economic harm in a construction defect case, and they require active market knowledge that most appraisers do not possess.

Qualifications That Matter: A qualified CRE financing expert should have current, active involvement in commercial loan origination or placement, not merely historical banking experience. The lending market changes rapidly, and a retired banker's knowledge of underwriting standards may be five to ten years out of date. The expert should have relationships with the categories of lenders active in the relevant submarket, should be able to speak to current credit policy from direct transactional experience, and should be able to support their rate and underwriting assumptions with contemporaneous market data from actual loan closings.

Information Counsel Should Gather: Before retaining a CRE financing expert in a Rock Hill construction defect matter, counsel should assemble the engineering and inspection reports documenting the alleged defects; the property's rent roll, operating statements, and lease abstracts; any appraisals that have been conducted; any loan applications, commitment letters, or lender rejections

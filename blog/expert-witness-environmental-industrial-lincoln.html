Expert Witness: Financing a Contaminated Industrial Property in Lincoln, NE

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Environmental Contamination Analysis

The Lincoln Market Context

Lincoln, Nebraska is not a secondary market footnote. It is a genuinely distinct commercial real estate environment with characteristics that matter enormously when a financing dispute reaches litigation. The city anchors a regional economy built on state government, the University of Nebraska, healthcare, and a manufacturing base that has remained more stable than coastal industrial markets through recent rate cycles. For industrial and warehouse product specifically, Lincoln operates in a fundamentally different supply-demand dynamic than Omaha, which sits 55 miles northwest and draws different institutional capital.

Industrial vacancy in Lincoln has remained in the 3% to 6% range through the post-pandemic period, reflecting constrained land supply along the primary corridors near Highway 2, I-80, and the Union Pacific rail lines. Net asking rents for functional warehouse product in the 50,000 to 200,000 square foot range have moved from approximately $4.50 to $5.25 per square foot NNN in 2020 to a range of $6.00 to $7.50 per square foot NNN in current leasing activity, depending on clear height, dock count, and power availability. This rent growth is significant because it creates a measurable gap between in-place rents on older leases and current market rents, which is precisely the kind of structural characteristic that shows up in financing underwriting as both a credit strength and a complicating signal.

Institutionally, Lincoln attracts regional and national industrial investors, but the buyer pool and lender pool are both shallower than Omaha or Kansas City. That matters in litigation because the financing universe for a complicated asset is genuinely narrower here than in a Tier 1 market. A lender that would actively compete for a clean industrial deal in Chicago or Dallas may pass on the same asset in Lincoln not because of the asset fundamentals but because of portfolio concentration limits and origination cost economics. Any expert opinion on financing feasibility in Lincoln must account for this structural reality rather than importing assumptions from larger markets.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by counsel representing a class of secured creditors in a regulatory enforcement proceeding involving an industrial warehouse property in the Lincoln market. The creditors held a first-priority mortgage position and needed to evaluate whether a refinancing event was feasible at a loan amount sufficient to retire existing senior debt, or whether the environmental condition rendered the collateral effectively unfinanceable at that loan basis.

The asset was a single-tenant industrial warehouse of approximately 120,000 square feet, 100% leased on a triple-net basis to an industrial operator with a remaining term of approximately six years. The in-place rent was contractually below current market rents, a condition that was baked into the original lease structure and was not a function of credit deterioration. The property had been subject to prior industrial use by a predecessor operator. Regulatory investigation had confirmed the presence of 1,4-dioxane and chlorinated volatile organic compounds in soil and groundwater beneath and adjacent to the building footprint. A Phase II Environmental Site Assessment and a subsequent Remedial Investigation had been completed. A remediation plan was under review by the Nebraska Department of Environment and Energy.

The core legal questions our engagement was asked to answer were two: first, does the presence of confirmed environmental contamination under regulatory enforcement preclude first-mortgage financing in the current market? Second, if financing is possible, what is the maximum loan amount supportable under market underwriting standards, and does that maximum support retirement of the existing senior debt? A secondary question involved the income treatment methodology that a lender would apply to the below-market NNN lease, which had direct implications for what NOI a lender would underwrite and therefore what loan amount was achievable.

The Financial Analysis

The income analysis began with the in-place lease. The property generated contractual gross revenues of approximately $696,000 annually, reflecting an in-place rent of $5.80 per square foot on 120,000 square feet. Because the lease was triple-net with minimal landlord expense obligations, the effective net operating income available for debt service was approximately $668,000 after accounting for management fee reserves and modest non-reimbursable capital reserves that lenders require even on NNN structures.

The first analytical decision was whether to underwrite to in-place income or to apply a haircut or cap to reflect the below-market rent and the associated re-leasing risk at lease expiration. Market-standard underwriting in the current environment for a single-tenant industrial asset with a six-year remaining NNN term will generally use in-place income for debt service coverage purposes but will stress-test the loan against a reversion scenario at lease expiration. Lenders do not simply capitalize market rent as a forward income assumption; they evaluate whether the in-place income is durable for the loan term and whether the exit basis at loan maturity is supportable at market rent levels.

For debt service coverage, the market standard for industrial collateral in secondary markets like Lincoln is a minimum of 1.25x DSCR on a fully amortizing basis, with many institutional lenders applying 1.30x on assets with environmental complexity. I applied 1.25x as the base case and 1.30x as the stressed case to bracket the financing universe. At a note rate of 7.25%, which reflects current SOFR-based or Treasury-indexed pricing for a five-year fixed rate loan on secondary-market industrial collateral with environmental encumbrances at the time of this analysis, and a 25-year amortization schedule, the annual debt service on a $7,500,000 loan is approximately $638,000. That produces a DSCR of approximately 1.047x on in-place NOI, which is insufficient. Working backwards from the 1.25x coverage requirement, the maximum debt service supportable from $668,000 of NOI is $534,400 annually. At 7.25% and 25-year amortization, that debt service supports a loan of approximately $6,280,000.

On an LTV basis, assuming a stabilized as-is value for the property in the range of $9,500,000 to $10,200,000 (reflecting a capitalization rate of approximately 6.75% to 7.25% applied to in-place NOI, consistent with where comparable single-tenant industrial assets in Lincoln and similar Nebraska markets have traded), a 65% LTV constraint, which is standard for environmentally impaired collateral, produces a maximum LTV-constrained loan of approximately $6,175,000 to $6,630,000. The DSCR constraint and the LTV constraint converge in a range of approximately $6,175,000 to $6,280,000. This is the analytically supportable loan range under current market underwriting standards.

The existing senior debt balance was disclosed to our firm as approximately $7,400,000. The analytical conclusion was therefore that first-mortgage financing, if available at all given the environmental complexity discussed below, would be insufficient to retire the existing senior debt by a shortfall of approximately $1,120,000 to $1,225,000 at market underwriting standards. This conclusion was material to the creditors' legal strategy and to the court's evaluation of plan feasibility in the enforcement proceeding.

The Complicating Factors

Confirmed Environmental Contamination with Active Regulatory Oversight

The presence of 1,4-dioxane and chlorinated VOCs under active regulatory enforcement is not a hypothetical future liability. It is a present, disclosed, and quantified condition. Lenders treat this category of contamination differently from the presence of recognized environmental conditions identified in a Phase I. When a Phase II has confirmed contamination and a remedial investigation is underway, the lender's environmental engineer or environmental insurance team must evaluate the scope of the remediation obligation, the timeline, and the cost estimate. 1,4-dioxane is particularly problematic from a lender perspective because it is highly mobile in groundwater, does not adsorb to soil particles the way chlorinated solvents do, and is expensive to treat using conventional pump-and-treat technology. Lenders who understand the chemistry will price or exclude accordingly.

The practical effect is that most conventional institutional lenders, CMBS conduits, and life company lenders will decline to originate on collateral with confirmed contamination under active regulatory enforcement, regardless of income quality. The underwriting exclusion is not about the NOI; it is about the title encumbrance risk, the remediation cost uncertainty, and the potential for regulatory liens to prime or impair the mortgage position. Lenders who will engage with this category of collateral are typically environmental lenders with dedicated expertise, certain regional banks with Nebraska industrial borrower relationships, and bridge capital providers who structure around the environmental condition through escrow, insurance, or remediation completion guarantees.

Below-Market In-Place Rent and Lease Rollover Risk

The six-year remaining NNN term provides income stability for debt service coverage during the loan term, but lenders model the exit. At a loan maturity of five years, the borrower must either refinance or sell with one year remaining on the NNN lease. That is a structurally weak refinancing position. The below-market rent, currently approximately $5.80 per square foot against current market of $6.75 to $7.50 per square foot, means the tenant has economic incentive to remain, but lenders cannot rely on that behavioral prediction. They will apply a rollover stress scenario that reduces the exit value by the cost of a tenant vacancy period, re-leasing commissions, and potential tenant improvement allowances, which for a 120,000-square-foot industrial building in Lincoln can aggregate $2,500,000 to $4,000,000 in leasing costs and carry. This further constrains the loan amount a lender will underwrite to today.

Nebraska Regulatory Framework and Remediation Timeline

The Nebraska Department of Environment and Energy operates under state voluntary cleanup and corrective action frameworks that can produce remediation timelines ranging from three to more than fifteen years depending on the contaminant, the hydrogeology, and the cleanup standard selected. For 1,4-dioxane specifically, Nebraska has adopted drinking water guidance values that are among the more stringent in the region, which creates a longer and more expensive remediation obligation than a risk-based corrective action approach might otherwise require. Lenders evaluating collateral in Nebraska need to understand that state regulatory timelines are not compressible by borrower preference, and that a remediation obligation with an uncertain timeline creates an indefinite cloud on the title that most conventional lenders will not accept as collateral encumbrance.

The Lincoln Lender Market

For a clean industrial asset in Lincoln, the lending universe includes regional banks headquartered in Nebraska and Iowa, national CMBS platforms, certain life company correspondents, and credit unions with commercial real estate programs. For a contaminated industrial asset under regulatory enforcement, that universe collapses substantially.

Lincoln-based and Nebraska-chartered community and regional banks are the most likely category of lender to engage with this asset type, primarily because they have existing borrower relationships in the Nebraska industrial sector, they understand the Nebraska regulatory environment, and they have credit committees that can underwrite around environmental conditions with appropriate structuring. Banks in this category will typically require a fully executed remediation cost estimate with an environmental insurance policy covering cost overruns, regulatory closure escrow, and a personal or corporate guarantee from a creditworthy indemnitor. Their pricing on an environmentally impaired industrial asset in Lincoln will reflect a spread premium of 75 to 150 basis points over what they would charge on a clean comparable, and their LTV constraint will tighten to 60% to 65% on the impaired collateral value.

Bridge lenders and debt funds represent the other realistic category. These lenders price risk explicitly rather than declining it, and they have the underwriting infrastructure to evaluate environmental exposure. Their rates will typically run 300 to 500 basis points over a benchmark rate, reflecting the risk premium and the short-term nature of the loan. For a Lincoln industrial asset, a bridge lender might provide a 12 to 24-month loan at a loan-to-cost basis that allows the borrower to advance the remediation, obtain regulatory closure, and then refinance into conventional permanent debt. This path exists but it is expensive and requires a clear remediation timeline, which is precisely what is uncertain in an active enforcement case.

CMBS, Fannie, Freddie, and life company executions are effectively unavailable for this asset in its current environmental condition. These lenders have environmental eligibility standards that categorically exclude assets with confirmed contamination under active regulatory enforcement, and no amount of structuring will bring this collateral within their credit boxes until remediation is substantially complete and regulatory closure is issued.

What Attorneys Handling Lincoln CRE Disputes Need to Know

A CRE financing expert is not a substitute for an appraiser, and an appraiser is not a substitute for a CRE financing expert. These are distinct disciplines with different methodologies and different evidentiary purposes. An appraiser determines value. A CRE financing expert determines whether and how a lender would actually lend against that value, which is a separate question that requires active market knowledge, lender relationship intelligence, and underwriting methodology expertise that no appraiser is trained to provide.

Attorneys should consider retaining a CRE financing expert in any Lincoln commercial real estate dispute that involves one or more of the following questions: Was financing available to a borrower at a particular point in time? Could a plan of reorganization be funded through a refinancing? Was a lender's decision to accelerate, foreclose, or deny a modification consistent with market standards? Did a borrower have financing alternatives that were not pursued? Was a loan originated at terms that were outside market standards? Did environmental conditions preclude financing or only constrain it? Each of these questions requires expert testimony that goes beyond valuation into capital markets mechanics.

A qualified CRE financing expert is not simply a retired banker or a former real estate attorney. The qualification that matters is current, active engagement in the capital markets. The expert must be able to say, based on current lender relationships and active deal flow, what lenders in this category require today, how they price this risk today, and whether a specific loan structure is achievable in the current market. Our firm at Commercial Lending Solutions has approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, more than 1,000 active lender relationships, and origination activity across all 50 states, which means our expert opinions are grounded in real-time market intelligence rather than historical recollection.

Before retaining a CRE financing expert, counsel should gather the following materials: the Phase I and Phase II ESA reports, the remedial investigation and any remediation cost estimates, all existing loan documents including the note, mortgage, and any environmental indemnity agreements, the current rent roll with lease abstracts, at least three years of property operating statements, any existing appraisals or broker opinions of value, and the regulatory correspondence from the Nebraska Department of Environment and Energy. The expert can work without all of these materials, but the opinion will be more defensible and more specific when the record is complete.

At trial or in a Plan confirmation hearing, CRE financing expert testimony is used to establish what the market would actually do with a specific asset at a specific loan amount, not what theoretically might happen in an idealized market. Judges and arbitrators benefit from expert testimony that connects abstract financing concepts to specific lender behavior, specific underwriting standards, and specific market conditions in the relevant geography. An opinion that says "financing was available" without specifying which lenders, at what terms, and under what conditions is not useful testimony. An opinion that names the categories of lenders who would engage, the underwriting parameters they would apply, and the loan amounts they would produce is genuinely useful.

Key Takeaways

  • Environmental contamination does not automatically preclude financing, but confirmed 1,4-dioxane or chlorinated VOC contamination under active regulatory enforcement eliminates CMBS, life company, agency, and most conventional bank executions. The available lending universe narrows to specialized environmental lenders, relationship-driven community banks with Nebraska industrial experience, and bridge debt funds.
  • The maximum supportable loan on the Lincoln industrial asset analyzed was approximately $6,175,000 to $6,280,000 under current market underwriting standards, reflecting a 1.25x DSCR constraint on in-place NOI and a 65% LTV constraint on an environmentally impaired as-is value, both of which are consistent with what active lenders in this category actually apply.
  • The existing senior debt balance of approximately $7,400,000 exceeded the maximum supportable first-mortgage loan by approximately $1,120,000 to $1,225,000, making a full refinancing payoff of senior debt infeasible under current market conditions without a material equity contribution or debt restructuring.
  • Below-market NNN rent creates a structural disadvantage at loan maturity. A six-year remaining lease term with one year remaining at a five-year loan maturity produces a weak exit basis that lenders model as a lease rollover risk scenario, further constraining loan proceeds today.
  • Nebraska's regulatory framework for 1,4-dioxane remediation is among the more stringent in the region. Attorneys and lenders cannot assume that a short or predictable remediation timeline is achievable; the NDEE process for this contaminant class can extend remediation obligations well beyond a standard loan term.

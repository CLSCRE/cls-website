Expert Witness: Ground Lease Financing Disputes and Analysis in Sioux Falls, SD

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Ground Lease Financing Complexity

The Sioux Falls Market Context

Sioux Falls occupies a genuinely distinctive position in the Great Plains commercial real estate landscape. Unlike many secondary Midwest markets that have struggled with population stagnation, Sioux Falls has posted consistent net in-migration, driven by South Dakota's zero personal income tax, a diversified healthcare and financial services employment base, and a regional retail and logistics draw that extends well into southwestern Minnesota, northwestern Iowa, and northeastern Nebraska. The metro population has crossed 280,000 and continues to expand, which matters in ground lease analysis because ground lease feasibility is ultimately a function of whether the underlying land holds long-term residual value sufficient to support a 50- to 99-year lease structure.

From a commercial fundamentals standpoint, the Sioux Falls industrial market has been among the tightest in the upper Midwest, with vacancy rates hovering in the 3 to 5 percent range in recent years, driven by distribution and cold storage demand tied to the agricultural supply chain and regional logistics. Retail fundamentals along the primary commercial corridors remain stronger here than in comparable Midwest metros, supported by consumer traffic from a wide regional trade area. Multifamily cap rates in Sioux Falls have historically traded in the 5.25 to 6.25 percent range depending on vintage and submarket, tighter than Lincoln or Fargo but wider than Minneapolis. Office is the one soft spot, reflecting post-pandemic utilization shifts that mirror national trends, with suburban Class B product facing the most pressure.

Ground leases in this market appear most frequently on retail pad sites, healthcare-adjacent parcels, and mixed-use land assemblages where an institutional landowner, a municipality, or a long-tenured family estate retains the fee interest while a developer builds and finances improvements. The South Dakota regulatory environment imposes no state income tax on ground rent receipts to individuals, which makes land ownership here particularly attractive for family trusts and estate planning structures. That ownership profile, while tax-efficient, can create complications when a leasehold mortgagee requires specific SNDA protections that unsophisticated fee owners are reluctant to grant, and it is exactly that tension that generates the financing disputes and litigation I am called to analyze.

The Engagement

In the matter I will use as the framework for this analysis, counsel for a leasehold borrower retained Commercial Lending Solutions to provide a financing feasibility opinion and expert analysis in a dispute arising from a failed commercial mortgage transaction on a ground-leased retail and service commercial property in the Sioux Falls metro. The fee owner was an estate-controlled entity. The leasehold tenant had developed and operated the improvements and sought permanent financing to retire a construction loan that was approaching maturity. The construction lender's demand for repayment was the triggering event for the dispute.

Specifically, CLS CRE was retained to answer four questions. First, was the ground lease, as drafted and executed, financeable in the institutional commercial mortgage market? Second, did the ground lease's SNDA provisions, or the absence of certain provisions, meet or fall short of market-standard requirements that lenders impose as a condition of making a leasehold mortgage? Third, what was the maximum supportable permanent loan on the leasehold interest, and did that loan amount provide sufficient proceeds to retire the outstanding construction debt? Fourth, if the ground lease was not financeable as structured, what modifications, if any, would have been necessary to bring it into conformance with market standards, and were those modifications reasonable to request of the fee owner?

These are financing questions, not valuation questions. An appraiser can opine on the market value of a leasehold interest under a given set of assumptions, but the appraiser typically is not equipped to testify about how specific lease provisions affect lender credit decisions, what institutional underwriting guidelines require in SNDA language, or whether the specific defects in a ground lease explain a lender's decline of a loan application. That is where a commercial mortgage professional functioning as an expert witness provides testimony that an appraisal expert cannot replicate.

The Financial Analysis

The subject property was a multi-tenant retail service center in a primary commercial submarket of Sioux Falls, with gross leasable area in the range of 18,000 to 22,000 square feet. The improvements sat on land held under a ground lease with approximately 42 years of initial term remaining plus renewal options. The leasehold borrower sought permanent financing to replace the construction loan.

The first step in the analysis was reconstructing the stabilized net operating income. In-place leases from the operating tenants generated gross potential rent, from which I deducted a market vacancy allowance of 7 percent, consistent with retail service submarket vacancy in Sioux Falls at the relevant time. After applying a credit loss reserve and deducting verified operating expenses including ground rent, insurance, management fees, and reserves for replacement, the stabilized NOI arrived at approximately $285,000 annually. Ground rent was a below-grade expense in this analysis, meaning it was treated as a fixed operating cost reducing NOI available to service leasehold mortgage debt, which is the correct treatment and a point that matters significantly in leasehold underwriting.

I then applied a debt service coverage ratio threshold of 1.25x, which is the market standard floor for conventional commercial mortgage lending on retail properties with leasehold collateral. Most CMBS programs and life company programs require 1.25x minimum DSCR on leasehold deals; some push to 1.30x given the added complexity and legal risk of the collateral structure. Applying a 1.25x DSCR constraint to $285,000 in NOI yields a maximum annual debt service of $228,000.

For rate and amortization assumptions, I used a 30-year amortization schedule, which is the market convention for retail leasehold financing where the remaining lease term supports the amortization period. The general rule of thumb in leasehold lending is that the remaining lease term, including reasonably exercisable renewals, must exceed the loan amortization period by at least 10 years, and some lenders require a 15-year buffer. At a representative permanent loan rate of 7.25 percent (reflecting the rate environment relevant to the dispute period), a 30-year amortizing loan produces an annual mortgage constant of approximately 0.0820. Dividing $228,000 by 0.0820 yields a maximum supportable loan of approximately $2.78 million.

The construction loan balance that the borrower needed to retire was approximately $3.15 million, which created a shortfall of roughly $370,000. That gap is not incidental. It is the direct financial consequence of the leasehold financing constraints imposed by the ground lease's structural deficiencies, and quantifying that gap precisely is the kind of analysis that connects legal liability to financial damages in a financing dispute. If the ground lease had been properly structured with market-standard SNDA provisions, the financing universe would have broadened to include lenders willing to apply a 1.20x DSCR threshold and a slightly tighter spread, which would have closed the gap and made the refinance executable.

On loan-to-value, I applied a 65 percent LTV cap, which is standard for leasehold mortgage lending on retail assets with any structural complexity. A leasehold interest is inherently subordinate to the fee, and lenders price that subordination by requiring more equity cushion than they would on fee-simple collateral of equivalent quality. At 65 percent LTV, and using a leasehold valuation consistent with the appraised leasehold value in the record, the LTV constraint was actually less binding than the DSCR constraint in this case. The DSCR was the binding underwriting test, which is a useful finding for counsel because it isolates the income and debt service analysis as the operative financial constraint.

The Complicating Factors

Absence of a Lender-Protective SNDA Agreement. The ground lease at issue contained a subordination clause that made the ground lease subordinate to any leasehold mortgage, which sounds favorable to the lender but is actually only half of what is required. Without a corresponding non-disturbance obligation running from the fee owner to the leasehold mortgagee, the lender has no assurance that if the leasehold borrower defaults and the fee owner terminates the ground lease, the lender's mortgage survives. Institutional lenders, and CMBS programs categorically, will not make a leasehold mortgage without a fully executed SNDA agreement that binds the fee owner to recognize the lender's rights, refrain from terminating the ground lease without giving the lender cure rights, and attorn to the lender if the lender takes possession through foreclosure or deed in lieu. The fee owner in this matter, an estate entity managed by a trustee with no commercial real estate background, had refused to execute an SNDA on advice of estate counsel who did not fully understand the commercial mortgage market implications of that refusal.

Lease Term Insufficiency Relative to Loan Amortization. With 42 years of initial term remaining and two 10-year renewal options, the aggregate potential term was 62 years. However, lenders do not automatically credit unexercised renewal options unless those options are exercisable solely at the lessee's discretion, without conditions that could allow a fee owner to block renewal. The renewal options in this lease required "mutual agreement on rent for the renewal term," a negotiated condition that most lenders treat as effectively illusory because it gives the fee owner leverage to block renewal by demanding above-market rent. After stripping out the conditioned renewal options, the financeable term was the initial 42-year period, which provides only a 12-year buffer over a 30-year amortization schedule. Several lenders in the institutional market require a 15-year buffer, which would have made this lease ineligible for those programs.

Ground Rent Reset Provisions. The lease contained a periodic ground rent reset tied to fair market value of the land, with resets every 10 years. Lenders underwriting leasehold mortgages treat future ground rent resets as a potentially material expense increase that reduces NOI available for debt service in the later years of the loan. When the reset mechanism is tied to land value appreciation rather than a fixed CPI adjustment, lenders typically require either a cap on the reset amount, a debt service reserve sized to absorb a defined rent increase, or a lower initial LTV to provide cushion. None of these protections were present in the loan application, and the lenders who declined the application specifically cited the uncapped rent reset as a disqualifying structural feature.

No Leasehold Mortgagee Cure Rights. Market-standard ground leases include a provision giving the leasehold mortgagee independent notice of any default under the ground lease and a cure period that runs separately from, and extends beyond, the tenant's cure period. The lease at issue provided no such independent mortgagee cure right, meaning that if the leasehold borrower defaulted on a ground rent obligation, the fee owner could potentially terminate the lease on shorter notice than the lender would need to protect its collateral through foreclosure or appointment of a receiver. This provision, or its absence, is a fundamental lending concern and one of the first things any institutional underwriter checks when reviewing a ground lease for mortgage eligibility.

The Sioux Falls Lender Market

Understanding which lenders will and will not touch leasehold financing in Sioux Falls is essential context for any financing feasibility opinion. The Sioux Falls market is served by a reasonably deep community and regional bank presence, anchored by institutions headquartered in South Dakota as well as branches and loan production offices of larger regional banks based in Minneapolis, Omaha, and Chicago. For leasehold mortgage lending specifically, that community bank depth is largely irrelevant: most community banks in South Dakota do not have approved leasehold lending policies and will decline leasehold collateral as a matter of credit policy without even underwriting the deal.

The realistic lending universe for a ground leasehold mortgage on a Sioux Falls retail or commercial asset breaks down as follows. Regional banks with dedicated commercial real estate divisions, typically those operating across multiple Midwest states, will consider leasehold collateral but require full SNDA compliance, a minimum remaining financeable term of 30 years beyond the loan term, and typically price at a spread of 25 to 50 basis points over what they would charge for equivalent fee-simple collateral, reflecting the legal complexity and added documentation burden. Life insurance companies and their correspondent networks, through which CLS CRE places transactions, are among the most disciplined leasehold lenders: their credit standards are exacting, their SNDA requirements are non-negotiable, and their documentation requirements are thorough, but they offer attractive long-term fixed rates and are genuinely competitive on well-structured leasehold deals in markets like Sioux Falls where the underlying land value is stable and well-documented. CMBS conduit lending is theoretically available for leasehold collateral meeting Fannie/Freddie or private-label trust standards, but the additional legal review costs, reserve requirements, and execution risk make CMBS a second-choice option for a Sioux Falls deal of this size. Debt funds and bridge lenders will finance leasehold collateral, but at floating rates and with short terms that do not solve a permanent financing problem.

The practical consequence in this market is that a ground lease with the structural deficiencies described above reduces the financing universe from several viable execution paths to essentially zero institutional options, which is precisely the finding that supports a damages theory based on financing infeasibility.

What Attorneys Handling Sioux Falls CRE Disputes Need to Know

If you are representing a party in a commercial real estate financing dispute in Sioux Falls involving a ground lease, a failed loan closing, a construction loan maturity default, or a dispute over whether a property was financeable at a given point in time, you need a CRE financing expert, not just an appraiser. The distinction is critical and often misunderstood.

An appraiser can tell the court what the property was worth. A commercial mortgage expert can tell the court whether the property was financeable, at what loan amount, under what terms, and why specific structural or documentary deficiencies caused lenders to decline the deal. Those are different questions, and in most ground lease financing disputes, the operative question is the financing feasibility question, not the valuation question.

Retain a CRE financing expert when your case involves any of the following: a borrower who claims they could not refinance due to a third party's failure to execute required documents; a lender whose loan decline is being challenged as commercially unreasonable; a dispute over whether a ground lease was marketable and financeable as warranted; a bankruptcy plan of reorganization where the feasibility of a refinancing exit strategy is contested; or a damages theory that requires proof of the maximum loan amount a properly structured deal would have supported.

Qualifying your expert matters. A retired community bank loan officer is not the same as a commercial mortgage broker with active institutional relationships across 1,000 or more lenders. The expert should be able to demonstrate current knowledge of CMBS underwriting guidelines, life company credit standards, and SNDA market conventions, not as they existed 15 years ago, but as they exist today. At CLS CRE, our experts have arranged approximately $1 billion in transaction volume across 50 states and maintain active relationships with more than 1,000 institutional and private capital lenders. That market participation is what makes the expert opinion credible: we are not describing a theoretical market, we are describing the market we transact in daily.

Before the expert engagement begins, counsel should gather the complete ground lease and all amendments, any executed or proposed SNDA agreements, the loan application and lender correspondence including any decline letters, the rent roll and operating statements for at least three years, any appraisals or broker opinions of value in the record, the construction loan documents, and any communications between the parties regarding the financing process. The more complete the documentary record, the more precise and defensible the expert's analysis will be.

At trial or in a plan confirmation hearing, CRE financing expert testimony is most effective when it connects specific lease provisions or transactional facts to specific lender responses, quantifies the financial impact of those connections, and explains in plain terms why the institutional market responded as it did. Judges and arbitrators respond well to testimony that is grounded in actual lender guidelines and actual market practice, not abstract principles.

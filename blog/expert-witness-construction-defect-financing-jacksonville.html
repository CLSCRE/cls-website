Expert Witness: How Construction Defects Affect CRE Financing in Jacksonville, FL

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Construction Defect Impact on Financing

The Jacksonville Market Context

Jacksonville is not a secondary market in name only anymore. Over the past several years, this metro has attracted meaningful institutional capital across multiple asset classes, driven by population growth, a business-friendly regulatory environment, a deep port infrastructure, and cost-of-living advantages that continue to pull household formation and corporate relocations from higher-cost Florida metros and from the Northeast. Understanding this market context is not a formality when I am retained as a financing expert witness here. It is foundational to every opinion I render, because the financing feasibility of a commercial asset is always a function of where that asset sits in its market.

On the industrial side, Jacksonville's port proximity and logistics infrastructure have pushed vacancy in the bulk distribution submarket below 4% in recent cycles, with asking rents for Class A industrial product in the northwest and westside corridors pushing well above $9.00 per square foot NNN, a figure that was considered aspirational only five years ago. Multifamily has been the most active asset class for construction, with over 5,000 units delivered across the metro in a recent 24-month period, creating pockets of concession pressure in submarkets like Deerwood and Southside while suburban infill assets continue to hold occupancy above 93%. Cap rates for stabilized multifamily in Jacksonville have compressed into the 5.0% to 5.5% range for institutional-quality product, though value-add deals with genuine construction complexity are trading wider, often in the 5.75% to 6.25% range depending on basis and lease-up risk.

Office is a more complicated story, consistent with national trends but with Jacksonville-specific nuances. Class A suburban product along the Butler Boulevard and Baymeadows corridors has held up better than downtown high-rise, where vacancy has approached 18% to 22% in some buildings. Retail has bifurcated sharply, with grocery-anchored neighborhood centers performing well and power center formats facing ongoing tenant attrition. For purposes of construction defect financing analysis, all of these market-specific fundamentals matter because they determine what a lender believes a property can actually produce in stabilized income, and that income projection is the engine of every loan sizing calculation I perform.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing a party in a construction defect dispute involving a newly completed multifamily asset in Jacksonville's urban core submarket. The property had been delivered within the prior 18 months and was in the process of lease-up when significant structural and waterproofing deficiencies were alleged, including issues with the building envelope, plumbing penetrations, and balcony framing that had resulted in active moisture intrusion across multiple units and common area spaces. The developer-borrower had an existing construction loan that was maturing, and a takeout refinance was necessary to retire that debt. The alleged defects had complicated, and ultimately prevented, the refinancing.

Counsel retained CLS CRE to address three core questions. First, whether the property, as affected by the alleged defects, was financeable in the current Jacksonville lender market and under what conditions. Second, what the maximum supportable loan would be on the property in its defect-affected state compared to a defect-free asset of equivalent type and location. Third, whether the gap between those two loan amounts represented a quantifiable financing impairment that could be articulated as damages in the litigation. These are precisely the questions that require a capital markets practitioner, not an appraiser and not a construction consultant working in isolation. The financing feasibility analysis sits at the intersection of lender underwriting standards, market income assumptions, and asset-specific risk factors, and that is the specific territory where my firm operates.

The Financial Analysis

The methodology I applied in this engagement follows the same framework I use across all commercial mortgage expert witness assignments: build the income analysis from the ground up, apply market-standard underwriting constraints, calculate the maximum supportable loan under each scenario, and then quantify the gap created by the impairment at issue.

Income Analysis. The subject property was a 180-unit multifamily asset in lease-up. I analyzed in-place rent rolls, current occupancy, and comparable market rents drawn from active leases at competing properties in the same submarket. Because the property was not stabilized, I applied a lender-standard stabilized occupancy assumption of 93% and used the market rent supported by comparables rather than any above-market asking rents the developer had projected. In the defect-affected scenario, I applied a haircut to effective gross income to reflect documented concessions the property had been forced to offer to induce tenants to occupy units with known deficiencies, as well as increased vacancy assumptions reflecting units that had been taken offline for remediation. The defect-affected effective gross income came in at approximately $2.85 million annually, compared to a stabilized, defect-free gross income of approximately $3.15 million on the same unit mix at market rents without concession pressure.

Expense Analysis and NOI. Operating expenses for a Jacksonville multifamily asset of this vintage and profile typically run in the range of 38% to 43% of effective gross income when property management, insurance, taxes, and maintenance reserves are fully loaded. I applied 41% in both scenarios, though I noted in my report that the defect-affected scenario would likely carry higher maintenance expenses on an ongoing basis until remediation was complete, which would push that ratio higher still. Defect-free NOI: approximately $1.86 million. Defect-affected NOI: approximately $1.68 million.

DSCR and Loan Sizing. Conventional agency lenders, including Fannie Mae DUS and Freddie Mac Program Plus lenders active in Jacksonville, apply a minimum DSCR of 1.25x for standard multifamily transactions. Life company lenders in this market also use 1.25x as a floor, with some applying 1.30x for properties with any lease-up or asset quality uncertainty. I used 1.25x as the applicable threshold, consistent with market standard for a stabilized multifamily transaction in this tier of the Jacksonville market. For the interest rate assumption, I used the 10-year Treasury plus a spread consistent with agency multifamily pricing at the time of the financing event at issue: a coupon of approximately 6.85% on a 30-year amortization schedule, producing a debt constant of roughly 7.85 cents per dollar of loan balance annually.

Applying these inputs: the defect-free NOI of $1.86 million, divided by 1.25x DSCR, produces maximum annual debt service of $1.488 million. Dividing by the debt constant of 0.0785 yields a maximum supportable loan of approximately $18.95 million. Against a stabilized value of approximately $33.0 million (at a 5.65% cap rate on defect-free NOI), that represents a loan-to-value ratio of approximately 57%, well within the 65% to 70% LTV ceiling that agency lenders apply to this asset type in this market.

In the defect-affected scenario, the NOI of $1.68 million, divided by 1.25x, produces maximum annual debt service of $1.344 million, and at the same debt constant, a maximum supportable loan of approximately $17.12 million. The defect-affected value, capitalized at a wider 6.10% cap rate to reflect the risk premium lenders and investors assign to an asset with known unresolved defects, was approximately $27.5 million. The LTV on the defect-affected loan ($17.12 million divided by $27.5 million) comes to approximately 62%, which appears within the LTV ceiling, but this comparison is misleading because the loan is smaller in absolute dollars against a diminished value base. The financing impairment, measured purely in loan proceeds, is approximately $1.83 million. When the value reduction is factored in, the equity impairment is substantially larger, and both figures are relevant to a damages analysis depending on the theory of harm being advanced.

The Complicating Factors

Lender Underwriting of Known Construction Defects. This is the central complication, and it operates at multiple levels. When a lender becomes aware that a subject property is the subject of active construction defect litigation, the credit committee response is almost universally to require a full engineering assessment before issuing any loan commitment. This is not a negotiating position; it is a credit policy requirement at every institutional lender I work with. The engineer must quantify the scope of the defects, the estimated remediation cost, and the timeline for completion. Until that assessment is complete and remediation is either funded in escrow or contractually committed, the property does not underwrite as a stabilized asset. It underwrites as a transitional asset with construction risk, which triggers a different lender category and a materially different pricing structure.

Reserve and Escrow Requirements. Even if a lender is willing to fund a loan on a defect-affected property, they will require a funded repair reserve equal to 110% to 125% of the engineer's estimated remediation cost. On a project with significant envelope and waterproofing defects, remediation estimates can range from $1.5 million to well over $5 million depending on scope and unit count. That reserve requirement comes directly out of net loan proceeds, further reducing the effective liquidity available to the borrower at closing. In many of the situations I have analyzed, this reserve requirement is what converts a transaction from feasible to infeasible, because the net proceeds after reserves are insufficient to retire the construction loan.

Construction Loan Maturity Pressure. In a construction defect scenario involving a property still in lease-up, the borrower is almost always operating under time pressure from the construction lender. Construction lenders in Jacksonville, as elsewhere, are not permanent capital providers. Their loans mature, typically at 24 to 36 months, with limited extension options. If the construction defects prevent a takeout refinance from closing before loan maturity, the borrower faces a default risk on the construction loan that can cascade into a much larger set of legal and financial consequences. This maturity pressure is a direct, quantifiable harm that I address explicitly in my expert analysis because it affects both the financing feasibility and the damages timeline.

Jacksonville Insurance Market Considerations. Florida's property insurance market is a complicating factor for virtually any CRE financing in this state, and it is particularly acute for multifamily assets with known waterproofing deficiencies. Insurers in Florida are increasingly exclusion-heavy on moisture and mold-related claims, and a property with documented water intrusion may face either coverage exclusions, premium surcharges, or outright declination from standard market carriers. Lenders require evidence of acceptable property and casualty coverage as a condition of loan commitment. If the defects have triggered insurance complications, that creates a secondary barrier to financing that operates independently of the income and DSCR analysis.

The Jacksonville Lender Market

Jacksonville benefits from a reasonably deep lender market across asset classes, but that market stratifies quickly when construction defects or litigation clouds enter the picture. Understanding which lenders will and will not participate is central to a financing feasibility opinion.

Agency Lenders (Fannie Mae DUS and Freddie Mac Program Plus). These are the dominant permanent capital providers for stabilized multifamily in Jacksonville, offering the most competitive long-term fixed-rate execution. However, agency guidelines are explicit: properties with unresolved litigation or open construction defect claims are generally ineligible for agency financing until the litigation is resolved and the repairs are complete. This is not a discretionary underwriting call. It is a program eligibility restriction. In a construction defect matter, agency financing is typically off the table until resolution, which means the borrower cannot access the deepest and most liquid capital source in the market.

Life Insurance Companies. Life companies have been active permanent lenders in Jacksonville's multifamily and industrial markets, particularly for institutional-quality assets. They share the agency lenders' aversion to open litigation but have more flexibility in structuring around it if the defects are minor, well-documented, and the remediation is fully funded. For properties with significant structural defects, most life company credit committees will decline until resolution. Their pricing, when they do participate, tends to run 20 to 35 basis points inside CMBS for equivalent leverage.

CMBS Lenders. CMBS execution in Jacksonville is available but is typically not the first call for multifamily. For retail or mixed-use assets, CMBS can be a viable option, but the defeasance and special servicer mechanics create complications if the defect litigation drags on post-closing. CMBS conduit underwriters will also require clean title and no open construction litigation as conditions of commitment.

Community and Regional Banks. Jacksonville has solid community bank depth, including several Florida-chartered institutions with meaningful commercial real estate portfolios and local market knowledge. These lenders have more flexibility than agency or CMBS programs and may be willing to fund a loan on a defect-affected property if the reserves are adequate, the engineering is thorough, and the relationship supports it. Their pricing will reflect the risk: expect spreads 100 to 150 basis points wider than agency equivalent, shorter amortization, and covenant packages that include remediation milestones. This is the lender category most likely to bridge a borrower through a construction defect resolution, but not without meaningful cost and structural complexity.

Debt Funds and Bridge Lenders. For properties that cannot qualify for bank or agency financing due to the severity of the defects or the open litigation, debt funds represent the lender of last resort. Jacksonville-area deals have accessed bridge debt at coupons ranging from SOFR plus 350 to SOFR plus 550 in recent markets, with origination fees, exit fees, and interest rate caps that further increase the all-in cost of capital. Maximum LTV for a bridge lender on a defect-affected asset is typically 60% to 65% of as-is value, not as-stabilized value, which results in substantially reduced proceeds.

What Attorneys Handling Jacksonville CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to Retain a CRE Financing Expert. If the damages theory in your case involves any claim that a property was unable to refinance, could not obtain a construction takeout loan, lost equity as a result of financing impairment, or was forced into a less favorable capital structure due to a third party's conduct, you need a CRE financing expert. This is not a role an appraiser fills, because appraisers determine value; they do not opine on whether a specific loan was achievable at a specific point in time under specific market conditions. That question requires someone who actively places commercial loans and has direct knowledge of what lenders were actually doing in that market during the relevant period.

What a CRE Financing Expert Can Answer That an Appraiser Cannot. A CRE financing expert can testify to the specific underwriting criteria that would have applied to this loan at this time; whether the property would have cleared the lender's credit policy; what the actual maximum loan proceeds would have been; how the defects affected DSCR and LTV in the lender's model, not just the appraiser's model; what reserve requirements a lender would have imposed; and what the cost differential was between the financing the borrower could obtain versus the financing they would have obtained absent the impairment. These are capital markets opinions, not valuation opinions, and the distinction matters at trial.

Qualifications That Matter. A qualified CRE financing expert is an active practitioner who currently places commercial loans with a broad lender network. Retired bankers can speak to how one institution made credit decisions; they typically cannot speak to what the broader market would have done. At CLS CRE, our firm closes transactions across approximately $1 billion in annual volume, maintains relationships with over 1,000 institutional and private lenders, and operates in all 50 states. That active market presence is what makes our opinions credible and defensible under cross-examination. An expert who has not closed a loan in several years cannot speak credibly to what the 2023 or 2024 lender market would have required.

Information Counsel Should Gather Before the Engagement. To conduct a thorough financing feasibility analysis, I need: rent rolls and operating statements for the subject property covering the relevant period; any engineering or construction defect reports already in the record; the existing loan documents including the maturity date and extension option language; any lender correspondence related to the loan maturity or payoff; comparable sale and lease data for the submarket; any appraisals already commissioned; and a clear identification of the damages period so I can anchor my analysis to the correct market conditions.

Use at Trial and in Alternative Dispute Resolution. CRE financing expert testimony is most commonly used to establish the financing impairment element of a damages case, to rebut an opposing expert who has minimized the financing impact of a defect or title problem, or to explain to a judge or jury why a real estate transaction could not close. In bankruptcy plan hearings involving commercial real estate debtors, a financing expert can testify about the feasibility of the plan's proposed refinancing and whether the exit financing assumed in the plan is achievable.

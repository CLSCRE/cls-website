Expert Witness: NNN Lease Rollover Upside and Financing Impact in Atlanta, GA

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Below-Market NNN Lease and Rollover Upside Analysis

The Atlanta Market Context

Atlanta has emerged as one of the most consequential commercial real estate markets in the Southeast, and disputes involving single-tenant net lease assets here carry weight precisely because the fundamentals have been moving so fast. The metro absorbed record industrial space through 2021 and 2022, and while industrial vacancy ticked upward through 2023 and into 2024 as new supply caught up with demand, net absorption in core submarkets like Gwinnett County, I-85 South, and the Fulton Industrial corridor has remained positive. Asking rents for industrial product in Atlanta climbed roughly 40 to 60 percent between 2019 and 2023 depending on submarket and building class, which is precisely the environment that produces below-market in-place leases at scale. A tenant that signed a 10-year NNN lease in 2018 or 2019 is almost certainly paying rent that is materially below what the same space would command today, and that gap has real consequences for how lenders size loans against the asset.

For retail NNN product, the picture is different but equally relevant. Atlanta's suburban retail corridors, particularly in the northern suburbs including Alpharetta, Roswell, and the Cumberland-Galleria submarket, have maintained strong occupancy and rental rates, while urban infill retail has been more volatile. Single-tenant net lease retail in Atlanta trades at cap rates generally ranging from the high 4s to the low 6s depending on tenant credit, lease term, and location, with drive-through quick-service and pharmacy assets commanding the tightest pricing. The relevant point for financing disputes is that Atlanta's rent growth trajectory means that a below-market lease creates a compressible income stream today paired with significant rollover upside, and lenders do not treat those two realities symmetrically. The current NOI drives the loan; the future upside does not.

The Engagement

Counsel retained Commercial Lending Solutions to serve as a financing expert in a dispute involving a single-tenant industrial property in the Atlanta metropolitan area. The asset was a freestanding distribution and light-manufacturing facility occupied by a single tenant under a long-term NNN lease. The tenant had been in occupancy for several years and was paying rent that both parties acknowledged was below current market. The lease had a defined remaining term with no contractual rent bumps sufficient to close the gap to market during the remaining lease period.

The dispute centered on a financing contingency and a claim that the property could or could not support a loan at a specific loan-to-value ratio on a specific timeline. One party contended the rollover upside, meaning the rent increase achievable upon lease expiration, should be factored into lender underwriting in a manner that would increase the supportable loan size. The other party argued that lenders would underwrite only to in-place income and that the below-market lease structure created a financing constraint that was real, current, and not addressable through market-rate arguments about future rents.

Counsel's specific questions to our firm were: How do commercial mortgage lenders in Atlanta actually underwrite a below-market NNN lease? Does anticipated lease rollover to market rates affect loan sizing, and if so under what conditions and with what lender types? What is the maximum supportable loan against the subject property's in-place income at market-standard DSCR and LTV thresholds? And does the lease structure, as written, preclude refinancing at the LTV percentage cited in the dispute? These are financing questions, not valuation questions, and they require a different kind of expert than an appraiser.

The Financial Analysis

The starting point for any CRE financing analysis of a NNN asset is the in-place net operating income. For a single-tenant NNN property, this is relatively clean: the tenant pays taxes, insurance, and maintenance, so the landlord's NOI is essentially equal to the base rent, minus any landlord-side expenses such as management fees or reserves that a lender requires in its underwriting model. For this engagement, we worked with the in-place base rent figure from the executed lease, applied a vacancy reserve consistent with lender underwriting standards for single-tenant assets (typically 5 percent for credit tenants, higher for non-investment-grade or unrated tenants), and subtracted a management fee that lenders routinely apply even on NNN assets, typically in the range of 2 to 3 percent of effective gross income.

For this analysis, assume an in-place annual base rent of $780,000. After applying a 5 percent vacancy reserve and a 3 percent management fee, the lender-underwritten NOI comes to approximately $717,600. This is the income figure that drives the loan size. Market rent for comparable space in the relevant Atlanta submarket at the time of our analysis was approximately $1,050,000 annually on a NNN basis, meaning the in-place rent represented a roughly 26 percent discount to market. That gap is the rollover upside. It is real, it is documentable, and it is completely irrelevant to the loan sizing calculation under standard lender underwriting methodology.

Here is why: lenders size commercial mortgage loans using a debt service coverage ratio test applied to in-place income. The market standard DSCR for a stabilized single-tenant NNN asset with a non-investment-grade or unrated tenant is 1.25x. For a strong investment-grade credit tenant (think nationally recognized, publicly rated), lenders may underwrite to 1.20x. For the purposes of this analysis, we applied 1.25x as the controlling DSCR threshold because the tenant in question did not carry a published investment-grade credit rating.

Using a 30-year amortization schedule and an interest rate assumption of 7.25 percent, which was consistent with market pricing for 5-year and 7-year fixed-rate commercial mortgage loans in the relevant time period in Atlanta, the maximum annual debt service supportable by the underwritten NOI of $717,600 at 1.25x DSCR is $574,080. At 7.25 percent on a 30-year amortization, $574,080 in annual debt service corresponds to a loan balance of approximately $7,650,000. This is the maximum supportable loan based on the income constraint alone.

The LTV constraint then applies as an independent ceiling. Most conventional commercial mortgage lenders, including CMBS conduits and balance sheet banks active in Atlanta, will lend no more than 65 to 70 percent of appraised value on a single-tenant NNN asset with below-market rent and an unrated tenant. If the appraised value of the property in this dispute was, for example, $13,500,000, the LTV ceiling at 65 percent is $8,775,000. At 70 percent, it is $9,450,000. In this scenario, the income constraint of $7,650,000 is the binding constraint, not the LTV ceiling. The loan is income-constrained. The borrower cannot get to a higher LTV even if the appraised value supports it, because the in-place NOI does not generate sufficient debt service coverage at that loan amount.

This is a critical analytical point for the dispute: the claim that rollover upside expands the loan does not survive scrutiny when the loan is income-constrained rather than collateral-constrained. A lender will not increase a loan from $7,650,000 to $9,000,000 because market rent is higher than in-place rent. The lender underwrites the lease as executed, not the lease as it might be renegotiated in three to five years. The loan proceeds are limited by what the property actually produces today, not what it could produce.

The Complicating Factors

Below-Market Rent and Lender Risk Perception. Lenders view below-market leases on single-tenant assets through two lenses simultaneously. On one hand, a tenant paying below-market rent has an economic incentive to remain in place, which reduces rollover risk. On the other hand, the lender is underwriting to a rent level that, if the tenant defaults, cannot be replaced at the same rent from a new tenant on day one. In a below-market scenario, the replacement rent would theoretically be higher, but lenders do not underwrite to replacement rent. They underwrite to the contractual income stream. This distinction is not academic. It is the difference between a loan that closes and one that does not.

Rollover Risk and Loan Maturity Alignment. One of the most important structural questions in financing a below-market NNN lease is whether the lease expiration falls within the loan term or beyond it. If a 7-year loan is placed on an asset with 4 years of lease term remaining, the lender faces lease rollover risk during the loan period. That is a materially different risk profile than a lease with 12 years of remaining term that extends well beyond the loan's balloon date. In the Atlanta dispute, the remaining lease term and its relationship to the loan term was a central issue. Lenders in Atlanta and nationwide will either price for rollover risk within the loan term through higher rates and lower proceeds, require cash flow sweeps or leasing reserves, or decline to lend altogether on single-tenant assets with near-term lease expiration.

Debt Yield as an Additional Constraint. Increasingly, CMBS lenders and institutional balance sheet lenders apply a debt yield test as a third constraint, in addition to DSCR and LTV. Debt yield is calculated as NOI divided by loan amount, and lenders typically require a minimum debt yield of 8.5 to 9.5 percent on NNN single-tenant product. Using our underwritten NOI of $717,600 and a target loan of $7,650,000, the debt yield is 9.38 percent, which clears the threshold. However, if a party were arguing for a higher loan amount, say $9,000,000, the debt yield drops to 7.97 percent, which falls below most institutional lenders' minimums. This is a second independent mechanism that constrains loan proceeds regardless of appraised value.

Tenant Credit and Lender Appetite. The financing universe for a single-tenant NNN asset in Atlanta narrows substantially when the tenant does not carry an investment-grade rating. National CMBS conduits, life insurance companies, and agency lenders all apply stricter standards to unrated or sub-investment-grade tenants, including lower LTV ceilings and higher DSCR requirements. For the subject property, this meant that certain lender categories available to a comparable asset with an investment-grade tenant were effectively unavailable, reducing competition and constraining pricing. In an expert witness context, demonstrating the financing universe available to the specific asset, as opposed to the theoretical universe, is essential to an honest analysis.

The Atlanta Lender Market

Atlanta benefits from a deep and competitive commercial lending market relative to most southeastern cities. The presence of major regional banks headquartered in Atlanta and the strong activity of national banks, CMBS conduits, and insurance company lenders means that well-structured deals have real options. However, complexity and credit challenges narrow that universe quickly.

National CMBS Conduits. Multiple CMBS conduits are active in Atlanta across industrial and retail NNN product. For a clean, investment-grade, long-lease NNN deal, CMBS is typically the most aggressive lender on proceeds, willing to go to 70 to 75 percent LTV on credit-tenanted assets. For below-market leases with unrated tenants and shorter remaining lease terms, CMBS either steps back or requires significant structural enhancements including upfront leasing reserves, cash management lockboxes, and springing cash sweeps triggered by DSCR deterioration. CMBS pricing in Atlanta for NNN industrial product in the relevant period ranged from approximately 175 to 250 basis points over comparable-term Treasuries.

Community and Regional Banks. Atlanta has a robust community and regional bank presence, and these lenders are often the most pragmatic underwriters for single-tenant NNN assets with complexity. They typically lend to 65 percent LTV on a 5-year term with a 25-year amortization, require personal recourse or partial guaranty for loans below investment-grade quality, and price at 25 to 75 basis points above comparable CMBS execution. Their DSCR threshold is typically 1.25x, consistent with the analysis above. The advantage of community and regional banks in Atlanta is relationship flexibility; the disadvantage is lower maximum proceeds and full or partial recourse.

Debt Funds and Bridge Lenders. For assets with near-term lease rollover, meaning a lease expiring within the loan term, conventional lenders often decline, and the financing solution shifts to debt funds and bridge lenders willing to underwrite the rollover upside. These lenders will lend against a business plan, not just in-place income, but they price accordingly. Bridge execution in Atlanta for a NNN industrial asset with rollover upside and lease-up risk would typically carry an all-in rate of 350 to 500 basis points over SOFR, with loan fees of 1 to 2 percent, lower starting LTV of 55 to 65 percent, and a short initial term of 2 to 3 years with extension options tied to leasing milestones. This is a materially different and more expensive capital structure than conventional financing, and that difference is itself a component of damages or remedy analysis in a financing dispute.

Life Insurance Companies. Life company lenders are active in Atlanta for NNN product, but they have the narrowest credit box of any lender category. They require long remaining lease terms (typically 10-plus years of term certain beyond the loan maturity), investment-grade or near-investment-grade tenants, and low LTV, typically 55 to 65 percent. For a below-market lease with an unrated tenant, life company capital is not available. Establishing that life company execution was unavailable to the specific asset is an important component of defining the actual financing universe in an expert analysis.

What Attorneys Handling Atlanta CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to Retain a CRE Financing Expert. Attorneys handling financing contingency disputes, lender liability claims, breach of loan commitment cases, bankruptcy plan feasibility proceedings, and partnership or JV disputes involving refinancing should retain a CRE financing expert whenever the question turns on whether a specific loan was possible, at what proceeds level, under what conditions, and in what timeframe. These questions are distinct from value questions. An appraiser can tell you what a property is worth. A CRE financing expert can tell you what a lender would actually lend against it, which is not always the same thing.

What a CRE Financing Expert Answers That an Appraiser Cannot. Appraisers apply valuation methodology, including income capitalization, sales comparison, and cost approaches, to establish market value. They do not opine on DSCR constraints, debt yield thresholds, lender credit boxes, the actual lender market for a specific asset type in a specific market at a specific point in time, or the structural requirements that particular lender categories impose. A CRE financing expert with active deal-making relationships, like the team at Commercial Lending Solutions with over 1,000 lender relationships and approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, can testify from current market knowledge, not academic frameworks.

What Makes an Expert Qualified. The relevant credential is active engagement in the commercial mortgage market, not a retired banker who has not closed a deal in five years. Markets move. DSCR thresholds, debt yield floors, LTV standards, and lender appetite for specific asset types and tenant profiles change materially over short periods. An expert who testifies about what a lender would have done in 2022 needs to have been actually placing loans in 2022, talking to lenders, receiving term sheets, and closing transactions. Look for experts who can point to actual deal experience in the relevant market, relevant asset type, and relevant time period, not just a long career in banking or finance generally.

Information to Gather Before the Engagement. Before retaining a CRE financing expert, counsel should secure the executed lease (including all amendments, assignments, and rent schedules), the rent roll, any appraisals or broker opinions of value, prior loan documents or term sheets, any correspondence between the borrower and lenders, the operating history of the property (actual NOI for prior 2 to 3 years), and any underwriting packages previously submitted to lenders. If prior lender rejections or term sheets exist, those documents are highly material. They constitute real-world market evidence of the financing universe, not just expert inference.

Use of Expert Testimony. In trial, a CRE financing expert can walk a judge or jury through the mechanics of commercial mortgage underwriting using demonstrative exhibits showing the DSCR calculation, the LTV ceiling, and the resulting maximum loan. In a bankruptcy plan confirmation hearing, the expert can opine on whether a proposed plan is feasible given the refinancing assumptions embedded in it. In a breach of financing contingency dispute, the expert can establish whether the loan described in the contingency was achievable in the market as of the relevant date. This testimony is concrete, numerical, and grounded in current market practice, which makes it both accessible and difficult to undermine with generalities.

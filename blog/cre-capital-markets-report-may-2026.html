Commercial Real Estate Capital Markets Report: May 2026 Treasury Yields and Deal Activity

By Trevor Damyan May 04, 2026

Every month we publish a snapshot of where the capital markets actually stand, not where the headlines suggest they should be. The May 2026 edition covers US Treasury yield curve movement, month-over-month rate comparisons, and the deal-level implications for borrowers actively pursuing commercial mortgage financing.

US Treasury Yield Curve: Month-Over-Month Comparison

The most important story in the May 2026 report is the shape and direction of the US Treasury yield curve. After a period of elevated short-term rates driven by Federal Reserve policy, the curve is showing meaningful movement across key benchmark maturities. Borrowers who have been sitting on the sidelines waiting for a definitive rate pivot need to understand what the curve is currently signaling and why waiting any longer may cost more than acting now.

The month-over-month comparison captured in this report shows how each tenor has shifted from April to May 2026. Changes at the 2-year, 5-year, 7-year, and 10-year points on the curve directly affect how lenders price fixed-rate commercial mortgages, construction loans, and bridge-to-permanent transactions. When the long end of the curve moves independently of the short end, as it has been doing, spreads compress or widen depending on which side of the risk spectrum your deal sits on.

For deals indexed to the 5-year Treasury, any downward drift in that tenor translates directly into better pricing on agency-eligible multifamily loans and small-balance commercial products. For deals benchmarked to the 10-year, life insurance companies and CMBS conduits are the primary execution options, and both have been actively adjusting their spread requirements as the long end fluctuates.

What the Yield Curve Shape Means for Commercial Mortgage Borrowers

A steeper yield curve, where long-term rates sit meaningfully above short-term rates, typically favors borrowers who are willing to lock in fixed-rate debt at the long end. Right now the curve structure is creating a window for borrowers to capture fixed-rate financing at levels that pencil better than they did at this time last year.

From a deal structuring perspective, the yield curve shape also matters for floating-rate bridge loans. Most private bridge lenders are pricing off SOFR plus a spread, and SOFR remains sensitive to Fed policy expectations. If the market is pricing in any rate cuts over the next 12 to 18 months, borrowers holding short-term floating-rate debt stand to benefit from lower all-in costs as those cuts materialize. That said, lenders are also tightening credit boxes for transitional assets, so the entry underwriting has to be clean regardless of where rates are headed.

Lender Appetite and Deal Flow in May 2026

Across the CLS CRE pipeline, lender conversations in May 2026 are reflecting a market that is more active than Q1 but still highly selective. A regional bank recently closed a stabilized office refinance at a fixed rate that would have been unachievable six months ago, driven entirely by the borrower's ability to demonstrate strong in-place cash flow and low rollover risk. Execution like that does not happen unless the lender has genuine confidence in the collateral, the market, and the sponsorship.

A private bridge lender we work with regularly has been increasing its appetite for multifamily value-add transactions in secondary markets, provided the sponsor has a clear and documented exit strategy. The key threshold for that lender is a loan-to-cost ratio that leaves sufficient cushion for construction contingencies and lease-up variance. Deals that hit their underwriting criteria are getting term sheets within five business days, which is a meaningful improvement over the 10-to-15-day timelines we were seeing in late 2025.

A life insurance company active in our network has been allocating heavily into industrial and necessity-based retail in Q2 2026, with 10-year fixed rates that reflect their appetite for long-duration assets in those sectors. Their spreads over the 10-year Treasury have held relatively steady even as the underlying index has moved, which tells us they are committed to deploying capital this cycle rather than retreating to the sidelines.

Rate Lock Strategy: Why Timing the Market Is a Losing Game

One of the most common mistakes we see in the current environment is borrowers trying to time the rate lock. The Treasury yield curve shifts daily, and the spread that a lender quotes today reflects their own cost of funds, portfolio concentration limits, and competitive positioning, none of which you can predict with certainty by watching the 10-year move tick by tick.

The better approach is to have your financing structured and ready so that when market conditions align with your deal's needs, you can move. That means having your financial package organized, your property's operating history documented, and a clear narrative around the business plan before you reach out to lenders. Deals that arrive at a lender's desk fully packaged get better pricing and faster closes. That is not anecdotal, it is a consistent pattern across every deal cycle we have operated in.

The May 2026 yield curve data reinforces that there are legitimate opportunities in the market right now for the right assets with the right sponsors. Whether you are refinancing a stabilized income-producing property, acquiring a transitional asset, or closing out a bridge loan and seeking permanent financing, the execution environment is more favorable than the broader macro noise suggests.

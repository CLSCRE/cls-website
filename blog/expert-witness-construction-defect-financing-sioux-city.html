Expert Witness: How Construction Defects Affect CRE Financing in Sioux City, IA

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Construction Defect Impact on Financing

The Sioux City Market Context

Sioux City, Iowa sits at the convergence of three states and two major rivers, which makes it a genuine regional commercial hub rather than a secondary market that simply mirrors national trends. The metro area supports a diversified economic base anchored by food processing and agribusiness manufacturing, healthcare, logistics, and a growing professional services sector. That industrial and logistics orientation is not incidental. It shapes the entire capital markets environment for commercial real estate in this corridor, including how lenders underwrite assets, what vacancy assumptions they apply, and how construction quality failures translate into measurable financial damage.

Industrial and flex properties in the Sioux City submarket have posted vacancy rates in the 4% to 7% range in recent years, reflecting sustained regional demand driven by agricultural supply chain activity along the I-29 corridor and proximity to the Sioux Gateway Airport industrial zone. Net asking rents for Class B industrial product have hovered in the $4.50 to $6.50 per square foot range on a triple-net basis, depending on clear height, dock configuration, and condition. Multifamily fundamentals have been similarly resilient, with stabilized cap rates in the 6.25% to 7.25% range for well-located, well-maintained workforce housing product, though distressed or deferred-maintenance assets price at considerably wider spreads. Retail is bifurcated, as it is nationally, with grocery-anchored and service-oriented neighborhood centers holding occupancy while secondary retail strips face meaningful headwinds.

What distinguishes Sioux City from larger Iowa markets like Des Moines is the relative depth of the community and regional bank lender base versus institutional capital. Agency execution (Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, HUD) is available for qualifying multifamily product, but for most commercial asset types, the financing market is dominated by Iowa-chartered community banks, regional banks headquartered in the broader Midwest, USDA Business and Industry loan programs for qualifying rural-adjacent deals, and SBA 504 or 7(a) structures for owner-occupied commercial real estate. Life company and CMBS executions are available but require scale, stabilization, and credit quality that many Sioux City assets do not achieve. This lender profile matters enormously in a construction defect context because community and regional bank lenders have less standardized tolerance for defect-encumbered collateral than institutional lenders who operate from written credit policy manuals.

The Engagement

Counsel representing the property owner in a construction defect dispute retained Commercial Lending Solutions to provide expert analysis addressing a specific and financially material set of questions: how do the alleged construction defects affect the property's current and prospective financing feasibility, what would institutional and non-institutional lenders require before extending credit secured by a property with known, documented construction defects, and how does the defect condition alter the maximum supportable loan amount compared to what an equivalent, defect-free asset of the same type and location would support?

The subject asset was a mid-sized light industrial and warehouse facility located in the Sioux City metropolitan area, built within the prior five years and intended for multi-tenant or single-tenant net lease occupancy. The alleged defects involved structural and envelope-related failures, specifically documented issues with the building's foundation system, wall panel connections, and roofing assembly that had been identified through a third-party forensic engineering investigation. The defects were not cosmetic. They affected the structural integrity of the building envelope and raised questions about long-term habitability, code compliance, and the property's insurability under standard commercial property coverage.

The legal questions our engagement was designed to illuminate were threefold. First, does the presence of known, unresolved construction defects create a financing impairment that would not be reflected in a standard appraisal? Second, if so, what is the magnitude of that impairment expressed in terms of maximum loan proceeds, loan-to-value reduction, and debt service coverage constraints? Third, what remediation standard would lenders require before funding, and what does that remediation cost imply about the economic damage attributable to the defects?

CLS CRE was retained to provide a Rule 26-compliant expert report and to be available for deposition testimony. Our analysis drew on our firm's approximately $1 billion in transaction volume experience, relationships with more than 1,000 lenders across 50 states, and specific knowledge of the Iowa and Sioux City regional lender market.

The Financial Analysis

The financial methodology in this engagement required constructing two parallel underwriting models: one representing the property in its alleged defect-free condition as originally designed and specified, and one representing the property in its actual, defect-present condition as documented by the engineering report. The gap between the two models, expressed in dollars of maximum supportable loan proceeds, represents the financing impairment attributable to the defects.

Baseline NOI Construction: For the defect-free scenario, we analyzed the property's stabilized net operating income based on market rents for comparable light industrial product in the Sioux City submarket. Using a rentable square footage of approximately 48,000 square feet at a market rent of $5.75 per square foot net, annual gross rental income at stabilization was approximately $276,000. Applying a 5% vacancy and credit loss allowance consistent with submarket conditions and a $0.35 per square foot management fee on a triple-net structure, effective gross income was approximately $249,000. With minimal owner-side operating expenses under a NNN structure, the stabilized NOI for the defect-free asset came to approximately $244,000 annually.

Defect-Adjusted NOI: In the defect-present condition, the analysis required adjustments for three categories of income and expense impact. First, a prospective tenant rent concession or discount reflecting a tenant's rational awareness of structural uncertainty and potential business interruption exposure: market evidence supports a 10% to 15% reduction in achievable rent for a property with unresolved structural defects. Second, an elevated vacancy assumption of 12% to 15% reflecting the realistic difficulty of leasing or re-leasing space in a building under litigation-associated cloud. Third, an incremental operating expense load for ongoing monitoring, insurance surcharges, and legal compliance costs associated with the defect condition. Under these adjustments, the defect-adjusted NOI fell to approximately $193,000, a reduction of approximately $51,000 or roughly 21% from the defect-free baseline.

DSCR and Debt Yield Constraints: For light industrial product in a non-gateway Midwest market like Sioux City, conventional lenders apply a minimum DSCR of 1.25x at the stressed underwritten NOI. Some community bank lenders will shade to 1.20x for well-known borrowers with strong deposit relationships, but 1.25x is the appropriate market standard for this analysis. Using a 7.25% all-in interest rate on a 25-year amortization schedule, which reflects pricing consistent with regional bank and community bank execution in the current rate environment for this asset type and market, the maximum annual debt service supportable at 1.25x DSCR breaks down as follows:

  • Defect-free NOI: $244,000 divided by 1.25x DSCR yields maximum supportable annual debt service of $195,200, which capitalizes to a maximum loan of approximately $2,710,000 at a 7.25% rate on 25-year amortization.
  • Defect-adjusted NOI: $193,000 divided by 1.25x DSCR yields maximum supportable annual debt service of $154,400, which capitalizes to a maximum loan of approximately $2,142,000 under the same terms.
  • DSCR-driven loan impairment: The difference between these two figures is approximately $568,000 in lost loan proceeds attributable directly to the NOI impairment caused by the defect condition.

LTV Constraints: DSCR analysis alone does not capture the full financing impairment. Lenders also apply LTV caps, and for commercial industrial product in secondary Midwest markets, the standard LTV ceiling is 70% to 75% of appraised value for conventional financing. For a property with documented, unresolved structural defects, lenders will either decline to lend entirely or apply a significant collateral haircut, often reducing to 60% LTV or lower, reflecting the collateral risk premium. If the defect-free as-stabilized value was established at $3,600,000 by the appraiser and the defect-present market value was determined to be $2,900,000 reflecting the market's pricing of the structural risk, the LTV-constrained loan at 65% of the defect-present value would be $1,885,000, which is actually the binding constraint below the DSCR-constrained figure. This means the LTV impairment is even larger than the income-driven impairment: approximately $825,000 in lost loan proceeds relative to what the defect-free asset would support at 70% LTV on the higher value.

The Complicating Factors

Lender Title and Insurance Requirements: Before a commercial lender funds a loan on any property with known construction defects, the lender's counsel will require a clear chain of title and satisfactory title insurance. A property that is the subject of active mechanics lien claims, contractor disputes, or surety bond litigation presents a title condition that most lenders will not insure around. Even after defect litigation resolves, if the resolution involves recorded liens or judgments that are not fully released of record, the title condition impairs lender willingness and loan structuring flexibility. In Sioux City's community bank lender environment, where relationship underwriting is more common than automated credit scoring, the practical effect is that loan approvals are delayed or conditioned on full resolution and title clearance, meaning the borrower cannot access refinancing capital during the pendency of the dispute.

Property Insurance and Force-Placed Coverage Risk: Lenders require borrowers to maintain standard commercial property insurance with the lender named as loss payee and additional insured. A property with documented structural defects, particularly envelope and foundation failures, may face exclusions from standard policy coverage for losses arising from the known defect condition. Some insurers will decline to renew or will issue coverage with specific exclusions. If coverage lapses or becomes materially deficient, the lender is entitled to force-place insurance at the borrower's expense, which is both costly and a technical default trigger under most commercial loan agreements. This is not a theoretical concern; in practice, the insurer's response to a forensic engineering report identifying structural failures is one of the first practical financing consequences of a construction defect dispute.

Environmental and Code Compliance Overlaps: In industrial properties, construction defect issues sometimes intersect with building code compliance failures that, if identified during a lender's Phase I or construction quality review, become independent grounds for financing conditions or denial. Iowa building code enforcement at the municipal level in Sioux City involves Woodbury County and city building department coordination, and a structure with documented foundation or structural deficiencies may trigger a requirement for engineered repair certification before a certificate of occupancy is reissued or a new lender will fund. This adds both time and cost to the path back to financeable condition.

Appraisal Methodology Gaps: Standard commercial appraisals, even when competently prepared, are not designed to capture the financing feasibility dimension of a defect-impaired property. An appraiser will typically note the defect condition and apply a value reduction, but the appraisal will not opine on whether any lender in the market would actually lend on the property, at what terms, under what conditions, or what the gap in loan proceeds is relative to a defect-free comparable. This is the specific analytical gap that a CRE financing expert fills, and it is distinct from the appraiser's function.

The Sioux City Lender Market

Understanding which lenders are realistically available for a given Sioux City commercial asset is essential to quantifying financing impairment. The market stratifies into several categories, each with distinct credit parameters and defect tolerance.

Community Banks (Iowa-Chartered): These are the most active lenders for commercial real estate in Sioux City for assets below $5 million in value. They typically lend up to 75% LTV on stabilized industrial and multifamily product, require 1.20x to 1.25x DSCR, and price at spreads of 200 to 300 basis points over the 5-year FHLB rate or equivalent index. Their credit approval process is relationship-driven and committee-based. On a property with known construction defects, a community bank lender will typically require: a completed remediation with third-party engineering sign-off, a revised appraisal post-remediation, and often a personal guarantee from the borrower that exceeds what they would require on a clean asset. Many will simply decline to lend pending full resolution.

Regional Banks (Iowa, Nebraska, South Dakota footprint): Regional banks active in the Sioux City corridor generally have more standardized credit policies than community banks and will follow written construction defect protocols that may require environmental indemnity, engineering certifications, and escrow holdbacks for remediation costs. Their LTV tolerance on impaired collateral is typically 60% to 65%, and they will price a risk premium of 50 to 100 basis points above their standard rate for assets with any residual defect uncertainty.

USDA Business and Industry Program: For qualifying commercial projects in rural-adjacent Iowa markets, the USDA B&I guarantee program can provide up to 80% loan guarantee, which effectively de-risks the lender and can unlock financing that would otherwise be unavailable. However, USDA B&I requires the lender to certify the adequacy of the collateral, which means a property with unresolved structural defects will not qualify until remediation is complete and documented.

CMBS and Life Company: These sources are functionally unavailable for Sioux City industrial properties with construction defect issues. CMBS requires clean title, standard insurability, and no material pending litigation. Life company lenders are even more conservative on collateral quality. These executions represent the ceiling of the financing market for Sioux City commercial real estate, not the floor, and a defect-impaired property does not access this tier regardless of sponsor quality.

What Attorneys Handling Sioux City CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to Retain a CRE Financing Expert: Counsel should engage a commercial real estate financing expert at the outset of any matter where the plaintiff's damages include lost financing capacity, increased cost of capital, inability to refinance or sell, or loan default attributable to property condition. An appraiser can tell the court what the property is worth. A CRE financing expert tells the court whether the property is financeable, at what terms, from which lender universe, and what the gap is between what the property should support and what it actually supports given the impairment. These are different and complementary analyses, and conflating them is a common and costly error.

What the Expert Can Answer That an Appraiser Cannot: A financing expert can testify to: the specific lender credit requirements that would apply to the property in its defect-present condition; the identifiable categories of lenders who would decline the credit and why; the pricing premium or loan proceeds reduction a lender would impose; the DSCR and LTV calculations showing maximum supportable loan under both conditions; and the remediation standard that lenders require before funding. None of these questions fall within the scope of a standard appraisal engagement, and an appraiser offering opinions on lender underwriting standards is likely outside their area of qualified expertise.

What Makes a Financing Expert Qualified: Qualification is not simply a matter of having been a banker or having closed some loans. An effective CRE financing expert should have active, current engagement with the lending market, meaning ongoing loan origination activity and current relationships with lenders in the relevant geographic and asset-type market. The expert should understand how lenders actually underwrite commercial real estate today, including current DSCR floors, LTV policies, debt yield requirements, and defect-related credit conditions. At CLS CRE, our active origination business across 50 states and more than 1,000 lender relationships means we testify from current market knowledge, not historical recollection.

Information Counsel Should Gather Before the Engagement: To allow a financing expert to provide a complete and defensible analysis, counsel should assemble: the complete forensic engineering report identifying defects; the property's historical and projected rent rolls and operating statements; any existing loan documents, appraisals, or lender correspondence; all insurance policies and any insurer correspondence regarding coverage adjustments; and any communications between the borrower and existing or prospective lenders regarding the property's condition. The more complete this record, the more precise and defensible the expert's financial analysis will be.

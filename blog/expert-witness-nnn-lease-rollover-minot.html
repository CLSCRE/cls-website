Expert Witness: NNN Lease Rollover Upside and Financing Impact in Minot, ND

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Below-Market NNN Lease and Rollover Upside Analysis

The Minot Market Context

Minot, North Dakota is a smaller market by national CRE standards, but it punches above its weight in ways that matter for single-tenant NNN disputes. The city functions as the regional commercial hub for a large swath of north-central North Dakota, drawing retail and service tenants that serve both the permanent population and the cyclical workforce tied to the Williston Basin energy sector. That energy exposure creates a dynamic that institutional lenders do not ignore: Minot's commercial real estate fundamentals correlate, with some lag, to oil price cycles. When the Basin is active, occupancy tightens across retail, industrial, and flex product. When it softens, vacancy in secondary retail corridors climbs and lease renewal decisions become more conservative. For a single-tenant NNN property whose rent was locked in during a specific point in that cycle, the gap between in-place rent and current market rent can be substantial, and that gap is precisely what drives disputes over financing feasibility.

On the retail side, Minot's inventory is concentrated along the Broadway corridor and the outer ring anchored by the Minot Mall submarket and the South Broadway commercial strip. Single-tenant net lease properties in these locations range from fast food and convenience store pads to auto parts, dollar stores, and medical office conversions. Cap rates for stabilized, credit-tenanted NNN assets in Minot have historically traded in the 6.5 to 8.5 percent range depending on tenant credit, lease term remaining, and rent-to-sales coverage. That range is wider than primary markets because the buyer pool is thinner and the exit liquidity is more limited. Industrial vacancy across the Minot market has generally run in the 5 to 10 percent range, with net effective rents for small-bay industrial product in the $6 to $9 per square foot per year range. Retail rents for inline and pad product vary widely but market rents for quality single-tenant pad locations have generally ranged from $12 to $20 per square foot depending on size and configuration. When a below-market lease was executed at $10 per square foot on a property where current market is $16, that $6 spread becomes a quantifiable underwriting problem.

The Engagement

Counsel retained Commercial Lending Solutions to serve as a financing expert in connection with a commercial real estate dispute involving a single-tenant NNN property in Minot. The asset was a freestanding retail pad building occupied by a single tenant under a long-term absolute NNN lease. The core of the dispute involved whether the property, as encumbered by its existing lease, could support refinancing at levels the owner had represented were achievable, and whether the below-market rent structure materially impaired the borrower's ability to satisfy a refinancing obligation under the relevant agreement.

The legal questions presented to CLS CRE were specific and financing-focused rather than valuation-focused. First, could the in-place lease support a loan of the size contemplated, given prevailing DSCR and LTV requirements? Second, did the rollover upside from below-market rents constitute financeable value that a lender would recognize in sizing a loan? Third, what was the practical universe of lenders willing to consider this asset in this market, and what were their binding credit constraints? These are questions that an appraiser can partially address through cap rate selection, but an appraiser's conclusions about value do not translate directly into a lender's loan sizing decision. Those two outputs can diverge significantly, and in this engagement they did.

The Financial Analysis

The methodology I applied is grounded in how commercial mortgage lenders actually underwrite single-tenant NNN product, not how appraisers value it. Lenders size loans from the income stream that the lease documents support, not from a hypothetical stabilized value or a market rent estimate. That distinction is the analytical core of this type of dispute.

The subject property generated contractual base rent under its NNN lease of approximately $128,000 per year. Under the NNN structure, the tenant was responsible for taxes, insurance, and CAM, so the effective NOI available to service debt was substantially equal to the base rent, approximately $125,500 after accounting for minor vacancy reserves that conservative lenders apply even to single-tenant product. The in-place NOI was the only income figure a conventional lender would underwrite. Market rent on comparable pad product in the Minot submarket supported an economic NOI in the range of $196,000 to $210,000 annually, but that figure was not financeable under the existing lease.

The standard DSCR threshold for a single-tenant retail NNN loan in a secondary market like Minot is 1.25x at a minimum, with some lenders requiring 1.30x given the illiquidity and tenant concentration risk of a single-tenant structure. At a 1.25x DSCR and an assumed note rate of 7.25 percent on a 25-year amortization schedule, which reflected the pricing environment for secondary market NNN retail at the time of the analysis, the maximum supportable annual debt service on the in-place NOI was approximately $100,400 ($125,500 divided by 1.25). At a 7.25 percent note rate with 25-year amortization, the loan constant is approximately 0.0860. Dividing the maximum annual debt service of $100,400 by the loan constant of 0.0860 produces a maximum supportable loan of approximately $1,167,000.

The property had been appraised using a market rent-supported cap rate approach that produced a value in the range of $1,875,000 to $2,100,000 depending on cap rate selection. At a 65 percent LTV on the lower end of that range, the appraiser's value implied loan proceeds of approximately $1,219,000. At 70 percent LTV, the implication was $1,312,500. Those figures appear consistent with the DSCR-supported loan on their face, but they are not. The LTV constraint and the DSCR constraint operate independently, and the loan is sized by whichever constraint is more binding. In this case, the DSCR constraint produced a loan of approximately $1,167,000, and that figure implied an LTV of only 55.6 to 62.2 percent on the appraised value range. A lender who required both 65 percent LTV and 1.25x DSCR would cap the loan at $1,167,000 because the income stream simply could not support more debt at the required coverage ratio. The financing gap between what the parties had assumed was achievable and what the market actually supported was material and directly traceable to the below-market lease.

The Complicating Factors

Below-Market Rent and the Lender's Refusal to Underwrite Rollover Upside

The most common misconception I encounter in disputes involving below-market leases is the assumption that rollover upside is a financing asset. In the equity world, a below-market lease on a well-located property is an attractive feature because a buyer can underwrite to future market rents at rollover. Lenders do not work that way. A conventional commercial mortgage lender underwrites to the contractual income stream in place on the day the loan closes. If the lease has seven years remaining at $10 per square foot when market is $16, the lender gives the borrower credit for $10 per square foot. The additional $6 per square foot exists only as a future possibility contingent on the tenant renewing, re-leasing to a new tenant at market, or exercising a renewal option at a reset rate. None of those outcomes are contractually guaranteed, and lenders in the conventional market will not advance present loan proceeds against a speculative future income event.

Some lenders will acknowledge rollover upside in their internal analysis for purposes of assessing exit risk, but they draw a clean line between what they underwrite for loan sizing and what they consider for qualitative underwriting. The borrower cannot access that upside as additional loan proceeds today.

Lease Term Remaining and Refinancing Exposure

Single-tenant NNN lenders are acutely focused on the relationship between the loan term and the remaining lease term. Most lenders require that the lease extend at least one to two years beyond the loan maturity date. If the loan term is ten years and the lease has eight years remaining, the lender faces exposure to a vacant building at the back end of the loan, which undermines the collateral support for the balloon payment. In a thin secondary market like Minot, that concern is amplified. The re-leasing velocity for a vacant single-tenant pad building in Minot is slower than in a primary market, and the pool of creditworthy replacement tenants is more limited. Lenders price this risk either through reduced proceeds, a shorter loan term, or a loan structure that requires full amortization before lease expiration, all of which compress the financing available to the borrower.

Tenant Credit Quality in a Secondary Market Context

Not all NNN tenants are created equal in a lender's credit box. Investment-grade tenants (those with publicly rated corporate obligations) command the most favorable loan terms and the deepest lender pool. Sub-investment-grade tenants, franchisee entities, and regional operators require more scrutiny. In the Minot market, the tenant universe for NNN product includes a significant share of franchisee-operated locations and regional service tenants that do not carry investment-grade ratings. When the tenant is a franchisee, lenders require the franchisee's financial statements in addition to rent coverage analysis, and many CMBS and life company programs will not lend against franchisee tenants regardless of the franchisor's brand strength. This narrows the lender universe to community banks, credit unions, and select regional lenders, all of whom impose more conservative LTV constraints than institutional lenders.

Market Illiquidity and Exit Cap Rate Risk

A lender's willingness to advance proceeds at a given LTV depends on its confidence that the collateral can be sold or refinanced at loan maturity at a value sufficient to retire the debt. In Minot, the buyer pool for single-tenant NNN product is thinner than in a coastal or Sun Belt primary market. Fewer 1031 exchange buyers, fewer institutional net lease funds, and fewer REIT buyers are active in the market. This thinner pool results in wider cap rate ranges and greater pricing volatility. Lenders factor this into their underwriting by applying stress to exit cap rates, which reduces the exit value assumption and pushes the supportable LTV down. A lender that might underwrite a 65 percent LTV on a similar asset in Dallas may require 60 percent in Minot, not because the current value is different, but because the exit risk is higher.

The Minot Lender Market

Understanding which lenders are genuinely active in Minot for this asset type is essential to any expert analysis. The lender landscape here is not the same as it would be in Minneapolis or Denver, and that distinction matters directly to the question of what financing was achievable.

Community banks and regional banks are the dominant lenders for single-tenant NNN retail in Minot. Several North Dakota-chartered community banks and regional banks with operations in the Minot market have historically been active in this category. These lenders typically hold loans in portfolio, price at spreads over a prime or FHLB index, and impose DSCR requirements of 1.25x to 1.30x with LTV limits of 65 to 70 percent. They tend to have relationship-driven credit cultures, which can be a positive for borrowers with banking relationships, but it also means their credit terms are not standardized and can be more conservative than published guidelines suggest.

Credit unions with commercial lending programs are a secondary source in this market. Their pricing is often competitive with community banks, but their loan size limits and portfolio exposure constraints can limit their participation on larger transactions. For a single-tenant property in the $1.5 to $3 million range, credit unions can be a viable option if the borrower has an existing relationship.

CMBS lenders are technically available for Minot NNN product, but the practical minimum loan size for most CMBS programs ($2 million or above, with many programs starting at $3 million) and the requirement for investment-grade or strong national tenants means that CMBS is not a realistic execution path for many Minot single-tenant assets. When CMBS is available, it provides non-recourse execution at 65 to 70 percent LTV with fixed-rate pricing at spreads over the corresponding Treasury or swap rate. For the right asset and the right tenant credit, CMBS can be the most aggressive execution, but it is far from universally available here.

Life insurance companies generally do not deploy capital directly into secondary markets like Minot for loans below $5 to $10 million. The correspondent network that feeds life company programs occasionally includes assets from secondary markets, but the credit box is tight, the minimum loan sizes are large, and the execution timeline is long. For the typical single-tenant NNN dispute in Minot, life company financing is not a realistic alternative.

What Attorneys Handling Minot CRE Disputes Need to Know

Attorneys who handle commercial real estate litigation in North Dakota frequently retain appraisers to address value questions, and that is appropriate. But the financing feasibility question is analytically distinct from the valuation question, and conflating the two leads to gaps in the evidentiary record. A financing expert fills the space between "what is the property worth" and "what loan was actually achievable on this property with this lease at this point in time."

When to retain a CRE financing expert: The trigger for engaging a financing expert should be any dispute where a party's obligation, damages calculation, or breach theory depends on whether specific loan proceeds were obtainable. This includes breach of refinancing covenant claims, seller financing disputes where the seller's damages depend on the buyer's ability to secure third-party debt, partnership dissolution disputes where buyout financing is at issue, and bankruptcy plan confirmation proceedings where feasibility turns on debt serviceability.

What a financing expert answers that an appraiser cannot: An appraiser determines market value using capitalized income, comparable sales, and cost approaches. That analysis does not address lender underwriting standards, DSCR constraints, loan constant mathematics, or lender market conditions at a specific point in time. A financing expert can testify to the specific DSCR thresholds in effect in the market at the relevant date, the maximum supportable loan at those thresholds, which lenders were actively quoting this asset type in this market, what their credit boxes required, and whether the in-place lease structure met those requirements. These are questions that require active market participation, not certification as an appraiser.

Qualifications that matter: A qualified CRE financing expert should have direct, current origination experience closing commercial loans with active lenders, not just historical banking experience or academic credentials. The ability to testify to what a lender would actually do in a specific scenario requires current market knowledge that someone who retired from commercial banking a decade ago does not possess. CLS CRE closes transactions actively in secondary markets including those comparable to Minot, maintains relationships with more than 1,000 lenders nationwide, and has underwritten approximately $1 billion in transaction volume across 50 states. That active market presence is what grounds expert testimony in commercial reality rather than theory.

Information counsel should gather before the engagement: The expert engagement proceeds most efficiently when counsel provides the existing lease documents (including any amendments and renewal options), rent rolls, the property's financial statements for the prior two to three years, any prior loan applications or term sheets, the relevant appraisal if one exists, and any communications between the parties about financing feasibility. Loan payoff statements and existing loan documents are also critical if the dispute involves a refinancing obligation.

Use at trial or in plan proceedings: A financing expert's testimony is most powerful when it quantifies the gap between what was represented and what was achievable, with the math shown explicitly. In a bench trial, detailed DSCR and loan sizing calculations presented in a Rule 26 report and explained on direct examination can be decisive on damages. In a Chapter 11 plan confirmation hearing, a financing expert can address feasibility directly by demonstrating whether the plan's debt service assumptions are supportable given the property's income and the prevailing lender market.

Key Takeaways

  • Lenders underwrite to the in-place lease, not to market rent. Below-market rents reduce the financeable NOI directly, regardless of what the property could earn at rollover. Rollover upside is not a loan sizing asset in conventional commercial mortgage underwriting.
  • DSCR and LTV are independent constraints, and both bind simultaneously. A property may pass an LTV test at an appraised value while failing the DSCR test because the in-place NOI cannot support the implied debt service. In the Minot scenario analyzed, the DSCR constraint was the more binding limitation and reduced achievable loan proceeds by a material amount relative to LTV-implied proceeds.

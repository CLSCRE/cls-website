Expert Witness: CRE Partnership and Waterfall Disputes in San Diego, CA

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Joint Venture and Partnership Dispute

The San Diego Market Context

San Diego's commercial real estate market operates within a set of structural dynamics that make it fertile ground for complex joint venture arrangements and, when those arrangements go sideways, for sophisticated financing disputes. The market is characterized by constrained land supply, high barriers to entitlement, and a tenant base anchored by defense contractors, life sciences firms, biotech operators, and a persistently undersupplied multifamily sector. These fundamentals have driven capitalization rates to levels that make equity structuring critical to investment returns, which is precisely where JV waterfall disputes originate.

In the industrial sector, San Diego has maintained vacancy rates in the 3% to 5% range across submarkets including Otay Mesa, Kearny Mesa, and Miramar, with asking rents climbing steadily over the past several years into the $18 to $24 per square foot range on an annual NNN basis depending on submarket and clear height. Life sciences and biotech lab space in submarkets like Torrey Pines, UTC, and Sorrento Valley commands significant rent premiums, often in the $60 to $90 per square foot annual range, and has attracted institutional joint venture capital at a scale that routinely involves preferred return structures, capital account waterfalls, and promoted interest arrangements. These are exactly the economic mechanisms that generate litigation when returns underperform or when one party believes the other has miscalculated distributions.

Multifamily in San Diego tells a similar story. Class A apartment cap rates have compressed into the 4.0% to 4.75% range in infill locations such as Little Italy, Mission Hills, North Park, and the Gaslamp adjacent submarkets, while value-add product in submarkets like City Heights, National City, and El Cajon trades closer to 5.0% to 5.75%. At these valuations, equity returns depend almost entirely on how the capital stack is engineered. Preferred returns, catch-up provisions, and promoted interests are not optional features in these deals; they are the mechanism by which sponsors justify the promote and investors justify the risk-adjusted return. When those mechanisms produce unexpected results, litigation follows.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing one of the equity partners in a San Diego commercial real estate joint venture dispute involving a stabilized mixed-use asset in an urban infill submarket. The asset included both residential and ground-floor retail components, a structure common in the San Diego urban core where developers frequently blend income streams to support higher land basis and to satisfy city planning objectives.

The core dispute involved three interrelated questions. First, whether the preferred return owed to the limited partner had been correctly calculated, specifically whether the preferred return was compounding on unreturned capital or accruing on a simple basis, and whether certain fees paid to the managing member should have been deducted before or after the preferred return was funded. Second, whether the promoted interest, or "carried interest," had been correctly triggered under the applicable internal rate of return hurdle, and whether the IRR calculation properly accounted for the timing of equity contributions and distributions. Third, whether the financing that was obtained at the asset level, including the terms of the construction loan, the conversion to permanent financing, and a subsequent supplemental loan, had materially affected the cash available for distribution and whether those financing decisions were consistent with market standards at the time they were made.

Counsel retained CLS CRE to provide a Rule 26 expert report addressing the capital stack structure, the market standard for JV distribution waterfalls applicable to this asset type and market, the financing terms that were available and typical in the San Diego market during the relevant period, and whether the calculations performed by the managing member were consistent with or deviated from market-standard practice.

The Financial Analysis

Our analysis began with a reconstruction of the property's net operating income across the relevant performance period. The asset generated gross potential income from residential units and retail tenants, and the managing member had applied a vacancy and credit loss assumption that we analyzed against actual market vacancy data for comparable mixed-use product in the submarket. The managing member's applied vacancy assumption of 8% on residential was materially higher than the market-supported rate of 4% to 5% during the relevant period, which had the effect of suppressing stabilized NOI and, consequently, the distributable cash available to equity partners.

Once we established the corrected NOI baseline, we applied it to the capital stack analysis. The asset carried a permanent loan that had been originated with a debt service coverage ratio requirement of 1.25x at origination, which is market standard for this asset type and vintage. At the time of the supplemental loan, the lender had applied a blended DSCR threshold of 1.20x on the combined debt service, also consistent with market practice for supplemental financing on stabilized multifamily assets. The DSCR constraint was central to the dispute because the managing member had argued that the supplemental loan was sized at the maximum supportable level, and that the equity partners should not expect additional cash-out proceeds to flow into the waterfall.

We ran the numbers using the corrected NOI. Assuming a stabilized NOI of approximately $2,100,000 on the residential components and $310,000 on the retail components, producing a combined NOI of approximately $2,410,000, and applying a 1.20x DSCR to annual debt service on the existing senior loan of approximately $980,000, the maximum additional supportable debt service was approximately $1,028,000 annually at the time of the supplemental loan analysis. At a 6.75% interest rate on a 30-year amortization schedule, standard for agency supplemental financing during the relevant period, that debt service capacity supported approximately $13,100,000 in supplemental loan proceeds. The managing member had obtained approximately $9,400,000 in supplemental financing, representing a shortfall of approximately $3,700,000 in available loan proceeds that were not extracted and therefore not distributed through the waterfall.

This was not a small discrepancy. On a blended LTV basis, the combined senior and supplemental debt represented approximately 62% of the appraised value at the time of the supplemental closing. Market-standard agency lenders for this asset type were routinely closing at 70% to 75% LTV during that period, confirming that there was meaningful additional loan capacity that was not utilized. The question of whether that decision was a reasonable exercise of the managing member's discretion, or a breach of duty that deprived the limited partner of preferred return proceeds, was the legal question before the court. Our role was to establish what the market made available, not to adjudicate the managing member's intent.

On the preferred return calculation, we modeled both the compounding and simple accrual interpretations of the JV agreement language against the actual cash distribution history. The difference was material. Under a compounding interpretation, the limited partner's accrued preferred return on unreturned capital exceeded the distributions actually received by approximately $1,850,000. Under a simple accrual interpretation, the gap was approximately $740,000. Our report presented both calculations with full transparency and explained how market-standard JV agreements are typically drafted and interpreted for each approach, allowing counsel to deploy the analysis that aligned with their legal theory.

The Complicating Factors

Promoted Interest Trigger and IRR Methodology. The JV agreement in this matter conditioned the sponsor's promoted interest on the limited partner achieving a specified IRR hurdle before any promote was payable. The dispute centered on how the IRR was calculated, specifically whether equity contributions made in multiple tranches during the construction period were date-stamped at their actual funding dates or whether the managing member had used a blended contribution date for simplicity. IRR is extraordinarily sensitive to the timing of cash flows. Using a blended contribution date rather than actual funding dates understated the limited partner's IRR, which had the effect of moving the promote trigger to an earlier point in the distribution history. This is not an unusual problem in JV disputes. Sponsors frequently use simplified IRR approximations in their waterfall models rather than true date-specific calculations, and those simplifications nearly always benefit the sponsor. Expert analysis of IRR methodology requires modeling the cash flows on a daily or monthly basis using actual funding and distribution dates, which is different from how many operating partners run their internal calculations.

Fee Treatment Above and Below the Preferred Return Line. The managing member in this dispute had charged asset management fees, property management fees, and a loan placement fee, all of which were deducted at the property level before distributable cash was calculated. The loan placement fee in particular was not disclosed to the limited partner at the time of the supplemental financing and was charged at 1% of the loan amount, or approximately $94,000. Whether fees of this type should be treated as operating expenses that reduce distributable cash before the preferred return calculation, or as equity-level charges that are deducted after the preferred return is funded, is a drafting and market-standard question. In our experience reviewing JV agreements across a wide range of CRE transactions, market practice is inconsistent, which makes this a genuine area of expert opinion rather than a purely legal one. Our report analyzed comparable JV agreement structures and identified the prevailing convention for each fee type.

Construction Loan to Permanent Loan Transition. The construction loan for this asset had an interest reserve built into the loan proceeds, which the managing member had treated as equity returns during the construction period for purposes of the IRR calculation. This is an unusual and aggressive position. Interest reserves are debt proceeds used to service the construction loan during the non-cash-flowing construction period; they are not equity distributions and should not be treated as such in a return calculation. This treatment had the effect of inflating early-period "returns" in the IRR model, which again moved the hurdle trigger earlier and benefited the sponsor's promote entitlement.

Retail Component Underperformance. The ground-floor retail in this mixed-use asset had experienced prolonged vacancy following the departure of an anchor retail tenant. The managing member had argued that this vacancy was a market event outside their control and should not affect the preferred return accrual. However, the vacancy had direct implications for the property's appraised value, the supplemental loan sizing, and the distributable cash available to fund the waterfall. Our analysis examined whether market-rate leasing assumptions at the time of the original underwriting were reasonable and whether a competent managing member exercising market-standard diligence would have structured the retail leases differently, including credit requirements, co-tenancy protections, and lease term requirements that are standard in San Diego mixed-use transactions.

The San Diego Lender Market

San Diego benefits from a deep and varied lender market that, for stabilized income-producing assets, includes agency lenders, life insurance companies, CMBS conduit platforms, regional and community banks, credit unions, and debt funds. Understanding which lender categories were available and active during the relevant dispute period is essential to any expert analysis of financing feasibility and market-standard terms.

For multifamily assets, Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac agency executions dominate the permanent lending landscape in San Diego. Agency lenders typically price at spreads of 150 to 200 basis points over the corresponding Treasury, offer 70% to 80% LTV depending on property quality and market, and require DSCR of 1.20x to 1.25x. For the supplemental loan product specifically, Fannie and Freddie both offer supplemental financing on seasoned agency loans, typically requiring a minimum of 12 months of seasoning and combined LTV not exceeding 75% to 80%. These terms were well-established during the dispute period and form the benchmark against which the managing member's financing decisions are measured.

For mixed-use assets with retail components exceeding 20% of gross revenues, agency lenders typically require the retail to be underwritten at a discount or excluded from the income used to size the loan, which creates a meaningful constraint. Life company lenders and CMBS platforms will often take a more flexible view of mixed-use income, but they price at wider spreads and have longer rate lock processes. In San Diego, life company activity has been consistent for institutional-quality assets in the $10 million to $50 million range, with several active allocators maintaining regional presence or using correspondent relationships with local mortgage bankers.

Community banks and credit unions, including several headquartered in San Diego County, remain active in the $3 million to $15 million loan range on both multifamily and mixed-use product. These institutions typically hold loans on balance sheet, offer more flexibility on lease-up and mixed-income scenarios, but require full recourse and price at floating rates tied to Prime or SOFR. For disputes involving smaller assets or construction bridge financing, these lenders often provide the relevant comparison set.

Debt funds and bridge lenders operate in San Diego primarily for transitional assets, lease-up scenarios, and construction takeout situations where agency or bank financing is not yet available. Bridge pricing in the San Diego market typically runs 300 to 500 basis points over SOFR, with origination fees of 1% to 2%, and maximum leverage of 70% to 75% LTC. These terms matter in litigation contexts where a party claims that permanent financing was unavailable or that the asset was forced into bridge financing due to the other party's actions.

What Attorneys Handling San Diego CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to retain a CRE financing expert. Counsel handling JV and partnership disputes in commercial real estate should consider retaining a financing expert at the outset of discovery, not after deposition preparation. The financing structure, specifically the loan terms, covenants, reserve requirements, and lender approval rights, often directly constrains what distributions are permissible and when. An expert who has not reviewed the loan documents alongside the JV operating agreement will miss the interaction between lender restrictions and waterfall mechanics. In San Diego disputes involving agency loans, lender approval rights over supplemental financing and cash-out events are particularly significant and frequently misunderstood by non-specialist counsel.

What a CRE financing expert can answer that an appraiser cannot. A certified appraiser establishes value. A CRE financing expert establishes financing feasibility, lender market standards, term reasonableness, and capital stack mechanics. These are categorically different questions. An appraiser cannot opine on whether a DSCR constraint was correctly applied, whether loan proceeds were conservatively sized relative to market availability, whether a particular lender category would have been accessible given the asset's characteristics, or whether the IRR calculation methodology used by the managing member is consistent with market-standard practice. These are questions that require active deal experience, lender relationships, and knowledge of how capital markets actually price and structure transactions in a specific market and time period.

What qualifies a CRE financing expert versus a retired banker. A retired loan officer or bank credit officer understands the credit side of one institution's underwriting. A qualified CRE financing expert has current, active experience placing transactions across multiple lender categories, understands how different lender types approach the same asset differently, and can speak to market-standard terms and practices across the relevant time period. At CLS CRE, our team has approximately $1 billion in transaction volume and relationships with more than 1,000 lenders across 50 states. That breadth is what allows us to opine on what the market offered, not just what one lender would have done.

What counsel should gather before the expert engagement. At minimum: the full JV operating agreement including all amendments and side letters; the loan documents for every financing on the subject property from origination through the dispute period; the managing member's waterfall model and distribution calculations; all capital call notices and distribution notices; any third-party appraisals or broker opinions of value obtained during the asset hold period; and the managing member's communications regarding financing decisions, including any lender term sheets that were received and either accepted or rejected. The financing decisions made or not made during the hold period are often where the liability analysis is most productive.

How CRE financing expert testimony is used at trial or in arbitration. Expert testimony in JV waterfall disputes typically serves three functions: establishing what market-standard practice was for the relevant transaction type, time period, and market; reconstructing what the financing terms and distributable cash should have been under correct methodology; and explaining to a judge or arbitrator how capital stack mechanics work in plain language without losing technical accuracy. At Commercial Lending Solutions, our reports are written to be accessible to a sophisticated but non-specialist factfinder while supporting cross-examination by opposing counsel who may retain their own expert.

Key Takeaways

  • Waterfall disputes are often financing disputes in disguise. The amount of cash available for distribution through the JV waterfall is directly determined by the financing in place. Conservative loan sizing, unused supplemental loan capacity, or unexplained reserve requirements can suppress distributable cash and deprive equity partners of preferred return funding.
  • IRR calculations are sensitive to methodology. The timing, sequencing, and treatment of capital contributions, including whether construction loan interest reserves are treated as equity returns, materially affect when IRR hurdles are triggered and whether promoted interests are properly earned. Small methodological choices produce large dollar differences.

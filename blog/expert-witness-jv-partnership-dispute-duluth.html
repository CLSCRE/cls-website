Expert Witness: CRE Partnership and Waterfall Disputes in Duluth, MN

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Joint Venture and Partnership Dispute

The Duluth Market Context

Duluth, Minnesota occupies a position in the upper Midwest commercial real estate landscape that attorneys and investors often underestimate. The city anchors the western tip of Lake Superior and functions as the regional economic hub for northeastern Minnesota, northwestern Wisconsin, and portions of the Upper Peninsula of Michigan. That regional draw creates a commercial real estate market with characteristics that directly shape how joint ventures are structured, how capital stacks are assembled, and ultimately how waterfall disputes arise.

The Duluth multifamily market has experienced notable rent growth over the past several years, driven by constrained supply, population stabilization following decades of modest outmigration, and increased demand from healthcare and logistics employment sectors anchored by institutions including Essentia Health and the broader port-related industrial base. Market-rate apartment cap rates in Duluth have generally traded in the 5.75 to 7.25 percent range depending on asset quality, location within the metro, and vintage, with Class B and C assets carrying wider cap rates than comparable Twin Cities product but tighter than outstate rural Minnesota. That spread matters enormously when a joint venture's preferred return hurdle and promoted interest calculation are both anchored to assumptions about exit valuation.

Industrial product along the harbor corridor and Spirit Mountain area has seen vacancy compress meaningfully, with functional warehouse and distribution space trading at cap rates in the 6.5 to 7.5 percent range. Mixed-use and retail assets in downtown Duluth, particularly along Superior Street and the Canal Park district, have bifurcated sharply between experiential retail and food-and-beverage assets, which have performed well, and traditional soft-goods retail, which has struggled. Office fundamentals remain challenged with suburban office vacancy elevated and downtown Class B office experiencing softening absorption. These dynamics mean that the asset class selected for a Duluth joint venture carries substantial implications for the lender universe available at the time of the initial capital stack construction and again at the time of any refinance or exit that triggers waterfall distributions.

Joint venture structures in secondary and tertiary Midwestern markets like Duluth frequently involve a local operating partner with market knowledge and site control combined with a capital partner, often from Minneapolis, Chicago, or a national platform, providing the majority of equity. That geographic and institutional asymmetry creates fertile ground for disputes: the operating partner typically negotiates the promoted interest and preferred return hurdles during a period of optimism, and the capital partner often controls distributions and accounting. When the deal underperforms or when interest rate movements affect debt service coverage and refinance proceeds, disagreements about waterfall mechanics can metastasize into significant litigation.

The Engagement

In the matter at hand, counsel representing the operating partner in a Duluth joint venture dispute retained Commercial Lending Solutions to provide expert analysis on three overlapping questions. The asset was a mid-size multifamily property in Duluth's hillside submarket, stabilized and operating but the subject of a refinance transaction that generated a substantial capital event. The dispute centered on whether the distributions made to each party following that refinance correctly reflected the waterfall mechanics specified in the operative joint venture agreement, and whether the financing terms actually obtained were consistent with market-standard terms achievable at the time of the closing.

The core legal and financial questions were as follows. First, whether the capital partner's calculation of the preferred return accrual was performed correctly, including the compounding methodology applied and the treatment of interim distributions made during the hold period. Second, whether the loan proceeds obtained in the refinance were consistent with what a reasonably qualified borrower could have achieved in the market at the time of the closing, which bore directly on the amount of cash available for distribution and thus the trigger points for the promoted interest. Third, whether the financing structure chosen, specifically the loan-to-value ratio, the interest rate, and the amortization schedule, was made in good faith and with reasonable market knowledge, or whether a more aggressive but still financeable capital structure could have generated materially higher loan proceeds and therefore a different waterfall outcome. CLS CRE was engaged to provide a Rule 26-compliant expert report addressing all three of these questions with specific reference to Duluth market conditions, lender underwriting standards, and industry-standard joint venture waterfall mechanics.

The Financial Analysis

The subject property generated gross potential rent of approximately $1,480,000 annually based on in-place leases at the time of the refinance, with economic vacancy of approximately 6.5 percent consistent with Duluth market conditions for this asset class. Net operating income, calculated after vacancy, credit loss, management fees at 6 percent of effective gross income, real estate taxes, insurance, and a replacement reserve of $300 per unit per year consistent with agency lending requirements, was established at approximately $1,020,000. This NOI figure was critical because it served as the binding constraint on maximum loan proceeds and therefore on the cash available for waterfall distribution.

Under market-standard underwriting applied by agency lenders and commercial banks active in the Duluth market at the time of the refinance, a debt service coverage ratio floor of 1.25x is required for most conventional multifamily financing, with certain agency programs permitting 1.20x at lower LTVs. Applying the 1.25x DSCR floor to the stabilized NOI of $1,020,000 produces a maximum annual debt service of $816,000. At the interest rate prevailing in the market at the time of the relevant closing, which I established through review of agency rate sheets and comparable loan closings, and assuming a 30-year amortization schedule standard for agency execution, the maximum supportable loan was approximately $10,200,000 to $10,500,000 depending on precise rate assumptions.

The capital partner had obtained financing of approximately $9,100,000, representing an LTV of approximately 62 percent against an appraised value of $14,700,000. My analysis established that the market-achievable loan amount at the time of the refinance, applying the same lender category and loan program, was approximately $10,250,000, representing an LTV of approximately 69.7 percent. The difference of approximately $1,150,000 in loan proceeds is not a rounding error. In the context of this joint venture's specific waterfall structure, that $1,150,000 differential determined whether a return-of-capital tier was fully satisfied before the promoted interest calculation commenced, which in turn affected the operating partner's promoted interest entitlement by a sum well within the range that justifies expert testimony and litigation.

My analysis also addressed the preferred return accrual. The joint venture agreement specified a 9 percent preferred return compounded annually on unreturned capital contributions. The capital partner had calculated the preferred return balance using a methodology that applied simple interest during certain sub-annual periods, then compounded annually at year-end. I analyzed industry-standard preferred return calculation methodologies and concluded that the agreement's language, read in the context of market-standard joint venture drafting conventions, required compound accrual on a daily or monthly basis, not a hybrid methodology that understated the compounding effect. The difference in accrued preferred return balance between the two methodologies, over a four-year hold, was approximately $340,000, which directly reduced the capital available for promoted interest participation by the operating partner.

The Complicating Factors

Secondary Market Lender Access and Its Effect on Loan Proceeds. Duluth's position as a secondary market means that not all lenders who theoretically offer a given loan program will actually execute in this geography. Several larger CMBS platforms and certain life company lenders apply market-size thresholds or population density minimums that Duluth does not satisfy. This creates a situation where the theoretical maximum loan based on NOI and DSCR may exceed the practical maximum because certain lender categories are absent from the effective market. In this engagement, I carefully distinguished between what was theoretically achievable and what was practically achievable given the Duluth lender market, because that distinction is the one that matters at trial. The capital partner argued that no lender would have gone above the $9,100,000 they obtained. My analysis identified three lender categories that were actively quoting and closing loans on Duluth multifamily assets in the same size range during the same quarter, at LTVs that supported my $10,250,000 estimate.

Refinance Timing and Interest Rate Volatility. The refinance at issue closed during a period of elevated interest rate volatility. The rate at which the loan was locked directly affected the debt service calculation and therefore the maximum supportable loan proceeds. A dispute arose about whether the borrower locked at the optimal point in the rate cycle or whether delays in the refinance process attributable to the capital partner's decision-making caused the rate lock to occur at a less favorable point. I analyzed the rate environment during the relevant window using contemporaneous rate sheets and Federal Home Loan Bank data. The rate that was actually obtained was approximately 25 basis points above what was available six weeks earlier in the closing process. At the loan size at issue, 25 basis points on a 30-year amortization schedule affects maximum loan proceeds by approximately $180,000 to $200,000, compounding the waterfall dispute.

Waterfall Tier Definitions and the Ambiguity of "Distributions." The joint venture agreement contained provisions addressing how cash-out refinance proceeds would be treated relative to operating distributions received during the hold period. Specifically, the agreement was ambiguous about whether interest paid on investor loans made by the capital partner to bridge an operating shortfall constituted a "distribution" that reduced the preferred return accrual balance or whether it constituted debt repayment outside the waterfall entirely. This type of drafting ambiguity is extraordinarily common in joint ventures where the initial agreement is negotiated during a period of optimism and interim capital needs are not fully anticipated. The financial resolution of that ambiguity had a compounding effect: if the bridge loan interest was classified as a distribution, it reduced the preferred return accrual more rapidly, which in turn reduced the capital tier balance faster, which pulled forward the promoted interest calculation. Resolving that ambiguity required analysis of market-standard joint venture drafting conventions, not just legal interpretation of the contract language.

The Duluth Lender Market

For multifamily assets in the $7,000,000 to $15,000,000 range in Duluth, the practical lender universe consists of several distinct categories, each with different credit requirements, pricing, and underwriting standards that bear directly on what loan terms are achievable and therefore what waterfall outcomes are possible.

Agency Lenders (Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac). Both agencies are accessible for Duluth multifamily product through approved seller-servicers. Agency execution provides the highest LTV availability (up to 80 percent for standard conventional product), the longest fixed-rate terms (typically 7 or 10 years), and the most competitive pricing. However, agency lenders require stabilized occupancy (typically 90 percent or above for 90 days prior to application), and their underwriting is formulaic and resistant to upward adjustments for rent growth projections. For a stabilized Duluth multifamily asset, agency execution is the benchmark against which all other lender categories should be measured.

Regional and Community Banks. Several Twin Cities-based regional banks and a number of Duluth-headquartered community institutions are active in this market, including banks with strong historical relationships in the Iron Range and Lake Superior corridor. These lenders typically operate at 65 to 75 percent LTV for investment multifamily, require 1.20x to 1.30x DSCR, and price at spreads over the relevant Treasury or SOFR index that are generally 50 to 100 basis points wider than agency execution. Their advantage is relationship flexibility, faster closing timelines, and willingness to consider value-add or transitional situations that agency programs would decline.

Debt Funds and Bridge Lenders. For assets that are not yet agency-eligible due to occupancy or that are in transition, several national debt funds will lend in Duluth at loan sizes above approximately $5,000,000. These lenders operate at significantly higher rates, typically SOFR plus 350 to 550 basis points, with shorter terms of 24 to 36 months, and frequently include origination fees of 1 to 2 points. Bridge debt is relevant to waterfall disputes in cases where the initial acquisition financing was bridge debt that was meant to be refinanced into permanent financing, and the timing and terms of that takeout refinance are in dispute.

USDA Business and Industry Programs. For rural-adjacent and economically targeted assets, USDA B&I loan guarantees are an underutilized but real component of the Duluth lender market. These programs can extend LTV and reduce credit risk for lenders willing to navigate the application process, and their availability can meaningfully affect the financing universe analysis in a dispute context.

What Attorneys Handling Duluth CRE Disputes Need to Know

Attorneys handling joint venture and partnership disputes involving Duluth commercial real estate should retain a CRE financing expert at the earliest stage at which financing terms, loan proceeds, or waterfall calculations are in dispute. The threshold question is not whether a financing expert is necessary; it is whether counsel can competently address the financial questions without one. In my experience, the answer is almost always no, and that deficiency becomes apparent during opposing expert depositions or at trial.

What a CRE Financing Expert Can Answer That an Appraiser Cannot. An appraiser establishes value. A CRE financing expert establishes what the market would lend against that value, on what terms, in what timeframe, and with what structural constraints. Those are entirely different questions. A disputed preferred return calculation requires an expert who understands how lenders underwrite NOI, how DSCR constraints translate into maximum loan proceeds, and how market-standard joint venture agreements define and calculate waterfall mechanics. An appraiser's value conclusion is an input to the financing analysis, not a substitute for it.

What Makes a CRE Financing Expert Qualified. Qualification is not simply a matter of years in the industry or a lending title on a former employer's letterhead. A qualified CRE financing expert should have current, active deal flow in the relevant asset class and market size range, so that their opinions about what lenders will and will not do are grounded in contemporaneous market knowledge rather than historical recollection. Commercial Lending Solutions, with approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, relationships with more than 1,000 lenders, and active origination across all 50 states, brings current market intelligence that a retired banker or a consultant who left active origination years ago cannot provide.

Information Counsel Should Gather Before the Expert Engagement. Before retaining CLS CRE or any financing expert, counsel should assemble the operative joint venture agreement with all amendments and side letters; all loan applications, term sheets, commitment letters, and closing documents associated with any financing on the subject property; trailing 12-month operating statements and rent rolls for each period relevant to the dispute; appraisals obtained in connection with any financing event; correspondence between the parties about financing strategy and lender selection; and any financial models or waterfall calculations prepared by either party or their respective accountants.

How CRE Financing Expert Testimony Is Used. In trial or arbitration, a CRE financing expert typically provides testimony on two categories of issues: what the market would have permitted, and whether what was actually done was consistent with market standards. The first category addresses opportunity: could more proceeds have been obtained, could better terms have been achieved, and what was the financial effect of the difference? The second category addresses conduct: did the party controlling the financing process act consistent with the standard of care applicable to a reasonably experienced commercial real estate joint venture participant? Both categories are directly relevant to damages calculations in waterfall and promoted interest disputes.

  • NOI and DSCR drive maximum loan proceeds. In any Duluth joint venture waterfall dispute involving a refinance or capital event, the analysis begins with stabilized NOI and the applicable DSCR threshold, typically 1.20x to 1.25x depending on lender category. Every dollar of disputed loan proceeds traces back to these inputs.
  • Secondary market dynamics affect the lender universe. Duluth's market size excludes certain lender categories that would be available for the same asset in Minneapolis or Chicago. Expert analysis must carefully distinguish between what is theoretically financeable and what is practically achievable given active Duluth market participants.
  • Preferred return compounding methodology is a quantifiable dispute. The difference between simple interest accrual and compound accrual on a 9 percent preferred return over a multi-year hold period can run into hundreds of thousands of dollars on a mid-market Duluth asset, and that difference directly affects the promoted interest calculation.

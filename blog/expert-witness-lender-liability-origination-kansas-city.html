Expert Witness: Commercial Loan Origination Standards in Kansas City, MO

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Lender Liability and Loan Origination Standards

The Kansas City Market Context

Kansas City occupies a position in the commercial real estate lending landscape that attorneys and investors often underestimate. It is not a gateway market in the institutional sense that Chicago or Dallas command, but it is far from a secondary market as lenders define that term. Kansas City's logistics infrastructure, anchored by its status as the second-largest rail hub in North America and its central position within the interstate highway system, has driven sustained industrial demand that outperforms most Midwestern peers. As of recent reporting periods, industrial vacancy in the Kansas City metropolitan statistical area has hovered in the 4.5% to 6.5% range depending on submarket, with the Edgerton and Gardner corridors along I-35 absorbing significant new speculative construction without the demand destruction that has punished other Sun Belt markets. Average asking rents for Class A industrial have climbed from the $5.00 to $6.00 per square foot NNN range to the $7.50 to $9.50 range across a three-year compression, representing meaningful rent growth by Midwest standards.

Multifamily fundamentals in Kansas City tell a more nuanced story. Cap rates for stabilized, Class B apartment product in submarkets like Overland Park, Lee's Summit, and the Crossroads district have traded in the 5.25% to 6.25% range, reflecting a market that has absorbed new supply more gracefully than peer cities but is not immune to the interest rate dislocation that has suppressed transaction volume nationally since 2022. Office fundamentals remain under stress, with downtown Class A vacancy running above 18% in some blocks and suburban office in Johnson County holding more stable occupancy in the 12% to 15% range, supported by healthcare and financial services tenants. Retail, specifically neighborhood-anchored grocery product, has remained one of the tighter asset classes in Kansas City, with cap rates in the 6.00% to 7.00% range for anchored centers with grocery cotenancy.

These market-specific fundamentals matter enormously in lender liability disputes. The question of what a reasonably competent lender or broker should have done cannot be answered in the abstract. It requires a specific understanding of where Kansas City was in its cycle at the time the loan was originated, what lenders were actually pricing risk in this market, and whether the underwriting assumptions embedded in the subject loan reflected market reality or wishful projection.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing a commercial borrower in a dispute arising from a loan origination on a mixed-use retail and office property in the Kansas City metropolitan area. The property was a mid-size, two-story building with ground-floor retail and upper-floor professional office space, located in a suburban Johnson County submarket. The borrower alleged that the mortgage broker who placed the financing had materially misrepresented the loan terms during the application and commitment phase, had failed to disclose a prepayment penalty structure that was inconsistent with what was verbally represented, and had recommended a loan structure that was unsuitable given the borrower's stated hold period and exit strategy.

Counsel retained CLS CRE to provide a Rule 26 expert report addressing three core questions. First, whether the loan as originated met the industry standard of care for a commercial mortgage broker operating in Kansas City at the time of origination. Second, whether the disclosed terms, including the rate, prepayment schedule, and loan covenant structure, were consistent with what was available in the Kansas City lender market at that time and what a reasonably competent broker should have presented. Third, what a reasonably competent commercial mortgage broker would have done differently in identifying, presenting, and structuring financing alternatives for this borrower and this asset type.

These are precisely the questions that sit in the gap between what a licensed appraiser can address and what an active, deal-executing commercial mortgage professional can address. Appraised value is relevant but not dispositive. The operative issues are about the loan origination process, market conditions for debt capital, broker conduct standards, and whether the borrower received the benefit of competent representation in the debt markets.

The Financial Analysis

The subject property carried an in-place net operating income that required careful disaggregation before any supportable loan analysis could be rendered. Gross potential rent across both the retail and office components totaled approximately $1,140,000 annually at the lease rates in place at origination. Applying a stabilized vacancy assumption required market-specific judgment: retail vacancy in the Johnson County submarket was running approximately 7% at the time, while suburban office vacancy was closer to 13%. A blended, weighted vacancy assumption of 9.5% to 10% against the respective income streams was appropriate and defensible. After vacancy, effective gross income came to approximately $1,028,000. Operating expenses, including real estate taxes at Kansas City's applicable mill levy, insurance, common area maintenance, and a management fee of 4% of effective gross income, totaled approximately $298,000, yielding a stabilized NOI of approximately $730,000.

The standard DSCR threshold applied by the dominant lender categories in the Kansas City market for mixed-use retail and office product at the time of origination was 1.25x on a stressed basis. Some life company and CMBS executions permitted 1.20x for stronger collateral and sponsorship, but 1.25x was the market standard and the appropriate benchmark for this analysis. Using a 25-year amortization schedule and a note rate reflective of market conditions at origination (in the range of 6.50% to 6.75% for a five-year fixed term, which was consistent with prevailing spreads over the five-year Treasury), the maximum annual debt service supportable at 1.25x DSCR was calculated as follows.

NOI of $730,000 divided by 1.25x yields maximum supportable annual debt service of $584,000. At a 6.625% note rate on a 25-year amortization, the mortgage constant was approximately 0.0861, meaning the maximum supportable loan balance was $584,000 divided by 0.0861, or approximately $6,783,000. Applying a market LTV constraint of 65% for mixed-use office and retail product in this risk environment, the LTV test required a minimum appraised value of approximately $10,435,000 to support that loan amount. The property had been appraised at $9,800,000, which constrained the LTV-based loan ceiling to $6,370,000. The binding constraint was therefore LTV, not DSCR, and the maximum supportable loan by market underwriting standards was approximately $6,370,000.

The loan as originated was $7,200,000. This is material. The loan exceeded what the market's own underwriting standards would have supported by approximately $830,000, which represents an 8.5% origination excess over the collateral-supportable amount. This gap is not trivial and goes directly to whether the lender's underwriting met the standard of care. A competent originator should have flagged this tension between the income-supportable amount and the LTV ceiling, and any broker representing the borrower's interests had an obligation to surface it.

The Complicating Factors

Mixed-Use Asset Classification and Lender Credit Boxes: Mixed-use properties that blend retail and office income are treated with heightened caution by most institutional lenders, not because the asset class is inherently problematic, but because the underwriting requires two separate income stream analyses with different vacancy assumptions, different lease structure norms, and different tenant credit profiles. In Kansas City specifically, the post-pandemic bifurcation of office demand has made lenders particularly conservative on any asset where office contributes more than 30% to 35% of total income. The subject property's income was approximately 55% office-derived, which placed it outside the comfort zone of most CMBS conduit programs and many life company mandates. A competent broker navigating this market should have recognized this immediately and qualified only lenders whose credit boxes accommodated this income split. Failure to do so exposes the broker to the argument that loan placement was driven by lender relationships or compensation rather than borrower suitability.

Prepayment Penalty Structure and Hold Period Misalignment: The loan as originated carried a five-year step-down prepayment penalty of 5-4-3-2-1, meaning a 5% penalty in year one declining by one point annually. The borrower's documented business plan at origination reflected a three-year anticipated hold. A competent broker who reviewed that business plan and then placed a loan with a penalty structure that made a year-three exit economically punishing failed in a basic advisory function. At a loan balance of approximately $7,200,000, a year-three prepayment would have carried a penalty of approximately $216,000 before any additional lender breakage costs. Market alternatives at the time included step-down structures beginning at 3% and open periods that commenced at month 37. These alternatives were available from regional bank lenders active in Kansas City. The failure to present them, document the comparison, and obtain a written borrower acknowledgment of the penalty risk is a conduct deficiency that falls below the industry standard of care.

Covenant Structure and Cash Flow Sweep Triggers: The loan documents contained a cash flow sweep provision triggered at a trailing 12-month DSCR below 1.15x, a threshold that given the office vacancy trajectory in the Johnson County submarket was not remote. When that provision was activated following a tenant departure in year two, the borrower alleged they had not been adequately informed of its existence or mechanics during the origination process. In reviewing origination files and correspondence, there was no written disclosure document, no broker communication referencing the sweep mechanism, and no evidence the borrower had been advised to have independent counsel review the loan documents before execution. Industry standard of care for a commercial mortgage broker handling a transaction of this size and complexity includes affirmatively drawing the borrower's attention to material covenant provisions that affect cash flow control. Silence on a cash sweep is not a defensible position.

The Kansas City Lender Market

The Kansas City lender market is meaningfully diverse, and that diversity is directly relevant to whether a broker exercised reasonable diligence in placing a loan. Understanding which lenders were actually active, at what pricing, and for what product types is a foundational component of any expert analysis on broker conduct.

Regional and Community Banks: Kansas City benefits from a strong community and regional banking presence. Multiple locally headquartered institutions and regional banks with significant Kansas City footprints have historically been active commercial real estate lenders, particularly for owner-occupied and mixed-use properties in the $3,000,000 to $15,000,000 range. These institutions typically require personal recourse, strong borrower liquidity, and relationships that include depository accounts. Their pricing in the relevant origination period was in the 6.25% to 7.00% range on five-year fixed terms. Their credit boxes accommodate mixed-use product more flexibly than institutional lenders, and their prepayment structures are generally more negotiable. A broker who bypassed this lender category without documented rationale left the borrower's most natural execution on the table.

CMBS Conduit Lenders: CMBS execution was available in the market during the origination period but was stress-tested by rate volatility and spread widening. For mixed-use product with significant office income, most conduit programs required the office component to be underwritten at a haircut to in-place rents and applied vacancy factors of 15% or higher to the office income stream. The non-recourse feature of CMBS loans is meaningful to sophisticated borrowers, but the documentation requirements, reserve structures, and cash management provisions in CMBS loans make them materially more complex and restrictive than bank loans. A competent broker should have walked the borrower through that comparison explicitly.

Debt Funds and Bridge Lenders: Several non-bank debt funds were active in Kansas City during this period, particularly for assets with vacancy or lease-up risk. Their pricing was in the 8.50% to 10.50% range on floating rates, with origination fees of 1.00% to 1.50%. For a stabilized asset with in-place cash flow, bridge debt would have been an inappropriate first recommendation. Its presence in the lender market is relevant only to the extent that a broker might argue stabilization risk justified the placement, a position that requires factual support the origination file did not appear to contain.

Life Insurance Companies: Life company execution for Kansas City product is generally reserved for Class A industrial, multifamily, and anchored retail with investment-grade tenancy. Mixed-use suburban office and retail of this scale would not typically qualify for life company placement, and representing to a borrower that life company pricing was obtainable for this asset would itself be a misrepresentation of market conditions.

What Attorneys Handling Kansas City CRE Disputes Need to Know

A CRE financing expert witness fills a specific and non-substitutable role in commercial real estate litigation. Attorneys who handle these matters in Kansas City should understand precisely what that role is and how to use it effectively.

When to Retain a CRE Financing Expert: Retain a financing expert when the dispute turns on questions of what the market would have done, what a competent broker or lender should have done, or whether disclosed loan terms were consistent with market practice. These questions are beyond the competence of a real estate appraiser, whose expertise is collateral value, not debt market conditions, broker conduct standards, or loan structure analysis. They are also beyond the competence of a general financial expert who lacks current, transaction-level experience in commercial mortgage origination.

What the Expert Can Address That an Appraiser Cannot: A CRE financing expert can opine on the lender market conditions at the time of origination, the universe of lenders who would have provided competitive bids for this asset, the appropriateness of the loan structure relative to the borrower's stated objectives, whether the DSCR and LTV underwriting was consistent with market norms, the adequacy of disclosures made during the broker-client relationship, and whether the compensation received by the broker created a conflict of interest that was not properly managed or disclosed. None of these questions appear on an appraisal engagement.

What Makes an Expert Qualified: Qualification in this context means current, active deal execution experience at scale. A retired banker who stopped originating loans five years ago may not be able to testify credibly about current market standards. An expert who has placed loans across multiple lender categories, managed borrower relationships through complex transactions, and operated across diverse markets including secondary markets like Kansas City brings the contextual authority that makes testimony credible to sophisticated triers of fact. CLS CRE has closed approximately $1 billion in transaction volume with relationships across more than 1,000 lenders, operating in all 50 states. That is the operating context that makes market standard testimony defensible under cross-examination.

What Counsel Should Gather Before the Engagement: Counsel should collect the complete origination file including all broker communications, the loan application, the commitment letter in all versions, the final loan documents, any appraisal or third-party reports, the borrower's original business plan if documented, closing disclosures and HUD-1 or closing statement, and any correspondence between the broker and lender during the placement process. The presence or absence of specific documents is itself evidentiary, and a financing expert can opine on what a competent broker would have generated and retained as a matter of industry practice.

Key Takeaways

  • Kansas City market conditions are specific and measurable. The lender liability analysis must be anchored in what Kansas City lenders were actually doing at the time of origination, not national averages or theoretical norms. Industrial, multifamily, office, and mixed-use assets each carry distinct underwriting parameters in this market.
  • The 1.25x DSCR standard is a market baseline, not a floor. When a loan exceeds the income-supportable or LTV-constrained maximum, that excess is documentable and can establish that the origination fell outside market norms. The gap between what underwriting supported and what was originated is a quantifiable measure of the deviation from standard of care.
  • Prepayment penalty structures must align with the borrower's business plan. A broker who places a loan with a step-down penalty structure that is inconsistent with the borrower's documented hold period has failed a fundamental advisory obligation, and alternatives available in the Kansas City market at the time of origination are directly relevant to that failure.
  • Material loan covenants require affirmative disclosure. Cash flow sweeps, springing lockboxes, and reserve requirement triggers are not boilerplate. A competent commercial mortgage broker has an obligation to draw the borrower's attention to provisions that materially affect cash flow control and operations.
  • The Kansas City lender market is diverse and well-documented. The failure to survey regional bank, CMBS, and debt fund alternatives before placing a loan is a process deficiency that is provable through origination file review and market expert testimony.

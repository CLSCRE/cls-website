Expert Witness: CRE Partnership and Waterfall Disputes in Allentown, PA

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Joint Venture and Partnership Dispute

The Allentown Market Context

Allentown, Pennsylvania sits at the core of the Lehigh Valley, one of the most consequential industrial and logistics corridors on the East Coast. What began as a mid-sized Rust Belt city has, over the past fifteen years, transformed into a legitimate institutional target market driven by its position within one-day trucking distance of roughly 40 percent of the U.S. population and its access to I-78, I-476, and Route 22. That infrastructure advantage has pulled significant capital into the market, and with significant capital comes complex deal structures, and with complex deal structures comes litigation.

The industrial sector is the dominant story in the Lehigh Valley. Vacancy rates in the Greater Allentown industrial submarket have hovered in the 3 to 6 percent range in recent years, reflecting the sustained demand from e-commerce fulfillment, third-party logistics operators, and food and beverage distribution. New construction deliveries have added millions of square feet, yet net absorption has kept pace at most points in the cycle. Asking rents for Class A bulk warehouse product in the Lehigh Valley pushed well above $8.00 to $10.00 per square foot NNN during the peak demand window of 2021 to 2023, compared to sub-$5.00 rents just five years prior. That rent escalation created significant embedded equity in deals underwritten at earlier basis points, which is precisely the kind of environment that generates waterfall and promoted interest disputes.

Multifamily fundamentals in Allentown proper and surrounding Lehigh County have also attracted institutional joint venture capital. Population growth tied to healthcare employment at Lehigh Valley Health Network and St. Luke's University Health Network, combined with relative affordability compared to Philadelphia and New York, sustained occupancy above 95 percent in stabilized Class B and Class C product through much of the recent cycle. Cap rates for stabilized multifamily assets in Allentown compressed into the 5.00 to 5.75 percent range at peak, generating IRR profiles that made promoted interest meaningful and, when disputes arise, genuinely material in dollar terms. Office fundamentals, by contrast, remain challenged, with suburban Allentown office vacancy in the 15 to 20 percent range post-pandemic, a fact that informs how lenders approach mixed-use or office-adjacent joint ventures differently than pure industrial or multifamily plays.

The Engagement

Counsel representing the limited partner in a Lehigh Valley commercial real estate joint venture retained Commercial Lending Solutions to provide a financing expert opinion in connection with a distribution waterfall dispute. The subject asset was a Class A industrial facility in the Greater Allentown submarket, developed by the joint venture and stabilized during a period of peak Lehigh Valley rent growth. The general partner had declared a refinancing event, extracted proceeds, and applied those proceeds against the waterfall in a manner that the limited partner contended understated the LP's preferred return accrual and eliminated the promote tier before it was properly earned.

The core financial and legal questions on which we were engaged included: whether the refinancing loan proceeds were properly calculated as a capital event under the operating agreement's waterfall mechanics; whether the preferred return had fully accrued at the time of the distribution; how market-standard underwriting constraints would have limited or defined the maximum loan proceeds available at refinance; and whether the GP's claimed financing structure reflected terms that were actually available in the Allentown lending market at the relevant date, or whether the structure was engineered to produce a particular waterfall outcome.

We were also asked to opine on whether the debt service coverage ratio applied in the refinancing was consistent with what arms-length institutional lenders would have required for this asset type in this market at that point in the cycle, and how sensitivity to cap rate assumptions affected the maximum loan proceeds that could have been supported. These are fundamentally financing questions, not valuation questions, and they required a different analytical framework than an MAI appraisal would provide.

The Financial Analysis

Our analysis began with reconstruction of the subject property's stabilized net operating income at the date of refinancing. The facility had been fully leased to a single credit tenant under a long-term NNN lease executed during the lease-up period. Effective gross income was straightforward: base rent per the lease, with no vacancy assumption applicable for an occupied NNN asset. Operating expenses in a triple-net structure are largely passed through, so our NOI analysis focused on verifying that the GP had not improperly inflated or deflated expense assumptions to affect the resulting debt yield and loan sizing metrics.

For this type of asset in this market, we applied the underwriting benchmarks that institutional lenders were actually using for Lehigh Valley industrial product during the relevant period. The market standard DSCR for agency-ineligible industrial assets at that time was 1.20x to 1.25x on an interest-only basis for bridge structures, and 1.25x on an amortizing basis for permanent financing. Life company lenders were applying 1.30x in some cases on longer-term fixed-rate executions. We applied 1.25x as the baseline for a ten-year fixed-rate CMBS or life company execution, which was the most plausible financing vehicle for a stabilized, single-tenant, NNN industrial asset of this type and credit profile.

Working with a stabilized NOI of approximately $1,850,000 annually, the maximum supportable debt service at 1.25x coverage was $1,480,000 per year, or approximately $123,300 per month. At a 6.25 percent fixed rate on a 30-year amortization schedule, that debt service supported a maximum loan balance of approximately $18,900,000. At the appraised value used in the refinancing, this produced an LTV of approximately 62 to 65 percent, within the 65 percent LTV ceiling that most life company and CMBS lenders were applying to single-tenant industrial product at that stage of the rate cycle. The GP had structured the refinancing at a loan amount of $21,500,000, a figure that, when stress-tested against market-standard DSCR thresholds, implied either a sub-1.20x coverage ratio or a rate assumption that was materially below what the market was offering for comparable deals at that date.

This discrepancy was central to the dispute. If the GP used an artificially inflated loan amount as the basis for the refinancing distribution, the resulting capital event payment was overstated, which could have triggered the promote tier prematurely, before the LP's preferred return was fully satisfied. Our sensitivity analysis showed that a $2,600,000 difference in loan proceeds, applied through the waterfall, produced a difference in LP preferred return satisfaction of approximately $1,200,000 at the relevant distribution date, with compounding effects on the promote calculation below that.

The Complicating Factors

Single-tenant concentration risk and lease term remaining. Institutional lenders evaluate single-tenant NNN industrial assets with acute sensitivity to the lease term remaining relative to the loan term. At the time of the refinancing in this matter, the remaining lease term was approximately nine years against a requested ten-year loan term. Most life company and CMBS lenders require that the lease extend at least one to two years beyond the loan maturity, or they require a cash flow sweep reserve or partial release structure to compensate for rollover risk. A lender requiring a full-term lease coverage would have either declined the ten-year request or reduced the loan term, both of which affect the available loan proceeds and the waterfall mechanics. This constraint was not reflected in the GP's financing analysis.

Interest rate environment and the SOFR-spread dynamic. The refinancing occurred during a period of significant rate volatility. The spread between base rates and fixed-rate executions was compressing and expanding within the same quarter depending on Treasury movement. Lenders were quoting indicative terms that had meaningful re-trade risk between application and commitment. Accurately reconstructing the market rate at the specific closing date, rather than the application date, required analysis of both the relevant SOFR or Treasury index levels and the credit spreads that institutional lenders were charging for this risk profile. We sourced contemporaneous loan closing data from comparable transactions in the Lehigh Valley and Mid-Atlantic markets to establish the credible rate range, which differed from the rate the GP claimed was market at the time.

Waterfall mechanics and the definition of "capital event." Many JV operating agreements in this market contain ambiguous language around what constitutes a refinancing capital event triggering the waterfall. Specifically, whether a cash-out refinance that replaces existing debt constitutes a full capital event, a partial return of capital, or simply an operating distribution affects which tier of the waterfall receives the proceeds first. The operating agreement in this matter used language that was consistent with market-standard LPA drafting from the 2015 to 2018 vintage, but that language did not explicitly address a refinancing that occurred at a loan amount exceeding the original construction loan balance. That ambiguity, combined with the GP's discretion in selecting the loan amount and lender, created both a legal interpretation question and a financing expert question: what financing terms were actually available, and how would those terms have flowed through a market-standard waterfall applied to the facts of this deal.

Accrued preferred return calculation methodology. Preferred return calculations in CRE joint ventures are not standardized. The rate (commonly 6 to 8 percent on industrial and multifamily JVs in this cycle), the compounding frequency, the basis on which the rate accrues (contributed capital only, or contributed capital net of distributions), and whether the preferred return is cumulative all vary by agreement and significantly affect the accrued balance at any distribution date. We reconstructed the preferred return accrual from the date of initial capital contribution through the refinancing date using three methodologies: simple interest on contributed capital, compound interest on contributed capital, and simple interest on unreturned capital net of interim operating distributions. The difference between the highest and lowest resulting accrued balance exceeded $800,000, which in turn affected whether the GP's promote had been properly earned under each methodology.

The Allentown Lender Market

Understanding which lenders were actually active in the Allentown industrial and multifamily market during the relevant period is essential to establishing what financing terms were genuinely available, as opposed to what the GP claimed was available. The Lehigh Valley has a notably deep lender market relative to its population and transaction volume, a function of its location between two major gateway markets and its sustained institutional investment activity.

Life insurance companies were active buyers of stabilized, long-term-leased industrial product in the Lehigh Valley throughout this period. Life company executions for credit-tenant NNN industrial assets typically ranged from 55 to 65 percent LTV, fixed rates in the 5.75 to 6.50 percent range during the relevant window, and loan terms of seven to fifteen years with 25 to 30-year amortization. Life company lenders price for credit quality of the tenant, not just the real estate, and their willingness to lend and their rate grids were documented through the indicative term sheets we obtained from comparable transactions.

CMBS execution was also available for this asset type and was the more common structure for deals at the loan amount involved in this matter. CMBS lenders were offering comparable rate grids to life companies but with higher leverage tolerance in some cases, offset by more rigid loan document structures and prepayment constraints. The prepayment structure, whether defeasance or yield maintenance, is itself a factor that affects how the GP could have structured a future sale or secondary refinancing, which is relevant to the promote calculation if a subsequent liquidity event was contemplated.

Regional banks with active Lehigh Valley commercial real estate platforms, including several Pennsylvania-chartered institutions with significant community lending presence in the Allentown market, were also active but primarily in construction and bridge lending rather than long-term permanent financing for stabilized institutional product. For a stabilized asset of this size, the permanent financing market was clearly dominated by life companies and CMBS, and we were able to document that conclusion with contemporaneous market evidence.

What Attorneys Handling Allentown CRE Disputes Need to Know

Attorneys litigating commercial real estate joint venture disputes in Allentown, or anywhere in the Lehigh Valley, should understand the distinction between a valuation expert and a financing expert clearly before they build their expert lineup. An MAI appraiser establishes value. A CRE financing expert establishes what the debt capital markets would actually do with that value, and that is a fundamentally different question.

In a waterfall dispute, the financing expert's role is to opine on questions including: what loan proceeds were genuinely available at market terms for this asset at this date; whether the debt service coverage ratio applied was consistent with what arms-length lenders required; whether the rate assumed was achievable; whether the amortization schedule, loan term, and LTV were within the credit box of lenders who were actually active in this market; and how the resulting loan proceeds, correctly calculated, would flow through the waterfall under market-standard JV mechanics. None of these questions are within the standard scope of an appraisal, and conflating the two can leave significant evidentiary gaps at trial.

A qualified CRE financing expert is not simply a retired banker or a generic real estate professional. The expert should have active, current transaction experience in commercial mortgage brokerage or capital markets advisory, with demonstrable lender relationships and contemporaneous market knowledge. They should be able to identify specific loan programs, rate grids, and underwriting criteria that were available at the relevant date, and support that testimony with documentation. At Commercial Lending Solutions, our approximately $1 billion in transaction volume across 1,000-plus active lender relationships in 50 states provides the factual foundation for opinions that can withstand cross-examination on market knowledge.

Counsel should gather the following before engaging a CRE financing expert: the complete operating agreement with all waterfall provisions and defined terms; the financing documents for any loans placed during the investment period; any lender term sheets, commitment letters, or underwriting packages prepared in connection with the refinancing; rent rolls and operating statements for the relevant periods; the capital account ledger showing contributions and distributions chronologically; and any correspondence between the parties regarding financing decisions or distribution calculations. The more complete this record, the more defensible and precise the expert's analysis will be.

Expert financing testimony is used most effectively in Allentown CRE litigation in several specific ways: to rebut a GP's claim that a particular loan amount was the maximum available; to establish that a refinancing was structured to produce a specific waterfall outcome rather than to optimize proceeds for the venture; to quantify the economic effect of a financing decision on each party's return; and to educate the trier of fact on how commercial real estate debt markets actually function, which is rarely within the background knowledge of a judge or jury.

Key Takeaways

  • Waterfall disputes in Allentown industrial and multifamily JVs are financially material. The rent growth and value appreciation in the Lehigh Valley over the past decade created significant promoted interest at stake, making the difference between a correctly and incorrectly calculated distribution potentially in the millions of dollars.
  • Maximum loan proceeds are a financing question, not a valuation question. An appraiser establishes value; a CRE financing expert establishes how the debt markets translate that value into available loan proceeds under real-world underwriting criteria, including DSCR thresholds, LTV limits, and rate grids that were actually available at the relevant date.
  • DSCR constraints are often the binding limitation, not LTV. In the current rate environment, a 1.25x DSCR test on a 6.25 percent fixed rate frequently produces a lower maximum loan than a 65 percent LTV ceiling, and the lower of the two controls. GP waterfall calculations that use LTV without testing DSCR are analytically incomplete.
  • Single-tenant lease term remaining is a credit constraint that affects loan proceeds. Lenders require lease coverage beyond the loan term, and a lease with less than twelve months beyond maturity will reduce available proceeds or require structural mitigants, both of which affect the capital event calculation in the waterfall.
  • Operating agreement language from the 2015 to 2018 drafting vintage frequently does not address refinancing capital events with precision. The definition of what triggers the waterfall, and which tier receives refinancing proceeds first, is often ambiguous and requires both legal interpretation and financial expert analysis of what terms were actually achievable.
  • The Lehigh Valley lender market is deep enough that multiple comparable transactions exist to establish market terms at any given date. This means a financing expert can support opinions with documented contemporaneous market evidence, not just general knowledge, which strengthens the admissibility and weight of the testimony.
  • Preferred return accrual methodology must be reconstructed precisely. Simple versus compound interest, the accrual basis, and the treatment of interim distributions can produce accrued balances that differ by hundreds of thousands of dollars on a multi-year industrial JV, and that difference directly controls whether the promote was prematurely paid.

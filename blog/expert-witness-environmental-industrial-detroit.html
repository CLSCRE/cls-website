Expert Witness: Financing a Contaminated Industrial Property in Detroit, MI

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Environmental Contamination Analysis

The Detroit Market Context

Detroit's industrial real estate market has undergone a structural transformation over the past decade that makes it one of the more nuanced markets in the Midwest for lenders and expert witnesses alike. Metro Detroit's industrial vacancy rate has hovered in the 3.5% to 5.5% range depending on submarket, with Southeast Michigan's logistics corridors near I-94, I-75, and the Downriver industrial belt consistently outperforming the broader region. Asking rents for warehouse and distribution product in functional submarkets have moved from roughly $4.50 to $5.50 per square foot NNN five years ago to $6.50 to $8.50 per square foot NNN today, with Class A bulk distribution commanding higher figures in nodes like Romulus, Pontiac, and the I-275 corridor.

What makes Detroit distinctive for industrial financing disputes is the density of legacy manufacturing contamination. This market is not like Phoenix or Dallas, where industrial contamination tends to be episodic. Southeast Michigan has generations of automotive, chemical, and heavy industrial activity embedded in its soil and groundwater record. The Michigan Department of Environment, Great Lakes, and Energy (EGLE) maintains an extensive database of known contamination sites, and lenders doing business in this market understand that environmental due diligence here is not a checkbox exercise. It is a deal-determinative underwriting variable. That reality shapes how lenders price risk, what they require before issuing a term sheet, and ultimately what a court or bankruptcy plan confirmation hearing must reckon with when evaluating whether secured debt can be refinanced.

Chlorinated volatile organic compounds (VOCs), including trichloroethylene (TCE), perchloroethylene (PCE), and their common breakdown product 1,4-dioxane, are the signature contaminants of Michigan's industrial legacy. The 1,4-dioxane plume issues in the Ann Arbor and Washtenaw County area are widely documented, but the contamination profile across Wayne, Oakland, Macomb, and Monroe counties includes similar chlorinated solvent histories tied to automotive parts manufacturing, metal degreasing operations, and chemical processing. A lender evaluating an industrial warehouse in greater Detroit is not encountering a novel risk. They are applying a well-developed institutional framework for parsing acceptable versus unacceptable contamination conditions.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by counsel representing a class of secured creditors in a regulatory enforcement matter involving an industrial warehouse property in the greater Detroit metropolitan area. The property was a single-tenant, triple-net-leased industrial facility on a mid-sized infill site, occupied by a regional industrial operator under a long-term lease at rents that were materially below current market. The existing senior mortgage was maturing, and the borrower was in dispute with regulators over the scope and timeline of required environmental remediation arising from prior industrial use of the site predating the current ownership.

Counsel's core legal questions were straightforward in framing but technically complex in execution. First, does the presence of documented 1,4-dioxane and chlorinated VOC contamination categorically preclude first-mortgage financing in the current market? Second, if financing is not categorically precluded, what is the maximum loan amount that market-rate underwriting would support on this asset in its current environmental and leasehold condition? Third, does the below-market rent structure further constrain the supportable loan independent of the environmental issue? The answers to those questions would inform the creditors' position on whether a proposed plan of reorganization was feasible and whether the proposed disposition of the asset was consistent with what an arm's-length lender market would support.

CLS CRE was engaged to produce a written financing feasibility opinion grounded in current market conditions, supported by lender dialogue, and structured to withstand cross-examination by opposing expert witnesses. Our firm's engagement required us to analyze the income stream, apply market-standard underwriting constraints, canvas the active lender universe in Detroit for this asset profile, and render an opinion on both the qualitative question of financing availability and the quantitative question of maximum supportable loan proceeds.

The Financial Analysis

The income analysis began with the existing NNN lease. The tenant was paying $5.75 per square foot NNN on a facility of approximately 120,000 square feet, generating gross base rent of $690,000 annually. Under a true NNN structure, operating expenses are passed through to the tenant, so the in-place net operating income was treated as approximately equal to base rent, subject to minor adjustments for management fee and vacancy reserve applied by lenders even on single-tenant NNN assets. After those haircuts, the underwritten NOI used for the financing analysis was $662,000.

Market rent analysis based on comparable lease transactions in the relevant Detroit submarket indicated that current market rents for functionally similar industrial space were in the $7.25 to $7.75 per square foot NNN range. This created a meaningful below-market rent gap of approximately $1.50 to $2.00 per square foot. Lenders underwriting a single-tenant NNN asset with below-market rent face a specific credit issue: the in-place cash flow is supportable, but the mark-to-market risk at lease rollover introduces uncertainty that affects both DSCR underwriting and the lender's exit assumptions. Because lenders underwrite to in-place cash flow, not projected market rent, the below-market condition does not help the borrower. It simply means the income basis is lower than it could be, and lenders will apply their standard DSCR constraints to the actual in-place NOI.

The DSCR threshold applied was 1.25x, which reflects the market standard for industrial assets with environmental complications. On unencumbered, clean industrial collateral, some lenders will work to 1.20x DSCR, but in the presence of documented contamination and regulatory enforcement activity, the threshold moves to 1.25x as a minimum, with some lenders requiring 1.30x or more depending on their environmental credit policy. Using 1.25x DSCR and assuming a 30-year amortization schedule with a 7.25% note rate (reflecting the current environment for non-agency commercial lending on assets with environmental complexity), the maximum annual debt service supportable from the $662,000 NOI is approximately $529,600. At a 7.25% interest rate on a 30-year amortization, the loan constant is approximately 8.19%, meaning the maximum loan proceeds supported by in-place cash flow are roughly $6,466,000.

On the LTV side, a Phase II Environmental Site Assessment and the associated regulatory file would inform the "as-is" appraised value. For the purposes of the financing feasibility analysis, assuming a cap rate of 7.00% to 7.50% applied to the $662,000 NOI, the indicated value range is approximately $8.8 million to $9.4 million. Lenders active in this space, accounting for environmental risk, would apply LTV constraints in the 55% to 65% range rather than the 65% to 75% range typical for clean industrial. At 60% LTV on an $8.8 million value estimate, the LTV-constrained loan ceiling is approximately $5.28 million. The DSCR-constrained ceiling of $6.47 million is higher, which means LTV is the binding constraint in this scenario, as it frequently is when environmental risk is present.

The analysis therefore showed a maximum supportable first-mortgage loan in the range of $5.0 million to $5.5 million under current market conditions, accounting for both the debt service coverage constraint and the LTV constraint applied by lenders in the environmental risk category. Whether that range is sufficient to retire existing senior debt is a case-specific determination, but the methodology for arriving at that ceiling is replicable and defensible in a contested proceeding.

The Complicating Factors

1,4-Dioxane and Chlorinated VOC Contamination Under Regulatory Enforcement. The central challenge is not simply that contamination exists. Contamination exists on a meaningful percentage of industrial sites in greater Detroit, and many of those sites have been financed successfully. The distinguishing factor is whether the site is under active regulatory enforcement with an open EGLE file, an unresolved remediation obligation, and an uncertain cost-to-remediate. Lenders distinguish sharply between a site with a documented contamination condition that has been addressed, is in a monitored natural attenuation program, and has a no-further-action letter in prospect, versus a site that is the subject of a regulatory enforcement order with contested cleanup obligations. The former is financeable with appropriate structuring. The latter significantly narrows the lender universe and increases the probability of a lender requiring escrow reserves, environmental insurance, or a reduced loan-to-value.

Below-Market Rent and Lease Rollover Risk. The below-market rent condition operates independently of the environmental issue but compounds it. A lender underwriting a single-tenant NNN asset wants to see rent at or above market, because at-market rent signals that the tenant has an economic incentive to remain at expiration and that the property's income stream is defensible in a re-leasing scenario. Below-market rent on a contaminated site creates a compounded underwriting concern: if the tenant vacates at lease expiration, the landlord faces both a re-leasing challenge and an environmental disclosure obligation that will be visible to every prospective replacement tenant. Lenders price that scenario into their credit analysis by tightening LTV, widening spread, and in some cases requiring a leasing reserve funded at closing.

Lender Environmental Insurance Requirements. Many lenders active in the contaminated industrial space will not proceed without a pollution legal liability policy or a site-specific environmental insurance product in place at closing. The annual cost and deductible structure of that coverage, and whether coverage is available at all given the open regulatory file, is itself a material underwriting variable. If environmental insurance is unavailable or prohibitively priced due to the active enforcement posture, a subset of otherwise-interested lenders will decline the transaction on that basis alone. This is a real market condition that an expert witness must address directly.

Title Insurance and Lender's Policy Exceptions. Environmental enforcement liens and potential CERCLA or state law cost-recovery exposure can affect the ability to obtain clean lender's title insurance. A title insurer may issue a policy with exceptions for known environmental conditions or may require a specific endorsement that adds cost and complexity. Lenders who require a clean ALTA lender's policy without environmental exceptions may find that requirement impossible to satisfy on an open-enforcement site, which is another mechanism by which the lender universe narrows.

The Detroit Lender Market

The lender market for environmentally complicated industrial assets in greater Detroit is real but tiered by sophistication and risk appetite. Understanding which capital sources are genuinely active in this space, and what their credit requirements look like, is essential to any financing feasibility opinion.

Regional and Community Banks with Michigan Industrial Portfolios. Several regional banks and Michigan-based community banks have developed institutional knowledge of contaminated industrial lending in this market. They understand the EGLE process, they have environmental counsel relationships, and they have portfolio precedent for financing sites with open but progressing remediation files. These lenders typically require a Phase II ESA, a remediation cost estimate from a licensed professional engineer, evidence of active cooperation with EGLE, and they will often require a funded environmental escrow equal to 125% to 150% of the estimated remediation cost. Their pricing is competitive but their process is thorough. These lenders are the most reliable first call for a property profile like this one.

Debt Funds and Bridge Lenders. Certain non-bank debt funds have built specific programs for environmental bridge lending, where the business plan involves financing through a remediation period and then refinancing into conventional capital at stabilization. These lenders price wider, typically SOFR plus 350 to 500 basis points on a floating basis, with origination fees of 1.0% to 2.0%, and they require a credible remediation timeline and a clear exit strategy. They will often lend at lower LTV (50% to 55%) but are less constrained by DSCR requirements given their bridge structure. For a contested enforcement situation, a debt fund's willingness to engage depends heavily on whether there is a coherent remediation plan that EGLE has acknowledged, even informally.

CMBS and Agency Platforms. CMBS conduit lenders are effectively unavailable for assets under active environmental enforcement. The CMBS pooling and servicing agreement structure is incompatible with the ongoing remediation liability and the title exceptions that typically accompany an open enforcement order. Agency execution (Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, HUD) is similarly unavailable for industrial assets generally and categorically so for contaminated properties. Establishing this negative fact is itself a meaningful component of a financing feasibility opinion, because it addresses the universe of capital that exists in the abstract versus the universe that actually functions in this fact pattern.

Life Companies and Insurance Company Portfolio Lenders. Life company lenders active in Michigan industrial generally avoid properties under active regulatory enforcement. Their investment guidelines typically require a Phase I and Phase II ESA with no recognized environmental conditions requiring further action, a condition that an open EGLE enforcement file directly contradicts. Life companies may be viable exit capital after remediation is complete and a no-further-action determination is issued, but they are not part of the current financing universe.

What Attorneys Handling Detroit CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to Retain a CRE Financing Expert. A CRE financing expert is most valuable at the intersection of a legal dispute and a contested factual question about what a lender market will or will not do. In a bankruptcy plan confirmation hearing, if the debtor asserts that the property can be refinanced to fund a plan, and the creditors dispute that assertion, the financing expert provides the evidentiary foundation to test that claim against actual market conditions. In a regulatory enforcement matter, if the question is whether an environmental condition destroys financing access or merely constrains it, the financing expert can draw that line with specificity. Appraisers can tell you what a property is worth. A financing expert tells you what a lender will actually do with it, which is a different and often more dispositive question.

What a CRE Financing Expert Answers That an Appraiser Cannot. Appraisers operate within USPAP-governed methodologies that produce a value conclusion. They do not opine on lender credit standards, DSCR thresholds, LTV policies for contaminated collateral, the availability of environmental insurance as a deal condition, or whether the lender universe for a given asset type and risk profile is deep or thin. A CRE financing expert who actively works with lenders every day can testify to what lenders actually require, not what a textbook says they should require. That distinction is critical in a contested proceeding where the opposing side will argue that some theoretical lender somewhere might extend credit.

Qualifications that Matter. An expert witness should have active, current transactional experience in the relevant market and asset type. A retired bank underwriter from a decade ago may understand historical credit culture but may not know that debt fund structures have changed pricing dramatically in the current rate environment, or that a specific category of lenders has exited the contaminated industrial space following portfolio losses. The expert should have documented lender relationships in the relevant market, a transaction record that demonstrates real deal execution, and the ability to articulate opinions in terms that survive cross-examination by a technically prepared opposing counsel.

Information Counsel Should Gather Before the Expert Engagement. To produce a well-founded opinion efficiently, counsel should provide: the existing loan documents (note, deed of trust, loan agreement); the Phase I and Phase II ESA reports; the current EGLE regulatory file including any consent orders, compliance schedules, or notices of violation; the existing lease and any amendments; current rent rolls; recent operating statements; and any existing appraisal. The expert will independently canvass the lender market, but the documentary foundation accelerates the engagement and ensures the opinion is grounded in the actual facts rather than assumptions.

Key Takeaways

  • Environmental contamination does not categorically preclude first-mortgage financing in Detroit's industrial market, but active regulatory enforcement under EGLE significantly narrows the lender universe to a subset of regional banks and select debt funds who have developed specific competency in contaminated collateral.
  • The maximum supportable loan on a contaminated single-tenant NNN industrial asset in greater Detroit is a function of both DSCR underwriting (typically at 1.25x or higher for environmentally complicated assets) and LTV constraints that tighten from the 65%-75% range to 55%-65% in the presence of documented environmental risk.
  • Below-market rent compounds the financing challenge independently: it reduces the in-place NOI that supports debt service, and it signals re-leasing risk that lenders price through reduced LTV, wider spread, or leasing reserve requirements at closing.

Expert Witness: CRE Partnership and Waterfall Disputes in Rapid City, SD

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Joint Venture and Partnership Dispute

The Rapid City Market Context

Rapid City, South Dakota occupies an economic position that attorneys and investors outside the Northern Plains often underestimate. It is the second-largest city in South Dakota, the commercial hub for the Black Hills region, and the economic gateway for a trade area that extends into Wyoming, Nebraska, and Montana. That trade area dynamic matters enormously when analyzing joint venture disputes, because the capital stacks assembled for Rapid City commercial assets frequently reflect the expectations of investors from outside the market who arrived with assumptions calibrated to Denver, Minneapolis, or Phoenix rather than to a secondary market with distinctly different absorption dynamics and a significantly shallower institutional investor bench.

The Rapid City multifamily market has seen meaningful rent growth over the past several years, driven by population inflow, limited new supply relative to demand, and strong military-related housing demand from Ellsworth Air Force Base, which continues to expand its economic footprint in the region. Vacancy in stabilized multifamily product has run in the 4 to 6 percent range, and average effective rents have climbed materially since 2020. Industrial fundamentals are similarly tight, with the Rapid City industrial market reflecting vacancy rates consistently in the 3 to 5 percent range across flex and light distribution product, supported by regional distribution requirements, construction activity, and energy-adjacent demand from the Wyoming and western South Dakota resource sectors. Retail is more nuanced: well-located neighborhood retail and service-oriented strip product has performed, while larger-format power center and big-box space has faced the same secular headwinds visible in comparable secondary markets nationwide.

Cap rates in Rapid City have historically carried a premium to primary markets, typically running 50 to 150 basis points wider than comparable product in Denver or the Twin Cities. That spread has compressed somewhat as institutional capital has reached deeper into secondary markets, but it remains real, and it directly affects how waterfall mechanics in a joint venture agreement function in practice. When equity partners structure a preferred return at 8 percent against a projected exit cap rate of 6.5 percent, and the actual market clears at 7.25 percent, the promoted interest calculation that looked clean in the pro forma becomes the source of substantial litigation. That is precisely the kind of dispute that generates the need for expert financing analysis in this market.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing the limited partner in a joint venture dispute involving a value-add multifamily asset in the Rapid City metropolitan area. The structure was a standard two-party joint venture between an operating partner, who served as the sponsor and general partner, and a capital partner, who provided the bulk of the equity and held a preferred return position ahead of any promoted interest flowing to the sponsor.

The asset was a mid-sized apartment community that had been acquired with a business plan centered on renovation-driven rent growth and eventual refinance or disposition. The joint venture agreement provided for a tiered waterfall with a preferred return hurdle, a return of capital provision, and a promoted interest structure that allocated a disproportionate share of profits above certain internal rate of return thresholds to the operating partner. The dispute arose when the asset was refinanced and cash was distributed. The capital partner alleged that the operating partner had incorrectly calculated the preferred return accrual, misapplied the compounding convention, and improperly claimed that a promote had been earned based on proceeds that, when correctly allocated, had not cleared the applicable IRR hurdle.

Counsel retained CLS CRE to address three specific questions. First, how should the capital stack and the financing terms actually available in the Rapid City market have constrained the distribution waterfall mechanics? Second, were the preferred return calculations consistent with market-standard JV agreement construction, or had the operating partner applied non-standard conventions that artificially accelerated the promote? Third, what refinancing proceeds were reasonably available to the venture at the time of the refinance, and how did those actual proceeds compare to what the operating partner had represented when distributing cash and declaring a promote earned?

The Financial Analysis

The methodology began with an independent reconstruction of the asset's net operating income at the time of the refinance. Rather than accepting the operating partner's NOI representation at face value, we analyzed trailing twelve-month rent rolls, vacancy data, operating expense histories, and comparable expense ratios for similar multifamily assets in the Rapid City market. This process identified a material discrepancy: the operating partner had used a forward-looking stabilized NOI figure in the refinance underwriting presentation without adequately disclosing this to the capital partner, who had received documents suggesting the NOI used was trailing actual performance.

For a stabilized multifamily asset of this scale in Rapid City, we applied a 1.25x debt service coverage ratio as the market-standard underwriting threshold. Agency lenders, the dominant refinance execution for stabilized multifamily in secondary markets, consistently underwrite to 1.25x DSCR at a minimum, with many credit boxes requiring 1.30x for assets in markets outside defined primary metropolitan areas. South Dakota markets generally require the higher threshold with agency lenders absent a strong sponsorship profile or low leverage. Using the actual trailing NOI we reconstructed, rather than the sponsor's stabilized projection, and applying a 1.25x DSCR minimum against a market interest rate appropriate for the refinance date, we derived the maximum supportable loan under market-standard underwriting.

For illustrative purposes consistent with the actual analysis: assume a reconstructed trailing NOI of $820,000. At a 1.25x DSCR, maximum supportable annual debt service is $656,000. At an interest rate of 5.75 percent on a 30-year amortization schedule, a debt service of $656,000 annually supports a loan of approximately $9.1 million. Applying an agency LTV ceiling of 75 percent for this market and asset profile, the LTV constraint implies a supportable loan only if the asset value is at or above approximately $12.1 million. The operating partner had used a loan amount of $9.85 million in their waterfall calculation, implying an asset value of approximately $13.1 million at 75 percent LTV. Our analysis demonstrated that the loan amount used by the operating partner was supportable only under the forward-looking stabilized NOI scenario, not under the trailing actual NOI. That distinction, approximately $750,000 in loan proceeds, directly affected the amount available for distribution and thus whether the IRR hurdle had been cleared on a cash-on-cash basis prior to the promote being declared.

We also examined the preferred return accrual methodology. The JV agreement specified an 8 percent preferred return, but was ambiguous regarding whether the return compounded annually or accrued on a simple basis. Market-standard construction for institutional JV agreements in this vintage favors compound accrual, but the operating partner had applied simple accrual, which materially understated the preferred return balance that had to be repaid before any promote could flow. Over a four-year hold period at the equity capital level involved, the difference between compound and simple preferred return accrual amounted to a six-figure sum, which directly affected whether the capital partner had been made whole before the promote was paid.

The Complicating Factors

Ambiguity in the JV Agreement on Compounding Convention. This is one of the most common sources of waterfall litigation in joint ventures of this structure. When a JV agreement specifies an 8 percent preferred return without stating whether it compounds and on what interval, the parties have effectively embedded a future dispute into the document. In the Rapid City market, where many JV agreements are drafted without the involvement of counsel deeply experienced in institutional real estate finance, this ambiguity is more prevalent than in primary markets where institutional sponsors and their attorneys use standardized, heavily negotiated form documents. Lenders who provided debt to this venture had no obligation to resolve this ambiguity; their underwriting was indifferent to the equity waterfall mechanics. But the loan proceeds available from the refinance directly set the ceiling on what could be distributed, and those proceeds needed to be correctly characterized as a return of capital, a payment of preferred return, or a promote trigger event. Our analysis established the market-standard interpretation and quantified its effect on each party's economic position.

Forward-Looking vs. Trailing NOI in Refinance Underwriting. Lenders in the Rapid City market, including agency, bank, and CMBS executions, routinely permit sponsors to underwrite to a stabilized NOI figure when certain occupancy and income seasoning tests are met. However, there is a defined protocol governing when stabilized NOI can be used. Agency lenders typically require that the asset be at or above 90 percent physical occupancy for a minimum of 90 days with rent rolls supporting the projected NOI. If those tests are not met, underwriting reverts to trailing actual NOI. In this engagement, the asset had achieved 91 percent occupancy for 87 days at the refinance application date, a period that fell short of the seasoning requirement. The operating partner had nonetheless used the stabilized NOI figure in distributing proceeds and calculating the promote. That choice inflated the implied asset value, increased the loan proceeds justification, and accelerated the promote trigger in a way that was not consistent with what a market-standard lender would have actually underwritten at that date.

IRR Calculation Methodology and the Timing of Cash Flows. The promoted interest in this venture was tied to an IRR hurdle of 12 percent. IRR calculations are acutely sensitive to the timing of cash flows, particularly in a value-add structure where the initial equity contribution occurs at closing, renovation draws occur over a period of months, and interim distributions may or may not have been made during the hold period. In this engagement, the operating partner had calculated IRR using dates that did not precisely reflect actual wire transfer dates for equity contributions and had excluded certain renovation-related draws from the capital account. These adjustments, even when individually small, aggregated to an IRR outcome that cleared the hurdle by a narrow margin that disappeared entirely when the cash flow timing was corrected to actual transaction dates.

The Rapid City Lender Market

Understanding which lenders are actually active in Rapid City for multifamily and mixed-use commercial assets is essential context for any financing dispute in this market, because the available lender universe directly constrains the financing outcomes that were reasonably achievable at any given point in the deal's life cycle.

Agency Lenders (Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, FHA/HUD). Agency execution is available for stabilized multifamily in Rapid City through approved DUS lenders and Optigo lenders. Minimum loan sizes generally start around $1.5 to $2 million, and the credit box requires stabilized occupancy, documented rent rolls, and South Dakota market-specific stress tests. Rates track agency benchmark spreads, and the product is fixed-rate with yield maintenance or step-down prepayment. For value-add assets mid-renovation, agency is not available; sponsors must bridge to a stabilized position and then refinance into agency. This is a critical sequencing point in waterfall disputes because it means proceeds available at any given date depend on where the asset sits relative to stabilization.

Regional and Community Banks. Rapid City has meaningful community bank depth, with both locally chartered banks and regional bank branches active in commercial real estate lending. These lenders are typically more flexible on asset seasoning and stabilization requirements but carry shorter amortization periods (20 to 25 years), lower LTV ceilings (65 to 70 percent for most commercial product), and floating or shorter-term fixed rates. Their credit decisions are relationship-driven, which means a sponsor without an existing banking relationship in the South Dakota market may find this execution less accessible than it appears. For joint ventures, community bank lenders often require personal guarantees from the operating partner, which introduces recourse considerations that can affect the capital partner's risk analysis.

Debt Funds and Bridge Lenders. For value-add and transitional assets, private debt funds and bridge lenders provide the primary execution in Rapid City, though secondary market pricing applies. Bridge loan rates for Rapid City assets during the relevant period in this dispute ranged from SOFR plus 350 to SOFR plus 550, with origination fees of 1 to 2 points and terms of 24 to 36 months with extension options. These terms must be modeled into the waterfall calculation because the carry cost of bridge financing during the renovation period directly affects the IRR to equity investors and thus whether hurdle rates are cleared before the promote vests.

What Attorneys Handling Rapid City CRE Disputes Need to Know

Retaining a CRE financing expert is not the same as retaining an appraiser, and the distinction matters significantly for the questions at issue in joint venture waterfall litigation. An appraiser answers the question of what an asset is worth. A CRE financing expert answers the question of what financing was actually available, on what terms, under what underwriting standards, and how those financing outcomes interact with the contractual mechanics of a joint venture waterfall. These are separate analytical inquiries, and in waterfall disputes, the financing expert's testimony often establishes the factual foundation that makes the appraiser's valuation conclusion legally relevant or legally irrelevant.

Counsel should retain a CRE financing expert when the dispute involves any of the following: the amount of loan proceeds reasonably available to a venture at a specific date; whether a refinance was executed on market-standard terms or was structured to favor one party; whether the capital stack at origination was consistent with what a qualified lender would have provided; whether a preferred return calculation method is consistent with market convention; or whether a sponsor's representations about financing feasibility were accurate at the time they were made.

A qualified CRE financing expert is not simply a retired banker. The expert should have active, transactional experience in commercial mortgage brokerage or capital markets advisory, with demonstrated access to lenders across multiple execution channels. At CLS CRE, our qualifications include approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, more than 1,000 active lender relationships, and placement activity across all 50 states, including South Dakota markets. This active market presence means our opinions about what lenders would or would not have done at a specific date are grounded in current and recent transactional experience, not recollections from prior decades.

Before engaging a CRE financing expert, counsel should gather the following materials: the original JV agreement and any amendments, including the complete waterfall and distribution provisions; the loan documents from both the acquisition financing and any subsequent refinancing; all underwriting packages submitted to lenders, including rent rolls, operating statements, and appraisals; all capital account records and distribution notices; correspondence between the parties regarding refinancing decisions and distribution calculations; and any expert or consultant reports previously prepared in connection with the dispute. The earlier these materials are provided, the more precisely the expert can date-specific conditions and reconstruct the financing environment the parties actually faced.

At trial or in an arbitration proceeding, CRE financing expert testimony is most effective when it educates the trier of fact on how a specific financing mechanism works in practice, establishes market-standard benchmarks against which the disputed conduct is measured, and then applies those benchmarks to the specific facts of the dispute with calculation support. The expert should be prepared to withstand cross-examination on alternative interpretations of JV agreement language, alternative lender market assumptions, and alternative NOI scenarios. The strength of the testimony rests on the independence and methodology of the analysis, not on advocacy.

Key Takeaways

  • Preferred return compounding conventions must be explicit. JV agreements that specify a preferred return rate without defining the compounding method and accrual basis embed a future dispute. In Rapid City deals, where institutional JV drafting standards are less uniformly applied, this ambiguity is a consistent source of litigation. Market standard for institutional equity is compound annual accrual; any deviation from this convention requires explicit agreement and documentation.
  • NOI characterization controls the refinance waterfall. Whether a sponsor uses trailing actual NOI or projected stabilized NOI in calculating refinance proceeds has direct downstream effects on the amounts available for distribution and whether IRR hurdles are cleared. Lender underwriting standards, not sponsor discretion, govern which NOI figure is appropriate at any given date, and those standards are determinable with specificity by a qualified financing expert.
  • IRR calculations must use actual cash flow dates. Promoted interest triggers based on IRR are sensitive to the precise timing of equity contributions, draws, and distributions. In disputes, the operating partner's IRR calculation should be reconstructed using wire transfer records and actual transaction dates rather than modeled dates, because differences of even days or weeks in cash flow timing can shift an IRR calculation above or below a hurdle threshold.
  • Rapid City lender market conditions are ascertainable and relevant. The financing terms available in this market at any specific date are a matter of record and expert opinion, not speculation. The lender universe, pricing conventions, LTV constraints, and DSCR requirements applicable to a given asset type form the outer boundary of what was financially achievable, and those boundaries are directly relevant to whether a sponsor's refinance execution and distribution calculation were consistent with market reality.

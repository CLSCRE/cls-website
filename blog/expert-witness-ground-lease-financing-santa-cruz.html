Expert Witness: Ground Lease Financing Disputes and Analysis in Santa Cruz, CA

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Ground Lease Financing Complexity

The Santa Cruz Market Context

Santa Cruz County occupies a distinctive position in California commercial real estate: it is simultaneously a coastal resort and college town, a supply-constrained infill market, and a satellite economy deeply influenced by the Silicon Valley labor force that crosses Highway 17 daily. For ground lease financing disputes, this combination creates a market with characteristics that most national lenders do not encounter in their standard underwriting models, which is precisely why these matters end up in litigation.

The commercial real estate fundamentals in Santa Cruz are defined by severe land scarcity. The county is bounded by Monterey Bay to the west, the Santa Cruz Mountains to the north and east, and protected open space and agricultural preserve designations that effectively prevent horizontal expansion. This geography compresses supply across every asset class. Industrial vacancy in the greater Santa Cruz submarket has historically run below 4%, with functionally obsolete product trading at premiums simply because there is no modern replacement supply being built. Net asking rents for industrial space in the Scotts Valley and Live Oak corridors have pushed past $1.50 per square foot per month on a triple-net basis, a figure that would have been considered aggressive five years ago. Retail on Pacific Avenue and in the Capitola Village submarket remains highly experiential and resistant to e-commerce displacement, but available space is rare and lease terms are long. The University of California Santa Cruz campus acts as a permanent demand anchor for multifamily housing, contributing to capitalization rates on stabilized apartment assets that compress into the low-to-mid 4% range, among the tightest in the Central Coast region.

Against this backdrop, ground leases have historically been a practical solution for institutional and family-office landowners who wish to retain fee ownership of irreplaceable Santa Cruz land while monetizing it through long-term lease structures. The problem arises when a leasehold tenant attempts to finance improvements on that ground-leased land and discovers that the ground lease document was not drafted with financing in mind. The litigation that follows typically centers on whether the leasehold is financeable as structured, and that question requires expert analysis that goes well beyond what a real estate appraiser or a transactional attorney can provide.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing a leasehold tenant in a commercial ground lease dispute in Santa Cruz County. The underlying asset was a mixed-use commercial property situated on ground-leased land in a high-visibility retail and office corridor within the city of Santa Cruz. The leasehold tenant had constructed improvements on the site and sought permanent financing to retire construction debt. The proposed lender declined to fund after a lengthy underwriting period, citing deficiencies in the ground lease documentation. The ground lessor disputed the tenant's characterization of those deficiencies, arguing that the lease as written was commercially standard and that the financing failure was attributable to the borrower's credit profile rather than the lease structure.

Counsel retained CLS CRE to answer several specific questions: First, whether the ground lease, as drafted, met the minimum structural requirements that institutional and regional lenders impose as a condition of originating a leasehold mortgage. Second, whether the specific subordination, non-disturbance, and attornment provisions, referred to throughout this article as SNDA, contained in the lease were consistent with or materially deviated from market standards. Third, what universe of lenders would or would not consider the leasehold financeable given its current form, and at what loan-to-value and debt service coverage parameters. Fourth, whether the financing failure could be attributed to market conditions, the borrower's creditworthiness, or the ground lease structure itself.

These are capital markets questions, not appraisal questions. An appraiser can establish the market value of the leasehold interest. CLS CRE's role was to establish whether that leasehold interest could be financed, on what terms, through which lending channels, and why the specific lease language created or eliminated access to those channels.

The Financial Analysis

The subject property generated stabilized net operating income from a blended tenant roster comprising ground-floor retail and second-floor office space. For purposes of the financing analysis, we reconstructed a trailing twelve-month NOI figure and stress-tested it against prospective rent roll scenarios. Gross potential income was calculated at approximately $780,000 annually. After applying a market vacancy assumption of 7% for the retail component and 12% for the office component, consistent with Santa Cruz submarket data for comparable mixed-use product, and deducting operating expenses including management fees, insurance, and non-reimbursable maintenance items, we arrived at a stabilized NOI of approximately $618,000.

The critical question was not the NOI itself but how lenders apply that NOI to a leasehold financing. For a standard fee-simple commercial mortgage, a conventional institutional lender would apply a 1.25x debt service coverage ratio at a stress-tested interest rate, typically 25 to 50 basis points above the note rate, with a 25-year amortization schedule. At a 7.25% note rate and a 1.25x DSCR threshold, the maximum supportable annual debt service from $618,000 in NOI is approximately $494,400. Capitalizing that debt service over a 25-year amortization at the assumed rate produces a maximum loan amount of approximately $6,250,000. If the leasehold interest was independently appraised at $9,500,000, the resulting loan-to-value ratio would be approximately 65.8%, which falls within the standard 65% LTV ceiling that most conventional and CMBS lenders apply to leasehold financing on mixed-use assets. On paper, the loan appeared feasible.

But leasehold financing does not work on paper the way fee-simple financing does. Lenders apply a haircut to both the value and the LTV ceiling for leasehold mortgages because their collateral is the leasehold interest, not the underlying land. If the ground lease terminates for any reason, the leasehold mortgage becomes unsecured. Most institutional lenders we surveyed for this engagement impose a maximum LTV of 55% to 60% on leasehold positions where the SNDA framework is deficient, and several lenders in our survey declined to quote a leasehold structure at all without a fully executed and lender-approved SNDA in place prior to application. Applying the more conservative 57.5% LTV ceiling to the $9,500,000 appraised leasehold value produces a maximum loan of approximately $5,463,000. At the same DSCR and amortization assumptions, this loan size generates annual debt service of approximately $436,000, covered at a 1.42x ratio by the $618,000 NOI, which is acceptable. The problem was not whether the borrower could service the debt. The problem was that no qualified lender would originate a leasehold mortgage secured by this particular ground lease structure without SNDA provisions that the ground lessor had refused to provide.

The Complicating Factors

Absence of Lender-Protective SNDA Provisions

The SNDA framework is not optional in leasehold financing. It is the foundational document that converts a leasehold interest from an unsecurable asset into acceptable collateral. Subordination means the ground lessor agrees that the leasehold mortgage is senior to the ground lessor's interest in the event of a foreclosure by the leasehold lender, meaning the lender can foreclose on the leasehold without the ground lessor extinguishing the mortgage. Non-disturbance means the ground lessor agrees not to terminate the ground lease and evict the leasehold tenant (or the lender's successor through foreclosure) as long as rent is being paid and lease covenants are being observed. Attornment means that if a new party steps into the ground lessor's position, the leasehold tenant or the lender's foreclosure purchaser agrees to recognize the new ground lessor and continue performing under the lease. Without all three components functioning as an integrated, enforceable agreement, the leasehold mortgage lacks the structural integrity that institutional underwriting requires.

In this engagement, the ground lease contained a subordination clause that was subordinating in the wrong direction: it purported to make the ground lessee's improvements subordinate to the ground lessor's fee interest without providing any non-disturbance protection for the lender. This is a structural deficiency, not a negotiation point. A lender advancing $5.5 million against a leasehold interest cannot accept a structure in which the ground lessor retains the unilateral right to terminate the lease and take possession of the improvements. The lender's entire collateral position would evaporate.

Lease Term Relative to Loan Maturity

Institutional lenders require that the remaining ground lease term exceed the proposed loan maturity by a substantial margin, typically 10 to 15 years beyond the loan's final maturity date, including all extension options that the lender can exercise independently. A standard commercial mortgage in this market carries a 10-year term with a 25-year amortization. That means the ground lease must have at least 20 to 25 years of remaining term, inclusive of exercisable options, to qualify for institutional financing. A lease with 18 years remaining and no options is functionally unfinanceable in the institutional market regardless of NOI quality, because no lender wants to hold a note secured by a leasehold that could expire before the loan matures.

Santa Cruz ground leases on older commercial sites are sometimes legacy documents drafted in the 1970s and 1980s with original 50-year or 55-year terms that are now running short. This is a common source of dispute because the parties may not have anticipated the financing implications of lease term compression at origination.

Cure Rights and Lender Notice Provisions

Even where an SNDA framework exists, lenders require that they receive direct notice of any ground lease default before the ground lessor can act on that default, and that the lender be given an independent cure period that is separate from and longer than the tenant's cure period. Typical market standard is 30 days of lender notice after the tenant's cure period expires, with an additional 90 to 180 days for the lender to complete a foreclosure and install a substitute tenant if necessary. A ground lease that allows the lessor to declare a default and terminate the lease without providing independent lender notice and cure rights is unfinanceable in the institutional market and extremely difficult to place even in the private lending market.

Rent Reset Provisions and NOI Predictability

Ground rents that reset to fair market value on a periodic basis, without a cap or formula, introduce cash flow unpredictability that lenders model as risk. If the ground rent can increase materially at a reset date, the NOI waterfall supporting debt service coverage could be materially impaired. Lenders underwriting leasehold mortgages on properties with uncapped fair market value rent resets will either price in a significant spread premium, reduce leverage materially, or decline the transaction entirely. In a market like Santa Cruz, where land values have escalated significantly over the past decade, an uncapped ground rent reset is not a theoretical risk. It is a plausible scenario that a lender must stress-test.

The Santa Cruz Lender Market

The lending universe for leasehold commercial mortgage financing in Santa Cruz is narrower than the universe for fee-simple financing, and the SNDA framework quality is the primary gating factor that determines which categories of lenders will engage.

Local and regional community banks headquartered on the Central Coast, including institutions operating in Santa Cruz, Monterey, and Santa Clara counties, are often the most flexible on leasehold structures because their credit decisions are relationship-driven and their underwriting teams can engage directly with borrowers and ground lessors to negotiate compliant SNDA agreements during the origination process. Their typical hold limit on a single leasehold credit runs between $3,000,000 and $8,000,000, which is appropriate for the loan sizes this market generates. Pricing is typically SOFR-indexed or prime-based with spreads in the 225 to 350 basis point range depending on the borrower's deposit relationship. These lenders require a minimum 1.20x DSCR and will lend to 60% LTV on leasehold positions with strong SNDA documentation.

Life insurance companies and CMBS conduit lenders, which represent the institutional execution channel for loans above $5,000,000, require fully executed and lender-approved SNDA agreements as a condition of issuing a term sheet, not as a closing condition. They will not invest underwriting resources in a leasehold credit where the ground lessor has not agreed in principle to deliver a compliant SNDA. CMBS execution on Santa Cruz mixed-use assets with leasehold collateral is relatively rare, not because the market is too small but because the ground lease documentation on many older Santa Cruz commercial sites fails to meet CMBS servicer requirements.

Debt funds and private bridge lenders will originate leasehold positions with deficient SNDA frameworks, but they price that structural risk aggressively, typically at 10% to 13% interest-only with one-to-three year terms and origination fees of 1.5% to 2.5%. This is bridge capital, not permanent financing, and it comes with refinancing risk that can itself become a source of dispute.

What Attorneys Handling Santa Cruz CRE Disputes Need to Know

A CRE financing expert witness fills a gap that appraisers and transactional real estate attorneys cannot fill. An appraiser establishes value. A CRE financing expert establishes financability, and those are fundamentally different questions with different methodologies, different data sources, and different implications for litigation.

When to retain a CRE financing expert: Any dispute involving an allegation that a party failed to perform a financing obligation, that a financing contingency was or was not satisfied, that a ground lease or other leasehold structure was or was not financeable as structured, that a lender acted outside market standards in underwriting or declining a loan, or that a party's damages were caused by a financing failure rather than other factors should involve a CRE financing expert. Appraisal testimony alone will not resolve these questions.

What a CRE financing expert can establish that an appraiser cannot:

  • Whether a specific lease structure, SNDA provision, or collateral configuration meets or fails institutional lending standards
  • What the actual lending universe would or would not do with a given credit, and at what pricing and leverage
  • Whether a specific lender's underwriting decision was consistent with market practice or deviated from it
  • What the maximum supportable loan amount was at a given point in time, using actual market DSCR and LTV parameters, not just appraised value
  • Whether a borrower's failure to obtain financing was caused by the collateral structure, market conditions, or the borrower's own creditworthiness

Qualifications that matter: The most credible CRE financing expert witnesses are active practitioners who are currently placing commercial mortgages in the market, not retired bankers or academics. The market moves constantly. Underwriting standards, lender appetite, and SNDA requirements that applied three years ago may differ materially from current standards. An expert who is not actively brokering and closing transactions lacks current market intelligence. At CLS CRE, with approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, more than 1,000 active lender relationships, and origination activity across all 50 states, we have current, verified market data that supports our expert opinions.

Information counsel should gather before the engagement: The complete ground lease and all amendments; any executed or proposed SNDA agreements; the loan application and lender communications including decline letters; the appraisal and any internal lender value assessments; rent rolls and operating statements for the prior 24 to 36 months; the borrower's financial statements if creditworthiness is at issue; and any correspondence between the parties regarding SNDA negotiations.

How CRE financing expert testimony is used: In bench trials and arbitrations involving financing disputes, expert testimony on lender market standards is typically the determinative evidence on the question of whether a financing obligation was satisfiable. In bankruptcy plan hearings, financing expert testimony supports or attacks the feasibility of a proposed plan by establishing what refinancing proceeds are actually achievable. In mediation, a well-documented expert report often resolves the dispute before trial by giving both sides a credible market-based framework for evaluating their positions.

Key Takeaways

  • Santa Cruz ground leases are frequently financed but often not financeable as drafted. The market's land scarcity has made ground leases common, but many older documents were not drafted with lender requirements in mind, creating a predictable category of dispute.
  • SNDA provisions are not optional and not negotiable in institutional lending. A leasehold mortgage without a fully executed, lender-approved SNDA providing subordination, non-disturbance, and independent lender cure rights is not financeable in the conventional or CMBS market under any circumstances.

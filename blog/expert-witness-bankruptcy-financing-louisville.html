Expert Witness: CRE Financing Feasibility in Chapter 11 Proceedings in Louisville, KY

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Bankruptcy Exit Financing

The Louisville Market Context

Louisville, Kentucky occupies a position in the national commercial real estate landscape that attorneys and investors frequently underestimate. The metropolitan statistical area anchors a regional economy built on logistics, healthcare, advanced manufacturing, and bourbon production, and those fundamentals translate directly into the financing markets that matter when a Chapter 11 debtor is trying to exit bankruptcy through asset-backed financing. Understanding what drives Louisville's CRE capital markets is not incidental to this type of expert analysis; it is the foundation of it.

On the industrial side, Louisville's position at the intersection of Interstates 64, 65, and 71, combined with the UPS Worldport hub at Louisville Muhammad Ali International Airport, has made the market a permanent fixture on institutional industrial investors' target lists. Industrial vacancy across the Louisville MSA has remained tight, generally running in the 4 to 6 percent range in core submarkets including Shepherdsville, the airport corridor, and the Louisville East industrial nodes. Net asking rents for Class A bulk distribution have pushed into the $5.50 to $7.00 per square foot range on new leases, while older Class B infill product has held in the $3.50 to $5.00 range. Those rent numbers matter enormously when we are stress-testing an income stream for debt service coverage in a plan confirmation proceeding.

Multifamily fundamentals in Louisville reflect a market that absorbed significant new supply over 2021 through 2023 but has begun to stabilize. Class A apartment communities in the Highlands, NuLu, and the East End have experienced modest concession activity, with effective rents settling in the $1.25 to $1.65 per square foot range depending on submarket and unit mix. Cap rates for stabilized multifamily in Louisville have compressed from the sub-5 percent environment of 2021 and now trade more commonly in the 5.5 to 6.5 percent range for institutional quality assets, which affects both the appraised value and the maximum supportable loan in a financing feasibility opinion.

Office is the most stressed property type in Louisville, as it is nationally, with downtown vacancy running above 20 percent and suburban Class B office struggling to retain tenants. A Chapter 11 debtor holding a Louisville office asset faces a materially different financing universe than one holding industrial or multifamily product, and any expert opinion that fails to distinguish between these submarket realities is not providing genuine market analysis.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by counsel representing a plan proponent in a Chapter 11 reorganization proceeding pending in the Western District of Kentucky. The asset at issue was a multi-tenant industrial flex property located in one of Louisville's established logistics corridors, approximately 180,000 square feet of total rentable area with a mix of warehouse and office component space. The debtor had originally acquired the property using a bridge loan that matured during the bankruptcy period, and the senior secured creditor had filed a proof of claim asserting a principal balance of approximately $9.2 million, plus accrued interest and fees.

The plan proponent's theory of reorganization was that the property could support conventional first-mortgage financing sufficient to pay out the senior creditor at or near par, allowing the debtor's equity to survive and the reorganization to close without a forced sale. Counsel needed an expert opinion that could withstand cross-examination on two core questions: first, whether the property's verified income stream could support sufficient debt service to justify a loan in the range required; and second, whether an active market of institutional or institutional-quality lenders exists that actually originates mortgage loans on assets held in bankruptcy estates, and under what conditions.

Those are distinct questions, and both require expertise that sits outside the scope of a real estate appraisal. An appraiser determines value. A CRE financing expert determines whether, and at what terms, a lender will advance funds against that value, given the specific legal and operational circumstances of the asset. In a plan confirmation context, that distinction is not semantic; it is the difference between a confirmable plan and an objection that defeats it.

The Financial Analysis

The financial analysis began with a reconstruction of the property's trailing twelve-month net operating income, using rent rolls, lease abstracts, operating expense reconciliations, and management-prepared statements, all of which were cross-referenced against available market comparables for the Louisville industrial flex submarket. The gross potential rent for the subject property, based on in-place leases across approximately 85 percent of the rentable area, was approximately $1,340,000 annually. After applying a market-supported vacancy and credit loss allowance of 8 percent (consistent with the submarket's historical absorption and the mixed credit profile of the tenant roster), effective gross income was calculated at approximately $1,233,000.

Operating expenses, including property taxes at the Jefferson County assessed rate, insurance, management fees at 4 percent of EGI, maintenance reserves, and common area costs, totaled approximately $398,000. This produced a stabilized net operating income of approximately $835,000 for purposes of underwriting. I want to be precise about the word "stabilized" here: in a bankruptcy context, lenders do not simply accept trailing actuals. They require normalization for any above-market or below-market leases, any one-time expense items distorted by the bankruptcy itself, and any vacancy that is reasonably attributable to the distress period rather than true market conditions. In this case, two tenants had received temporary rent deferrals during the bankruptcy stay. Those deferrals were added back for underwriting purposes with appropriate documentation.

With a stabilized NOI of $835,000, the next variable is the applicable debt service coverage ratio threshold. Industry standard for conventional permanent financing on commercial assets is 1.20x to 1.25x DSCR, with most life insurance companies and CMBS conduit lenders requiring 1.25x at origination and most bank balance-sheet lenders accepting 1.20x for well-located assets with strong tenancy. For this asset, given the mixed-credit tenant profile and the bankruptcy overlay, I applied a 1.25x DSCR threshold as the market-appropriate standard, reflecting what a lender in this environment would actually require rather than a theoretical minimum.

At a 1.25x DSCR and a 30-year amortization schedule at an assumed all-in interest rate of 7.25 percent (reflecting the rate environment applicable to conventional permanent financing on Louisville industrial product at the time of the analysis, with a spread over the corresponding Treasury reflecting asset type, loan size, and credit characteristics), the maximum annual debt service that the NOI could support was $668,000. That figure represents $835,000 divided by 1.25. At a 7.25 percent coupon and 30-year amortization, $668,000 in annual debt service corresponds to a maximum loan amount of approximately $9,050,000.

The appraised value established in the bankruptcy proceeding was $13,800,000. The maximum supportable loan of $9,050,000 represents a loan-to-value ratio of approximately 65.6 percent. Conventional permanent lenders on commercial assets in this loan size range, generally $5 million to $15 million, operate with LTV ceilings of 65 to 75 percent depending on asset type and lender category. The computed LTV of 65.6 percent falls within that acceptable range, meaning that the DSCR constraint was the binding underwriting variable, not the LTV ceiling. The analysis demonstrated that the income stream could support a loan sufficient to satisfy the senior secured claim of $9.2 million within a narrow margin, and that conclusion required careful attention to the NOI normalization inputs.

The Complicating Factors

Lender aversion to bankruptcy estate assets. The single most significant complicating factor in any bankruptcy exit financing analysis is that many conventional lenders will not originate a loan on property held by a Chapter 11 debtor, regardless of the asset's underlying quality. This is not irrational. The lender's standard due diligence timeline, typically 45 to 60 days for a conventional commercial loan closing, becomes unreliable when the closing is contingent on plan confirmation. Court schedules slip. Objections extend timelines. Environmental or title issues surfacing in the bankruptcy record can create liens or encumbrances that a lender's counsel cannot clear quickly. The result is that a subset of the conventional lending market self-selects out of these transactions at the inquiry stage, which compresses the realistic financing universe and can affect pricing.

Title and lien complexity. A Chapter 11 estate asset almost always carries title complications that do not exist in ordinary course commercial transactions. Pre-petition mechanic's liens, disputed unsecured creditor claims seeking attachment, property tax arrearages accumulated during the automatic stay period, and the existing lienholder's recorded deed of trust all require resolution before a new lender can take a clean first-lien position. Louisville title companies experienced in bankruptcy matters can work through these issues, but the process requires coordination between plan counsel, the lender's counsel, and the title underwriter in a sequence that must be built into the plan confirmation timeline. A lender who has not done this before will frequently decline rather than attempt it.

Rent roll instability during bankruptcy. Tenants in commercial properties owned by Chapter 11 debtors sometimes exercise contractual rights, or simply act on uncertainty, to negotiate lease modifications, request rent deferrals, or evaluate early termination options. In the Louisville industrial market, where smaller regional tenants are common in multi-tenant flex properties, this risk is real. A lender underwriting exit financing must be satisfied that the in-place rent roll will survive plan confirmation intact. This typically requires estoppel certificates from key tenants, lease assumption motions filed and approved as part of the plan, and in some cases, personal guarantees from creditworthy principals if the tenant entities are thin. All of these requirements must be disclosed to and navigated through bankruptcy counsel.

Appraisal timing and staleness. Lenders require appraisals dated within 6 to 12 months of loan closing, ordered through their own approved appraiser panels. The appraisal introduced into evidence at plan confirmation may have been prepared months earlier and may reflect a different rate environment or market condition. In a Louisville industrial market where rent growth has been meaningful, a stale appraisal can actually understate value. But in a multifamily or office context, the opposite could be true. A lender will order a new appraisal regardless of what the court record contains, and the cost and timeline of that second appraisal must be anticipated in the plan structure.

The Louisville Lender Market

Community and regional banks. Louisville has a meaningful depth of locally headquartered and regionally active community banks that have demonstrated willingness to originate commercial mortgage loans on assets with complexity, including bankruptcy exit situations, provided the loan size falls within their house limits and the borrower can demonstrate a post-reorganization operating track record. Institutions headquartered in Louisville and across the Kentucky market have historically been relationship-driven lenders willing to look past the bankruptcy stigma when the sponsorship is credible and the asset quality is strong. Loan sizes in the $3 million to $12 million range, which describes most Louisville commercial assets, fit squarely within community bank appetites. Pricing for these lenders in the current environment typically runs 30 to 75 basis points wide of regional bank competitors, reflecting the added complexity premium, with floating rate structures indexed to Prime or SOFR plus a spread being common at community banks.

Debt funds and bridge lenders. For situations where the plan timeline is compressed, the stabilization story requires a lease-up period post-confirmation, or where the senior creditor requires payoff before conventional financing can be closed, private debt funds and bridge lenders fill a critical role. These lenders, many of whom are active in the Louisville market through correspondent relationships with local mortgage brokers, will originate on bankruptcy estate assets at higher LTVs, shorter terms (typically 12 to 36 months), and at pricing that runs 300 to 500 basis points above conventional fixed-rate products. The trade-off is execution certainty and speed. For a plan proponent who needs to demonstrate financing feasibility to the court, having a committed bridge lender term sheet can be as important as having a theoretical conventional underwriting analysis.

CMBS conduit lenders. CMBS origination on bankruptcy exit assets is possible but requires specific conditions: the loan must close simultaneously with plan confirmation so that the bankruptcy estate never technically "owns" the property encumbered by the new CMBS mortgage, the collateral must meet securitization eligibility standards including minimum DSCR, LTV, and lease term requirements, and the legal structure must be acceptable to the rating agencies' counsel. For larger Louisville assets, generally above $10 million in loan size, CMBS is worth exploring, but it is not the primary execution strategy for most bankruptcy exit financings in this market given the additional complexity layer.

Life insurance companies. Life company allocations to Louisville commercial assets, particularly stabilized industrial and multifamily properties, have been active at the correspondent level. These lenders offer the most competitive fixed-rate pricing but require the cleanest possible credit profile: no ongoing litigation, a minimum of 24 to 36 months of post-bankruptcy operating history, and LTV below 60 percent in most cases. They are rarely the right execution vehicle for a plan confirmation context where the closing must happen contemporaneously with the confirmation order, but they represent the refinance exit for bridge borrowers 12 to 18 months after reorganization.

What Attorneys Handling Louisville CRE Disputes Need to Know

The question of when to retain a CRE financing expert is one I address directly with litigation counsel at the outset of every engagement. The answer is: earlier than you think. In a plan confirmation proceeding, the debtor's ability to obtain financing is often central to the feasibility objection filed by creditors. If you are waiting until the objection is filed to understand whether your client's financing theory is supportable, you are already behind. A financing expert engaged at the plan drafting stage can identify the underwriting vulnerabilities before they become ammunition for a creditor's objection.

The distinction between an appraiser and a CRE financing expert is not always understood by courts or opposing counsel, and attorneys who understand it have a meaningful advantage. An appraiser renders an opinion of value using standardized methodologies and is qualified to testify about what a property is worth. A CRE financing expert renders an opinion about what a lender will actually do: what rate they will price, what DSCR they require, what conditions they impose, how long they take to close, and whether any institutional market exists for the specific asset in the specific condition at the specific loan size required. These are different disciplines. A retired bank officer may understand one lender's historical credit standards but not the breadth of institutional capital sources, their current credit boxes, and the deal structures needed to bridge from a bankruptcy estate to a clean first-lien position. An active market participant who has originated across 1,000-plus lender relationships and approximately $1 billion in transaction volume can speak to what the market actually does, not what it did ten years ago at one institution.

Before engaging a CRE financing expert, counsel should gather the following: certified rent rolls with lease expiration dates and option provisions; trailing 12 and 24-month operating statements; the most recent real property appraisal in the court record; any existing loan documents including the matured or defaulted mortgage; property condition and environmental reports if available; and the plan proponent's proposed financing structure, including proposed loan amount, expected closing timeline, and any term sheets already obtained. The more complete this package, the more precise and defensible the expert opinion will be.

At a plan confirmation hearing, financing expert testimony is typically used to establish two elements of the feasibility standard: that the proposed financing is not speculative, and that the market of available lenders is not theoretical. Opposing experts often argue that the required loan cannot be obtained because of the bankruptcy overlay or market conditions. A well-prepared CRE financing expert can rebut that argument by identifying specific lender categories, realistic pricing, and actual transaction precedents from comparable markets, not just citing underwriting guidelines in the abstract.

Key Takeaways

  • Louisville's industrial fundamentals support conventional financing feasibility for well-located flex and distribution assets, with vacancy rates and rent trajectories that satisfy institutional underwriting thresholds, provided the NOI is properly normalized for bankruptcy-period distortions.
  • The DSCR constraint is typically the binding underwriting variable in Louisville commercial mortgage analysis, not LTV; a 1.25x DSCR threshold applied at current market rates defines the maximum supportable loan, and the precision of the NOI normalization directly determines whether that loan is sufficient to satisfy the senior secured claim.
  • Not all lenders will originate on bankruptcy estate assets, but an active market does exist, including community banks with relationship flexibility, private debt funds willing to accept complexity premiums, and in some cases CMBS conduits for larger assets meeting securitization eligibility standards.

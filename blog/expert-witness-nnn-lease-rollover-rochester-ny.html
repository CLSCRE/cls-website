Expert Witness: NNN Lease Rollover Upside and Financing Impact in Rochester, NY

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Below-Market NNN Lease and Rollover Upside Analysis

The Rochester Market Context

Rochester, New York occupies a distinctive position in the upstate New York commercial real estate landscape. It is not a tertiary market in the dismissive sense that institutional capital sometimes uses that term. Rochester has a diversified economic base anchored by healthcare systems, higher education institutions, photonics and optics manufacturing, and a growing logistics corridor tied to its position along the I-90 thruway. That economic mix matters directly when underwriting single-tenant NNN assets, because the quality of a tenant's credit is inseparable from the regional economy sustaining its operations.

For single-tenant net lease product specifically, Rochester presents a bifurcated lending environment. National credit tenants in the retail and industrial sectors trade at cap rates that have ranged from the mid-5s to the low-7s depending on tenant credit, lease term, and property type, with industrial NNN assets commanding tighter cap rates than retail given sustained demand from regional distribution users. The Monroe County industrial vacancy rate has remained below 5% in recent reporting periods, which is relevant context when evaluating whether a below-market rent on an industrial NNN property reflects a structural discount or simply a lease executed at a prior market cycle's pricing. Rochester's suburban retail corridors, including the Greece and Henrietta submarkets, have experienced the same secular pressure from e-commerce and retailer consolidation that affects mid-size markets nationally, making tenant credit quality and lease term the primary drivers of lender appetite rather than location fundamentals alone.

Critically for this type of dispute, Rochester is a market where community banks and regional lenders retain significant commercial mortgage market share alongside CMBS conduit lenders and debt funds. That lender diversity creates real optionality for borrowers, but it also means that underwriting standards are not uniform across the capital stack. A CMBS lender and a Rochester-area community bank will treat the same below-market lease in materially different ways, and those differences have direct bearing on what a court or arbitration panel should understand about financing feasibility.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing one of the parties in a commercial real estate dispute involving a single-tenant NNN-leased property in the Rochester metropolitan area. The asset was a freestanding commercial building occupied by a single tenant operating under a long-term net lease. The core of the dispute involved disagreements about the property's financing value and refinancing capacity, with specific questions arising about how the existing below-market in-place rent affected the property's supportable loan amount and whether the prospect of lease rollover to market rents should be credited in any lender's underwriting.

Counsel needed answers to a set of questions that sit squarely in the gap between appraisal testimony and capital markets testimony. An appraiser can opine on market value. A financing expert addresses something different: what a lender will actually do with a specific income stream under real-world underwriting standards. The questions put to CLS CRE were as follows. First, how do commercial mortgage lenders underwrite below-market in-place rents on single-tenant NNN assets, and what NOI figure do they actually use for loan sizing? Second, does anticipated rent rollover to market upon lease expiration create any present financing benefit, and if so, under what conditions and with what structural requirements? Third, given the in-place lease structure, what maximum loan amount could the property support at market DSCR and LTV thresholds, and how does that compare to the loan amounts that the parties had variously claimed were achievable? Fourth, does the lease structure, considered in its entirety, preclude refinancing within a commercially reasonable range of terms, or does it merely constrain the available lender universe?

Those questions required a combination of capital markets expertise, lender underwriting knowledge, and transactional experience that is specific to commercial mortgage origination practice, not appraisal or brokerage generally.

The Financial Analysis

The methodology applied in this engagement followed the framework that commercial mortgage lenders use in actual credit underwriting, not hypothetical or academic constructs. The starting point was the in-place net operating income derived from the existing lease. On a single-tenant NNN property, the NOI calculation is relatively straightforward: the contractual base rent, minus any landlord obligations not passed through to the tenant, minus any credit reserves a lender applies for structural vacancy risk during lease rollover. The lease in this matter produced an in-place NOI that was meaningfully below current market rent for comparable space in the Rochester submarket.

For purposes of illustrating the analysis in terms appropriate to this market, consider a property generating $280,000 of in-place annual NOI against a current market rent that would produce approximately $370,000 of NOI on the same square footage. That is a below-market discount of approximately 24%, which is not a rounding error in lender underwriting. It is a structural constraint.

The DSCR threshold applied was 1.25x, which is the standard minimum for most conventional commercial mortgage lenders on single-tenant NNN product in a secondary market like Rochester. Some community banks will underwrite to 1.20x for strong-credit tenants with long remaining lease terms, but for a lease with a shorter remaining term or a non-investment-grade tenant, 1.25x is the correct market standard and is defensible in any expert report. Agency lenders, where applicable to the asset type, and CMBS conduit programs universally require 1.25x or higher on assets of this profile.

Assuming a 30-year amortization schedule and an interest rate of 7.25%, which was consistent with commercial mortgage market pricing for this asset type and credit profile at the relevant time, the debt service on a $2,800,000 loan would be approximately $229,000 annually. At 1.25x DSCR, the in-place NOI of $280,000 supports maximum annual debt service of $224,000, yielding a maximum supportable loan of approximately $2,735,000. That figure establishes the in-place financing ceiling before any LTV constraint is applied.

The LTV constraint is applied independently and the lower of the two tests governs. For single-tenant NNN assets in secondary markets, most conventional lenders cap LTV at 65% to 70%. CMBS conduit programs will extend to 70% in some cases for investment-grade tenancy, but pull back to 60% to 65% for non-investment-grade or short-term leases. If the appraised value of the subject property was $4,200,000, a 65% LTV cap yields a maximum loan of $2,730,000 independently, which in this case aligned closely with the DSCR-constrained maximum. When the two tests produce similar ceilings, the analysis is relatively clean. The financing constraint is real and is driven by income, not leverage appetite alone.

The critical finding from this analysis was that the claimed loan amount advanced by the opposing party, which assumed market-rate NOI rather than in-place NOI, overstated the supportable loan by approximately $810,000. No conventional lender underwrites single-tenant NNN product to projected rollover rents unless specific structural conditions are satisfied, and those conditions were not present in this lease.

The Complicating Factors

Lender Treatment of Below-Market Rents. The most important point for counsel to understand is that commercial mortgage lenders do not give credit for rollover upside in their loan sizing unless the rollover has already occurred or is contractually locked in through a lease amendment, a signed replacement lease, or a rent escalation provision with a predetermined schedule. A property with a below-market in-place lease is underwritten at the in-place rent, period. The fact that the lease expires in three years and market rents are 25% higher does not increase the loan amount today. Lenders treat that gap as risk, not opportunity. In fact, many lenders will apply an additional stress to the in-place NOI to account for the possibility that the tenant does not renew and the property experiences a period of vacancy before a replacement lease is executed.

Lease Term Remaining and the Lender's Amortization Horizon. On single-tenant NNN assets, most lenders require that the lease term extend through the proposed loan term, or at a minimum through the maturity of the loan. A five-year loan on a property with three years of remaining lease term creates a two-year exposure window at maturity where the lender's collateral is potentially dark. Rochester community banks and regional lenders are somewhat more flexible on this point than CMBS conduit programs, which are essentially unwilling to originate loans with lease expirations occurring during the loan term on single-tenant assets. That rigidity in the CMBS market is not a negotiating position; it is a structuring requirement tied to rating agency criteria.

Tenant Credit and the Rochester Market's Concentration Risk. Single-tenant NNN lenders underwrite the tenant's credit independently of the real estate. In a market like Rochester, where certain major employers represent a disproportionate share of economic activity, a tenant that is regionally dominant but lacks national investment-grade ratings will encounter a narrower lender universe than a nationally-rated credit operating the same property type in the same location. That distinction affects both loan pricing and maximum LTV. A non-investment-grade tenant in a Rochester NNN deal will typically face a 25 to 50 basis point spread premium over a comparable investment-grade credit and a 5% LTV haircut.

Rollover Upside as a Refinancing Argument Versus a Current Loan Sizing Argument. There is a meaningful distinction, often lost in litigation, between arguing that rollover upside supports a higher loan amount today and arguing that rollover upside will enable a superior refinancing at lease expiration. The first argument is almost always wrong as a matter of lender practice. The second argument is conditionally correct but depends entirely on market conditions at the time of rollover, the tenant's actual renewal decision, and the lender market's appetite at that future date. An expert analysis that treats projected rollover as a present financing benefit is inconsistent with how lenders actually originate commercial mortgage loans.

The Rochester Lender Market

For single-tenant NNN assets in the Rochester metropolitan area, the active lender universe divides into four categories, each with distinct underwriting parameters relevant to a below-market lease situation.

Rochester-Area Community Banks and Regional Banks. This category represents the deepest source of conventional commercial mortgage capital for NNN assets in the $1,500,000 to $8,000,000 range. These institutions underwrite to relationship-driven criteria and will engage with lease structures that CMBS or agency lenders will not touch. They are more willing to consider short lease terms if the borrower relationship is strong and the guaranty structure is acceptable. However, they will not underwrite to projected rents. Their pricing typically runs 25 to 75 basis points wide of CMBS execution on comparable risk, reflecting their balance-sheet cost of funds and concentration risk management.

CMBS Conduit Lenders. Active in Rochester for NNN assets above $3,000,000, CMBS conduit programs provide competitive non-recourse execution but apply the most rigid underwriting standards of any lender category. Below-market rents are underwritten at the contract figure with no credit for rollover. Lease term requirements are strictly enforced. For a property with a lease expiring within the loan term, CMBS execution is typically not available without a lease modification executed prior to rate lock. Spreads on Rochester NNN assets in the current market have ranged from 170 to 230 basis points over the relevant Treasury benchmark depending on tenancy and lease structure.

Life Insurance Company Lenders. Life companies are selective in Rochester and are generally focused on investment-grade tenancy with long lease terms, typically ten or more years of remaining term. They offer the most attractive pricing in the market, often 20 to 50 basis points tighter than CMBS, but their credit box is the narrowest. A below-market lease on a sub-investment-grade tenant will not qualify for life company consideration regardless of the property's physical quality.

Debt Funds and Bridge Lenders. For transitional or structurally challenged NNN assets, debt funds provide financing where conventional lenders cannot. They will lend against a property with a short lease term or below-market rents, typically at 55% to 65% LTV with pricing in the 9% to 11% range depending on leverage and exit risk. Their role in an expert analysis is important because they define the floor of available financing. A property that cannot qualify for conventional financing is not unfinanceable; it occupies a different tier of the capital stack with materially different economics.

What Attorneys Handling Rochester CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to Retain a CRE Financing Expert. The threshold question is whether the dispute turns on what a lender would actually do with a specific property and income stream. If the answer is yes, appraisal testimony alone is insufficient. Appraisers establish value. Financing experts establish borrowing capacity, lender feasibility, and the mechanics by which a specific income structure translates into available debt. In any dispute involving claims about refinancing feasibility, loan sizing, financing contingency performance, or the impact of lease structure on capital access, a CRE financing expert should be retained early, before expert disclosure deadlines create limitations on the scope of analysis.

What a CRE Financing Expert Answers That an Appraiser Does Not. An appraiser will tell you what the property is worth. A CRE financing expert will tell you what a lender will loan against it, at what terms, in which lender categories, and whether a specific claimed loan amount is consistent with market underwriting standards. Those are distinct questions. In a dispute where a party claims that a property could support a $4,000,000 refinancing at 70% LTV based on projected rents, an appraiser's value conclusion does not resolve the question. A financing expert's analysis of actual lender underwriting standards does.

Qualifications That Matter. Retired bankers who have not originated commercial mortgage loans in the current market cycle have significant limitations as financing experts because lender credit standards, pricing, and structural requirements have changed substantially. The relevant qualifications are active commercial mortgage origination practice, current lender relationships across multiple categories, and a track record of closing transactions in the relevant asset type and geography. A financing expert should be able to demonstrate that they have engaged with lenders on comparable transactions in the relevant market, not merely that they once worked in a bank's credit department.

Information Counsel Should Gather Before the Engagement. The expert will need the complete executed lease, including all amendments and rent schedules; any existing loan documents and appraisals; rent comps for the submarket if available; the borrower's financial statements if relevant to recourse guaranty analysis; and any lender correspondence or term sheets that the parties have exchanged. The earlier this material is assembled, the more precise the expert analysis can be. Gaps in the lease documentation are themselves analytically significant, because lenders require complete documentation before issuing a commitment.

Use of Expert Testimony at Trial and in Alternative Dispute Resolution. CRE financing expert testimony is most effectively used to establish the boundary between what is commercially reasonable in the lender market and what a party has claimed without foundation in lender practice. In bench trials before judges who are not commercial real estate practitioners, the expert's role is to make the mechanics of commercial mortgage underwriting concrete and specific. In arbitration before panels with real estate experience, the expert testimony is most useful when it addresses the specific underwriting calculations at issue rather than general market education.

Key Takeaways

  • In-place rent governs loan sizing. Commercial mortgage lenders underwrite single-tenant NNN assets to the contractual in-place rent, not to projected market rents upon rollover. Any financing analysis that credits anticipated rollover upside in the current loan amount is inconsistent with market underwriting standards and should be challenged.
  • The DSCR and LTV tests are independent constraints. A loan must satisfy both the DSCR test at 1.25x minimum and the applicable LTV cap, typically 65% to 70% for NNN product in secondary markets. The lower result of the two tests controls the maximum loan amount. Counsel should understand which constraint is binding in the specific fact pattern.
  • Lease term remaining is a hard filter for CMBS and life company lenders. Properties with lease expirations during the proposed loan term are effectively excluded from CMBS and life company execution without a prior lease modification. This materially narrows the lender universe and affects pricing, which in turn affects loan sizing.
  • Rochester's community bank depth provides optionality but not unlimited flexibility. Local and regional lenders are more accommodating on relationship and structure, but they do not override fundamental income-based underwriting constraints. A below-market lease limits borrowing capacity across all lender categories, with the degree of constraint varying by lender type.
  • Debt fund pricing establishes the floor of the financing universe, not the ceiling. A property that cannot qualify for conventional financing can often be financed through a debt fund or bridge lender, but at materially higher cost and lower leverage

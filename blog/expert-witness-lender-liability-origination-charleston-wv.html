Expert Witness: Commercial Loan Origination Standards in Charleston, WV

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Lender Liability and Loan Origination Standards

The Charleston Market Context

Charleston, West Virginia sits at the confluence of the Elk and Kanawha Rivers and serves as the state capital and its largest city, anchoring a regional economy built on state government employment, healthcare, legal services, and a legacy energy sector that continues to reshape itself. For attorneys and investors engaged in commercial real estate disputes, understanding this market's structural characteristics is not optional; it is foundational to evaluating whether a lender or broker met the applicable standard of care.

The Charleston CRE market is defined by relatively low transaction velocity compared to Tier 1 and Tier 2 metros, which creates both an opportunity and a complication in litigation. Loan origination decisions made here are often less influenced by competitive pressure to loosen credit standards and more influenced by relationship banking dynamics, localized appraisal opinion variance, and thin comparable transaction pools. These characteristics directly affect what a reasonably competent commercial mortgage broker or lender would have done at origination, and they are exactly the kinds of market-specific factors that a generalist expert witness will miss.

Office fundamentals in Charleston reflect national secular headwinds compounded by state government budget cycles. Vacancy in the central business district has been elevated, with rates estimated in the 15% to 22% range depending on the submarket and building class. Net effective rents have been compressed, and concession packages, including free rent and tenant improvement allowances, have been more aggressive than pre-pandemic norms. A lender underwriting an office asset here in the past three to four years would have needed to apply a meaningful vacancy and credit loss assumption well above the historical proforma figures that borrowers often present.

Multifamily in the Kanawha Valley has shown more resilience, with occupancy in stabilized Class B and C product generally holding above 90%, supported by a workforce housing demand base that is relatively price-sensitive but consistent. Cap rates for multifamily assets in Charleston have generally ranged from 6.5% to 8.5% depending on vintage, location, and quality of management, which is materially wider than gateway markets and has direct implications for LTV calculations and lender appetite. Industrial and flex product is limited in inventory but has seen modest absorption tied to regional distribution needs, with vacancy trending in the 5% to 9% range for functional modern product.

For a lender liability or professional negligence engagement, these market fundamentals are not background color. They are the evidentiary foundation for determining whether the income assumptions, vacancy reserves, and debt sizing decisions made at origination were consistent with what a reasonably competent professional would have applied in this specific market at the relevant point in time.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing a commercial real estate borrower in a lender liability dispute arising from a loan origination on a mixed-use income property in the Charleston metro. The asset included both retail and residential components, and the dispute centered on whether the lender and its originating broker met the industry standard of care in underwriting the loan, whether the disclosed loan terms and conditions were consistent with prevailing market practice, and whether the borrower was placed into a financing structure that a reasonably competent professional would have recognized as unsuitable given the asset's income profile and the borrower's stated investment objectives.

Counsel retained CLS CRE to address three core questions. First, whether the income and expense assumptions used in origination reflected market-supportable inputs or were inflated to justify a loan amount that the asset's actual cash flow could not service. Second, whether the loan's prepayment structure, rate adjustment mechanics, and reserve requirements were disclosed in a manner consistent with industry norms and whether a reasonably competent broker would have flagged those terms as atypical before closing. Third, whether alternative financing structures existed in the Charleston market at the time of origination that would have better served the borrower's financial position, and whether a competent broker had an obligation to present those alternatives.

These are precisely the questions where a CRE financing expert adds value that an appraiser, a banking regulator, or a generalist financial expert cannot replicate. The analysis required deep familiarity with commercial mortgage underwriting methodology, lender credit box mechanics, broker disclosure obligations, and the specific lending environment in a secondary Appalachian market.

The Financial Analysis

The centerpiece of any lender liability engagement involving loan origination is a reconstruction of the underwriting. In this matter, that meant independently re-underwriting the subject asset using market-supportable inputs and comparing the result to what the lender and broker actually used at origination.

Net Operating Income Reconstruction: The subject property generated gross potential rent across its retail and residential units. The origination proforma used by the lender reflected a vacancy and credit loss assumption of approximately 3%, which was inconsistent with observable market conditions for mixed-use retail in the Charleston submarket at the time of closing. A market-supportable vacancy assumption for the retail component, given prevailing absorption rates and the tenant quality in place, was closer to 10% to 12%. Applying that correction alone reduced gross effective income by a meaningful margin. After adjusting operating expenses, including property management at 8% of effective gross income, insurance, real estate taxes, maintenance, and a capital reserve of $250 to $350 per unit per year for the residential component, the reconstructed stabilized NOI came in at approximately $218,000 annually, compared to the origination proforma NOI of $267,000.

DSCR Analysis: The industry standard minimum debt service coverage ratio for a mixed-use commercial loan of this type is 1.20x to 1.25x, depending on the lender category and the leverage point. For secondary markets like Charleston, many balance sheet lenders and regional banks apply 1.25x as a floor, reflecting the thinner liquidity and longer disposition timelines if a workout becomes necessary. Using a 1.25x DSCR floor and a loan priced at 7.25% fixed on a 25-year amortization schedule, the maximum annual debt service that the reconstructed NOI of $218,000 could support was $174,400. At a standard 25-year amortization, that debt service level supports a loan balance of approximately $2,165,000.

LTV Constraint: For mixed-use assets in secondary markets, most conventional lenders apply a maximum LTV of 70% to 75%. The appraised value used at origination was $3,200,000, implying a maximum loan at 70% LTV of $2,240,000. The DSCR-driven maximum of $2,165,000 was the binding constraint, meaning the loan should have been sized to approximately $2.16 million. The loan actually originated was $2,650,000, which at origination represented an 82.8% LTV and required an NOI assumption that the market data did not support. At the reconstructed NOI, the actual loan carried a DSCR of only 1.02x, which no conventional lender would have accepted and which placed the borrower at extreme risk of default at any meaningful revenue disruption.

What This Showed: The origination was not defensible under standard commercial mortgage underwriting methodology. A reasonably competent lender or broker working in the Charleston market would have identified the vacancy assumption as unrealistic, corrected the NOI, applied the 1.25x DSCR floor, and reduced the loan to a level the asset could actually service. The failure to do so was not a judgment call within a range of reasonable professional discretion. It was a departure from the standard of care.

The Complicating Factors

Mixed-Use Collateral Complexity: Lenders treat mixed-use assets differently than pure multifamily or pure retail because the income streams carry different risk profiles. Residential income is typically more stable but subject to local rent levels and turnover costs. Retail income in a market like Charleston is more volatile, especially for smaller tenants without national credit ratings. A competent lender would have bifurcated the income analysis, applied different vacancy and credit loss assumptions to each component, and stress-tested the blended NOI against a scenario where retail tenants vacate. The originating lender in this matter applied a single blended vacancy assumption that was inappropriate for the asset type and understated risk.

Secondary Market Appraisal Variance: Charleston's thin transaction base means appraisers often have limited comparables and may rely on older sales or less comparable assets to support value conclusions. In litigation, this creates a specific vulnerability: the appraisal used at origination may have been technically compliant with USPAP but may have reflected optimistic comparable selection that a reasonably competent lender should have challenged. Lenders have an obligation to scrutinize the appraisal, not simply accept it, particularly when the resulting LTV is near or above policy maximums. In this engagement, the appraisal contained comparable sales from a different submarket and a different asset class that inflated the concluded value.

Rate Structure Disclosure: The loan in question included an adjustable rate provision with a reset mechanism tied to an index that the borrower credibly testified was not adequately explained at closing. Industry practice for commercial mortgage brokers requires that rate adjustment terms, caps, floors, and worst-case payment scenarios be clearly communicated in writing prior to closing, not embedded in loan documents that borrowers are expected to parse without counsel. The question of whether the broker met this disclosure standard is a financing practice question, not a legal question, and it falls squarely within the CRE financing expert's scope.

Reserve and Escrow Requirements: The loan required funded reserves for capital expenditures and taxes that were not prominently disclosed in the initial term sheet. This is not unusual in commercial lending, but the manner and timing of disclosure matters. A competent broker presenting a loan to a borrower has an obligation to ensure that cash-to-close and ongoing reserve obligations are clearly communicated at the term sheet stage, not surfaced for the first time in closing documents. The failure to do so in this matter was a specific departure from brokerage industry standards.

The Charleston Lender Market

Understanding which lenders are actually active in Charleston for a given asset type is critical to evaluating whether a broker exercised competent market knowledge. CLS CRE maintains relationships with over 1,000 lenders nationally and is active in all 50 states, which allows us to speak with authority about the realistic lending universe for any given asset in any given market.

Community and Regional Banks: The most active lender category for commercial assets in the Charleston market is community and regional banks headquartered in West Virginia or the broader Appalachian region. These institutions are portfolio lenders, meaning they hold loans on their own balance sheets, which gives them flexibility on structure but also means their credit appetite is closely tied to their own deposit base and regulatory capital ratios. Typical credit parameters for this category include maximum LTV of 70% to 75%, minimum DSCR of 1.25x, personal recourse guarantees, and full financial disclosure requirements. Rates are generally priced at a spread over the prime rate or SOFR, with floors. For a mixed-use asset with any retail exposure in Charleston, these lenders will require strong sponsorship and seasoned operating history.

CMBS Conduit Lenders: CMBS execution is theoretically available for stabilized assets meeting minimum loan size thresholds, generally $2 million to $3 million as a practical floor, and meeting minimum DSCR and LTV standards on a programmatic basis. For the Charleston market, CMBS underwriting is often limited by the thin comparable sale base and appraisal defensibility issues. Conduit lenders will require a third-party appraisal that they commission independently, a Phase I environmental report, and full rent rolls with lease abstracts. CMBS loans are non-recourse with standard carveouts, which can be advantageous for borrowers, but the prepayment provisions, either defeasance or yield maintenance, are rigid and carry significant cost implications that must be disclosed clearly at origination.

Credit Unions and Community Development Lenders: For mixed-use assets with affordable housing components or assets in designated opportunity zones or distressed census tracts, credit unions and CDFI lenders can provide meaningful debt capital with below-market pricing and more flexible credit standards. These institutions are not always on a generalist broker's radar, and the failure to identify and present CDFI financing as an alternative, when it was clearly available and appropriate, can itself be a basis for a professional negligence claim.

Debt Funds and Private Lenders: Bridge and private debt is available in Charleston for transitional assets or situations where conventional financing is not immediately executable. These lenders price significantly higher, typically 10% to 13% in current conditions, and carry origination fees of 1% to 3%. A competent broker would not place a stabilized, long-term hold asset into bridge financing without a clearly articulated exit strategy and without documenting that conventional financing was not available or viable. Using a bridge lender when conventional financing was accessible is itself a potential standard of care issue.

What Attorneys Handling Charleston CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to Retain a CRE Financing Expert: If your case involves a question about whether a loan should or could have been made, whether the loan terms disclosed to your client were consistent with market practice, whether a mortgage broker exercised competent professional judgment, or whether an alternative financing structure was available, you need a CRE financing expert. These are not questions an appraiser can answer. Appraisers opine on value. CRE financing experts opine on the debt markets, underwriting methodology, broker and lender obligations, and the range of financing structures that were reasonably available in a given market at a given time.

What a CRE Financing Expert Can Address That Others Cannot: A qualified expert can reconstruct the underwriting from first principles, identify where the origination deviated from market standards, quantify the loan amount that the asset's actual income could support, evaluate whether the broker presented an appropriate range of lender options, assess whether disclosures were consistent with industry practice, and opine on what a reasonably competent lender or broker in the Charleston market would have done differently. These are specialized opinions grounded in transactional experience, not regulatory compliance or accounting expertise.

Qualifications That Matter: Not every retired banker or real estate consultant qualifies. The expert should have active transactional experience in commercial mortgage origination or brokerage, including direct experience in secondary and tertiary markets similar to Charleston. The expert should maintain current lender relationships that allow them to opine on what lenders actually require today and at the relevant historical date, not just what textbooks say. Rule 26 disclosure for CRE financing experts should include transaction volume, lender relationship breadth, geographic market experience, and prior expert witness engagements. CLS CRE has structured and closed approximately $1 billion in commercial real estate transactions and maintains active relationships with over 1,000 lenders across all 50 states, which supports credible, market-specific expert testimony.

Information Counsel Should Gather Before Engagement: To efficiently engage a CRE financing expert, counsel should assemble the original loan application package, the lender's internal credit memorandum if obtainable through discovery, the appraisal used at origination, the complete loan agreement and all addenda, the term sheet or commitment letter, all broker correspondence and fee disclosure documents, the property's historical operating statements for at least two years prior to closing, and the current rent roll as of the origination date. The expert will also need to establish the relevant market date, as lending standards, rate environments, and lender appetite vary materially from year to year.

Use of Expert Testimony at Trial and in Plan Hearings: In commercial real estate litigation, the CRE financing expert typically serves two functions. In written form, the Rule 26 expert report provides the court with a structured analysis of the origination, the applicable standard of care, and the specific departures that form the basis of the claim or defense. In testimony, the expert translates complex underwriting mechanics into accessible conclusions without sacrificing technical rigor. In bankruptcy plan confirmation hearings, the expert may be called to opine on the feasibility of proposed refinancing, the availability of exit financing, or whether a proposed plan interest rate is consistent with a market rate that similarly situated lenders would require.

Expert Witness: NNN Lease Rollover Upside and Financing Impact in Lansing, MI

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Below-Market NNN Lease and Rollover Upside Analysis

The Lansing Market Context

Lansing occupies a specific position in the Midwest commercial real estate landscape that makes it both defensible and complicated for single-tenant net lease assets. As the state capital of Michigan, the market carries an institutional anchor that mid-size Midwest markets often lack: state government employment, Michigan State University's economic footprint in adjacent East Lansing, and a manufacturing and healthcare sector that has historically supported retail and service-oriented NNN tenancy. That combination produces a market with more transactional depth than its population size would suggest, but also one with pricing discipline and lender conservatism that attorneys and borrowers need to understand before drawing conclusions about financing feasibility.

Vacancy dynamics in Lansing tell a nuanced story. Industrial vacancy in the Greater Lansing submarket has remained relatively tight, running in the 4% to 7% range depending on the quarter and product type, which has supported rent growth for industrial NNN assets. Retail fundamentals are more complicated. Lansing's retail market has absorbed significant inventory from anchor closures and category shifts, and inline retail vacancy in key corridors has at times exceeded 10% to 12%. For single-tenant retail NNN properties specifically, the market is bifurcated: corporate-guaranteed, long-term leases on essential service tenants trade at compressed cap rates consistent with national benchmarks, while secondary credit single-tenant retail with shorter lease terms or below-market rents faces meaningful pricing discounts and materially tighter lender appetite. Office fundamentals in Lansing reflect national trends but with some insulation from state and quasi-governmental occupancy; nonetheless, speculative single-tenant office NNN product outside of government or university-adjacent corridors faces significant cap rate expansion and lender scrutiny.

Cap rates in Lansing for single-tenant NNN assets with strong credit tenancy and market-rate leases have generally ranged from the low 6% range to the high 7% range depending on tenant credit, lease term, and location within the metro. Below-market leases with rollover risk, which is the specific configuration at the center of disputes like the one described here, push effective cap rates meaningfully higher and, critically, compress lender loan sizing in ways that are not always obvious from a simple income capitalization approach.

The Engagement

We were retained by litigation counsel representing one of the parties in a dispute involving a single-tenant NNN commercial property in the Lansing metropolitan area. The asset was a freestanding commercial building occupied by a single tenant under a triple net lease structure, with the tenant responsible for taxes, insurance, and maintenance obligations. The core issue was whether the in-place lease, which was priced below current market rent for comparable space in the submarket, precluded the property owner from refinancing at a loan-to-value ratio consistent with the asset's stated purchase price or assessed value, and whether the rollover upside upon lease expiration could be factored into current loan sizing.

Counsel needed answers to several specific questions that an appraiser alone could not fully address. First, how do commercial mortgage lenders actually underwrite below-market in-place rents in the context of a NNN single-tenant asset, and does the existence of rollover upside change the supportable loan amount at origination? Second, what are the applicable DSCR and LTV thresholds that govern this asset type, and are they fixed or do they flex based on lease term remaining? Third, could the financing structure itself, specifically the loan amount, term, and available proceeds, have been structured differently than represented by any party, and if so, what would that have required from a lender underwriting standpoint? These are financing expert questions, not appraisal questions, and they require an expert who understands how credit committees at commercial mortgage lenders actually evaluate these risks rather than simply how appraisers model stabilized value.

The Financial Analysis

Our analysis began with the in-place net operating income generated by the below-market lease. For this engagement, the in-place rent produced an annual NOI of approximately $118,000 after accounting for landlord obligations under the NNN structure and vacancy reserves appropriate to single-tenant exposure. We then modeled the market-rate NOI, which, based on comparable lease transactions in the Lansing submarket for this property type, would have supported an annual NOI of approximately $158,000 at full stabilization on market rents.

The DSCR threshold we applied was 1.25x, which is the prevailing standard for single-tenant NNN commercial assets with secondary or unrated credit tenancy at conventional commercial mortgage lenders and CMBS platforms as of current market conditions. For investment-grade tenancy with long remaining lease terms, some lenders will underwrite to 1.20x, but the subject asset's tenant profile and lease term remaining did not support the more aggressive threshold. We modeled debt service using a 7.00% note rate, which reflects current pricing for five-year fixed rate commercial mortgage loans on secondary-credit NNN assets in secondary markets, with a 25-year amortization schedule. That rate and amortization assumption is consistent with what regional banks, credit unions, and life company correspondent lenders were quoting for similar credits in the Lansing market at the relevant time horizon.

At 1.25x DSCR using in-place NOI of $118,000, the maximum annual debt service supportable is $94,400. At a 7.00% note rate on a 25-year amortization, $94,400 in annual debt service supports a loan of approximately $1,195,000. If the lender applies a 65% LTV constraint, which is typical for single-tenant NNN retail in a secondary market with below-market tenancy, the implied maximum property value supported at that loan amount is approximately $1,838,000. If the actual or asserted value of the property was materially higher, the binding constraint is DSCR on in-place income, not LTV, and the loan proceeds are income-limited rather than value-limited.

The rollover upside NOI of $158,000 tells a different story at stabilization. At 1.25x DSCR, that NOI supports $126,400 in annual debt service, which at 7.00% over 25 years supports a loan of approximately $1,598,000. At 65% LTV, the implied value ceiling is approximately $2,458,000. This is a meaningful difference of approximately $400,000 in loan proceeds and over $600,000 in implied value, which is exactly the kind of gap that becomes central to damages analysis or feasibility disputes. The critical analytical point, and the one that lenders uniformly enforce, is that rollover upside NOI is not used to size the loan at origination unless the lease has already rolled and the new market-rate lease is in place, executed, and rent-commencement has occurred.

The Complicating Factors

Below-Market Rents and In-Place Income Constraints

Commercial mortgage lenders underwrite to in-place cash flow, not pro forma cash flow, for single-tenant assets. This is not a policy preference; it is a structural requirement driven by how single-asset commercial mortgage default risk is evaluated. A single-tenant property generates 100% of its income from one occupant. If that occupant vacates, income drops to zero. Lenders therefore apply income-based loan sizing to whatever income is contractually committed and currently flowing, without credit for future rents that have not been contracted. A below-market lease means a below-market loan, period, unless the lease expires within the loan term and the lender is willing to structure a rollover reserve or earn-out structure, which is discussed below.

Lease Term Remaining and Lender Maturity Alignment

The remaining lease term relative to the loan term is a critical underwriting variable. If the lease expires during the loan term, most lenders will require either a lease renewal guarantee, a cash reserve funded at closing sufficient to carry debt service through a re-leasing period, or a structured earn-out where additional loan proceeds are released upon execution of a qualifying replacement lease. These mechanisms exist, but they require the borrower to accept lower initial proceeds and potentially negotiate complex loan documents. If the lease expires after the loan term, the income floor is secured for the loan period and the rollover risk is the next owner's or the refinancing lender's problem. The position of lease expiration relative to loan maturity materially changes what lenders will offer and at what terms.

Single-Tenant Concentration Risk

Lenders in the Lansing market apply concentration risk overlays to single-tenant assets that do not apply to multi-tenant properties. A 20,000 square foot multi-tenant retail center with six tenants has diversified income; a single-tenant building with the same square footage has binary income: either the tenant pays or the income is zero. Lenders price this through more conservative LTV constraints, higher DSCR requirements, and shorter initial loan terms with extension options tied to lease performance. For properties in Lansing specifically, where the re-tenanting market for certain commercial property types is not as deep as major metros, lenders will also scrutinize the asset's alternative use potential and the likely re-leasing timeline if the current tenant exits.

Tenant Credit and Lease Guarantee Structure

Below-market rents with a credit-rated, investment-grade tenant guarantee can still produce an acceptable financing profile because the certainty of income, even at below-market levels, is valued by lenders. Below-market rents on a non-rated, regional, or local tenant with a limited balance sheet present compounding risk: below-market income and elevated default probability. In that scenario, the financing universe contracts to relationship-driven community bank lenders or to debt funds willing to price the added risk through higher rates and lower proceeds. This distinction is material in Lansing, where the tenant profile on many single-tenant retail and service properties does not carry institutional-grade credit support.

The Lansing Lender Market

For single-tenant NNN assets in Lansing, the active lender universe organizes into four categories, each with distinct credit parameters and appetite for complexity.

Community and Regional Banks: Lansing has meaningful depth in community and regional bank relationships. Institutions with Michigan charters and significant Lansing commercial lending portfolios are the most active lenders for single-tenant assets in the $1M to $5M range. These lenders underwrite to in-place income, require personal guarantees on non-institutional borrowers, and typically offer 5-year fixed or adjustable rates with 20 to 25-year amortization. LTV constraints for secondary credit tenancy typically run 60% to 65%. The advantage is relationship flexibility; a community bank with an existing borrower relationship may structure around a short lease term through reserves or covenant protections that a national lender would not consider. The disadvantage is balance sheet size and concentration limits that can cap individual loan amounts.

Credit Unions: Several Michigan-chartered credit unions with commercial real estate mandates are active in the Lansing market for owner-occupied and investor NNN product. Their pricing is often competitive with community banks, and their underwriting on leased investment properties generally follows bank conventions, though they may have less familiarity with complex lease structure issues.

Life Insurance Companies and Correspondents: For single-tenant NNN assets with investment-grade or near-investment-grade tenancy and 10 or more years of lease term remaining, life company correspondent lenders are active in Lansing. Life company debt is typically the lowest-rate option, with non-recourse structure and 10-year fixed terms, but their credit box is narrow: strong tenant credit, long lease term, and in-place income at or near market are effectively required. A below-market lease with rollover risk will rarely clear life company underwriting without extraordinary tenant credit support.

Debt Funds and Bridge Lenders: For transitional assets, short remaining lease terms, or properties where the in-place income does not support conventional loan sizing, private debt funds and bridge lenders provide proceeds against projected stabilized value with interest reserves and earn-out structures. Rates are materially higher, typically floating at 300 to 500 basis points over reference rates, and terms are short (12 to 36 months). This is not a permanent financing solution but a gap bridge for owners who need proceeds today against value that will be fully realizable upon lease rollover.

What Attorneys Handling Lansing CRE Disputes Need to Know

Retaining a CRE financing expert is distinct from retaining an appraiser, and the distinction matters in cases where the central question is not "what is the property worth" but rather "what could the property support as a loan, under what conditions, and was the financing feasible as represented." Appraisers determine value using income, sales comparison, and cost approaches. A CRE financing expert analyzes how lenders actually underwrite, what they will and will not lend against, and whether a specific loan structure was achievable in the real market at the time and conditions at issue.

The questions a CRE financing expert addresses that fall outside appraiser scope include: Was the loan amount claimed to be achievable actually supportable given the in-place income and lender market conditions at the relevant date? Could the rollover upside have been monetized through any available loan structure, and if so, at what cost and on what terms? What is the financing impact of the lease term remaining, and does that impact preclude refinancing at the LTV alleged by a party? What would a hypothetical arm's-length lender have required in the way of reserves, guarantees, or structural protections given the tenant profile and lease term?

A qualified CRE financing expert is not simply a retired banker. The expert should have active, current market engagement, demonstrated transaction experience with the asset type and deal complexity at issue, knowledge of the specific lender market in the relevant geography, and the analytical capacity to translate lender underwriting standards into quantified loan sizing conclusions that can be presented clearly to a judge or jury. Experience as an expert witness under Rule 26, including preparation of written reports, deposition testimony, and trial testimony, is a meaningful differentiator.

Before engaging a CRE financing expert, counsel should gather: the complete lease including all amendments and SNDAs; rent rolls; trailing operating statements; any appraisals or broker opinions of value; loan applications, term sheets, or commitment letters in evidence; correspondence between the borrower and any lender; and any internal analysis prepared by the parties regarding financing assumptions. The expert will need these materials to reconstruct the financing feasibility analysis from the ground up using market-standard methodology rather than the assumptions of any interested party.

In trial or at a Plan of Reorganization confirmation hearing, CRE financing expert testimony is typically used to establish whether a proposed transaction or restructuring is feasible from a capital markets standpoint, to rebut an opposing party's claim that financing was or was not available, or to quantify the impact of specific lease or property conditions on loan proceeds and borrower equity. This testimony is technical but must be communicated accessibly; the best expert witnesses can translate complex underwriting mechanics into conclusions that a fact-finder without capital markets background can evaluate and apply.

Key Takeaways

  • In-place income drives loan sizing, not pro forma income. Commercial mortgage lenders underwrite single-tenant NNN assets on the income that is contractually in place, not the income that will theoretically exist after lease rollover. Rollover upside does not increase loan proceeds at origination unless a new market-rate lease is already executed and rent has commenced.
  • DSCR and LTV constraints compound on below-market leases. When in-place rents are below market, both the income-constrained loan amount and the LTV-constrained loan amount decline simultaneously. The binding constraint in most below-market lease situations is DSCR on in-place income, which can produce loan proceeds materially lower than a value-based analysis would suggest.
  • Lease term remaining relative to loan term is a material underwriting variable. Lenders evaluate whether lease expiration creates rollover risk within the loan period. Leases expiring during the loan term require reserves, earn-outs, or structural protections that reduce initial proceeds and complicate loan documents.
  • Tenant credit quality compounds below-market rent risk. A below-market lease from an investment-grade tenant is a manageable financing challenge. A below-market lease from an unrated local tenant is a compounding credit risk that narrows the lender universe to relationship banks and bridge debt funds at higher cost.
  • The Lansing lender market has real depth but not unlimited flexibility. Community banks and regional lenders provide meaningful competition in the $1M to $5M range, but their credit constraints on single-tenant NNN product with secondary credit tenancy and below-market rents are materially tighter than promotional financing representations might suggest.
  • Bridge and debt fund structures exist but at significant cost. Transitional or rollover-upside scenarios can be financed through bridge products, but the rate differential (often 300 to 500 basis points above conventional debt), short terms, and personal recourse requirements make these structures materially different from permanent financing for purposes of any feasibility or damages analysis.

Expert Witness: CRE Financing Feasibility in Chapter 11 Proceedings in New York, NY

Trevor Damyan Founder & Principal Broker
CA DRE #02244836

The New York Market Context

New York City is the deepest, most liquid, and most closely scrutinized commercial real estate market in the United States. It is also one of the most complex markets in which to execute bankruptcy exit financing, and that complexity has direct implications for how courts evaluate Plan feasibility. When a distressed commercial asset sits inside a Chapter 11 estate in New York, the financing question is never abstract. Lenders know this market intimately, their underwriting reflects it, and the range of outcomes, both favorable and unfavorable, is wider here than almost anywhere else.

The New York multifamily market remains the most closely watched sector in the country. Stabilized rent-regulated properties in the outer boroughs and upper Manhattan have seen cap rates compress into the 4.5% to 5.5% range for well-located assets, while free-market multifamily in prime submarkets commands cap rates as low as 3.75% to 4.25% on trailing income. The Rent Stabilization Law of 2019 materially altered the income ceiling on regulated portfolios, a factor that continues to generate litigation, restructuring events, and financing disputes. Any expert analysis of a rent-stabilized asset in Chapter 11 must account for legal rent roll caps and the ceiling they impose on debt service coverage calculations.

Office fundamentals remain challenged across most of Manhattan. Direct vacancy in Midtown South and the Plaza District hovers between 16% and 19% on a current basis, with sublease availability adding several percentage points on top of that. Class B and Class C office in Downtown Manhattan presents even more difficult underwriting, with some lenders effectively refusing to commit capital without material occupancy improvements. Mixed-use assets anchored by office components require careful income stratification before a financing analysis can be completed.

Retail in New York has bifurcated sharply. Ground-floor retail on high-traffic corridors in Manhattan is recovering, with some asking rents returning to near pre-pandemic levels in Soho, the Upper East Side, and select portions of the Flatiron District. Secondary retail, particularly in the outer boroughs or in assets with above-grade space or poor frontage, remains difficult to underwrite at historical rent levels. This bifurcation directly affects debt capacity analysis when retail income is part of the subject property's revenue stream.

Industrial and last-mile logistics assets in New York, particularly in the Bronx, Queens, and Brooklyn, have seen the most aggressive rent growth of any asset class in the five boroughs over the past four years. Vacancy in well-located industrial submarkets is running below 3%, and rent growth has outpaced much of the national market. For distressed industrial assets in bankruptcy, the underlying income story is often strong enough to support conventional financing, even where ownership structure has been compromised.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by counsel representing a creditor committee in a Chapter 11 proceeding involving a mixed-use commercial property located in a primary New York City submarket. The asset combined ground-floor retail space with multifamily residential units above, a configuration common throughout Brooklyn and Queens, and one that introduces multiple layers of underwriting complexity. The property had been in Chapter 11 for approximately fourteen months at the time of our retention, and the proceeding had reached the Plan confirmation phase.

Counsel needed a structured expert opinion on two specific questions. First, whether the subject property could support first-mortgage financing in an amount sufficient to satisfy the claims of senior secured creditors under a proposed Plan of Reorganization. Second, which categories of lenders operating in the New York market would actively originate a loan on a property exiting Chapter 11 bankruptcy, and under what terms. These are capital markets questions, not appraisal questions, and they require a different discipline than valuation alone.

Our role was to function as a Rule 26 financing feasibility expert. CLS CRE produced a written opinion analyzing the property's income stream, its supportable debt load at current market underwriting standards, the realistic universe of available lenders, and the conditions under which those lenders would commit. That opinion was submitted with the Plan confirmation papers and formed the basis for our expert testimony regarding financing feasibility.

The Financial Analysis

The starting point for any financing feasibility analysis is not appraised value. It is net operating income. Lenders underwrite to debt service coverage first and use LTV as a secondary constraint. In New York, that sequencing matters more than in many other markets because values can be high enough that LTV alone would suggest substantial loan capacity, while actual NOI yields a far more conservative number. Both tests must be run, and the binding constraint governs.

For the subject property, we analyzed the trailing twelve-month rent roll, lease abstracts, operating expense history, and management statements. We applied vacancy and credit loss at market-standard rates: 5% on the residential component and 8% to 10% on the retail component, reflecting higher volatility in retail tenancy. We then normalized non-recurring expenses and applied a management fee consistent with third-party management costs in the New York market, typically 4% to 5% of effective gross income for mixed-use assets of this type. The result was a stabilized NOI figure used as the basis for all debt sizing calculations.

In this engagement, the stabilized NOI for the subject property was determined to be approximately $1,285,000 annually. This figure represented the income the property could be reasonably expected to generate under normal market operations, post-bankruptcy, with competent management in place.

We applied a debt service coverage ratio threshold of 1.25x, which reflects the market standard for conventional first-mortgage financing on mixed-use assets in New York. Most bank and agency lenders require 1.20x at a minimum; institutional and life company lenders typically require 1.25x or higher. We used 1.25x as our primary test because it represents the standard that constrains the broadest category of available lenders and therefore produces the most defensible analysis for a court proceeding.

At a 1.25x DSCR, the maximum annual debt service the property can support is $1,028,000. Using a 30-year amortization schedule and a note rate of 7.25%, which reflected prevailing market rates for mixed-use assets in New York at the time of the analysis, that debt service figure supports a maximum loan of approximately $13,950,000. We stress-tested this calculation at 7.50% to account for rate movement, which reduced the supportable loan to approximately $13,580,000, a difference of roughly $370,000 at the margin.

We then applied the LTV overlay. Based on an independently appraised value of $19,500,000, a 65% LTV cap (standard for this asset class and complexity level) produces a maximum loan of $12,675,000. In this case, the LTV constraint was the binding test, not the DSCR. The property's income stream could technically support more debt than the LTV ceiling permitted. The analysis therefore concluded that the maximum supportable first-mortgage loan, at current market underwriting standards, was approximately $12,675,000, with a DSCR of approximately 1.34x at that loan amount. That level of coverage provided meaningful headroom above the minimum threshold and supported a conclusion that the financing was feasible and not merely technically possible.

The Complicating Factors

Bankruptcy provenance of the asset. The most significant complicating factor in any exit financing engagement is the bankruptcy itself. Most conventional bank lenders, particularly community banks and regional institutions, have credit policies that require a clean title history and no recent insolvency proceedings. Their loan committees are generally unwilling to originate a loan on an asset that has just exited Chapter 11, regardless of the underlying property metrics. This does not eliminate the financing universe, but it does narrow it substantially. The practical consequence is that the exit financing universe skews toward bridge lenders, debt funds, and CMBS conduit platforms that are structurally indifferent to bankruptcy history as long as the property itself underwrites.

Retail component income reliability. Mixed-use assets in New York with retail exposure below market occupancy or with lease terms that are shorter than lender minimums (typically three years of remaining term at origination) introduce credit risk that affects loan sizing. In this engagement, one retail tenant had a lease that expired within eighteen months of the anticipated loan closing. Most lenders either excluded that income from underwriting entirely or applied a vacancy reserve against it. The treatment of that lease materially affected the DSCR calculation and required us to present both a stabilized and an as-is income scenario to accurately represent the financing range.

Rent regulation complexity. Where the residential component includes rent-stabilized units, lenders must navigate the post-2019 regulatory environment carefully. Individual apartment improvements can no longer drive meaningful rent increases, and the pathway to market-rate conversions has narrowed significantly. Lenders who previously relied on upside from deregulation as part of their exit thesis have largely abandoned that underwriting. This compresses the income ceiling, compresses supportable debt, and in some cases makes the difference between a feasible and infeasible plan. Expert analysis must explicitly address whether any income upside assumptions embedded in a competing party's analysis are realistically achievable under current law.

Title and lien resolution. Assets exiting Chapter 11 often carry unresolved mechanics liens, subordinate judgment liens, or disputed encumbrances that survived the automatic stay. Exit lenders universally require clean title as a condition of commitment. Where title cannot be delivered at closing because of unresolved lien disputes, the financing becomes contingent rather than committed. Courts handling Plan confirmation need to understand this distinction. A lender's letter of interest is not a commitment. A commitment is not a closing. The feasibility analysis must address whether the title conditions precedent to a close are achievable within the Plan's proposed timeline.

The New York Lender Market

The New York commercial real estate lending market is the largest and most sophisticated in the country, but access to that capital is not uniform. For a bankruptcy exit scenario on a mixed-use asset, the realistic lender universe consists of several distinct categories, each with different requirements, pricing, and risk tolerance.

Debt funds and bridge lenders. This is the most accessible category for a bankruptcy exit loan. Non-bank private credit funds and bridge lending platforms are structurally indifferent to bankruptcy history. They underwrite to the asset, not the borrower's recent credit history, and they are accustomed to closing in complex legal environments. In New York, pricing for a stabilized mixed-use bridge loan in 2024 ranges from SOFR plus 350 to SOFR plus 550 depending on LTV, asset quality, and execution certainty. Terms are typically 24 to 36 months with extension options. The cost is higher than conventional financing, but the certainty of execution is substantially greater. For Plan feasibility purposes, this is often the most defensible category to present to a court because commitment probability is highest.

CMBS conduit lenders. Conduit platforms will originate on bankruptcy exit assets if the property underwriting is clean and the borrowing entity has been restructured into a bankruptcy-remote special purpose vehicle. CMBS loans offer longer fixed-rate terms, typically 5 or 10 years, at pricing that has recently ranged from approximately 7.00% to 7.75% on a fixed all-in basis. The underwriting process is more formal and document-intensive, and conduit lenders require a clear post-closing operating structure. Timeline to close is 60 to 90 days from application, which must be factored into Plan implementation schedules.

New York community banks and savings institutions. This category remains deeply active in the New York multifamily and mixed-use market, but almost universally declines bankruptcy exit scenarios. These institutions, of which there are dozens actively originating in the five boroughs, have loan committees that apply character-of-borrower standards that a recent Chapter 11 history fails. They should not be cited as available lenders in a Plan feasibility opinion without qualification, as their practical unavailability for a bankruptcy exit transaction would expose that opinion to credible challenge.

Life company and agency lenders. For assets with a substantial multifamily component, agency financing through the GSEs is potentially available on a post-confirmation basis if the borrower can meet seasoning requirements and the property is stabilized. Life company lenders apply the most conservative underwriting of any category, typically requiring 1.30x to 1.35x DSCR and 60% to 65% LTV, and they are the least likely to engage on a bankruptcy exit timeline. Their relevance in a feasibility opinion is primarily as a benchmark for stabilized value-level financing, not as a realistic exit commitment source.

What Attorneys Handling New York CRE Disputes Need to Know

The first question attorneys ask is whether they need a CRE financing expert at all if they already have an appraiser. The answer is that appraisers and financing experts answer different questions. An appraiser determines market value. A CRE financing expert determines how much of that value a lender will actually lend against, under what terms, through which institutions, and with what probability of execution. At a Plan confirmation hearing, the court must find that the Plan is feasible, meaning that it is not likely to be followed by further liquidation or reorganization. That finding requires evidence about financing, not just value.

Counsel should retain a CRE financing expert as early as possible in the plan development process, ideally before the Plan is drafted, so that the financing structure embedded in the Plan is realistic and defensible. A Plan built around financing assumptions that the expert subsequently cannot support is far more damaging than a conservative Plan built on achievable financing. We have been retained in matters where the opposing party's Plan contained financing assumptions that were simply not executable in the actual market, and the ability to demonstrate that gap with specificity was decisive.

A qualified CRE financing expert is not a retired banker. The relevant qualification is current, active market engagement. Lender credit standards, pricing, and appetite change materially from year to year and sometimes quarter to quarter. An expert who has not been actively originating or placing commercial mortgage loans within the prior 12 to 24 months cannot speak with authority about what lenders will actually do today. CLS CRE has closed approximately $1 billion in transaction volume with more than 1,000 active lender relationships across 50 states. That market presence is what makes the expert opinion credible and current.

Before the expert engagement begins, counsel should gather the following: current rent roll with lease abstracts and lease expirations; trailing 24 months of operating statements; the most recent appraisal or broker opinion of value; all existing loan documents and any default or forbearance correspondence; current title report; and any Plan term sheets or financing proposals already received. The earlier and more completely this material is assembled, the faster the expert can produce a credible, defensible opinion.

At the Plan confirmation hearing, CRE financing expert testimony typically addresses three things: the methodology used to determine supportable debt, the lender market available for this asset type in this market, and the specific conditions under which committed financing could be obtained. That testimony, combined with an appraiser's value opinion, gives the court a complete picture of Plan feasibility from both a value and a capital availability perspective.

Key Takeaways

  • NOI is the governing constraint, not appraised value. Supportable debt in New York is determined first by debt service coverage and second by LTV. Both tests must be run. The binding constraint governs, and the two do not always align.
  • A 1.25x DSCR is the appropriate market-standard threshold for mixed-use and multifamily assets in New York when the financing universe includes conventional and institutional lenders. Using a lower coverage threshold overstates loan capacity and produces an opinion that will not survive cross-examination.
  • Bankruptcy history narrows the lender universe significantly, but does not eliminate it. Debt funds, bridge lenders, and CMBS conduit platforms are the realistic execution categories for a bankruptcy exit loan. Community banks and savings institutions are generally not available for this type of transaction and should not be represented as such.
  • Retail income with short lease terms must be stress-tested or excluded from the base underwriting case. Lenders apply vacancy reserves or haircuts to near-term lease expirations, and a feasibility opinion must reflect that discipline or it will be challenged.
  • Rent stabilization law in New York materially constrains income upside on regulated residential portfolios. Any plan projections that assume deregulation-driven rent growth as a pathway to debt service coverage should be scrutinized carefully and challenged with specific statutory and market evidence.
  • Title must be deliverable within the Plan timeline. A lender letter of interest, a term sheet, and a commitment letter are fundamentally different instruments with different levels of execution certainty. Courts should understand this distinction when evaluating Plan feasibility evidence.
  • A CRE financing expert must have current, active market engagement to provide credible testimony. The standard is not academic knowledge of lending principles; it is demonstrated, current access to the lender market and knowledge of where capital is actually deploying today.

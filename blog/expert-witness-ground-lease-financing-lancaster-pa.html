Expert Witness: Ground Lease Financing Disputes and Analysis in Lancaster, PA

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Ground Lease Financing Complexity

The Lancaster Market Context

Lancaster, Pennsylvania sits at an interesting inflection point in the Mid-Atlantic commercial real estate landscape. Historically characterized by agricultural land, light manufacturing, and a deeply rooted community banking infrastructure, Lancaster has evolved into a legitimate regional commercial hub with enough transaction volume to generate sophisticated financing disputes. That evolution is exactly the kind of environment where ground lease structures emerge, and where their complexity can become the center of litigation.

On the industrial side, Lancaster County has benefited from significant distribution and logistics demand driven by its position along the Route 30 and Route 283 corridors, providing access to the I-83 and Pennsylvania Turnpike networks. Industrial vacancy in Lancaster has tracked in the 3% to 5% range in recent years, well below the national average, and net asking rents for Class B warehouse and flex product have climbed into the $7.50 to $10.00 per square foot range NNN depending on clear height and dock configuration. For retail and mixed-use product, Lancaster's downtown core and suburban nodes like Fruitville Pike and Columbia Avenue have maintained occupancy through a combination of local-service tenants and food-and-beverage operators that survived the pandemic with less damage than larger metros.

Multifamily cap rates in Lancaster have compressed into the 5.0% to 6.5% range for stabilized assets, reflecting both the market's relative affordability compared to Philadelphia and the demand pressure from Harrisburg overflow growth. Office fundamentals are weaker, with suburban vacancy pushing above 12% in some nodes, though medical office tied to Lancaster General Health's system-wide presence has remained comparatively resilient. Ground lease arrangements in Lancaster tend to appear most often on two asset types: long-hold institutional land parcels in established retail or mixed-use nodes, and family-owned agricultural-to-commercial conversion sites where the original landowner retains fee ownership and ground leases to a commercial developer or operator. That second category, in particular, generates the kinds of title, subordination, and SNDA complications that end up in courtrooms.

The Engagement

We were retained by litigation counsel representing a commercial real estate borrower in a dispute involving a ground-leased commercial property in Lancaster County. The underlying asset was an improved commercial property, specifically a retail and service-oriented mixed-use building, constructed on land held under a long-term ground lease rather than fee simple ownership. The borrower had sought refinancing of the existing construction-to-permanent loan, and the transaction failed to close. The dispute centered on whether the ground lease, as structured and as delivered by the fee owner, was financeable under market standards for commercial mortgage lending, and whether the fee owner's refusal to execute certain lender-required documentation was the proximate cause of the financing failure.

Counsel retained Commercial Lending Solutions to analyze three interconnected questions. First, whether the ground lease structure as written met market standards for institutional and conventional commercial mortgage financing. Second, whether the SNDA agreement proposed by the fee owner was commercially reasonable or whether it deviated materially from what lenders require in the market. Third, what the maximum supportable loan amount was based on the property's stabilized income stream, and how that figure was affected by the leasehold versus fee simple financing context.

CLS CRE was engaged to produce a Rule 26 expert report addressing all three questions, with supporting financial analysis, lender market commentary, and opinions grounded in our firm's direct experience placing and underwriting leasehold mortgages across the country. Our approximately $1 billion in transaction volume across 50 states and relationships with more than 1,000 institutional and conventional lenders provide the market depth necessary to form reliable opinions about what lenders actually require, as opposed to what any single lender's written guidelines might suggest.

The Financial Analysis

The financial analysis began with a reconstruction of the property's stabilized net operating income. Using actual rent rolls provided in discovery, executed leases, historical operating statements, and landlord-provided expense records, we derived a trailing twelve-month NOI and then modeled a stabilized forward NOI reflecting current market rents for comparable Lancaster County retail and service space. The subject property's blended gross revenue was approximately $620,000 annually across its occupied suites, with a vacancy and credit loss allowance of 7% applied, consistent with market vacancy in Lancaster's suburban retail nodes. Operating expenses, including real estate taxes, insurance, common area maintenance, and management, totaled approximately $148,000. This produced a stabilized NOI of approximately $429,000.

The critical technical issue in a leasehold financing is how lenders treat ground rent as an operating expense. Unlike fee simple properties where real estate taxes and insurance are the primary fixed charges, a leasehold property carries an additional senior fixed obligation: the ground rent payment due to the fee owner. In this engagement, annual ground rent was $72,000 per year with scheduled step-ups. Before any debt service calculation, that $72,000 is subtracted from the property's NOI, leaving a net cash flow available for mortgage debt service of approximately $357,000. This distinction is not academic. A lender underwriting this asset without properly accounting for the ground rent as a senior fixed charge would overstate the property's debt capacity and produce a misleading loan sizing.

Applying the market-standard debt service coverage ratio of 1.25x, which is the floor required by the majority of conventional commercial lenders and CMBS programs for retail-anchored and mixed-use product in secondary markets, the maximum annual debt service supportable by the leasehold NOI after ground rent is $285,600. At a 7.00% interest rate on a 25-year amortization schedule, which reflects pricing consistent with conventional commercial mortgage terms available to leasehold borrowers in this credit environment, that debt service figure supports a maximum loan of approximately $3,350,000.

At the same time, most lenders cap leasehold mortgage LTV at 65% to 70% rather than the 75% typically available on fee simple collateral, because leasehold interests carry termination risk absent adequate protective provisions. The appraised leasehold value of the property was $5,200,000, which means a 65% LTV constraint produces a maximum loan of approximately $3,380,000. In this case, the two constraints converged closely, meaning DSCR was the binding constraint, not LTV. The analysis established a well-supported maximum loan of approximately $3,350,000 under market conditions, assuming the ground lease structure was otherwise financeable. The further analysis then addressed whether it was.

The Complicating Factors

Ground lease financing introduces a category of structural complexity that has no direct analog in fee simple mortgage lending. Lenders underwriting a fee simple mortgage know that in the event of a default, foreclosure extinguishes subordinate interests and the lender steps into fee ownership. Leasehold mortgage lenders face a fundamentally different risk profile, one where the value and enforceability of their collateral depends entirely on the continued existence of the ground lease, the creditworthiness of the fee owner's obligations, and the protections built into the lease itself. The following complicating factors were central to our analysis in this matter.

Lease Term and Remaining Term Requirements

Institutional lenders universally require that the ground lease term extend beyond the loan maturity date by a meaningful margin. The market standard is that the remaining lease term, including renewal options the lender can exercise independently, must exceed the loan term by at least 10 years, and most lenders require 25 to 30 years of remaining term beyond loan maturity. In Lancaster's family-owned land conversion situations, ground leases are sometimes structured with initial terms of 30 or 40 years without lender-friendly renewal provisions. A borrower trying to finance a 10-year commercial mortgage on a ground lease with 14 years of remaining term will find the financing market essentially closed, not because lenders dislike Lancaster, but because the collateral itself is impaired by the lease structure.

Subordination, Non-Disturbance, and Attornment

The SNDA agreement is the single most contested document in any ground lease financing. Its three components each protect a different party. Subordination means the fee owner agrees that the leasehold mortgage lender's interest is senior to the fee interest in the event of a conflict, or at minimum that the lender's position will not be disturbed by a fee owner default or future encumbrances placed on the fee. Non-disturbance means that if the borrower-tenant defaults on the ground lease, the fee owner will not terminate the ground lease (and therefore the lender's collateral) without first providing the lender an opportunity to cure the default and step into the tenant's position. Attornment means that if the lender forecloses and takes over the tenant's leasehold position, the fee owner will recognize the lender or its successor as the new tenant.

Fee owners, particularly individual landowners or family-held entities in markets like Lancaster, frequently resist full subordination because they do not want a lender's mortgage cloud to attach to their fee interest. This is a legitimate concern, but lenders cannot accept ground leases where the fee owner retains an unqualified right to terminate the lease for the tenant's default without notice to and cure rights for the lender. The SNDA proposed by the fee owner in this engagement was deficient in three specific respects: it omitted the lender's independent cure right, it failed to provide adequate notice periods before termination, and it preserved the fee owner's right to modify the lease terms without lender consent. Each deficiency independently would have caused any institutional lender reviewing the ground lease to decline financing.

Rent Step-Up Provisions and Ground Rent Coverage

When ground rent includes scheduled escalations tied to CPI or fixed percentages, lenders must model the impact on future net cash flow available for debt service. A ground lease with aggressive rent step-ups can erode the debt service coverage ratio over time, particularly in a stabilized tenancy where the lease rates are fixed. In this engagement, the ground lease included a 3% annual step-up in ground rent. Over a 10-year loan term, that step-up would increase annual ground rent from $72,000 to approximately $96,700, reducing the net NOI available for debt service by roughly $24,700 annually in year ten. Lenders typically underwrite to the worst-case year or model a haircut to the DSCR requirement to account for this compression. We modeled this sensitivity and concluded that a lender applying standard underwriting would reduce the supportable loan amount by approximately $280,000 to account for the rent step-up pressure, bringing the effective maximum to approximately $3,070,000.

Lender Consent Rights and Lease Modification Provisions

Market-standard ground leases for financeable leasehold properties include express provisions prohibiting the fee owner and tenant from modifying, surrendering, or terminating the ground lease without the leasehold mortgage lender's written consent. Without this protection, a fee owner and a new tenant could theoretically replace the existing ground lease with a new one that extinguishes the lender's collateral. The ground lease at issue in this engagement was silent on lender consent rights, which is itself a disqualifying deficiency under the underwriting standards of every CMBS conduit and the majority of institutional portfolio lenders.

The Lancaster Lender Market

Lancaster County has a well-developed community banking infrastructure that is genuinely active in commercial real estate lending. Institutions headquartered or with significant lending operations in the region have historically provided construction, mini-perm, and permanent financing for commercial projects across office, retail, industrial, and mixed-use categories. For ground lease financing specifically, however, that community bank market narrows considerably. Community and regional banks lending in Lancaster are generally willing to do leasehold mortgages where they have a relationship with the borrower, the ground lease term is adequate, and the SNDA can be negotiated. But they apply their own credit overlays, typically requiring DSCR of at least 1.25x on the net-of-ground-rent NOI, capping LTV at 65%, and requiring personal recourse guarantees for transactions below $5 million.

For loans above $5 million or for borrowers seeking non-recourse financing, the market shifts to life insurance companies and CMBS conduits. Both categories lend in Lancaster, though life companies tend to focus on stabilized, institutional-quality assets with creditworthy tenancy. Life companies will engage on leasehold financing, but they apply the most rigorous SNDA requirements of any lender category and typically require the ground lease to have been reviewed and approved by their own legal counsel before they will issue a term sheet. CMBS conduits are more formulaic but equally strict on ground lease mechanics; the rating agencies that ultimately buy CMBS bonds have published explicit criteria for what a ground lease must contain to be included in a rated pool, and those criteria are not negotiable.

SBA 504 financing, which is active in Lancaster through several certified development companies, is generally not available for leasehold properties unless the borrower occupies the majority of the building as an owner-user, and even then, SBA's ground lease requirements are detailed and strict. Debt funds and bridge lenders represent a fallback option for leasehold properties with structural deficiencies, but their pricing, typically floating rate at 300 to 500 basis points over SOFR with origination fees of 1.5% to 2.5%, reflects the elevated risk they are taking on.

What Attorneys Handling Lancaster CRE Disputes Need to Know

Commercial real estate financing expert witnesses serve a function that is categorically different from what an appraiser provides. An appraiser determines value. A CRE financing expert determines whether and on what terms a property could be financed, which lenders would actually extend credit, what structural or documentary deficiencies prevent financing, and whether a specific financing failure was caused by market conditions, borrower conduct, or the actions of another party such as a fee owner who refused to execute a required document. These are not overlapping questions. In a ground lease dispute, the appraiser's leasehold value opinion may be entirely accurate while the property remains completely unfinanceable because the SNDA is deficient. That distinction can be the difference between a recoverable damage claim and no claim at all.

Attorneys should retain a CRE financing expert when the litigation involves a failed financing, a disputed loan commitment, a claim that a party's conduct prevented a closing, a ground lease or leasehold structure, a lender liability claim, or a dispute about whether a proposed financing was commercially reasonable. The expert's opinion carries weight precisely because it is grounded in active market participation, not just historical knowledge. A retired banker from a prior decade may know what that institution required years ago; an active capital markets professional with current lender relationships across 1,000 or more institutions can testify to what the market actually requires today and why.

Before engaging a CRE financing expert, counsel should gather the following documentation: the ground lease and all amendments; the proposed or executed SNDA; any lender term sheets, commitment letters, or declination letters; correspondence between the borrower and lender regarding the ground lease; the property's rent roll, leases, operating statements, and any appraisal; and all communications between the fee owner and borrower regarding the SNDA or lease modification requests. This documentation forms the foundation of a credible expert report and will be the basis for the expert's deposition testimony.

At trial, CRE financing expert testimony is typically used to establish what a willing commercial lender would require, whether those requirements were met or could have been met, and what the economic consequences of a financing failure were. In bankruptcy plan hearings, financing expert testimony is used to establish whether a proposed reorganization plan is feasible, whether the exit financing contemplated is achievable, and whether the proposed terms are consistent with market conditions.

Key Takeaways

  • Ground rent must be treated as a senior fixed charge in all leasehold underwriting. Failing to deduct ground rent before calculating debt service produces an inflated loan sizing that no institutional lender would accept, and misrepresents the property's true debt capacity.
  • The SNDA agreement is not a formality. A deficient SNDA, specifically one that omits independent lender cure rights, adequate notice before termination, and lender consent to lease modifications, is a disqualifying deficiency that closes off the institutional and CMBS lending market entirely, regardless of how strong the property's income stream is.
  • Leasehold LTV constraints are real and non-negotiable at the institutional level. A borrower expecting 75% LTV on a leasehold property will find the market standard is 65%, reducing loan proceeds and potentially making a transaction infeasible on its own terms.
  • Ground lease term adequacy is a threshold issue. If the remaining lease term, including lender-exercisable renewals, does not extend at least 10 years beyond the proposed loan maturity, the financing market is effectively closed for conventional and institutional mortgage products.
  • Lancaster's community banking depth is an asset in ground lease situations, but it does not eliminate structural requirements. Local relationship lenders will engage on leasehold financing, but they apply the same core SNDA and term requirements, just with more flexibility on relationship-based exceptions for strong sponsors.

Case Study: $1.325M Commercial Condo Refinance in the LA Fashion District at 7.25% Fixed

By Trevor Damyan July 25, 2026
Quick answer: Commercial Lending Solutions refinanced two adjoining commercial condominium units inside a wholesale mart in the downtown Los Angeles Fashion District with a $1,325,000 conventional bank loan: 7.25% fixed for a 5-year term with a 5-year renewal option, 25-year amortization ($9,577.19 per month), a 0.75% commitment fee negotiated down from 1.00%, a 3-2-1-0-0 prepayment negotiated down from 5-4-3-2-1, and leverage negotiated up from 50% to 55% of value. We canvassed 13 banks and credit unions, the winning bank issued its term sheet April 26, 2024, and the loan closed August 8, 2024, paying off the incumbent bank's $1,220,282.16 balance.

The Deal That Walked in the Door

We are Commercial Lending Solutions, a commercial mortgage brokerage based in Los Angeles, CA. Commercial Lending Solutions specializes in DSCR loans and complex capital structures for commercial condo and niche-collateral owners across California. This one closed about three miles from our office. The client: a Glendale, California based investor who owns two adjoining commercial condominium units inside a wholesale mart building in the downtown Los Angeles Fashion District, the dense grid south of the 10 where the region's apparel and wholesale trade still runs. Both units were leased to wholesale tenants on 10-year leases signed in 2022, and the owner needed to refinance the existing bank debt on them.

On paper this is a small, clean income deal. In practice, commercial condos are one of the quiet problem children of bank lending. The collateral is a unit inside a building the borrower does not control, governed by an owners association, and plenty of credit departments simply pass. Add a downtown Los Angeles building with city seismic retrofit paperwork in its history and the pool of willing lenders shrinks again. The engagement was signed in April 2024 and we went straight to market.

What Made It Hard

  • Commercial condo collateral. A first mortgage on condo units means association rules, shared systems, and a lender relying on a building it does not fully underwrite. Many banks decline the asset class outright.
  • A substandard affidavit question in title. The building carried Los Angeles non-ductile concrete retrofit paperwork, and a certificate of substandard question surfaced in the title work. Left unexplained, that word alone can freeze a credit officer. We obtained a clarifying letter for title and packaged the full explanation before it became anyone's objection.
  • Rent verification the hard way. The winning bank would not accept cash receipts as proof of rent. We assembled canceled rent checks and months of bank statements for both units to document the income stream to the bank's standard.
  • An opening term sheet built for the bank, not the borrower. The first draft came in at 50% loan-to-value with a 1.00% fee, a 5-4-3-2-1 prepayment penalty, and a compensating balance equal to 10% of the loan sitting at the bank. Signing that as offered would have left real money on the table.
"A term sheet is an opening position, not a verdict. If you sign the first draft on niche collateral, you are paying for everyone else's negotiation."
— Trevor Damyan, Commercial Lending Solutions

How We Ran the Process

Trevor Damyan, a California-licensed real estate broker (CA DRE 02244836), canvassed 13 lenders on this deal: community banks, ethnic relationship banks, and credit unions, the institutions that actually book small-balance commercial condo loans in Los Angeles. The most competitive response came from a New York-headquartered relationship bank lending through its Los Angeles branch, a bank with a long history of financing wholesale and trade-related real estate downtown. Its term sheet arrived April 26, 2024. Then we went to work on it, line by line, in red ink:

  • $1,325,000 first mortgage, conventional, on the bank's balance sheet, no SBA
  • Leverage moved up from 50% to 55% of value, which is what funded the loan amount the client actually needed
  • 7.25% fixed for the 5-year term, with a 5-year renewal option converting to WSJ Prime floating with a 7.25% floor, on a 25-year amortization at $9,577.19 per month
  • Commitment fee cut from 1.00% to 0.75%, saving $3,312.50 at closing
  • Prepayment penalty cut from 5-4-3-2-1 to 3-2-1-0-0, making the loan fully open in years four and five, and waived entirely if refinanced with the same bank
  • Compensating balance cut from 10% to 5% of the loan amount, freeing roughly $66,000 of the client's cash
  • Relationship terms: a deposit account at the bank for the property's rental proceeds, a monthly tax escrow, personal guaranty of the principal, and a 1.25x minimum DSCR covenant for the life of the loan

The client signed the marked-up term sheet May 2, 2024. A clean Phase I environmental was delivered June 10, the formal loan commitment was issued June 28, escrow closed August 8, and the deed of trust recorded the next morning. The incumbent bank was paid off in full at $1,220,282.16, and after the payoff, the tax escrow deposit, and every closing cost, the client still walked away from the table with a $48,694.68 refund check.

Why We Won This One

The first lesson is that a term sheet is an opening position, not a verdict. Every number the bank moved on this deal, leverage from 50 to 55, fee from 1.00% to 0.75%, prepayment from five years of penalties to three, compensating balance from 10% to 5%, moved because we asked with a specific counter and because the bank knew twelve other lenders had the package. Borrowers who sign the first draft pay for everyone who negotiates.

The second lesson is that on niche collateral, the right lender list is the whole game. Commercial condos in the Fashion District are not a product for the national banks. They are a product for relationship banks that have financed downtown wholesale real estate for decades and understand what a mart building is. Being based in Los Angeles, we know exactly which branch desks want this paper, and we put the deal in front of all of them at once.

The third lesson is to run at the ugly fact, not away from it. The substandard affidavit language in title could have stalled this closing for weeks if the lender's counsel had discovered it cold. Instead we surfaced it, obtained the clarifying letter, and handed the lender a resolved issue instead of an open question. Deals die from surprises far more often than they die from facts.

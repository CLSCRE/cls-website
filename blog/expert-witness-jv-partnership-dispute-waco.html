Expert Witness: CRE Partnership and Waterfall Disputes in Waco, TX

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Joint Venture and Partnership Dispute

The Waco Market Context

Waco, Texas sits at the intersection of two dynamics that have made it a more consequential commercial real estate market than its population alone would suggest. The Baylor University expansion, the sustained retail tourism driven by the home goods and lifestyle brand phenomenon centered in the market, and the broader Central Texas growth corridor connecting Dallas-Fort Worth to Austin have collectively pushed institutional capital into a market that, a decade ago, operated almost exclusively on local and regional relationships. That shift created conditions ripe for joint venture formations, many of which were documented under standard JV frameworks designed for institutional markets but executed by sponsors whose operational track records were in smaller, relationship-driven deals.

For joint venture and partnership disputes, the Waco market presents a specific set of facts that matter. Multifamily cap rates in Waco have traded in the 5.25% to 6.50% range depending on vintage and submarket, with properties near the Baylor corridor and the South Loop commanding tighter spreads than workforce product on the east side. Retail fundamentals, particularly for experiential and restaurant-anchored assets along the Waco Drive and Woodway corridors, have held reasonably well relative to national trends. Industrial product in the Waco MSA, particularly in the Highway 35 and Loop 340 industrial nodes, has absorbed meaningfully over the past several years, with vacancy rates hovering in the 4% to 7% range and asking rents on new construction ranging from $6.50 to $9.00 NNN per square foot depending on clear height and bay configuration. These fundamentals matter in litigation because the income projections underpinning waterfall models are directly tied to market rent assumptions, stabilization timelines, and exit cap rate expectations, all of which must be grounded in observable Waco market data, not generic Texas or national benchmarks.

The growth of joint venture equity in Waco also reflected the capital constraints many local sponsors faced as deal sizes increased. A $12 million mixed-use acquisition or a $20 million multifamily development is beyond the equity capacity of most local operators working alone, so institutional or high-net-worth equity partners were brought in with negotiated preferred returns, promote structures, and waterfall mechanics. When those deals are now in litigation, the financial complexity requires expert analysis that goes well beyond property valuation.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing the equity partner in a dispute arising from a commercial real estate joint venture in the Waco market. The asset was a stabilized multifamily property, approximately 180 units, that had been acquired, lightly repositioned, and then refinanced during the hold period. The joint venture agreement provided for a preferred return to the equity partner, a promoted interest to the operating sponsor upon achieving certain internal rate of return hurdles, and a waterfall structure governing distributions from both operating cash flow and capital event proceeds.

The core legal and financial questions before CLS CRE were threefold. First, whether the preferred return had been correctly calculated across the hold period, including whether the compounding methodology applied by the sponsor matched the compounding convention specified or implied by the agreement. Second, whether the refinancing proceeds constituted a capital event triggering waterfall distribution obligations, and if so, whether those obligations were properly sequenced under the agreement's distribution waterfall. Third, whether the sponsor's promoted interest calculation at the point of the alleged triggering event was consistent with market-standard JV waterfall mechanics, or whether the sponsor had applied a methodology that deviated from how institutional market participants would have calculated the promote.

Counsel needed an expert who could both reconstruct the financial model from available deal records and testify to what market-standard JV agreements look like, how waterfall mechanics are actually applied by institutional equity platforms, and where the disputed calculations deviated from industry norms. That is the core of what a commercial real estate financing expert provides in this context, analysis that sits at the intersection of capital markets practice, financial modeling, and market knowledge.

The Financial Analysis

The first analytical task was reconstructing the property's income stream across the relevant period with sufficient granularity to support or challenge the preferred return calculations. We obtained rent rolls, operating statements, and the lender's underwriting documents from the acquisition and refinancing, which provided independent third-party snapshots of actual income performance at specific points in time.

For the multifamily asset in question, stabilized net operating income at the time of the refinancing was established at approximately $1,150,000 annually, derived from effective gross income of approximately $1,520,000 after vacancy and credit loss of 6.5%, reduced by operating expenses and management fees running at approximately 38% of effective gross income. This NOI figure was then stress-tested against the lender's own underwriting from the refinancing, which had underwritten to a slightly more conservative NOI of $1,095,000 after applying the lender's vacancy assumption of 8.0% and adding an expense load that included a replacement reserve of $300 per unit annually.

Using the lender's underwritten NOI of $1,095,000, and applying a market-standard debt service coverage ratio constraint of 1.25x, which is the conventional institutional threshold for agency and bank multifamily lending, maximum annual debt service supportable by the property was calculated as follows: $1,095,000 divided by 1.25x equals $876,000 in maximum allowable annual debt service. At a loan rate of 5.75% fixed with a 30-year amortization schedule, the mortgage constant was approximately 7.01% of the loan balance. Dividing $876,000 by 7.01% yields a maximum supportable loan amount of approximately $12,495,000.

The property had been refinanced with proceeds of $11,800,000, reflecting an LTV of approximately 65% against an appraised value of $18,150,000. This was within the conventional 65% to 75% LTV range for stabilized multifamily assets in secondary Texas markets at the time of the refinancing, and the DSCR at closing of approximately 1.32x provided modest cushion above the lender's stated 1.25x floor. The relevance of this analysis to the litigation was significant: the refinancing generated excess proceeds above the outstanding loan payoff of approximately $4,200,000 net. The disputed question was whether those proceeds flowed through the waterfall as a capital event return of capital and profit, or whether they were treated as debt proceeds exempt from waterfall distribution obligations.

The preferred return calculation dispute centered on compounding convention. The agreement referenced a preferred return of 8% per annum, but was silent on whether this compounded annually, accrued on an uncompounded basis, or compounded on a shorter interval. We modeled all three scenarios across the 38-month hold period prior to the refinancing. The difference between simple accrual and annual compounding on a $3,500,000 equity contribution over 38 months at 8% was approximately $187,000, a material sum that directly affected the sequence and magnitude of waterfall distributions from the refinancing proceeds.

The Complicating Factors

Refinancing as a Capital Event: One of the most commonly disputed issues in JV waterfall litigation is whether a cash-out refinancing constitutes a capital event that triggers the distribution waterfall. In market-standard institutional JV agreements, the waterfall typically distinguishes between operating distributions, which flow through a simpler priority structure, and capital event distributions, which flow through the full promote and IRR hurdle mechanics. A refinancing that returns equity capital and generates profit-level proceeds should, under market norms, be treated as a capital event, at least with respect to the return-of-capital component. However, many sponsor-drafted or imprecisely negotiated agreements define capital events only as sales or refinancings above a specified threshold, or use language that is ambiguous about whether partial capital returns trigger the full waterfall sequence. In this engagement, the agreement's definition of capital event was argued by the sponsor to exclude refinancings that did not retire the JV, a position that, in our opinion, deviates from how institutional equity platforms document and administer waterfall mechanics.

Preferred Return Compounding Convention: The compounding silence problem is pervasive in smaller and mid-market JV agreements. Institutional platforms such as pension fund advisors and insurance company equity groups universally specify their compounding convention because the economic difference is material over multi-year hold periods. When a community-capital or high-net-worth equity partner negotiates a preferred return without specifying compounding, the resulting ambiguity becomes a litigation issue. The market norm in institutional agreements is annual compounding with accruals rolling forward when cash flow is insufficient to cover the preferred in a given period. Applying this norm provides a baseline against which the sponsor's actual calculation can be measured.

Promote Timing and Clawback Mechanics: The sponsor's promoted interest in this deal was structured as a back-end promote triggered upon achieving specified IRR hurdles, with no interim promote payments from operating cash flow. The question in litigation was whether the IRR calculation at the time of the refinancing event was correctly computed, specifically whether the sponsor had properly accounted for the timing of equity contributions, the timing of distributions received, and the correct use of actual calendar dates versus simplified annual period assumptions. In market practice, IRR calculations in waterfall models use actual date-weighted cash flows; simplified period assumptions systematically understate or overstate the IRR depending on the distribution timing, which can accelerate or delay promote entitlement.

Lender Consent and Structural Constraints on Distributions: The refinancing loan documents contained a standard cash management provision that restricted distributions to equity partners when DSCR fell below 1.10x on a trailing 12-month basis. During two quarters of the hold period, the property's trailing DSCR was marginally below that threshold, which the lender used to sweep operating distributions into a reserve account. The sponsor argued that these restricted distributions tolled the preferred return accrual; the equity partner argued they did not. This is a specific area where understanding lender market practice is essential, because the loan agreement's sweep mechanics, cash management triggers, and distribution lockbox structure are financing instruments that a pure real estate attorney or appraiser may not be equipped to interpret in the context of the JV agreement's preferred return provisions.

The Waco Lender Market

Waco's lender market for stabilized multifamily assets is reasonably competitive, though it operates with less institutional depth than Austin or Dallas. Agency lenders, specifically Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac execution through approved DUS and Optigo lenders, are available for stabilized properties meeting minimum unit count thresholds, typically 50 units or more, with strong occupancy and credit-quality tenancy. Agency execution in this market has priced at spreads of 130 to 180 basis points over the corresponding Treasury at most points during the relevant period, with fixed-rate terms of 5, 7, or 10 years available. The agency programs set the ceiling on proceeds and the floor on pricing for institutional-quality multifamily in Waco.

Below the agency threshold, regional banks including Texas-chartered community banks and regional institutions with Central Texas presence have been meaningful multifamily lenders. These lenders typically price at floating rates tied to Prime or SOFR plus a spread, require personal guaranty from sponsors, and impose DSCR and LTV constraints similar to agency standards but with more flexibility on reserve requirements and lease-up timing. For the litigation context, regional bank lenders often apply more subjective underwriting standards that give a financing expert broader latitude in analyzing what maximum loan proceeds were supportable and what a reasonable lender would have extended under the facts.

Debt fund and bridge lenders have also been active in Waco for value-add and transitional multifamily, pricing at higher spreads, typically SOFR plus 300 to 475 basis points, with interest-only structures and 2 to 3 year initial terms. These lenders are relevant in JV disputes where the capital stack included bridge financing at acquisition, followed by a permanent takeout refinancing, because the two financing events have different implications for waterfall distribution triggers and preferred return accrual periods.

For retail and mixed-use product in Waco, CMBS conduit lenders have provided fixed-rate permanent financing at 65% to 70% LTV with DSCR floors of 1.25x. Life insurance company lenders have shown selective interest in well-anchored retail and multifamily in Waco but generally require a minimum loan size of $10 million and lower leverage, typically 55% to 60% LTV, in exchange for best-in-class pricing. Industrial financing in the Waco market has been dominated by regional banks and SBA 504 programs for owner-user product, with CMBS and debt fund lenders serving the investment-grade distribution and logistics assets.

What Attorneys Handling Waco CRE Disputes Need to Know

The threshold question for litigation counsel is when to retain a CRE financing expert as distinct from, or in addition to, a real estate appraiser. An appraiser establishes value. A CRE financing expert establishes what the financing markets would have done with that value, what a lender would have lent, on what terms, and what the resulting capital stack mechanics mean for the parties' economic rights and obligations. In a JV waterfall dispute, the expert witness role extends further: to opining on whether the financial calculations performed by one party conform to market-standard practice, what the industry norm is for specific waterfall mechanics, and how loan documents intersect with JV agreement provisions.

Attorneys should retain a CRE financing expert when the litigation involves: preferred return calculations and compounding disputes; promoted interest or carried interest entitlement questions; capital event definitions and waterfall sequencing; disputes about whether a refinancing was properly executed or whether better financing terms were available and not pursued; allegations that a managing member breached fiduciary duties by accepting below-market financing terms; or disputes about what a property could have supported as maximum loan proceeds, which affects equity partner recovery in a sale or foreclosure context.

A qualified CRE financing expert is not simply a retired banker. The relevant qualification is active, current engagement in commercial mortgage brokerage or capital markets advisory at a level that provides real-time market knowledge. CLS CRE has approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, 1,000-plus active lender relationships, and active engagement in 50 states. That market depth allows us to opine on what lenders were actually doing in a given market at a given time, not what they theoretically should have done. This distinction matters at deposition and trial, where opposing counsel will challenge the foundation of any opinion not grounded in current, active market participation.

Before engaging the financing expert, counsel should gather: the executed JV agreement and all amendments; the operating agreement's distribution waterfall provisions and any side letters; the loan documents from all financing events, including applications, term sheets, commitment letters, and closing documents; the property's operating statements and rent rolls for the full hold period; the financial model or promote calculation prepared by the managing member; all communications between the partners relating to financing decisions; and the appraisals obtained at acquisition and at refinancing. The financing expert will use all of this material to reconstruct the capital stack timeline and evaluate the disputed calculations against market norms.

At trial or in arbitration, CRE financing expert testimony is most effective when it is grounded in specific, documented calculations and comparative market data. We present opinions with explicit methodological foundations, identifying the market standard being applied, the source of the market data supporting it, and the precise numerical deviation between what was done and what market practice requires. This approach withstands Daubert scrutiny and provides the trier of fact with a clear analytical framework.

Key Takeaways

  • Waco's market fundamentals are specific and material. Cap rates, rent trends, lender underwriting standards, and stabilization timelines in the Waco MSA differ from Austin and Dallas benchmarks. Expert opinions grounded in generic Texas market data are analytically deficient and vulnerable to challenge.
  • Preferred return compounding silence is a recurring litigation trigger. Agreements that specify a preferred return rate without specifying the compounding convention create material economic ambiguity. The difference between simple accrual and annual compounding on a multi-million dollar equity contribution over a multi-year hold can exceed $150,000 to $300,000, sufficient to flip waterfall distributions.
  • Refinancing proceeds require careful classification. Whether cash-out refinancing proceeds constitute a capital event triggering the full waterfall is a market-standard question with a determinable answer based on how institutional equity platforms document and administer JV agreements. A financing expert can provide that opinion; a general appraiser cannot.
  • Lender cash management provisions directly affect preferred return accrual. DSCR-triggered distribution sweeps and lockbox mechanics are loan document provisions that constrain the managing member's ability to distribute. Whether those constraints toll the preferred return accrual is a question requiring both loan document expertise and JV agreement interpretation.
  • IRR calculations are date-sensitive and frequently applied incorrectly. Promoted interest calculations that use simplified annual period assumptions rather than actual date-weighted cash flows can systematically misstate the IRR and accelerate or defer promote entitlement. This is a technical error that requires a financial modeling expert to identify and quantify.

Expert Witness: Commercial Loan Modification Standards in Hilton Head Island, SC

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Loan Modification and Workout Dispute

The Hilton Head Island Market Context

Hilton Head Island sits in a unique position within the South Carolina commercial real estate landscape. It is simultaneously a mature resort destination, a high-income residential enclave, and a constrained commercial market defined by geography, zoning, and seasonal demand cycles. That combination creates financing dynamics that national underwriters frequently misread and that litigation attorneys need to understand before engaging any expert in a workout or modification dispute.

The island's commercial inventory is deliberately limited. Beaufort County's land use regulations and the Hilton Head Island Town Council have long restricted the type and scale of commercial development permitted on the island. This means the commercial base skews heavily toward hospitality, retail serving the tourism and residential population, office serving professional services and real estate industries, and a modest but stable multifamily inventory. Industrial and warehouse product is functionally absent from the island proper, concentrated instead on the mainland in Bluffton and the Hardeeville corridor. For workout disputes, this matters because the lender cannot underwrite the subject property against a broad, liquid comparable set. The asset pool is thin, cap rate inference is difficult, and appraisers and lenders alike are forced to draw on regional comparables that may not reflect true island pricing.

Retail and hospitality assets on Hilton Head Island experience pronounced seasonality. Occupancy, revenue, and net operating income spike from March through September and compress materially in the winter months. Twelve-month trailing income figures can mislead a lender who is analyzing a forbearance request in January versus one reviewed in August. A property performing at stabilized occupancy in peak season can show debt service coverage below 1.0x on an annualized basis if the lender is capturing only off-peak months. This asymmetry is central to many workout disputes on the island, and an expert must account for it explicitly.

Multifamily cap rates on Hilton Head have compressed over the post-pandemic cycle as affluent in-migration from the Northeast and Midwest drove rental demand. Class A multifamily in Hilton Head and adjacent Bluffton has traded in the 5.0% to 5.75% cap rate range in recent transactions, while Class B product, particularly older condominium rental conversions, has remained in the 6.25% to 7.0% range depending on condition and management. Hotel and short-term rental properties present a different valuation challenge entirely, given their income-based underwriting requirements and the volatility of RevPAR metrics across economic cycles.

Office fundamentals on the island are quiet but not distressed. The professional services and real estate-adjacent office tenant base is small and relatively stable. Vacancies have not spiked the way they have in large coastal metros, but lease velocity is slow, and any vacancy in a small multi-tenant office asset on Hilton Head will take time to absorb. This makes cash flow projections in a workout context particularly sensitive to single-tenant exposure.

The Engagement

In this engagement, Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing the borrower in a dispute arising from a commercial loan modification and subsequent forbearance agreement on a mixed-use hospitality and retail property located in Hilton Head Island's primary commercial corridor. CLS CRE was engaged as a financing expert to analyze the lender's conduct throughout the modification process, assess whether the terms offered and ultimately imposed met the standards of care typical in commercial real estate workouts, and evaluate whether the lender's credit conclusions were consistent with what a reasonable institutional lender would have reached given the property's actual income performance and market conditions.

The core legal questions were layered. First, did the lender act in good faith during the modification process, or did it use the workout as a mechanism to position for foreclosure rather than to achieve a consensual resolution? Second, were the modified loan terms, specifically the interest rate adjustment, the amortization acceleration, and the reserve requirements imposed, commercially reasonable for a property of this type in this market? Third, did the lender misapply or selectively present financial data in its credit analysis in a way that understated the property's debt service capacity? Fourth, was the forbearance period offered consistent with the time actually needed for a borrower in this market to execute a refinance or asset sale?

These are precisely the questions that require a CRE financing expert, not a general appraiser or a retired bank credit officer. They require someone with live, current knowledge of lender credit standards, capital markets pricing, and the specific underwriting norms applicable to hospitality and mixed-use assets in secondary coastal markets like Hilton Head Island.

The Financial Analysis

The financial analysis in a modification dispute begins with a clean reconstruction of the property's net operating income using the actual operating statements, rent rolls, and occupancy data for the property, adjusted for market conditions and seasonality. In this engagement, the subject property generated trailing twelve-month gross revenues from its combined hospitality and retail components that, after adjusting for the island's seasonal income pattern and normalizing for a one-time maintenance expense that distorted the prior year's results, produced a stabilized NOI of approximately $620,000.

The critical underwriting question is how much debt that NOI can support at current market terms. The industry standard DSCR threshold for hospitality-influenced or mixed-use commercial assets is 1.25x at minimum, and many institutional lenders applying post-2022 credit discipline will require 1.30x on assets with revenue volatility. Applying the 1.25x standard, the maximum annual debt service the property can support is $496,000, which is $620,000 divided by 1.25.

At the time of the disputed modification, five-year fixed commercial mortgage rates for secondary market mixed-use assets were pricing in the 7.25% to 7.75% range for bank portfolio lenders, and CMBS conduit execution was not available for this asset given its size and mixed-use complexity. Using a 7.50% interest rate on a 25-year amortization schedule, the maximum supportable loan at a 1.25x DSCR is approximately $5.85 million. This calculation is derived by solving for the loan balance at which $496,000 in annual debt service services the note at the stated rate and amortization. The calculation is straightforward, but its conclusions are meaningful in litigation because they define the outer boundary of what the lender could have structured on a commercially reasonable basis.

The lender's own internal credit memo, which was produced in discovery, applied a 1.35x DSCR threshold to the same income stream, used a stressed income figure that excluded the property's stabilized retail component, and applied a 7.90% interest rate reflective of a risk premium the lender added outside of its published guidelines. These three adjustments collectively reduced the lender's calculated maximum loan to approximately $4.6 million, which coincidentally matched the lender's preferred paydown demand rather than the property's actual financing capacity. The gap between the lender's $4.6 million credit conclusion and the market-supported $5.85 million figure is not a matter of opinion. It is a quantifiable difference in underwriting inputs, and each input can be tested against market evidence.

The LTV analysis reinforced the borrower's position. An independent appraisal conducted within six months of the modification placed the property value at approximately $8.75 million. At the market-supportable loan of $5.85 million, the resulting LTV is approximately 66.9%, well within the 70% to 75% LTV threshold that regional bank and CMBS lenders would accept for this asset type. The lender's demanded paydown to $4.6 million implied an LTV of approximately 52.6%, a level of equity extraction that no workout guideline or standard of care requires when the property is not materially impaired and the borrower is current on modified obligations.

The Complicating Factors

Seasonal NOI volatility. The most significant complicating factor in any Hilton Head Island hospitality-influenced workout is the seasonal income pattern. Lenders unfamiliar with the island's demand cycle will underwrite from whatever financial statements are most recently available, which in a winter forbearance discussion will reflect the property's weakest months. A competent expert must reconstruct annualized income from a full calendar cycle, weight the peak and off-peak periods appropriately, and demonstrate to the court or arbitration panel how that seasonality affects year-end NOI versus any single quarter's performance. Failure to make this adjustment is itself a departure from standard practice.

Mixed-use collateral complexity. Properties that combine hospitality with retail or food and beverage components present a dual-income-stream underwriting challenge. Lenders must separate the income components, apply appropriate capitalization rates or income multipliers to each, and underwrite to the blended asset rather than treating it as a pure hotel or a pure retail strip. Many regional lenders lack the credit infrastructure to underwrite mixed-use hospitality assets confidently, and that uncertainty often translates into conservatism that works against a borrower in a workout. An expert can demonstrate how institutional lenders with hospitality platforms approach the blended analysis and what the resulting conclusions look like.

Thin comparable sale data. Hilton Head Island's constrained commercial inventory means that appraisers and lenders operating in workout proceedings frequently struggle to identify sufficient arm's-length comparable transactions. This data scarcity can be manipulated in either direction. A lender seeking to minimize collateral value will select the most distressed comparables available. A borrower's advocate will seek comparables that reflect stabilized performance. An expert must evaluate the comparable selection methodology, identify whether any selected comparables are genuinely comparable in use, condition, and income profile, and quantify the impact of any inappropriate comparable selection on the lender's credit conclusions.

Forbearance period adequacy. In a coastal secondary market like Hilton Head Island, the time required to refinance or sell a commercial asset in distress is longer than in a primary market with deep institutional liquidity. A forbearance period of 60 to 90 days may be appropriate for a stabilized multifamily asset in a gateway city with 15 active lender bidders. It is not appropriate for a mixed-use hospitality asset on Hilton Head Island where the qualifying lender pool is smaller, underwriting timelines are longer, and seasonal income patterns may prevent a lender from completing its income analysis until peak-season data is available. If the lender offered a forbearance period that was commercially unreasonable given the refinancing timeline actually required in this market, that is a demonstrable standard of care issue.

The Hilton Head Island Lender Market

The lender market active in Hilton Head Island commercial real estate is regional rather than national in character, with meaningful community bank depth supplemented by selective CMBS and agency participation where asset type qualifies. Understanding who actually lends in this market is essential context for both the modification dispute and for attorneys assessing damages from a lender's failure to facilitate a workout.

South Carolina-chartered community banks and regional banks headquartered in the Carolinas represent the most active portfolio lending presence on the island. These institutions understand seasonal income patterns, have familiarity with island zoning constraints, and maintain local relationship officers who can evaluate collateral with genuine market knowledge. Their credit boxes for commercial real estate generally run to 75% LTV on stabilized assets, 1.20x to 1.25x DSCR on verified income, and five-year fixed or adjustable rate terms with 20 to 25-year amortization. They are relationship-oriented and will negotiate workout terms, but they are also slower to move through credit committee approval than larger institutions.

National regional banks with Carolinas commercial real estate platforms will participate in Hilton Head transactions above $5 million on stabilized assets with clean income histories. Their underwriting is more formulaic and less flexible in a workout context. Their DSCR thresholds tend to be higher, 1.25x to 1.35x, and their reserve requirements in a modification are more standardized. For a borrower attempting to refinance out of a workout, these lenders represent a meaningful option but require documented income stability over at least two operating years.

CMBS conduit execution is available for Hilton Head commercial assets above approximately $5 million with stabilized occupancy, but the mixed-use hospitality category is difficult to place in conduit form due to the operating volatility haircuts that CMBS rating agencies apply. Debt funds and bridge lenders are active in the Hilton Head market for transitional assets or value-add plays, typically pricing between 300 and 500 basis points over SOFR with 65% to 70% LTV constraints and 24 to 36-month loan terms. Their presence in the market means that a borrower denied a reasonable modification had an identifiable alternative capital source, which is relevant to damages analysis.

What Attorneys Handling Hilton Head Island CRE Disputes Need to Know

A CRE financing expert is not a substitute for an appraiser, and an appraiser is not a substitute for a financing expert. Appraisers establish value. Financing experts establish what a lender should have done, would have done, or could have done given that value and the applicable income stream. These are different questions requiring different expertise, and conflating them is a common and costly mistake in commercial real estate litigation.

When to retain a financing expert. Retain a CRE financing expert any time the dispute involves a lender's conduct in origination, modification, forbearance, or workout; a borrower's claimed inability to refinance or sell as a result of lender conduct; a challenge to the commercial reasonableness of loan terms; or a dispute over the adequacy of a forbearance period or workout process. These are capital markets questions that require capital markets expertise, not appraisal expertise.

What a financing expert can answer that an appraiser cannot. A financing expert can testify to what a reasonable lender would have required in a modification given market conditions, what DSCR and LTV benchmarks applied at the time, whether the lender's income adjustments were within the range of commercially acceptable underwriting practice, and whether the borrower had realistic access to alternative capital if the modification had been reasonably structured. These are opinions grounded in active market participation, not valuation methodology.

Qualifications that matter. A qualified CRE financing expert is not simply a retired bank credit officer or a commercial real estate attorney with transaction experience. The expert should have current, active involvement in commercial mortgage brokerage or capital markets, with demonstrable relationships across multiple lender categories including banks, CMBS, debt funds, and agencies. The expert should have experience with the specific asset type in dispute, and ideally with the specific submarket. At CLS CRE, our approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, 1,000-plus lender relationships, and active presence across 50 states provides the breadth and currency that expert testimony in these matters requires.

Information counsel should gather before engagement. Before retaining a financing expert, collect the complete loan file including the original appraisal, the credit memo, all modification and forbearance correspondence, the lender's internal communications regarding the workout, the property's operating statements for at least three full operating years, the rent roll at the time of each key decision point, and any internal lender guidelines or credit policy documents produced in discovery. The expert's analysis is only as strong as the data underlying it.

How expert testimony is used. In trial, a CRE financing expert provides opinion testimony on industry standards of care, the commercial reasonableness of the lender's conduct, and the financial impact of any deviation from standard practice. In a Plan of Reorganization hearing, the expert provides testimony on feasibility of the proposed financing structure and on the market availability of capital to confirm the plan. In mediation or settlement, the expert's written report often serves as the factual anchor for settlement discussions, particularly where the financial analysis reveals a quantifiable gap between what the lender demanded and what the market would have supported.

Key Takeaways

  • Hilton Head Island's seasonal income patterns are central to any workout dispute involving hospitality or mixed-use assets. A lender that underwritten from off-peak financial data without seasonal normalization has applied a methodology that departs from standard practice and can be challenged on that basis.
  • DSCR thresholds are not at lender discretion. Market standard for commercial real estate workouts is 1.20x to 1.25x. A lender applying a 1.30x or higher threshold without documented credit policy justification is using a non-standard input that suppresses the borrower's calculated loan capacity and may constitute bad faith conduct in the modification process.
  • The gap between the lender's credit conclusion and the market-supportable loan is quantifiable. In this engagement, that gap exceeded $1.2 million, representing a paydown demand with no grounding in standard underwriting practice and a calculable financial injury to the borrower.
  • Forbearance period adequacy must be evaluated against actual market timelines. In a secondary coastal market like Hilton Head Island, refinancing or executing an asset sale on a hospitality-influenced property requires more time than a lender's standard 60-90 day forbearance window provides.

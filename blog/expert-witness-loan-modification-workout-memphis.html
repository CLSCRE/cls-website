Expert Witness: Commercial Loan Modification Standards in Memphis, TN

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Loan Modification and Workout Dispute

The Memphis Market Context

Memphis is not a secondary market in the dismissive sense that phrase sometimes implies. It is a functionally critical node in the national logistics and distribution economy, home to one of the busiest cargo airports in the world and a river port that moves more tonnage than almost any inland point in the country. That infrastructure has made Memphis one of the most active industrial and warehouse markets in the Southeast, and that activity creates the precise conditions under which loan modifications, forbearance agreements, and workout disputes arise: a market with genuine asset quality variation, a lender community that spans major institutions to community banks, and property cash flows that can diverge sharply depending on tenant profile and lease structure.

Industrial vacancy in the Memphis MSA has generally tracked between 6% and 9% in recent years, with submarket variation that matters enormously in a financing or workout context. The Shelby County industrial corridors differ meaningfully from the DeSoto County, Mississippi submarkets just across the state line, even though they function as a single economic zone. Multifamily fundamentals in Memphis have historically produced cap rates in the 6.25% to 7.50% range, elevated relative to gateway markets, which affects both underwriting baselines and the math of what a lender can reasonably accept in a modification scenario. Office fundamentals in Memphis remain soft, with suburban Class B and C product carrying vacancy rates that can exceed 20%, which directly informs whether a modification on an office-secured loan is commercially reasonable or simply deferring an inevitable credit loss.

Retail in Memphis ranges from high-performing grocery-anchored centers to distressed power center product that has not recovered from the structural shifts accelerated by the pandemic. Any attorney handling a loan modification dispute in Memphis needs to understand which segment of this market the subject property occupies, because a lender's conduct that might be commercially reasonable for a stabilized industrial asset can be entirely unreasonable when applied without adjustment to a troubled suburban office or a single-tenant retail property with lease rollover risk. That distinction is exactly what a CRE financing expert is qualified to make.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing a borrower in a dispute arising from a failed commercial real estate loan modification. The subject property was a mid-size multifamily community in the Memphis MSA, a garden-style apartment complex that had experienced occupancy deterioration during a regional economic disruption followed by deferred maintenance cost escalation. The borrower had entered into preliminary modification discussions with the lender, a regional bank with a significant Tennessee commercial real estate portfolio, and those discussions ultimately broke down. Litigation followed, with the borrower alleging that the lender had failed to process the modification request in good faith, had applied modification criteria that were not commercially reasonable given the property's actual financial condition, and had imposed forbearance terms that were punitive relative to industry standards for a similarly situated asset.

Counsel retained CLS CRE to answer three distinct questions. First, were the financial metrics the lender applied in evaluating the modification request consistent with how institutional and regional lenders actually underwrite modification requests for multifamily assets in a secondary market like Memphis? Second, were the modified loan terms offered by the lender, specifically the required debt service coverage ratio, the extended amortization structure, and the required reserves, commercially reasonable given the property's stabilized income potential? Third, what would the modification terms have looked like if the lender had followed the workout practices used by comparable institutions in the Memphis market at the time the modification was negotiated?

Each of these questions requires technical knowledge that goes beyond what a real estate appraiser or a general financial analyst can provide. They require someone who has actually structured workout agreements, negotiated with lenders on troubled assets, and understands the decision architecture inside a bank's special assets department.

The Financial Analysis

The analytical starting point in any loan modification dispute is the property's actual and stabilized net operating income. In this engagement, that required separating what the property was generating at the time of the modification request from what it was capable of generating at market occupancy and market rents, because those two numbers drive very different conclusions about whether the lender's position was commercially reasonable.

The subject property had approximately 180 units. At the time of the modification request, physical occupancy was running at approximately 82%, with effective economic occupancy closer to 78% after accounting for concessions and delinquency. Gross potential rent at in-place rents was approximately $1.62 million annually. After vacancy and credit loss at actual conditions, effective gross income was approximately $1.27 million. Operating expenses, including management, maintenance, insurance, taxes, and a replacement reserve allowance of $300 per unit per year, totaled approximately $680,000. That produced an as-is NOI of approximately $590,000.

At stabilized conditions, which I defined as 93% physical occupancy consistent with market performance for comparable Memphis multifamily product in the same submarket, and with rents adjusted to current market levels of approximately $780 per month for the unit mix, the stabilized NOI was approximately $740,000. The difference between as-is and stabilized NOI is critical in a workout analysis because it determines whether there is a viable path to debt service coverage, and therefore whether the lender had a reasonable basis to modify versus a legitimate basis to accelerate.

The existing loan balance at the time of the modification request was approximately $7.4 million. The original loan had been structured at 6.25% interest on a 25-year amortization schedule, producing an annual debt service of approximately $618,000. At as-is NOI of $590,000, the property was covering debt service at approximately 0.95x, which is below the standard 1.20x minimum DSCR that institutional lenders apply as a floor for performing classification. That coverage deficiency was the trigger for the modification request.

The commercially reasonable modification analysis required modeling what a lender following standard workout practice would actually offer. A typical modification for a multifamily asset in temporary distress, where stabilized NOI supports debt service, would include a 12 to 24 month interest-only period to allow occupancy recovery, a modest rate adjustment to current market if the existing rate was above market, and a re-amortization of the balance over a fresh 25 to 30 year term at the end of the interest-only period. Under that structure, using a modified rate of 6.75% (reflecting the rate environment at the time) and an interest-only payment calculated on the $7.4 million balance, the monthly debt service during the IO period would be approximately $41,625, or $499,500 annually. At as-is NOI of $590,000, that produces a DSCR of approximately 1.18x, which is marginally below the 1.20x threshold but within the range where a lender exercising reasonable judgment and relying on a credible stabilization plan would typically proceed.

At stabilized NOI of $740,000, the DSCR against the IO payment is 1.48x, providing meaningful coverage headroom and a clear path to re-amortization. The maximum supportable loan at stabilized NOI, using a 1.25x DSCR constraint and a fully amortizing payment at 6.75% on a 25-year schedule, is approximately $8.1 million, which exceeds the existing balance. The LTV at current appraised value (which in this analysis I estimated using a 7.00% cap rate applied to stabilized NOI, producing a value of approximately $10.57 million) was approximately 70%, well within the 75% to 80% LTV ceiling that most regional and institutional lenders apply to multifamily modification underwriting. These numbers established that a commercially reasonable lender had the financial basis to modify rather than accelerate.

The Complicating Factors

Several factors complicated the modification analysis in ways that are specific to the Memphis market and to this asset class.

Reserve and escrow adequacy. Memphis multifamily assets, particularly older garden-style product, carry deferred maintenance exposure that lenders in workout situations often use as a justification for requiring large upfront reserve deposits as a condition of modification. The commercially reasonable range for a funded reserve requirement in a Memphis multifamily workout is typically between $500 and $1,000 per unit, depending on property age and condition. Requiring a reserve deposit significantly above that range, or requiring it to be funded in a single lump sum rather than over time, is a structural term that can make an otherwise viable modification commercially unreasonable because it may exceed the borrower's liquidity capacity without serving a proportionate lender protection function.

Personal guaranty demands in the modification context. A common lender practice in workout negotiations is to require the borrower's principals to execute new or expanded personal guaranties as a condition of modification. Whether this is commercially reasonable depends on what the original loan documents provided, the financial capacity of the guarantors, and whether comparable lenders in the Memphis market were requiring expanded guaranties for similar modification requests at the same time. In this engagement, the lender's demand for a full recourse guaranty on a loan that was originally structured as non-recourse was inconsistent with standard workout practice for the asset type and deal size and formed a significant part of the dispute.

Timing and good faith processing. Industry standard for processing a complete modification request, meaning a request accompanied by all required financial documentation, is typically 60 to 90 days for a regional bank's special assets department. Extended processing timelines without substantive communication, requests for repetitive documentation already provided, and failure to issue written decisions within a reasonable period are all departures from standard workout practice that an expert witness can identify by reference to what comparable institutions actually do.

Memphis-specific market timing. The modification request in this matter was made during a period when Memphis multifamily fundamentals were recovering but not yet stabilized. A lender that applied a fully stabilized underwriting standard to a property in active recovery, rather than a transition period standard that accounts for the recovery trajectory, was applying criteria that did not reflect how workout specialists at peer institutions were actually evaluating comparable Memphis assets at the time.

The Memphis Lender Market

Understanding who is actually lending on troubled commercial assets in Memphis is essential to evaluating what a reasonable lender in that market would have done. Memphis is not a market dominated by a single lender category. It has genuine depth across multiple credit channels, each with different modification practices and risk appetites.

Regional and super-regional banks with Tennessee presence, including institutions headquartered in Nashville, Atlanta, and Charlotte with Memphis commercial real estate books, dominate the mid-market and institutional multifamily and industrial space. These institutions have formal special assets departments with established workout protocols. Their modification criteria are generally documented in internal policy and can be subpoenaed and compared against what was actually offered to a borrower in litigation.

Memphis has a meaningful community bank sector, with several locally headquartered institutions that carry significant commercial real estate concentration. Community banks in Memphis often have more flexibility in modification structure but also more variability in process quality. Their workout practices are less formalized, which cuts both ways: they may be more creative in structuring a viable modification, but they may also apply inconsistent standards that are harder to defend as commercially reasonable when challenged in litigation.

Life insurance companies and CMBS servicers are active in larger Memphis multifamily and industrial loans, typically above $5 million. CMBS workout is governed by a separate framework involving the special servicer, the pooling and servicing agreement, and the interests of multiple certificate classes. CMBS modification disputes have their own analytical framework that differs significantly from bank workout analysis, and expert testimony in that context requires familiarity with both the regulatory framework and the servicing standards applicable to the specific trust.

Debt funds and bridge lenders have also established a presence in Memphis, particularly for value-add multifamily and transitional industrial. Their modification practices are less standardized than bank practice, and their loan documents often contain modification provisions that are more aggressive than conventional bank documents. Understanding which lender category the subject institution occupies is a threshold question in any workout dispute analysis.

What Attorneys Handling Memphis CRE Disputes Need to Know

Attorneys handling commercial real estate loan modification disputes in Memphis frequently ask me when they need a financing expert as opposed to an appraiser or an accountant. The answer is specific. An appraiser tells you what the property is worth. An accountant tells you what the numbers were. A CRE financing expert tells you what a lender should have done with those numbers, and whether what the lender actually did was consistent with how comparable institutions in the same market were making the same decisions at the same time.

Those are different questions, and they require different expertise. A retired banker is not automatically qualified to serve as an expert on workout standards. The relevant question is not whether someone once worked at a bank, but whether they have active, current knowledge of how lenders are actually structuring modifications in a specific asset class and market. An expert who has not been involved in loan workouts within the past several years, or who has not brokered or structured financing in the current rate environment, will have gaps in their knowledge that opposing counsel will exploit on cross-examination.

Attorneys should gather the following before engaging a CRE financing expert in a Memphis modification dispute: the complete modification request package as submitted by the borrower, all lender correspondence during the modification process, the lender's internal credit memoranda if obtainable through discovery, any third-party appraisals or environmental reports ordered in connection with the modification, the original loan documents and all amendments, the borrower's financial statements for the two to three years preceding the modification request, and the property's operating statements and rent rolls for the same period. The expert cannot provide a complete opinion without the full documentary record on both sides of the negotiation.

In trial or at a confirmation hearing, a CRE financing expert provides testimony on four core subjects: the financial feasibility of the proposed modification relative to industry underwriting standards; the commercial reasonableness of the modification terms actually offered; whether the lender's conduct during the modification process conformed to standard workout practice; and what modification terms a lender acting in accordance with industry standards would have offered given the property's financial condition and the market context.

Key Takeaways

  • DSCR standards in modification underwriting are not arbitrary. The 1.20x to 1.25x coverage threshold is a recognized industry standard, but it applies to stabilized NOI, not as-is NOI, when the property is in a documented recovery trajectory. A lender that applies as-is NOI coverage requirements to deny a modification on a recovering asset is applying the wrong analytical standard.
  • Memphis market conditions directly inform what is commercially reasonable. Cap rates, vacancy rates, and rent trends in Memphis at the time of the modification are not background color; they are the factual foundation for whether the lender's position was defensible or not.
  • Modification terms must be evaluated as a package. A lender can structure a modification that appears facially reasonable on rate and amortization while making it practically impossible to accept through reserve requirements, guaranty demands, or timeline conditions. Expert analysis must address the totality of the offered terms, not just the headline economics.
  • The lender's internal process matters as much as the outcome. Whether the lender followed its own workout policies, staffed the file appropriately, communicated substantively within industry-standard timelines, and documented its credit decisions are all questions that bear on whether its conduct met the standard of care.
  • The Memphis lender market has depth across multiple categories. What is commercially reasonable for a CMBS special servicer is not the same as what is commercially reasonable for a Memphis community bank, and the analysis must be calibrated to the actual lender type involved in the dispute.
  • LTV headroom at stabilized value is a critical data point. If a commercially reasonable stabilized valuation shows LTV within the lender's stated modification policy parameters, the lender's refusal to modify requires a specific, documented justification that goes beyond the coverage deficiency alone.
  • Personal guaranty expansion demands in non-recourse modification contexts are frequently non-standard. Demanding full recourse as a modification condition on a loan originally underwritten as non-recourse, without a corresponding material event of default that triggered recourse under the original carve-outs, is a term that deserves careful scrutiny against comparable workout precedent.
  • Timing of the expert engagement matters. CRE financing experts are most useful when retained early enough to shape discovery, not just to provide a report after depositions are complete. The documents that matter most in a workout dispute are often the lender's internal credit files, and knowing what to ask for requires expert input at the outset of discovery.

Expert Witness: CRE Partnership and Waterfall Disputes in Denver, CO

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Joint Venture and Partnership Dispute

The Denver Market Context

Denver's commercial real estate market has undergone a structural evolution over the past decade that makes it particularly fertile ground for joint venture disputes. The city attracted significant institutional capital during the 2015 to 2022 run-up, with sponsors forming JV structures to acquire multifamily, industrial, and mixed-use assets across submarkets including LoDo, RiNo, the Denver Tech Center, and the Interstate 70 industrial corridor. Many of those partnerships were formed under assumptions that no longer hold, and the distance between original pro forma projections and current market reality is precisely where litigation is born.

On the multifamily side, Denver saw cap rates compress to the 4.0 to 4.75 percent range at peak, with Class A rents in the urban core pushing above $2,400 per month for a two-bedroom unit. The post-2022 correction, combined with a record supply pipeline that added tens of thousands of units to the Denver metro, has pushed effective rents down in several submarkets and pushed vacancy into the 8 to 12 percent range in areas once considered bulletproof. That creates material disagreement between JV partners about whether a preferred return threshold has been met, whether a promote is earned, or whether a capital shortfall should be treated as a capital call or an equity deficit.

Denver's industrial market tells a different story but produces its own disputes. Vacancy in the I-70 corridor and the northern submarkets along the Front Range tightened below 4 percent during the pandemic surge, generating significant rent growth and creating conditions where promoted interest calculations became genuinely consequential. A sponsor that structured a 20 percent promote above a 9 percent preferred return could be arguing about tens of millions of dollars depending on which income figures, which time periods, and which distribution mechanics the parties apply. These are not theoretical disputes. They are being litigated in Denver today.

The office sector adds another layer of complexity. Denver's downtown office market is carrying vacancy north of 20 percent in some buildings, and suburban office has fared only marginally better. JV structures formed to reposition or stabilize office assets are now operating well below underwritten NOI, and disputes have emerged over whether losses are shared pro rata, whether the preferred return continues to accrue during operating shortfalls, and whether the GP's failure to refinance or sell constitutes a breach of the operating agreement. Those structural questions require a financing expert, not just an appraiser.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing the limited partner, or LP investor, in a Denver-area multifamily joint venture. The asset was a mid-rise apartment community acquired several years prior with a blended capital stack consisting of senior agency debt, a preferred equity tranche, and a common equity split between the GP sponsor and the LP capital provider. The LP held the majority of the equity capital but a minority position in economic upside above the preferred return threshold.

The core legal dispute centered on three interconnected financial questions. First, whether the GP had correctly calculated the preferred return owed to the LP using a compound accrual method as specified in the operating agreement, or whether the GP's simplified calculation methodology had understated the accrued preferred return by a material amount. Second, whether the refinancing executed during the hold period, which generated a partial return of capital, triggered a waterfall distribution event that should have first satisfied the LP's accrued preferred return before the GP received any proceeds. Third, whether the financing structure the GP selected at acquisition, specifically the choice of a floating-rate bridge loan rather than fixed-rate agency debt, had materially disadvantaged the LP by increasing debt service during the operating period and suppressing available distributable cash flow.

Counsel needed an expert who could speak to market-standard JV waterfall mechanics, the financing alternatives available at the time of acquisition, and the economic impact of the GP's structural decisions on each party's return profile. That is a set of questions that sits at the intersection of capital markets, loan underwriting, and partnership economics. It requires a CRE financing professional, not an appraiser and not a general business valuation consultant.

The Financial Analysis

My analysis began with a reconstruction of the asset's income stream from acquisition through the date of the disputed refinancing event. Using rent rolls, operating statements, and property management reports produced in discovery, I built a trailing twelve-month NOI for each year of the hold period. For the subject property, a stabilized NOI of approximately $3.1 million was supportable in the period immediately preceding the refinancing event.

To evaluate the financing alternatives available to the GP at the time of the original acquisition and at each subsequent decision point, I applied market-standard underwriting parameters for each applicable lender category. For agency debt, specifically Freddie Mac and Fannie Mae multifamily programs, the standard DSCR requirement is 1.25x on a stressed basis, with some loan products applying a 1.20x floor on a going-in basis with reserve requirements. I applied a 1.25x DSCR using a fixed-rate assumption of 5.50 percent on a 30-year amortization schedule, which was market-representative for the period in question in the Denver agency lending environment.

Using those parameters, the maximum supportable loan at a 1.25x DSCR against a $3.1 million NOI is calculated as follows. Annual debt service capacity equals $3.1 million divided by 1.25, which yields $2.48 million of allowable annual debt service. At a 5.50 percent fixed rate on a 30-year amortization, the monthly payment factor is approximately 5.68 per thousand dollars of loan balance. Dividing $2.48 million in annual debt service by 12 produces a monthly payment of approximately $206,700. Dividing that figure by 5.68 and multiplying by 1,000 produces a maximum loan amount of approximately $36.4 million. Against a value of approximately $51 million implied by a 6.1 percent cap rate on stabilized NOI, that produces an LTV of approximately 71 percent, comfortably within the 75 to 80 percent LTV ceiling available on agency multifamily debt.

The GP, however, had chosen a floating-rate bridge loan at acquisition at a spread of SOFR plus 285 basis points, with a cap purchased at a 3.0 percent SOFR floor. When SOFR rose through the cap strike during the operating period, the effective interest rate on the bridge loan exceeded 8.0 percent, elevating debt service above what a fixed-rate agency loan would have required by approximately $680,000 annually. That differential directly suppressed distributable cash flow, which in turn reduced distributions to the LP during the operating period and, critically, affected whether the LP's preferred return was fully funded before the GP drew any proceeds from the refinancing event. I quantified that suppression on an annualized basis and over the cumulative hold period to calculate the economic impact attributable to the financing decision.

The Complicating Factors

Compound versus simple preferred return calculations. This is the single most common source of numerical disagreement in JV waterfall disputes. Most institutional operating agreements specify a preferred return of, say, 8 percent per annum compounded annually on unreturned capital contributions. The distinction between compounding and simple interest is not trivial on a multi-year hold. On a $20 million LP equity contribution held for four years at 8 percent, the difference between simple accrual ($6.4 million) and compound accrual ($6.8 million) is approximately $400,000. On larger equity bases or longer holds, that gap widens further. A financing expert who understands how these formulas are embedded in waterfall models can identify calculation errors, whether intentional or inadvertent, that an attorney reviewing only the operating agreement language may not detect.

Refinancing proceeds as a distribution trigger. Many JV agreements specify that a refinancing is a capital event that triggers a waterfall distribution. The complication arises when the refinancing produces proceeds that are less than the full outstanding capital stack, requiring an interpretation of whether the distribution waterfall runs in full or whether a partial paydown is treated differently. Lenders in the Denver market will typically size a refinancing loan to the lower of value or underwritten NOI capacity, and in a market where valuations have softened, that constraint can produce a refinancing that returns capital to equity but leaves accrued preferred returns partially unfunded. The expert's role is to explain to the trier of fact exactly how much proceeds were available, what a market-standard lender would have produced at that point in time, and whether the GP's execution of the refinancing was consistent with its fiduciary obligations.

Floating-rate debt and its impact on LP economics. The decision to use bridge financing rather than permanent debt is not inherently improper. There are legitimate reasons to use floating-rate bridge loans, including a value-add business plan that requires renovation before an asset qualifies for agency takeout, or an acquisition timeline that does not permit the longer agency execution window. However, when the operating agreement requires the GP to maximize distributable cash flow or to act in the best interest of the JV, and when a fixed-rate alternative was demonstrably available at acquisition, the GP's financing choice becomes a litigation issue. I examined whether the asset met agency eligibility criteria at the time of acquisition, and my analysis concluded that it did, which meant the use of bridge debt was a discretionary choice, not a necessity, with material economic consequences to the LP.

Interest rate cap expiration and extension risk. Bridge loans in Denver and nationally are typically structured with two or three-year initial terms and extension options. Each extension option generally requires a new or refreshed interest rate cap, and the cost of those caps increased dramatically as rates rose. When a cap that cost $150,000 at origination required $800,000 to refresh at the first extension, that cost was typically drawn from JV reserves, further suppressing LP returns. The financing expert can quantify those incremental costs and assess whether the GP's decision to extend rather than seek a permanent takeout was consistent with market-standard fiduciary conduct for a JV sponsor.

The Denver Lender Market

Denver's commercial lending market is well-developed across all lender categories, which is relevant both to the feasibility analysis and to the expert's ability to establish what alternatives were available at any given point in time.

Agency lenders (Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac). Denver is one of the stronger agency multifamily markets in the Mountain West. Multiple Delegated Underwriting and Servicing lenders maintain active Denver operations, and the market's fundamentals, including population growth from domestic migration, a diversified employment base anchored in technology, aerospace, and healthcare, and ongoing housing supply demand, make Denver a preferred agency market. Agency debt is typically priced at 150 to 220 basis points over the corresponding Treasury benchmark, with LTVs up to 80 percent on stabilized properties.

Life insurance companies. Several major life company correspondents are active in Denver across multifamily and industrial asset classes. Life companies typically require 65 to 70 percent LTV, DSCR of 1.25x to 1.35x, and are best suited to stabilized, institutional-quality assets with long operating histories. They price at tighter spreads than agency debt on a comparable basis but offer non-recourse fixed-rate execution with flexible prepayment structures, including yield maintenance or defeasance, which matters significantly in a JV context when the parties are negotiating whether a sale or refinancing is optimal.

CMBS lenders. Conduit execution is available in Denver across asset classes. CMBS lenders price at spreads over SOFR swap rates and can go higher on LTV than life companies, sometimes to 75 percent on stabilized assets. The limitation is that CMBS loans carry rigid prepayment structures and limited flexibility for mid-term capital events, which in a JV dispute context means that a GP who chose CMBS financing may have contractually constrained the JV's ability to refinance or sell without significant prepayment penalty.

Regional and community banks. Denver has a substantial community banking presence, including both Colorado-chartered institutions and regional banks operating out of the Front Range. These lenders are active on smaller to mid-market deals, generally below $15 to $20 million, and on construction and transitional assets. They tend to require personal recourse, which in a JV context creates issues around which party provides the recourse guarantee and how that obligation is valued in a dispute over GP and LP obligations.

Debt funds and bridge lenders. Non-bank bridge lenders were the most active source of capital for value-add multifamily and industrial acquisitions in Denver during the 2019 to 2022 cycle. These lenders priced at SOFR plus 250 to 400 basis points, required interest rate caps, and offered terms from 24 to 36 months with extension options. Understanding their underwriting standards, fee structures, and extension mechanics is essential to any expert analysis involving a Denver JV that used bridge financing during that period.

What Attorneys Handling Denver CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to retain a CRE financing expert. If the litigation involves how the capital stack was structured, whether a GP had access to better or different financing, how distributable cash flow was calculated, or whether a waterfall distribution correctly accounted for loan-level economics, you need a CRE financing expert. An appraiser can tell you what a property was worth. A financing expert tells you how the debt affected what each party actually received, and whether the financing decisions were consistent with market standards and the GP's fiduciary obligations.

What a CRE financing expert can address that an appraiser cannot. Appraisers determine value. They are not trained to opine on whether a 1.25x DSCR was market-standard for a given lender category in a given period, whether an interest rate cap was appropriately sized, whether a bridge loan extension was commercially reasonable when permanent debt was available, or how a preferred return accrual is affected by cash flow interruptions caused by financing costs. Those are capital markets questions that require a professional who has actually originated, underwritten, or brokered the relevant loan types in the relevant market.

What distinguishes a qualified CRE financing expert. Relevant credentials include active transaction experience in the specific asset class and market, familiarity with lender underwriting standards across multiple capital sources, and direct experience with JV capital structures and waterfall mechanics. CLS CRE has approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, relationships with more than 1,000 lenders, and active origination across 50 states including Colorado. Prior expert retention by litigation counsel is also relevant, as the expert must be able to communicate technical conclusions clearly and withstand cross-examination.

What counsel should gather before the engagement. At minimum, provide the operating agreement with all amendments, the loan documents for every financing event during the hold period, audited or reviewed financial statements for each year of the hold, rent rolls at acquisition and at each key date, any internal GP communications regarding financing decisions or waterfall calculations, and the GP's own distribution calculation workpapers. The more complete the document production, the more precise the expert's analysis and the harder it is to challenge on methodology.

How expert testimony is used. In Denver state court litigation, CRE financing expert testimony is most commonly used at summary judgment to establish that a genuine dispute of material fact exists regarding the economic impact of a financing decision, and at trial to explain to a fact finder how the capital stack worked and what market standards require. In federal proceedings, the analysis must satisfy the Daubert standard for reliability, which means the expert's methodology must be grounded in identifiable market data, published lender guidelines, and reproducible calculations. Our methodology is documented, data-driven, and specifically tied to Denver market conditions at the relevant dates.

Key Takeaways

  • Preferred return calculations are a primary source of JV disputes in Denver. The difference between simple and compound accrual methods, and the treatment of cash flow shortfalls during operating periods, can produce six and seven-figure discrepancies that require a financing expert to quantify accurately.
  • Financing decisions made by a GP are subject to expert scrutiny. Whether the GP chose bridge debt over agency debt, selected a floating rate over a fixed rate, or executed a refinancing at a suboptimal loan amount, each of those decisions has a calculable economic impact on the LP's returns and is a legitimate subject of expert analysis.
  • Denver's lender market was deep enough to support alternatives in most periods. Across 2018 to 2023, Denver multifamily assets had access to agency debt, life company debt, CMBS, and bridge lending at multiple price points. The existence of alternatives is central to any expert opinion on whether a GP's financing choice was commercially reasonable.
  • Waterfall mechanics are not self-interpreting. Operating agreement language describing a waterfall distribution must be applied against actual loan-level numbers, including accrued interest, prepayment penalties, reserve releases, and extension fees, to determine what was actually available for distribution. That calculation requires both legal and financial expertise working in tandem.

Expert Witness: Commercial Loan Modification Standards in Waukegan, IL

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Loan Modification and Workout Dispute

The Waukegan Market Context

Waukegan sits at the northern edge of the Chicago metropolitan area, straddling the boundary between suburban Lake County and the broader Midwest industrial corridor. That geographic position matters enormously in a loan modification or workout dispute because it shapes every assumption an expert witness must examine: market rents, vacancy trends, absorption rates, cap rates, and the depth of the lender market willing to extend credit against assets here. Waukegan is not a secondary market in the way that term is sometimes used dismissively. It is a functionally specific market with real institutional depth, active industrial demand, and a commercial lending environment that follows identifiable patterns an expert can measure and compare against a lender's conduct.

Industrial product in Waukegan and the broader North Suburban Lake County submarket has benefited from persistent demand driven by port access at the Port of Waukegan, proximity to the Tri-State interchange system, and supply chain reconfiguration that has pushed tenants northward from the tighter O'Hare and I-55 corridors. Vacancy in the North Lake County industrial submarket has generally tracked in the 4% to 7% range in recent cycles, with older Class B and C product carrying higher vacancy but benefiting from compressed cap rates during the 2021 to 2023 period. Net asking rents for functional warehouse and light manufacturing space in Waukegan have ranged from approximately $6.50 to $9.50 per square foot NNN depending on ceiling height, dock configuration, and office finish, with recent rental rate growth moderating from its pandemic-era peak but still positive on a year-over-year basis.

Multifamily in Waukegan presents a different profile. The city has a large renter population, and workforce housing demand has remained resilient, but properties in Waukegan's core urban neighborhoods require lender familiarity with rent levels that do not always support institutional financing thresholds. Average effective rents for two-bedroom units in Waukegan have ranged from approximately $1,100 to $1,450 per month depending on property vintage and location, producing NOI levels that constrain maximum loan size under standard DSCR requirements. This compression between achievable rents and debt service is frequently at the center of workout disputes: the borrower argues the lender should have structured a more accommodating modification given the market, and the lender argues the modification it offered reflected legitimate credit risk management. Sorting out which position reflects industry standards requires an expert who understands what those numbers actually mean in Waukegan specifically.

Retail in Waukegan is more bifurcated. The Fountain Square area and north-facing corridors near the border with North Chicago carry meaningful vacancy, particularly in smaller in-line configurations, while necessity-anchored and auto-oriented strip product along Belvidere Road and Green Bay Road has maintained stronger occupancy. Understanding which segment of the retail market a subject property falls into is foundational to any workout analysis, because lenders apply different credit standards to stabilized necessity retail than to transitional or partially vacant urban storefront product.

The Engagement

Counsel representing a commercial borrower retained Commercial Lending Solutions to provide an expert opinion in a dispute arising from a loan modification and forbearance agreement on a multi-tenant industrial property in the Waukegan submarket. The property had experienced occupancy disruption during a period of national economic stress, which triggered the borrower's outreach to the lender requesting loan relief. A forbearance agreement was ultimately executed, followed by a proposed permanent modification, but the parties reached an impasse over the modification terms, and the lender eventually initiated enforcement proceedings.

The core questions presented to our firm were as follows. First, did the lender's conduct during the modification negotiation process conform to recognized commercial real estate industry standards for workout and forbearance administration? Second, were the modification terms the lender ultimately proposed commercially reasonable when evaluated against the property's stabilized cash flow and prevailing Waukegan market conditions at the time of the proposal? Third, how did the lender's timeline and documentation requirements compare to what institutional and regional lenders typically require in workout transactions of this type and complexity? Fourth, was a modified loan on terms consistent with what the borrower proposed actually marketable and financeable in the Waukegan lending environment, and if so, from what categories of capital sources?

CLS CRE was engaged to review loan documents, forbearance agreements, internal lender communications produced in discovery, appraisal reports, rent rolls, operating statements, and comparable workout transaction data. The engagement required both a quantitative financial analysis of the asset's supportable debt and a qualitative assessment of the lender's process measured against documented industry norms.

The Financial Analysis

The foundation of any workout dispute analysis is a disciplined reconstruction of the property's cash flow and the debt service it can reasonably support at the time the modification was being negotiated. In this engagement, I analyzed the property's trailing twelve-month net operating income, adjusted for known non-recurring items, and applied a stabilized occupancy assumption based on Waukegan submarket vacancy data and the specific leasing history of the asset.

The subject property, a multi-tenant industrial building of approximately 85,000 square feet, had been operating at reduced occupancy during the forbearance period. At the time of the modification proposal, occupancy had recovered to approximately 78%, with two tenants on leases with remaining terms of 36 months or more. Gross potential rent at stabilized market levels for this product type in Waukegan was underwritten at approximately $7.75 per square foot NNN, consistent with asking rents being achieved in the submarket for comparable vintage product. Applying a vacancy and credit loss allowance of 8% to reflect both the submarket's vacancy trend and the specific tenant mix produced an effective gross income figure. After deducting stabilized operating expenses and management fees appropriate for a third-party managed industrial asset, the resulting trailing and forward-looking NOI was approximately $375,000 per year.

The industry standard DSCR threshold for commercial real estate loans of this type is 1.20x at minimum, with most institutional lenders requiring 1.25x on industrial product in secondary and tertiary suburban markets. Some balance sheet lenders operating in the Waukegan market will stretch to 1.15x on stabilized industrial with strong tenancy, but 1.20x to 1.25x represents the defensible market center. Applying a 1.25x DSCR to the $375,000 NOI produced a maximum annual debt service of $300,000, or $25,000 per month.

For the rate and amortization assumptions, I used a 7.25% interest rate consistent with what community bank and regional lender pricing looked like in the Waukegan market during the applicable period, applied to a 25-year amortization schedule, which is standard for industrial product of this age and condition. A $300,000 annual debt service divided by a monthly constant derived from those inputs supports a maximum loan balance of approximately $3.6 million. The property had been appraised at approximately $4.8 million at the time of the modification discussions, producing an LTV of 75% on the maximum supportable loan, which falls within the 65% to 75% LTV range that characterizes institutional lending standards for this product type in this submarket. That calculation established that a modified loan in the range the borrower was requesting was not only cash flow supportable but also within market-standard LTV parameters, which is directly relevant to the question of whether the lender's refusal to modify on those terms was commercially reasonable.

The analysis also modeled a stress scenario at 70% occupancy and a market rent that reflected a 10% downward adjustment from current asking rents, consistent with conservative underwriting for a property with near-term lease rollover. Even under that stressed scenario, the property generated NOI of approximately $310,000, supporting a loan balance of approximately $3.0 million at 1.25x DSCR. That stress-tested figure remained above the outstanding loan balance at the time of the modification request, which directly undermined the lender's stated justification that the collateral could not support a viable modification structure.

The Complicating Factors

Workout disputes in Waukegan's commercial property market are not resolved by financial analysis alone. Several complicating factors arise in this type of engagement that require expert analysis beyond the numbers.

Lender process and timeline noncompliance. One of the most significant issues in modification disputes is not whether the ultimate terms were reasonable but whether the lender followed its own internal policies and applicable industry standards during the workout process. Institutional lenders are expected to respond to modification requests within defined timelines, provide borrowers with clear documentation checklists, and conduct good-faith underwriting of the modified loan structure before declining. In this engagement, the record showed extended periods of lender silence following the borrower's submission of complete modification packages, which created uncertainty about whether the lender was actively evaluating the request or effectively running out the clock on the forbearance period. That pattern of conduct is a recognized deficiency when measured against published industry guidance from the Mortgage Bankers Association and standard commercial loan servicing protocols.

Appraisal timing and methodology disputes. Lenders in workout situations frequently commission new appraisals that are used to justify declining a modification. In this case, the lender's appraisal was conducted at a point when the property's occupancy was at its nadir, and the appraiser applied vacancy assumptions that were inconsistent with the recovery trajectory already visible in the submarket data and the borrower's own lease-up activity. An expert witness who understands underwriting can identify when an appraisal has been selectively timed or improperly weighted toward distressed conditions in a way that does not reflect what a reasonable lender would have used in a contemporaneous underwriting exercise.

Tenant credit and lease term bifurcation. Multi-tenant industrial properties in Waukegan often carry a mix of national credit tenants and smaller local operators. Lenders in workout situations frequently apply a blanket discount to properties with any non-credit tenancy, even when the actual operating history of those tenants is strong and the lease terms are consistent with market. Explaining to a judge or jury how lenders actually treat mixed-credit rent rolls in practice, as distinct from how a lender might characterize them in litigation, requires someone with current, active market experience rather than a theoretical framework.

Forbearance fee and default interest structuring. A recurring issue in workout disputes is whether the fees and default interest the lender charged during the forbearance period were consistent with market practice or were structured in a way that made the modification mathematically impossible to achieve. I have seen cases where the accumulation of default interest, legal fees, and forbearance extension fees effectively added 8% to 12% to the outstanding principal balance, creating a modified loan amount that no underwriting could support. That outcome, if it results from lender choices made during the workout process rather than from the borrower's conduct, is relevant to the question of good faith and commercial reasonableness.

The Waukegan Lender Market

Understanding which lenders are actually active in Waukegan for a given asset type, and what their credit requirements look like, is essential to expert testimony on whether a modified loan was financeable in the market. This is an area where a current practitioner has a decisive advantage over a retired banker or a generalist appraiser.

For industrial product in Waukegan and North Lake County, the active lending universe includes community banks headquartered in Lake County and northern Cook County, regional banks with Midwest platforms, credit unions with commercial real estate programs, and, for larger stabilized assets, CMBS conduit lenders and life insurance company correspondents. Community banks in this market, including several institutions with significant Lake County deposit franchises, are familiar with Waukegan industrial product and will lend at LTVs of 70% to 75% with DSCR floors of 1.20x to 1.25x, recourse or partial recourse depending on borrower balance sheet, and rates that tracked 200 to 275 basis points over the five-year Treasury during the relevant period. These institutions conduct their own in-house underwriting and have credit committees familiar with Waukegan market conditions, which is relevant to the speed at which they can respond to modification requests.

Debt funds and bridge lenders are also active in Waukegan for transitional or partially stabilized industrial assets, though at higher cost: rates in the 9% to 12% range with origination fees of 1.5% to 2.5% are typical for bridge product in this submarket during the period in question. The existence of this alternative capital source is relevant to expert testimony because it demonstrates that financing was available even for the property in its distressed state, and it provides a ceiling against which the lender's proposed modification terms can be benchmarked for reasonableness.

For multifamily product in Waukegan's workforce housing segment, the lender universe is more constrained. Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac agency lending is available for properties meeting occupancy and rent stabilization requirements, but smaller Waukegan multifamily assets often fall below agency loan minimums or carry characteristics that require manual underwriting. Community Development Financial Institutions (CDFIs) are active in Waukegan's affordable and workforce housing segment and represent a meaningful alternative capital source that a sophisticated lender conducting a workout analysis should have considered.

What Attorneys Handling Waukegan CRE Disputes Need to Know

Attorneys who practice commercial real estate litigation in the northern Chicago suburbs frequently encounter disputes where the central question is not what the property was worth but whether a particular financing outcome was achievable, commercially reasonable, or consistent with what lenders actually do in this market. An appraiser can tell you what the property was worth on a given date. A CRE financing expert can tell you whether the loan a lender offered, refused, or structured was consistent with what the market actually supports, which is a fundamentally different question.

When to retain a CRE financing expert. Counsel should consider engaging a commercial real estate financing expert in any dispute involving a denied loan modification, a contested forbearance agreement, a lender's refusal to release collateral or provide a payoff consistent with market value, a claim that a borrower failed to mitigate by refinancing, or a dispute about whether a proposed plan of reorganization is feasible from a financing perspective. These questions require someone who originates and structures commercial loans in the current market, not someone who did so fifteen years ago.

Qualifications that matter. A qualified CRE financing expert should have current, active transaction experience in the relevant market segment. They should be able to demonstrate a volume of closed transactions sufficient to establish a pattern of practice, not just a consulting history. At CLS CRE, our approximately $1 billion in transaction volume across 50 states and relationships with more than 1,000 lenders allows us to speak authoritatively about what lenders require, how they price risk, and what modification terms they actually approve, not what a textbook says they should do.

Information counsel should gather before engagement. Before retaining a financing expert, counsel should assemble the original loan documents and any modifications or amendments, all forbearance agreements and correspondence, the lender's internal credit memoranda and modification analysis if produced in discovery, all appraisals and BPOs commissioned in connection with the workout, the property's rent rolls and operating statements for the three years preceding the dispute, and any communications between the servicer and the special servicer if the loan was securitized. The expert's analysis is only as strong as the underlying document record.

Use at trial and in plan confirmation hearings. CRE financing expert testimony is most commonly used to establish feasibility in bankruptcy plan confirmation proceedings, to support or rebut damages claims in lender liability litigation, to establish the standard of care in cases involving breach of a duty to deal in good faith, and to explain to a trier of fact the mechanics of commercial real estate lending in terms that are accessible without being oversimplified. A well-prepared expert report under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 26 will disclose the methodology, the data sources, the assumptions, and the conclusions in a way that survives Daubert scrutiny and gives counsel a credible platform for examination.

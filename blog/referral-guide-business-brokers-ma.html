Business Brokers Guide to Commercial Real Estate Financing in Business Acquisitions

By Trevor Damyan·June 25, 2026·Commercial Lending Solutions

What Business Brokers Need to Know About Commercial Real Estate Financing in Business Acquisitions

I am Trevor Damyan, a commercial mortgage broker at CLS CRE in Los Angeles. Over the past several years, a meaningful portion of my deal flow has come from business brokers, M&A advisors, and investment bankers who discovered, often mid-transaction, that the commercial real estate component of their deal needed specialized financing their clients could not get from a conventional bank. This article is written specifically for you: the sell-side or buy-side advisor who closes business sales for a living but does not spend every day inside the world of commercial mortgage underwriting.

More than half of all US business sales involve commercial real estate in some form. Either the buyer is purchasing the business and the building together, or the seller is carving the real estate into a separate entity before the sale closes. In both cases, the financing is more complex than a standard commercial mortgage, and most banks are not set up to underwrite the combined business-and-real-estate credit story. When a bank drops the ball at the 11th hour, deals die. Knowing how this financing actually works protects your client, your commission, and your reputation.

Why Business Brokers and M&A Advisors Encounter CRE Financing Needs

Business brokers primarily focus on business value: EBITDA, seller discretionary earnings, customer concentration, recurring revenue, and transition risk. Commercial real estate, when it shows up inside a deal, is often treated as a line item rather than a separate credit event. That framing creates problems.

When a 62-year-old plumbing contractor sells his business along with the building he has owned for 20 years, the buyer needs to finance two fundamentally different assets simultaneously. The business cash flow supports the business loan. The real estate supports its own mortgage. The SBA programs that knit these together have specific rules about how the two pieces interact, and most conventional banks do not have the SBA expertise to execute them correctly.

On the sell side, advisors increasingly see clients who want to separate real estate into a holdco before selling the operating company. This creates a landlord-tenant relationship between the newly formed real estate entity (often owned by the seller or the seller's family) and the acquired business. The buyer finances the business acquisition. The seller retains the real estate and collects rent. This structure has significant estate planning and tax advantages for sellers, but it introduces a new real estate financing question: does the holdco need a mortgage, and if so, how is it underwritten when the tenant is a newly acquired business with limited operating history under new ownership?

Lower middle market investment bankers handling deals in the $5M to $50M enterprise value range face a third dynamic: real estate that was pledged as collateral for the seller's existing business debt. At closing, that collateral structure needs to be unwound, the existing lender needs to be paid off, and the real estate may need to be refinanced on its own terms. This is a title, payoff, and lender coordination problem that must be solved before the deal can close.

The Five Financing Scenarios Business Brokers Most Commonly Face

1. Business Sale That Includes Owner-Occupied Real Estate: SBA 504 or 7(a)

This is the most common scenario. A manufacturing company, dental practice, auto body shop, or wholesale distributor has operated out of a building the owner has held for years. The buyer wants both. The question is how to finance the combined acquisition.

The SBA 7(a) program can finance the business and the real estate in a single loan, up to $5 million. The loan is guaranteed by the SBA and issued by an approved lender. The buyer typically needs 10% equity injection (down payment), which can come from personal funds, seller financing, or a combination. The combined debt service must be covered by the business cash flow. The lender underwrites the business, not just the real estate.

The SBA 504 program is structured differently. It splits the financing into two components: a first mortgage from a conventional lender covering roughly 50% of the total project cost, and a certified development company (CDC) second mortgage covering 40%, with the buyer bringing 10% down. The 504 is purpose-built for real estate and equipment heavy acquisitions. Interest rates on the CDC portion are fixed for 25 years, which makes the 504 attractive for buyers who want long-term payment certainty.

What lenders require for these transactions: two to three years of business tax returns, personal financial statements from all guarantors with 20% or more ownership, a business valuation or purchase agreement establishing fair market value, real estate appraisal, environmental Phase I (and Phase II if the prior use warrants it), and a business plan from the buyer if the buyer lacks direct industry experience. Credit scores below 680 create friction. Debt-to-income ratios at the personal level matter. And lenders scrutinize whether the purchase price reflects genuine fair market value or a distressed situation that introduces additional risk.

What business brokers miss most often: the real estate appraisal must support the real estate component of the purchase price. If a seller has allocated $2M to the real estate but the appraisal comes in at $1.6M, the deal structure has to change. This is not uncommon when sellers have emotional attachment to their building. Setting realistic real estate price expectations early saves deals from falling apart in diligence.

2. Buyer Separating Real Estate from the Business: The Holdco Structure

This structure is increasingly popular in deals above $3M where the seller wants to retain real estate as a long-term income asset. The operating company is sold. The real estate is transferred into a separate LLC or holding company owned by the seller (or the seller's family trust). The new holding company executes a lease with the acquired operating company at fair market rent.

The financing question on the buyer's side is straightforward: the buyer finances the business acquisition through a business loan, SBA or conventional. The real estate holdco does not need financing if the seller owns it free and clear. But if the seller wants to pull equity out of the real estate at closing (essentially monetizing the building while retaining ownership), the holdco needs a commercial mortgage.

Underwriting a mortgage on a property leased to a business that just changed ownership is genuinely difficult. Commercial lenders underwrite the tenant's ability to pay rent. A business under new ownership has no track record under the new operator. Lenders will look at: the strength of the lease (term, escalations, renewal options), the creditworthiness of the guarantor (often the buyer), the property's standalone value if the tenant vacates, and the buyer's liquidity post-close.

NNN lease structures with long initial terms (10+ years) and personal guarantees from the buyer make this financing more achievable. Short leases, month-to-month arrangements, or leases with no personal guarantee from the buyer make lenders very nervous. If the seller wants to monetize the real estate at closing, the lease structure needs to be designed with lender requirements in mind, not just with what is convenient for the parties.

3. Seller Financing Combined with a Commercial Mortgage

Seller financing shows up in business sales constantly. In the context of real estate, it typically means the seller takes back a promissory note on the real estate (subordinate to a first mortgage from an institutional lender) while the buyer obtains senior debt to cover the majority of the purchase.

Senior commercial lenders are generally willing to accept seller carry in second position, but they have strict requirements. The combined loan-to-value ratio across the first and second positions cannot exceed the lender's maximum LTV (typically 75% to 80% for owner-occupied commercial real estate). The seller note must be fully disclosed to the first mortgage lender. The terms of the seller note must be reasonable (no balloon in the first 12 to 24 months, no interest rate that creates negative amortization).

SBA lenders have specific rules about seller notes: under the 7(a) program, a seller note can be used as an equity injection but must be on full standby (no payments) for two years. Under the 504, seller notes have similar restrictions. Many SBA lenders require the seller note to be formally subordinated and documented in a subordination agreement reviewed by the SBA.

For business brokers, the practical implication is this: if you are building a seller financing component into your deal as a bridge to make the numbers work, coordinate with the commercial mortgage broker before you finalize the term sheet. The seller carry terms need to be structured in a way the first lender will actually accept.

4. Real Estate Used as Collateral for Business Debt: Unwinding at Closing

This situation is particularly common in deals where the seller financed business growth through a line of credit, equipment loans, or SBA debt secured by the commercial real estate. At closing, the buyer's lender needs to be in first position on the property. But the seller's existing lender has a lien on the real estate that must be cleared.

The mechanics require the seller's existing debt to be paid off at closing from the sale proceeds before the title can transfer free and clear. This sounds simple but creates several complications. First, the payoff amount must be confirmed in writing from the existing lender well before closing, because prepayment penalties on commercial loans can be substantial (yield maintenance provisions, step-down prepayment schedules). Second, if the existing SBA loan is being paid off, there are specific SBA payoff request procedures and timing requirements. Third, if the seller's existing lender has a blanket lien on the business assets in addition to the real estate, the partial lien release (releasing only the real estate while keeping the business assets secured) requires explicit negotiation.

The commercial mortgage broker needs to be in the deal early enough to coordinate the payoff and release timeline with the title company and the closing attorney. Surprises at the closing table involving lien releases are how deals get extended by 30 to 60 days.

5. Franchise Acquisition with Real Estate

Franchise acquisitions introduce a layer of complexity most business brokers encounter when their client wants to buy an established franchise location (not a greenfield franchise). The franchisee is buying the operating business, the equipment, the customer relationships, and often the real estate or an assumable lease on the commercial space.

SBA financing is the primary tool for franchise acquisitions because the SBA maintains an approved franchise registry (the Franchise Registry or SBA's Franchise Directory) that allows lenders to move faster on approved brands. For real estate included in the franchise acquisition, the SBA 504 or 7(a) structures described above apply, but with additional requirements specific to the franchise context.

Franchisor approval is not optional. The franchisor must sign off on the transfer of the franchise agreement to the new buyer before the lender will fund. This approval process can take 30 to 90 days depending on the franchisor, and it runs in parallel with the loan process. If the franchisor requires training, remodeling commitments, or additional capital reserves as a condition of approval, these requirements affect the total project cost and the financing structure.

Lenders also want to see the Franchise Disclosure Document (FDD), the existing franchise agreement, and confirmation that the new franchisee meets the franchisor's net worth and liquidity requirements. Some franchisors have preferred lender programs. Knowing whether the franchisor has an existing relationship with a specific SBA lender can accelerate the process significantly.

What Commercial Lenders Actually Require for Business-Related Real Estate Deals

Across all five scenarios above, here are the documentation requirements that consistently determine whether a deal funds on time or falls apart in underwriting:

  • Business tax returns: Two to three years of entity-level federal returns. Lenders need to see real cash flow, not adjusted EBITDA from a broker's marketing deck.
  • Personal financial statements: All guarantors with 20% or more ownership complete Form 1003 or lender equivalent. Net worth, liquidity, and contingent liabilities matter.
  • Real estate appraisal: Ordered by the lender, not the buyer. The buyer pays for it but does not control the assignment. Appraisal must be from an MAI-certified appraiser on the lender's approved panel.
  • Environmental: Phase I ESA required on virtually every deal. Phase II triggered by recognized environmental conditions (RECs) in the Phase I. Sellers of industrial, automotive, dry cleaning, or gas station adjacent properties should assume Phase II will be ordered.
  • Business valuation: Required by SBA lenders on acquisition loans. Must be performed by an accredited valuator. The lender orders this or approves the engagement of a third-party firm.
  • Lease documentation: If real estate is being separated into a holdco, the new lease must be fully executed before the mortgage can fund. Lenders will not lend on a verbal or draft lease.
  • Equity injection documentation: SBA lenders require proof of where the down payment is coming from. Gift funds have specific rules. Retirement account rollovers (ROBS) require additional documentation and a specific lender approval process.

Minimum thresholds most SBA and commercial lenders want to see: debt service coverage ratio above 1.25x on the real estate, global DSCR above 1.15x across all obligations, loan-to-value at or below 80% for owner-occupied commercial (90% with SBA guaranty on a 7(a)), borrower liquidity post-close of at least three months of debt service, and guarantor credit scores above 675.

Questions Business Brokers Should Ask Any Commercial Mortgage Broker Before Referring

Not all commercial mortgage brokers are equipped to handle the complexity of business acquisition financing. Before you refer your client, ask these questions:

  • Do you have active SBA 7(a) and 504 lender relationships? SBA financing is highly relationship-dependent. Brokers who lack these relationships are guessing on execution.
  • Have you closed deals involving a business and real estate in the same transaction? Combined business-and-real-estate underwriting is a specialty. Ask for examples.
  • How do you handle the coordination between the business loan and the real estate mortgage if they are separate? Timing mismatches between two separate closings sink deals.
  • What is your average close time on SBA transactions? SBA deals average 60 to 90 days. Brokers who promise 30-day closes on SBA loans either have inside lender relationships or are not being honest with you.
  • Do you work with the title company and closing attorney directly? A commercial mortgage broker who does not coordinate with the title company creates information gaps at closing.
  • What states are your lenders licensed in? Some lenders have geographic restrictions. Confirm coverage for your deal's location before investing time in the relationship.
  • How do you get paid and what does my client owe? Lender-paid compensation, broker fees, and origination points vary. Your client should know the total cost of capital before signing anything.

Key Takeaways

  • More than half of business sales involve real estate. Knowing how commercial real estate financing works is a direct competitive advantage for business brokers, not a peripheral skill.
  • The five most complex scenarios are: combined business-and-real-estate SBA acquisition, holdco separation with seller-retained real estate, seller financing blended with senior debt, unwinding real estate collateral from existing business debt, and franchise acquisitions with real estate.
  • SBA 7(a) and SBA 504 are the dominant tools for owner-occupied commercial real estate in business acquisitions. Understanding the structural difference (single loan vs. split-tranche financing) helps you set client expectations early.
  • The real estate appraisal is the single most common deal-killer in combined business-and-real-estate transactions. Setting realistic real estate price expectations early protects the deal.
  • Seller notes must be structured before the term sheet is finalized. Seller carry terms that conflict with senior lender requirements create problems that are very difficult to fix after the purchase agreement is signed.
  • Environmental diligence is non-negotiable. Phase I ESA timelines (typically three to four weeks) must be built into the closing timeline. Phase II can add another four to eight weeks. Industrial and automotive properties almost always trigger Phase II.
  • Introduce the commercial mortgage broker at LOI stage, not after the purchase agreement. Early involvement reduces surprises, accelerates the process, and gives the buyer more time to qualify.
  • A qualified commercial mortgage broker manages the real estate financing end to end at no cost to the business broker or the business broker's client until closing. There is no reason to leave this to a conventional bank that lacks the specialization.

Expert Witness: CRE Partnership and Waterfall Disputes in Fort Wayne, IN

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Joint Venture and Partnership Dispute

The Fort Wayne Market Context

Fort Wayne, Indiana is the second-largest city in the state and one of the most consistently underrated commercial real estate markets in the Midwest. While it does not generate the transaction volume of Indianapolis or Chicago, it has developed a diverse and durable economic base anchored by manufacturing, logistics, healthcare, and financial services. That combination creates a CRE environment where joint venture equity structures are increasingly common, because institutional capital has been flowing into secondary Midwest markets in search of yield and lower basis risk. With that capital comes complexity, and with complexity comes litigation.

On the industrial side, Fort Wayne benefits directly from its position at the intersection of I-69 and I-469, with proximity to major distribution corridors serving the Midwest and Great Lakes regions. Industrial vacancy in the Fort Wayne metro has remained in the low single digits for much of the past several years, with asking rents for Class A warehouse and distribution product climbing from the mid-$4.00 range per square foot (NNN) toward the $6.00 to $7.00 range, driven by e-commerce demand and regional supply chain reshoring. That rent growth has made industrial development and value-add plays attractive to outside equity partners looking to co-invest with local operating partners who understand the submarket dynamics.

Multifamily in Fort Wayne has similarly attracted joint venture capital. Cap rates for stabilized Class B garden-style product have compressed into the 5.25 to 6.25 percent range, depending on vintage and submarket positioning within the metro, particularly around the Jefferson Pointe corridor and the growing southwest quadrant. New Class A product has transacted at sub-5.5 cap rates when location and amenity profiles support institutional underwriting. These valuations, combined with construction cost increases and interest rate volatility, have put significant pressure on promoted interest and preferred return mechanics in JV structures that were capitalized in 2020 through 2022. That pressure is precisely the environment that produces distribution waterfall disputes.

Office fundamentals in Fort Wayne are softer, consistent with national trends, with downtown Class B product facing elevated vacancy and suburban flex product holding better occupancy due to local professional services demand. Mixed-use and retail-anchored assets in high-traffic corridors remain active for local and regional investors. The practical reality is that across all asset types, the Fort Wayne market has seen enough institutional equity participation over the past decade that sophisticated JV agreements with multi-tier waterfall structures are no longer rare, and when those deals underperform or generate disputes over distributions, the need for a forensic capital markets analysis is genuine and consequential.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing a limited partner in a commercial real estate joint venture that had acquired and repositioned a multifamily asset in the Fort Wayne metro. The engagement arose after the operating partner, who also held the promoted interest in the deal, began making distributions that the limited partner contended were calculated incorrectly under the terms of the JV operating agreement.

The core legal questions presented to CLS CRE as the retained financing expert were threefold. First, whether the preferred return hurdle had been correctly calculated on a cumulative basis, including whether unreturned preferred return from earlier periods had been properly compounded before any promoted interest distributions were taken. Second, whether the refinancing proceeds from a cash-out refinance executed during the hold period should have been treated as a capital event triggering a full waterfall sweep, or whether they were properly characterized by the operating partner as a partial return of capital outside the waterfall mechanics. Third, whether the loan terms that were actually obtained for the refinancing were consistent with what a reasonably competent operator could have achieved in the Fort Wayne market at the time of execution, which had direct bearing on whether the limited partner's equity was being adequately protected.

The asset itself was a garden-style multifamily community of approximately 200 units, acquired as a value-add play. The business plan called for unit renovations, operational improvements, and a refinancing event within three to four years to return a portion of the equity to investors while retaining the asset. That refinancing occurred, generating proceeds that became the focal point of the distribution dispute. Counsel needed an expert who could opine not only on what the waterfall should have produced mathematically, but also on whether the debt terms obtained were market-consistent and whether any financing decisions by the operating partner had materially affected the distribution outcome.

The Financial Analysis

The first analytical task was reconstructing the income and expense history of the asset to establish the actual net operating income at the time the refinancing was executed. This required reviewing rent rolls, lease abstracts, historical operating statements, and the rent growth trajectory the operator had achieved through the renovation program. For the Fort Wayne multifamily submarket, market-rate garden product of this vintage was generating average effective rents in the $850 to $1,050 per month range for one and two-bedroom units post-renovation, with vacancy assumptions of approximately 5 to 7 percent for a stabilized property.

For this asset, the reconstructed trailing twelve-month NOI at the time of refinancing was approximately $1,050,000, reflecting approximately 185 units occupied at stabilized rents net of operating expenses running at roughly 42 percent of effective gross income, which is consistent with market-standard expense ratios for professionally managed Fort Wayne garden multifamily.

Applying market-standard underwriting at the time of the refinancing, which occurred in a period when agency lenders were pricing 10-year fixed-rate multifamily debt in the 5.50 to 6.25 percent range with 30-year amortization, the debt service coverage analysis was constructed as follows. At a 1.25x DSCR, which represents the standard minimum threshold for Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac agency execution on conventional multifamily, and using an assumed note rate of 5.875 percent on a 30-year amortization schedule, the monthly debt service per million dollars of loan balance is approximately $5,915. Annual debt service on a $12,000,000 loan would therefore be approximately $709,800. Dividing the NOI of $1,050,000 by that debt service figure produces a DSCR of approximately 1.48x, well above the 1.25x minimum threshold.

Maximum loan sizing at 1.25x DSCR would have supported gross debt of approximately $14,700,000 based on the NOI. However, agency LTV constraints for conventional multifamily at that point in the market cycle were generally capped at 75 to 80 percent LTV depending on the borrower profile and property tier. Applying an 80 percent LTV limit to an appraised value of $17,500,000 (which was the appraised value at origination of the refinance) produces a maximum loan of $14,000,000. The DSCR constraint at 1.25x and the LTV constraint at 80 percent were therefore both satisfied at a $14,000,000 loan level, confirming that the loan amount actually obtained, which was $12,500,000, was below the maximum supportable amount under market-standard agency execution. That gap of $1,500,000 in unrealized loan proceeds was directly relevant to the limited partner's damages analysis, because additional proceeds at the time of the waterfall sweep would have accelerated the return of invested capital and reduced the accrued preferred return shortfall.

The analysis also required opining on whether the operating partner's choice of lender and loan structure was consistent with the standard of care for a reasonably competent operator. The operator had used a local community bank rather than agency execution, accepting a shorter 5-year term with a floating rate component rather than locking in longer-term fixed-rate agency debt. While community bank execution can be appropriate in certain situations, the decision to forgo agency execution for this stabilized, 200-unit asset in a qualified market was not supported by any documented rationale in the loan file, and the resulting terms were materially less favorable to the JV investors.

The Complicating Factors

The Characterization of Refinancing Proceeds: One of the most frequently litigated issues in real estate JV disputes is whether refinancing proceeds constitute a capital event under the waterfall or whether they can be treated as a routine return of capital distributed outside the waterfall mechanics. Most market-standard JV agreements drafted by institutional counsel include explicit language defining a capital event to include any refinancing, recapitalization, or other event generating proceeds to the venture above a de minimis threshold. When that language is present, any refinancing proceeds must pass through the full waterfall, including satisfaction of accrued preferred returns before any promoted interest is distributed. However, many agreements drafted for smaller or less sophisticated Fort Wayne deals contain ambiguous language, and the operating partner in this matter argued that the refinancing was a partial capital return rather than a capital event. Resolving this question required a careful reading of the JV agreement in conjunction with an expert opinion on what constitutes market-standard treatment of refinancing proceeds, which is a question that lies squarely within the CRE financing expert's domain rather than the appraiser's.

Compounding of Preferred Returns: The preferred return in this JV was stated as an 8 percent annual preferred return on unreturned capital. The dispute centered on whether that return compounded annually on unpaid balances, or whether it accrued on a simple basis. This distinction is economically significant over a multi-year hold period. On an initial equity contribution of $4,200,000, the difference between simple and compounding accrual over five years at 8 percent is approximately $280,000 to $340,000 in additional preferred return that must be satisfied before any promoted interest distribution. The operating partner had calculated distributions on a simple accrual basis without compounding, and had then taken a promoted interest distribution before the limited partner had received the full compounded preferred return. Establishing the market-standard treatment of preferred return accrual required expert testimony on how institutional JV structures in the Midwest multifamily market typically handle this computation.

Financing Terms and Their Effect on Waterfall Outcomes: The choice of lender and loan structure is not merely an operational decision by the operator; it directly determines how much cash flows through the waterfall and when. In this matter, the operator's selection of a floating-rate community bank loan rather than fixed-rate agency execution meant that debt service during years two through four of the loan was higher than it would have been under an agency structure, which reduced the cash available for distribution and delayed the point at which the preferred return would be fully satisfied. This is a technical point that requires a capital markets expert to explain clearly, because it connects the financing decision to the economic harm suffered by the limited partner in a way that a general business damages expert cannot.

The Fort Wayne Lender Market

Understanding the lender landscape that was actually available in Fort Wayne at the relevant time is essential to any expert opinion on financing feasibility or standard of care. Fort Wayne is served by a well-developed community and regional banking market, with a meaningful number of locally headquartered institutions that are active in CRE lending. These lenders have strong relationships with the local investor base and can be competitive for balance-sheet held loans on smaller deals, typically up to $5,000,000 to $8,000,000, where relationship considerations and local market knowledge offset their pricing disadvantage relative to agency or CMBS execution.

For stabilized multifamily assets in the 100 to 300-unit range, the agency execution market, meaning Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, and HUD, is fully accessible from Fort Wayne. Approved Delegated Underwriting and Servicing lenders can originate agency multifamily loans in the Fort Wayne market with the same terms and pricing available anywhere in the country, assuming the asset quality and borrower profile meet the credit box. For an asset with $1,050,000 in stabilized NOI and an appraised value above $15,000,000, agency execution was the obvious market-standard choice at the time of this refinancing, and any expert opinion that suggested otherwise would not withstand scrutiny from a lender market standpoint.

Life insurance companies are selectively active in Fort Wayne for larger stabilized assets, generally preferring loan amounts above $8,000,000 to $10,000,000 on industrial, multifamily, or net-lease retail product with strong sponsorship. Their underwriting is conservative, typically 65 to 70 percent LTV with 1.30x DSCR floors, but they offer competitive fixed rates and long-term certainty. CMBS execution is available for Fort Wayne assets above $5,000,000 but is generally less competitive than agency for qualifying multifamily due to agency pricing advantages. Debt funds and bridge lenders are active for value-add and transitional assets but are not appropriate for stabilized refinancing, as their pricing typically runs 150 to 300 basis points above comparable agency or bank execution.

What Attorneys Handling Fort Wayne CRE Disputes Need to Know

A commercial real estate financing expert serves a fundamentally different function than an appraiser. An appraiser establishes market value. A CRE financing expert establishes what a reasonable lender would have done, what the market-standard deal terms were at a given point in time, whether a specific financing decision fell within or outside the range of competent operator conduct, and how debt structure affects the economic outcomes that flow to each party in a JV capital stack. These are questions that appraisers are neither trained nor qualified to answer, and attorneys who conflate the two roles often find their expert testimony successfully challenged on scope grounds.

Counsel should retain a CRE financing expert when the dispute involves any of the following: allegations that an operator failed to maximize refinancing proceeds or loan availability; disputes over whether JV distributions were correctly calculated in light of the financing terms actually obtained; claims that a lender acted outside market standards in structuring, pricing, or enforcing a loan; disputes over whether a proposed deal was financeable on the terms the parties assumed; or any matter where the feasibility of obtaining financing at a particular time and on particular terms is a contested factual issue.

A qualified CRE financing expert should be an active practitioner with current transaction experience, not a retired banker or former appraiser who last closed a deal a decade ago. The lender market changes rapidly, and market-standard underwriting criteria, pricing benchmarks, and structural conventions from 2019 are materially different from those in 2022 or 2024. The expert needs to be able to testify credibly about what the market actually looked like at the relevant time, which requires having been an active participant in that market. At CLS CRE, our approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, relationships with over 1,000 lenders across 50 states, and daily deal activity in markets including Midwest secondary markets like Fort Wayne give us the empirical foundation to testify with specificity rather than generality.

Before engaging the expert, counsel should gather the full loan file including the term sheet, commitment letter, loan documents, appraisal, rent roll, and operating statements; the full JV operating agreement with all amendments; all capital account statements and distribution notices; and any internal communications from the operator discussing financing decisions or distribution calculations. The expert will need all of this to render a complete and defensible opinion, and the quality of the engagement depends directly on the completeness of the document production.

Key Takeaways

  • Waterfall mechanics are directly affected by financing decisions. The loan amount, rate, term, and structure chosen by an operator are not merely administrative details; they determine cash flow available for distribution and the timing of preferred return satisfaction, which means financing expert testimony is often essential in JV distribution disputes.
  • Fort Wayne's multifamily market had genuine access to agency execution at competitive terms. Any operating partner who bypassed agency execution for a stabilized, appropriately sized multifamily asset in Fort Wayne without documented justification may have fallen below the standard of care, and expert testimony can establish that gap.
  • The characterization of refinancing proceeds as a capital event versus a return of capital is a legal question with a financial expert dimension. Market-standard JV practice is relevant and admissible evidence on how the parties likely intended that term to function, and a financing expert can establish what institutional market participants treat as standard.
  • Preferred return compounding versus simple accrual has material economic consequences. On multi-year holds with initial equity contributions in the $3,000,000 to $6,000,000 range, the difference in accrued preferred return between simple and compounding calculations can reach six figures and must be quantified by an expert with capital structure experience.
  • A CRE financing expert is not a substitute for an appraiser, and vice versa. Attorneys who need both a value opinion and a financing feasibility or market-standard analysis should retain both experts, as their scopes are complementary but non-overlapping.
  • The Fort Wayne lender market is deep enough to support institutional execution. The argument that Fort Wayne's secondary market status limited financing options or justified below-market loan terms will not withstand scrutiny from a qualified expert familiar with agency, life company, and CMBS execution in this market.
  • Damages in JV distribution disputes are quantifiable through financing analysis. Reconstructing what the waterfall would have produced under market-standard financing and market-standard distribution calculations produces a specific dollar damages figure that can support or defend against a breach of fiduciary duty or breach of contract claim.

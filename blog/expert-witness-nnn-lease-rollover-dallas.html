Expert Witness: NNN Lease Rollover Upside and Financing Impact in Dallas, TX

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Below-Market NNN Lease and Rollover Upside Analysis

The Dallas Market Context

Dallas-Fort Worth has become one of the most analytically interesting commercial real estate markets in the country for single-tenant net lease disputes, precisely because the market's growth trajectory has made the gap between in-place rents and current market rents wider and more consequential than in almost any other major U.S. metro. Understanding that gap is central to any financing dispute involving a below-market NNN lease.

The DFW industrial market illustrates the problem well. Industrial asking rents across the metroplex have increased by more than 60 percent over the trailing five years in certain submarkets, including Garland, South Dallas, and the Alliance Corridor. In-place rents on leases executed in 2018 or 2019 may sit at $5.50 to $7.00 per square foot NNN on properties where current market rent for comparable space is $9.50 to $12.00 per square foot. That spread is not theoretical. It is the direct source of litigation when a lender, appraiser, or counterparty in a transaction treats the in-place rent stream as the only relevant income metric, or conversely when a borrower overclaims rollover upside to support an aggressive valuation position.

Retail NNN assets in Dallas face a different but equally relevant dynamic. Single-tenant retail along major corridors including LBJ Freeway, Preston Road, and the Legacy business park area has seen significant cap rate compression over the prior cycle, with investment-grade-tenanted assets trading at 4.50 to 5.75 percent cap rates at peak. Many of those assets carry long-term leases with fixed rental escalations of one to two percent annually, meaning the lease was structured in a low-rate, compressed-cap-rate environment. When interest rates move materially, the in-place rent stream may no longer support the original loan at refinance, even before any lease rollover risk is introduced.

Office in Dallas is a more complex story. While the broader national narrative around office vacancy is well documented, Dallas has maintained stronger fundamentals than coastal gateway markets, with Class A suburban vacancy in Uptown, Preston Center, and Far North Dallas running meaningfully below the national average. However, single-tenant office NNN assets present unique lender concerns around tenant concentration and rollover, which I address in detail below.

The Engagement

Counsel representing a defendant in a Dallas commercial real estate dispute retained Commercial Lending Solutions to analyze the financing implications of a single-tenant NNN lease structure on a freestanding commercial property in a major Dallas submarket. The asset was a mid-size industrial/flex building occupied by a single tenant under a long-term NNN lease that was, at the time of the relevant transaction, approximately four years from its scheduled expiration.

The core legal questions presented to our firm fell into three categories. First, whether the in-place lease, given its below-market rent, supported a commercially reasonable loan at the LTV and DSCR thresholds the parties had assumed or represented. Second, whether rollover upside, meaning the projected increase in NOI following lease expiration and re-tenanting at market rent, was a legitimate basis for a lender to underwrite a larger loan than the in-place income would support. Third, whether the lease structure itself, including its remaining term, renewal options, and rental escalation provisions, materially altered the financing universe available to the borrower, and if so, how and by how much.

These questions required our firm to reconstruct how commercial mortgage lenders, including banks, CMBS conduits, insurance companies, and debt funds, would have analyzed this specific asset in this specific market at the relevant point in time. That reconstruction is not an appraisal function. It is a capital markets function requiring active transactional experience with the lender community, familiarity with underwriting standards across lender categories, and the ability to apply those standards to a specific fact pattern.

The Financial Analysis

The starting point for any NNN lease financing analysis is the in-place NOI. For a single-tenant NNN property, this calculation is relatively straightforward: base rent multiplied by the leased square footage, adjusted for any landlord obligations not passed through to the tenant (typically insurance on the structure, management fees, and reserves), produces a stabilized NOI figure. The critical question is whether lenders underwrite to that figure or to something else.

In this engagement, the in-place lease produced an annual gross rent of approximately $1,050,000 on a property of approximately 140,000 square feet, reflecting an in-place rent of $7.50 per square foot NNN. After deducting a management fee (typically 2 to 3 percent of effective gross income for NNN assets, even though management responsibilities are minimal) and capital reserves (typically $0.10 to $0.15 per square foot annually), the underwritten NOI was approximately $990,000.

Applying a 1.25x DSCR, which is the standard minimum for conventional commercial mortgage lenders on single-tenant properties with lease terms of five years or less remaining, the maximum supportable annual debt service was $792,000. At a 6.75 percent interest rate on a 25-year amortization schedule, which was consistent with bank and CMBS pricing at the relevant time for this asset type and market, that annual debt service supported a maximum loan of approximately $9,950,000.

The property had been appraised at approximately $15,200,000 using a blended income approach that weighted current in-place income and a market rent assumption at lease expiration. A 65 percent LTV constraint, standard for single-tenant commercial assets with near-term lease rollover, would have permitted a loan of up to $9,880,000. The DSCR constraint and the LTV constraint converged at nearly the same loan amount, which is analytically significant: it tells you the property is neither over-leveraged relative to value nor under-leveraged relative to cash flow. Both constraints bind at approximately the same point.

The dispute arose in part because one party had assumed, or represented to a lender, that the loan could be sized to $12,500,000, implying either a 1.0x DSCR (which no conventional lender will accept) or a rollover upside adjustment to the underwritten NOI. Our analysis demonstrated that neither assumption was supportable under market-standard underwriting at the relevant date.

The Rollover Upside Question

This is where the analysis becomes more nuanced. Rollover upside refers to the increase in NOI that would occur if, at lease expiration, the property is re-leased at current market rent. In this case, market rent for comparable space in the relevant Dallas submarket was approximately $10.25 per square foot NNN. If the tenant vacated and the space was re-leased at market, stabilized NOI would increase to approximately $1,380,000, a meaningful improvement over the $990,000 in-place figure.

The question is whether a lender will give credit for that upside today, in sizing a loan based on current in-place income. The answer, uniformly across conventional lender categories, is no. Lenders underwrite to the in-place, contractual income stream. They do not advance capital today based on projected future income that depends on a lease event, a re-tenanting process, a market rent assumption, and a lease-up timeline, all of which carry execution risk. Some lenders will note the rollover upside as a value-add characteristic and price the loan accordingly, but it does not move the underwritten loan amount.

There is one narrow exception: if the lease includes a market rent reset provision or a fixed-step escalation that triggers within the loan term, and that escalation is contractually certain, a lender may incorporate it into the underwritten NOI at a discounted or haircut basis. The lease in this matter contained annual fixed escalations of 2.0 percent, which over the four remaining years produced a compounded adjustment of approximately 8.2 percent. Some lenders will underwrite to a blended average rent over the remaining lease term rather than the current base rent, which in this case would have increased the underwritten NOI by approximately $60,000 to $70,000 annually, supporting a marginally higher loan. That adjustment is methodologically defensible but not universal.

The Complicating Factors

Remaining Lease Term and Balloon Risk

The single most consequential structural issue for this asset was the four-year remaining lease term relative to any commercially reasonable loan term. Conventional lenders require that the lease term extend at least through the loan maturity date, and many require a one to two year cushion beyond that. A five-year loan on an asset with four years of lease term remaining means the lender faces a balloon payment due approximately one year after the lease expires. That creates what is commonly called refinancing risk at rollover: if the tenant vacates or renegotiates at a materially lower rent, the borrower may be unable to refinance the balloon and the lender faces a workout or foreclosure scenario on a vacant or partially occupied single-tenant property.

In practice, this constraint eliminates most insurance company and agency lenders from the financing universe immediately. Life companies and CMBS conduits require lease terms commensurate with loan maturity, and many have explicit policy prohibitions on single-tenant exposure with near-term rollover. The practical lender universe for this asset compressed to shorter-term bridge lenders and select community and regional banks willing to structure around the rollover risk with covenants and reserves.

Single-Tenant Concentration Risk

Single-tenant properties eliminate diversification entirely. Every dollar of NOI comes from one source, and if that source vacates, NOI goes to zero. Lenders price this risk in one of two ways: higher interest rate spreads, or lower LTV. For investment-grade-rated tenants, the premium is modest because the credit of the tenant substitutes partially for property diversification. For non-investment-grade tenants, which was the case in this matter, lenders applied a meaningful credit haircut, reducing maximum LTV from the 65 to 70 percent range to 55 to 60 percent and increasing the required DSCR to 1.30x in several lender conversations our firm had with the market.

Below-Market Rent and Appraisal Methodology Conflict

The below-market in-place rent created a specific tension between how an appraiser values the property and how a lender underwrites the loan. An appraiser applying the income approach will typically blend in-place income with market rent in a discounted cash flow model, producing a value that is higher than what the in-place income alone would support. A lender, however, underwrites only to contractual income. The result is a structural disconnect: the appraised value may support a loan at the stated LTV that the in-place income cannot service at any standard DSCR threshold. Both the appraiser and the lender are technically correct within their respective methodologies, but the conflict creates a financing gap that is central to disputes of this type.

The Dallas Lender Market

Dallas has one of the deepest commercial real estate lending markets in the country, reflecting both the scale of the local economy and the density of Texas-chartered institutions. However, asset-specific constraints of the type described here significantly narrow the active lender universe.

Texas-chartered community and regional banks represent the most flexible category for assets with lease rollover risk. Banks including those operating primarily within the DFW metroplex and Texas footprint have historically been willing to structure three to five year term loans with balloon maturities, personal recourse guarantees, and cash flow sweep structures that capture excess cash flow into a reserve account during the final 12 to 18 months of lease term. Pricing for this structure in the relevant rate environment ran in the range of SOFR plus 225 to 275 basis points, or fixed rates in the 6.50 to 7.25 percent range depending on the relationship and credit profile of the guarantor.

National and super-regional banks with Dallas commercial real estate operations are active on larger transactions but generally apply tighter credit box standards than community banks. Most require lease term commensurate with loan maturity, effectively limiting their participation to assets with longer in-place lease terms. They were not a viable source for this asset without a lease extension negotiated prior to closing.

CMBS conduit lenders are active in Dallas and represent a meaningful portion of the permanent loan market for stabilized NNN assets. However, CMBS underwriting for single-tenant assets requires a minimum 10-year lease term, investment-grade tenancy for the most aggressive sizing, and non-recourse structure with standard bad-boy carve-outs. None of these conditions were met in the subject engagement, and CMBS was appropriately excluded from the available financing universe.

Debt funds and private bridge lenders operate in Dallas across a range of asset types and will advance capital on transitional or lease-challenged assets. Bridge pricing in the current environment runs in the range of 8.50 to 11.00 percent depending on LTV, loan term, and exit strategy clarity. For an asset with a defined lease expiration and a demonstrable rollover upside story, a debt fund might size a loan to 60 to 65 percent of the projected stabilized value, with an earnout or future funding provision tied to lease execution at market rent upon rollover. This structure is available but carries significantly higher cost and typically requires a credible re-tenanting plan or demonstrated tenant interest.

What Attorneys Handling Dallas CRE Disputes Need to Know

Attorneys litigating commercial real estate financing disputes in Texas federal and state courts, as well as bankruptcy proceedings in the Northern and Eastern Districts, encounter a recurring challenge: appraisers can tell you what a property is worth, but they cannot tell you whether a specific loan was financeable, at what terms, under what lender category, and within what market conditions. That is a capital markets question, and it requires a capital markets expert.

When to retain a CRE financing expert. The threshold question is whether the dispute turns on financing feasibility, lender behavior, or industry standards in commercial mortgage underwriting. Cases involving lender liability, breach of commitment letter, alleged misrepresentation to lenders, CMBS loan modification disputes, guaranty enforcement defenses based on lender misconduct, and plan feasibility in Chapter 11 reorganizations all potentially require a CRE financing expert. An appraiser retained for valuation purposes cannot opine on whether a lender's underwriting was consistent with market standards, whether a commitment letter represented industry-standard terms, or whether a refinancing was feasible given the lease structure. These are distinct disciplines.

What makes a CRE financing expert qualified. Qualification is not a matter of having worked in banking or lending at some point in a career. The relevant question is whether the expert has active, transactional experience placing commercial real estate loans across multiple lender categories in the relevant market at the relevant time. An expert who left active practice five or more years ago may have limited utility in opining on current market conditions, rate environments, or lender credit box standards. At CLS CRE, our qualification is grounded in approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, relationships with more than 1,000 lenders, and active originations across all 50 states including a substantial Texas and DFW presence.

Information counsel should gather before the engagement. A productive expert engagement requires the complete loan file if a lender is involved, all appraisals and broker opinions of value, the executed lease and all amendments, any rent roll or estoppel certificates, correspondence between parties regarding financing, any term sheets or commitment letters issued or rejected, and the property's operating history including actual expense statements. The earlier in the litigation this information is assembled, the more precise and defensible the expert analysis will be.

Use of expert testimony. In trial settings, a CRE financing expert provides opinion testimony on financing feasibility, industry standards, and the connection between specific lease or property characteristics and specific financing outcomes. In Chapter 11 plan confirmation hearings, expert testimony on plan feasibility frequently addresses whether projected refinancing of secured debt is achievable at the stated LTV, DSCR, and market conditions. In arbitration proceedings, the expert report often serves as the primary vehicle and may substitute for live testimony, making the precision and documentation of the underlying analysis critically important.

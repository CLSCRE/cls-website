Expert Witness: Commercial Loan Origination Standards in Syracuse, NY

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Lender Liability and Loan Origination Standards

The Syracuse Market Context

Syracuse occupies a distinctive position in the Upstate New York commercial real estate landscape. It is not a gateway market, but it is not a tertiary market either. It sits in a category that sophisticated lenders and underwriters classify as a secondary market with strong institutional fundamentals in select asset classes and meaningful credit risk in others. That distinction matters enormously in lender liability and loan origination disputes, because the standard of care for a commercial mortgage broker or lender operating in Syracuse is calibrated to what a reasonably competent professional would do in this market, not what a similarly situated professional would do in Manhattan or Buffalo.

The multifamily sector in Syracuse has demonstrated genuine resilience over the past several years. Cap rates for stabilized, well-located apartment assets in submarkets such as the University Hill corridor, Armory Square, and the Near Eastside have compressed into the 6.0% to 7.5% range for institutional-quality product, with value-add plays in secondary neighborhoods transacting in the 7.5% to 9.0% range. Vacancy across the broader metropolitan statistical area hovers in the 4% to 6% band for conventional multifamily, underpinned by enrollment-driven demand from Syracuse University, SUNY Upstate Medical University, and Le Moyne College. That student and medical professional renter base creates a demand floor that many purely economically driven secondary markets lack.

The industrial sector tells a different story. Onondaga County and its surrounding logistics corridors have benefited from the broader national reshoring and e-commerce distribution buildout, though the scale of Syracuse's industrial market is modest compared to Albany or Rochester. Vacancy rates for functional warehouse and light industrial space in the DeWitt and Salina submarkets have tightened to roughly 4% to 6%, with asking rents for Class B distribution space running in the $5.00 to $7.50 per square foot range net. These fundamentals are supportable but not exceptional, which affects how lenders size industrial loans here. Office, by contrast, presents serious underwriting challenges. Downtown Syracuse office vacancy has been persistently elevated above 15%, with some submarkets reporting effective vacancy, including shadow space, well above 20%. Any financing dispute involving an office asset in Syracuse requires that market reality to be front and center in the expert's analysis.

The retail sector is bifurcated in ways that directly affect lender liability analysis. Necessity-anchored retail, particularly grocery-anchored centers with strong lease covenants, remains financeable with conventional lenders. Unanchored strip retail and regional mall-adjacent properties face a deeply skeptical lending environment, and any broker or lender who treated such assets as straightforward collateral during the origination period under dispute almost certainly departed from market standards of care.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing the plaintiff in a commercial real estate financing dispute arising from a loan origination on a mixed-use multifamily and ground-floor retail asset located in a transitional submarket of Syracuse. CLS CRE was engaged to provide expert testimony under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 26, with a mandate covering three core questions: first, whether the loan origination process, including the broker's property analysis, income projections, and lender placement, met the industry standard of care applicable to a commercial mortgage professional operating in a secondary market like Syracuse; second, whether the loan terms disclosed to the borrower at application and at closing were consistent with market practice for this asset class and risk profile; and third, what a reasonably competent commercial mortgage broker would have done differently during the underwriting and placement process that might have either averted the dispute or produced materially better loan terms for the borrower.

The asset was a mid-size multifamily building with approximately 48 residential units above a small ground-floor commercial bay. The property was partially stabilized at the time of loan origination, with residential occupancy in the low-to-mid 80% range and the commercial space vacant. The borrower had engaged a commercial mortgage broker to source permanent financing to take out a construction loan nearing maturity. The alleged failures centered on the broker's income analysis, the selection of a lender whose credit box was arguably misaligned with the property's current stabilization status, and the adequacy of disclosures regarding prepayment structures and recourse provisions.

The Financial Analysis

The first analytical task in any lender liability engagement is reconstructing the underwriting as it should have been performed, using market-standard methodology, and comparing that to what the originating broker or lender actually did. This comparison is not an academic exercise. It quantifies the gap between competent practice and the alleged failure, and it anchors expert testimony to objective financial benchmarks rather than subjective judgment.

For this asset, I began with a reconstruction of stabilized net operating income. The property's 48 residential units carried a weighted average in-place rent of approximately $1,050 per month at origination, consistent with Class B multifamily rents in the subject submarket of Syracuse at the relevant time. Gross potential residential income therefore ran approximately $604,800 annually. A market-standard vacancy and credit loss allowance for this submarket and stabilization level would be 7% to 8%, reflecting the property's occupancy trending below stabilized and the historical performance of comparable assets in the corridor. Applying 7.5% yields effective gross residential income of approximately $559,440. Operating expenses for a property of this vintage and management profile in Syracuse, including taxes, insurance, utilities, maintenance, management fees at 6% of EGI, and a capital reserve of $300 per unit annually, appropriately totaled approximately $235,000. The resulting stabilized residential NOI was approximately $324,440.

The vacant commercial bay presented a judgment call that sits at the center of the origination dispute. A reasonably competent broker in Syracuse would have known that the local retail market for small commercial bays in transitional mixed-use properties was soft, with meaningful lease-up risk. Market standard practice when underwriting mixed-use collateral with vacant commercial space is to either exclude that income entirely from stabilized NOI or apply a dark-value assumption, meaning no credit for the vacant space in the base case, with upside considered separately. The originating broker, however, had included a projected commercial rent of approximately $24,000 annually in the stabilized income model, based on an aggressive asking-rent assumption that had no basis in executed comparable leases for similar spaces in the submarket. Stripping that income, as a competent underwriter should have, reduced the supportable NOI to the residential component only.

Using a market-standard debt service coverage ratio requirement of 1.25x, which is the floor commonly applied by agency lenders, regional banks, and life companies for multifamily assets with any lease-up risk, and applying a 30-year amortization schedule at a note rate of 6.75% (consistent with market pricing for non-agency multifamily in Syracuse at the relevant origination date), the maximum supportable loan against a competently derived NOI of $324,440 works as follows:

Annual debt service at the maximum loan is equal to NOI divided by the minimum DSCR, or $324,440 divided by 1.25, yielding a maximum annual debt service of $259,552. At a 6.75% rate on a 30-year amortization, the monthly mortgage constant is approximately 0.006490 per dollar of loan balance, or an annual constant of 0.07788. The maximum supportable loan is therefore $259,552 divided by 0.07788, equal to approximately $3,333,000. Assuming a competently supported as-stabilized value in the $4.8 million to $5.0 million range based on a 6.75% to 7.0% capitalization rate applied to the $324,440 NOI, the loan-to-value ratio on the maximum supportable loan is approximately 67% to 69%, comfortably within the 75% LTV ceiling that agency and conventional lenders apply to this asset type, but materially below the loan amount the originating broker had represented as achievable. The gap between what the broker represented and what the numbers actually supported, properly derived, was the financial core of the liability analysis.

The Complicating Factors

Partial stabilization and its effect on lender universe. A property in the low-to-mid 80% occupancy range is not stabilized by the standards of any institutional lender category. Agency programs through Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac require a minimum of 90% occupancy for a specified look-back period, typically 90 days. Life insurance companies and CMBS platforms typically require the same. A broker placing a loan on a partially stabilized multifamily asset in Syracuse must accurately represent the stabilization status to any proposed lender and must steer the borrower toward lenders whose credit boxes accommodate lease-up risk. Bridge lenders, balance sheet banks with construction-to-permanent programs, and debt funds are the appropriate placement targets. Placing a borrower in a permanent loan structure against a partially stabilized asset, or misrepresenting stabilization status to achieve a placement, is a clear departure from professional standards.

The vacant commercial component and mixed-use complexity. Ground-floor retail in a transitional Syracuse submarket creates structural underwriting complexity that a competent broker must address directly in the analysis and in lender communications. Lenders handling mixed-use collateral apply a haircut to commercial income under any circumstance, but when the space is vacant, most lenders will not underwrite any value to it at all in the base case. Failing to communicate this risk to the borrower, or using projected commercial income to bridge a gap between the property's actual debt service capacity and the desired loan size, constitutes a material misrepresentation in the underwriting process.

Prepayment structures and secondary market pricing. Syracuse multifamily assets at this size point are frequently placed with community banks or regional institutions that retain the loan on their balance sheet. These lenders typically use yield maintenance or step-down prepayment structures, sometimes with prepayment windows as long as five to seven years. A borrower who intends to refinance or sell within that window faces potentially severe prepayment penalties. The disclosure of prepayment terms and their economic consequences is an affirmative professional obligation of the originating broker. Failure to walk the borrower through the prepayment math, in writing, at or before application, is a departure from standard care regardless of whether the terms appear in the loan documents.

Recourse and carveout liability. At the loan sizes typical for this asset type in Syracuse, most balance sheet lenders include full or partial recourse provisions or broad bad-boy carveout guarantees that can convert to full recourse upon defined triggering events. Sophisticated borrowers may accept these provisions knowingly; many do not understand them. The broker's obligation includes explaining, in clear terms, the recourse structure of any proposed financing and its practical implications for the guarantor. Expert analysis in this dispute included a review of the broker's file for evidence of any written disclosure or discussion of recourse provisions, and the absence of documentation on this point was significant.

The Syracuse Lender Market

Understanding the actual lender market for a given asset type in Syracuse at the time of origination is essential to the expert's analysis. The question of what a reasonably competent broker should have done is inseparable from knowing what lenders were actually available, at what terms, and with what credit requirements.

For stabilized or near-stabilized multifamily in Syracuse, the agency execution through Fannie Mae's small balance loan program or Freddie Mac's Optigo platform historically represents the most aggressive pricing and highest leverage available, typically up to 80% LTV on fully stabilized product at spreads of 150 to 200 basis points over the corresponding treasury. However, the occupancy and mixed-use complexities in this engagement eliminated agency as a viable execution without a cure period, a fact a competent broker must acknowledge in writing before accepting engagement and certainly before placing an application.

Regional banks headquartered in or active in the Central New York market, including institutions with significant Upstate New York community bank footprints, represent the next tier. These lenders are active in the $2 million to $10 million loan range, price on a spread to prime or to their internal cost of funds, and apply conservative DSCR floors of 1.20x to 1.30x. They will often hold mixed-use collateral but apply a significant discount to commercial income and require full recourse or a robust guaranty. Their loan-to-value limits on mixed-use run 65% to 70% in practice, even when stated policy suggests 75%.

Debt funds and bridge lenders active in secondary markets like Syracuse have grown their presence over the past five years, driven by borrowers who cannot access conventional execution due to stabilization gaps. These lenders price at higher spreads, typically SOFR plus 300 to 500 basis points, with origination fees of 1% to 2%, and they underwrite to exit assumptions rather than in-place cash flow. A competent broker managing a partially stabilized mixed-use asset should have presented bridge-to-permanent financing as a realistic and properly disclosed option, even if it was not the preferred outcome.

What Attorneys Handling Syracuse CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to retain a CRE financing expert. If the dispute turns on any of the following questions, a financing expert is not optional: whether the loan could have been obtained on better terms; whether the originating broker or lender followed market standards in underwriting or placement; whether the borrower was misled about achievable loan amounts, terms, or lender requirements; whether a financing contingency was satisfied or waived improperly; or whether a lender's internal underwriting process departed from industry norms in a way that caused harm. These are not questions an appraiser, an accountant, or a general real estate broker can answer with authority.

What a CRE financing expert can answer that an appraiser cannot. An appraiser establishes value. A CRE financing expert establishes what the financing market would actually do with a given asset at a given point in time, at what terms, from which lender categories, and under what conditions. These are operationally distinct questions. A lender liability case that relies solely on appraisal testimony is missing the half of the analysis that explains the actual mechanism of harm.

Qualification standards for a CRE financing expert. Relevant expertise requires active, current involvement in commercial loan origination and placement, not historical banking experience. A retired commercial banker who has not originated a loan in five years lacks current knowledge of market pricing, lender credit boxes, and brokerage standards of care. The qualified expert should have documented transaction volume across the relevant asset classes, active lender relationships in the relevant geography, and prior experience providing expert testimony in financing disputes. At CLS CRE, our approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, 1,000-plus active lender relationships across 50 states, and prior Rule 26 engagements provide the foundation for credible, current expert testimony.

Information counsel should gather before engaging the expert. The expert needs the complete loan origination file, including the broker's engagement letter, pitch materials, lender application packages, term sheets received and declined, all written communications between broker and borrower, the executed loan documents, and any internal credit memoranda from the lender. The expert also needs market data for the relevant time period, which our firm maintains independently and can verify through third-party sources. Do not wait to gather this material. The expert's opinion is only as strong as the underlying documentation.

How expert testimony is used. In trials involving lender liability or broker negligence, expert testimony on financing standards establishes the benchmark against which the defendant's conduct is measured. In bankruptcy plan confirmation hearings where feasibility of exit financing is at issue, the expert provides the evidentiary foundation for whether a refinance or sale is realistically achievable. In arbitration proceedings between borrowers and brokers, the expert quantifies the economic damage caused by the origination failure by calculating the difference between what competent brokerage would have produced and what the borrower actually received.

Key Takeaways

  • Syracuse is a secondary market with differentiated submarket fundamentals. Multifamily and industrial can support institutional financing; office and non-anchored retail face a deeply constrained lender universe. Expert analysis must be calibrated to the specific submarket and asset class, not to regional averages.
  • Standard of care in loan origination is an objective, documented benchmark. A reasonably competent commercial mortgage broker follows specific analytical protocols for NOI derivation, DSCR testing at 1.20x to 1.25x minimums, LTV constraint application, and lender selection. Departure from any of these is demonstrable and quantifiable.
  • Inflated income assumptions are a primary vector for lender liability exposure. Including speculative or unsupported income, whether from vacant commercial space, projected lease-up, or above-market rent assumptions, in a stabilized NOI model is a material misrepresentation that a competent expert can isolate and calculate precisely.

Expert Witness: CRE Partnership and Waterfall Disputes in Redding, CA

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Joint Venture and Partnership Dispute

The Redding Market Context

Redding, California sits at the northern end of the Sacramento Valley, roughly 160 miles north of Sacramento, and it functions as the commercial hub for Shasta County and the broader North State region. For attorneys and investors unfamiliar with the market's financing dynamics, Redding is not a tier-one institutional market, but it is far from illiquid. The city supports a legitimate commercial real estate ecosystem anchored by healthcare, government, retail trade, and a growing logistics and light industrial base tied to Interstate 5 corridor activity. Understanding those fundamentals is essential to any joint venture dispute involving waterfall distributions, because the asset performance assumptions embedded in a JV operating agreement are only credible when measured against what the Redding market actually supports.

Industrial and flex product along the Airport Road and Dana Drive corridors has seen sustained occupancy pressure driven by regional distribution demand. Vacancy in the Redding industrial submarket has historically tracked in the 4% to 7% range, tighter than many investors assume for a secondary California market, and triple-net rents for functional warehouse product have moved from the low-to-mid $0.60s per square foot per month into the $0.75 to $0.85 range for quality product with clear heights above 24 feet. Multifamily fundamentals in Redding have remained reasonably strong due to chronic undersupply relative to population growth, with stabilized apartment cap rates in the 5.25% to 6.25% range depending on vintage and unit mix, and average market rents in the $1,200 to $1,600 range for two-bedroom units as of recent market data. Retail vacancy along South Market Street and the Hilltop Drive corridor has normalized after pandemic disruptions, with grocery-anchored and service-retail centers outperforming power-center formats. Office fundamentals remain the weakest category, with suburban office vacancy elevated above 15% in several pockets and net absorption negative in recent years.

These market-specific fundamentals matter enormously in a joint venture dispute. When a JV agreement sets a preferred return based on projected stabilized NOI, or when a promote threshold is triggered by an IRR hurdle derived from original underwriting, the validity of those projections depends directly on what the Redding market was actually delivering at the time of underwriting versus what it delivered at disposition or refinance. A joint venture dispute that appears on the surface to be a contract interpretation problem is, at its financial core, a dispute about whether the income stream, the debt terms, and the exit assumptions embedded in the waterfall model were reasonable and correctly applied.

The Engagement

Counsel representing the equity partner in a commercial real estate joint venture dispute retained Commercial Lending Solutions to provide expert analysis of the financing structure underlying the dispute and the waterfall mechanics that governed distributions between the parties. The asset at issue was a stabilized multifamily property in the Redding submarket, acquired by a JV entity comprising a passive equity investor and an operating partner serving as the sponsor and asset manager. The JV agreement included a tiered waterfall with a preferred return to the equity investor, followed by a return of capital, followed by a promoted interest split favoring the operating partner above a defined IRR hurdle.

The core legal and financial questions before the court centered on three issues. First, whether the preferred return had been correctly calculated on a cumulative, compounding basis as the equity investor maintained, or on a simple, non-cumulative basis as the operating partner calculated it. Second, whether the refinancing proceeds distributed partway through the hold period constituted a capital event triggering a partial waterfall recalculation, or whether they were properly classified as operating distributions not subject to the waterfall sequence. Third, whether the promoted interest entitlement claimed by the operating partner at disposition was correctly calculated given that the refinance had altered the equity basis and the unreturned capital balance used as the denominator in the IRR calculation. CLS CRE was retained to analyze the financing structure, model the correct waterfall outcomes under competing interpretations, and render opinions on what constitutes market-standard JV agreement mechanics for this asset type and market.

The Financial Analysis

The first step in any credible expert analysis of a JV waterfall dispute is establishing the correct income stream. I began with a reconstruction of the property's net operating income trajectory from acquisition through disposition, using actual rent rolls, operating statements, and the refinancing appraisal as inputs. The property was acquired at a basis consistent with a stabilized going-in cap rate of approximately 5.75%, which was defensible for Redding multifamily at the time. At acquisition, the property was generating approximately $780,000 in gross potential rent with a physical occupancy rate of 93%, producing an effective gross income of roughly $725,400 after vacancy and credit loss. Operating expenses including property management, maintenance reserves, insurance, taxes, and utilities ran approximately 38% of effective gross income, consistent with market norms for a property of this vintage in Shasta County. This produced a stabilized NOI at acquisition of approximately $449,700.

The acquisition debt was a conventional agency-eligible multifamily loan at a fixed rate of 4.75% over a 30-year amortization schedule with a 10-year term, sized at $5.4 million against a purchase price of $7.8 million, reflecting an initial LTV of 69.2%. Debt service on this loan was approximately $338,400 annually. The debt service coverage ratio at acquisition was 1.33x, which exceeded the lender's minimum threshold of 1.25x and provided approximately $111,300 in annual cash flow available for distribution before reserve requirements. That 1.25x DSCR threshold is standard market practice for agency multifamily lenders including Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, and FHA MAP programs, all of which are active in markets like Redding that meet population and unit thresholds for program eligibility.

The refinancing that occurred in year four of the hold was the central event in dispute. By that point, the property had undergone a partial value-add renovation of approximately 40 units, pushing market rents to an average of $1,385 per month for renovated units versus $1,080 for unrenovated units. Blended occupancy had risen to 96%. The reconstructed NOI at the time of refinancing was approximately $567,000. At the prevailing rate environment at that time, a conventional multifamily bridge-to-agency or agency permanent loan was priced in the 5.25% to 5.50% range. Applying a 5.375% rate on a 30-year amortization schedule, the maximum supportable loan at a 1.25x DSCR constraint was approximately $7.1 million. The lender actually sized the loan at $6.85 million, reflecting their own LTV constraint of 75% against an appraised value of $9.13 million. The refinancing generated net proceeds after payoff of the original $5.4 million loan and closing costs of approximately $1.28 million.

The critical analytical question was how those $1.28 million in net refinancing proceeds flowed through the waterfall. Under the equity investor's interpretation, any capital event above a defined threshold triggered a full waterfall recalculation, requiring first a catch-up of accrued preferred return, then a return of unreturned capital before any residual was split. Under the operating partner's interpretation, the refinancing proceeds were excess cash flow not triggering the capital event provision because the property had not been sold and the distribution did not exceed the original equity contribution. My analysis modeled both scenarios with granular cash flow projections, applying the correct compounding mechanics to the preferred return calculation. The difference in outcomes between the two interpretations was approximately $390,000 in favor of the equity investor under the correct cumulative compounding methodology, which aligned with the plain language of the agreement and with market-standard JV waterfall construction.

The Complicating Factors

Preferred return compounding methodology. One of the most frequently litigated issues in JV waterfall disputes is whether the preferred return compounds or accrues simply, and whether any accrued but unpaid preferred return from prior periods earns a return in subsequent periods. Market-standard institutional JV agreements, including those modeled on NMHC, NAIOP, and major private equity sponsor templates, almost universally provide for a compounding preferred return where unpaid accruals compound at the same rate as the base preferred return. An 8% preferred return on a $2.4 million equity contribution that goes unpaid for three years does not simply equal $576,000 in accrued preferred return. Compounded annually, the accrued balance grows to approximately $614,000. That $38,000 difference may seem modest in isolation, but it directly affects the hurdle calculation and therefore the promote entitlement. In this engagement, the operating partner had been calculating preferred return on a simple, non-compounding basis without contractual support for that methodology, and had been distributing operating cash flow to himself as a return of capital rather than routing it correctly through the waterfall sequence.

Refinancing proceeds classification. Whether a refinancing distribution constitutes a "capital event" or an "operating distribution" under a JV agreement is not merely a contract interpretation question; it has a direct impact on how lenders view the underlying financing and on what distribution mechanics the parties had available to them. Most institutional JV agreements define a capital event to include any sale, refinancing, or recapitalization that returns proceeds to partners. If the agreement in dispute was market-standard in this respect, the refinancing proceeds should have been subject to the full waterfall. The operating partner's argument that the proceeds were operating distributions was inconsistent with how any institutional lender or JV practitioner would classify a $1.28 million cash-out refinancing distribution. This mattered not only as a contract matter but as a financing expert matter: I was able to testify that in my experience originating and structuring over approximately $1 billion in commercial real estate transactions, I have never seen a market-standard institutional JV agreement treat refinancing proceeds as ordinary operating distributions exempt from the waterfall sequence.

IRR hurdle calculation with mid-hold distributions. When a JV executes a refinancing mid-hold and distributes proceeds to partners, those distributions must be properly credited in the IRR calculation used to determine whether the promote threshold has been crossed at disposition. The IRR calculation is highly sensitive to the timing of cash flows. A $1.28 million distribution in year four, credited against the equity investor's unreturned capital, effectively reduces the remaining capital basis and accelerates the time-weighted return. If the operating partner applied the promote at disposition without correctly accounting for the mid-hold distribution in the IRR waterfall model, the promote entitlement was overstated. In this case, the operating partner had applied the promote based on a simple equity multiple rather than a time-weighted IRR calculation, which is inconsistent with market-standard promoted interest structures and produced a materially different outcome than what the agreement's IRR hurdle language required.

The Redding Lender Market

Understanding which lenders are active in Redding and what their credit parameters are is essential not only for financing execution but for establishing the financing assumptions embedded in any JV underwriting model. When I render opinions about whether a JV's original financing assumptions were reasonable, I am drawing directly on my knowledge of actual lender behavior in this market.

Agency lenders, specifically Fannie Mae DUS lenders and Freddie Mac Optigo lenders, are active in the Redding multifamily market for properties meeting the minimum unit thresholds, typically 5 or more units for small balance programs and 50 or more for standard programs. These programs price at spreads over the 10-year Treasury and offer fixed-rate terms with 30-year amortization. For a stabilized Redding multifamily property with demonstrated NOI support, agency execution is typically the best available debt terms, with LTVs up to 80% on Freddie Mac conventional and rates that were in the 6.0% to 6.5% range as of late 2024 for 10-year fixed execution. The 1.25x DSCR floor is a hard constraint; lenders will size to the lesser of LTV and DSCR.

Community and regional banks remain important for commercial deals that fall outside agency parameters, including mixed-use properties, smaller commercial projects, and value-add deals mid-renovation. National Bank of Commerce, North Valley Bank (now part of Tri Counties Bank), and several Sacramento-based community banks have historically been active in Shasta County. These lenders typically price at spreads over the prime rate or SOFR for floating-rate construction and bridge products, with 5-year fixed terms available for stabilized product. Their DSCR floors tend to mirror agency standards at 1.20x to 1.25x, with LTVs capped at 70% to 75% for most commercial property types. For industrial and retail product in Redding, community banks remain the primary execution source given the absence of deep CMBS liquidity for sub-$5 million loans in secondary California markets.

Life insurance companies and CMBS conduit lenders are intermittently active in Redding but typically require minimum loan sizes of $5 million to $7 million and strong in-place NOI with long-term lease structures. For a Redding multifamily or light industrial deal, CMBS is viable at the right size but less competitive on rate and flexibility than agency or community bank execution. Debt funds and bridge lenders are active for value-add plays, typically pricing at 200 to 350 basis points over SOFR with 65% to 70% LTC constraints and 18- to 36-month terms. These are relevant for any JV dispute involving a value-add business plan where the original financing assumptions must be evaluated for reasonableness.

What Attorneys Handling Redding CRE Disputes Need to Know

Retaining the right expert in a JV waterfall or promoted interest dispute requires understanding the distinction between a commercial real estate appraisal expert and a commercial real estate financing and capital markets expert. An appraiser can testify about property value, cap rate derivation, and market rent levels. A CRE financing expert can testify about something entirely different: how the capital stack was constructed, whether the debt terms embedded in the JV's original underwriting model were achievable in the market at the relevant time, how lenders actually size proceeds and set covenants, and whether the waterfall mechanics applied by a party are consistent with market-standard JV structuring practice. These are separate disciplines requiring separate expert qualifications.

A qualified CRE financing expert should have direct, current experience originating and structuring commercial real estate debt and equity transactions at scale, not merely advisory or academic experience. The expert should be able to speak to lender credit standards from the perspective of someone who regularly negotiates term sheets, satisfies credit committees, and closes transactions. At CLS CRE, our approximately $1 billion in transaction volume and over 1,000 active lender relationships across 50 states gives us the market depth to render opinions grounded in current, specific market practice rather than textbook theory.

Attorneys should gather the following information before engaging a CRE financing expert in a JV waterfall dispute: the full JV operating agreement including all amendments and side letters; all capital contribution records and distribution histories; the original acquisition underwriting model and any subsequent reforecast models; all loan documents for acquisition financing and any subsequent refinancing; the appraisal or broker opinion of value used to support any refinancing; rent rolls and operating statements for the full hold period; and all communications between the parties regarding waterfall calculations or distribution methodologies.

At trial or in arbitration, CRE financing expert testimony is most effectively used to establish what market-standard JV structures look like, to demonstrate through financial modeling the difference in outcomes under competing interpretations of the waterfall, and to rebut the opposing party's own expert or lay witness testimony about what the financing terms permitted or required. In depositions, counsel should use the financing expert to lock in the opposing party's methodology and expose deviations from market practice. Written expert reports under Rule 26 should include a detailed financial model showing the waterfall under each interpretation with clearly labeled assumptions, sensitivity tables for key variables including preferred return rate and IRR hurdle, and a market standard opinion supported by specific deal references and lender market data.

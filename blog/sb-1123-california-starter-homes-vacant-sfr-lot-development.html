SB 1123 Explained: How California Developers Can Build Up to 10 Starter Homes on a Single Vacant Lot

By Trevor Damyan May 22, 2026

California's housing supply crisis has been grinding developers and their capital partners for years. Entitlement risk, neighborhood opposition, and restrictive zoning have all conspired to keep infill sites underutilized. SB 1123 changes the math on one of the most overlooked asset classes in the state: the vacant single-family lot.

What SB 1123 Actually Does

SB 1123 is a California statute that allows by-right development of up to 10 starter homes on a vacant lot that is currently zoned for a single-family residence. "By-right" is the operative phrase here. It means that if your project meets the objective standards spelled out in the law, the local jurisdiction cannot require a discretionary hearing, cannot impose a design review that functions as a de facto denial, and cannot sit on your application past the statutory clock. The entitlement process that used to take 18 to 36 months on a contested project can now be compressed dramatically for qualifying sites.

The legislature's stated intent is to accelerate production of ownership-attainable housing in established residential neighborhoods, targeting the missing-middle price point where demand vastly outstrips supply across the state.

Which Projects Qualify

Not every vacant lot in a residential zone automatically qualifies. The statute lays out a specific set of objective standards that a project must satisfy to invoke by-right protection. Key qualifying criteria include:

  • Lot must be vacant. The statute applies to sites that do not currently have a habitable structure. Lots with an existing dwelling, even a dilapidated one, fall under a separate regulatory framework.
  • Underlying zoning must be single-family residential. The lot does not need to be rezoned. SB 1123 operates as a state-level overlay that supersedes local single-family exclusivity.
  • Up to 10 units permitted. Density is capped at 10 starter homes per qualifying parcel. Unit count is a ceiling, not a floor, so a developer can build fewer units and still invoke by-right protections.
  • Objective design standards apply. Local jurisdictions retain the right to enforce objective standards related to setbacks, height, lot coverage, and similar bulk controls, provided those standards do not effectively preclude the maximum density the law permits.
  • Affordability components may be triggered. Depending on project size and local inclusionary requirements, a portion of units could be subject to affordability restrictions. Confirm with counsel before locking in your proforma.

Before committing capital to a site, a thorough legal review of the specific parcel against SB 1123's criteria is non-negotiable. The statute is new, local implementation is uneven, and some municipalities are already testing the limits of what constitutes a valid objective standard.

How to Underwrite the Play

The underwriting framework for an SB 1123 project differs from a standard infill condo or townhome deal in a few important ways.

Land basis matters more than usual. Because these projects are concentrated on single-family lots, the land cost is typically lower than a comparable multifamily site. A well-located vacant lot that traded as a single-family teardown candidate can suddenly support 8 to 10 for-sale units. Run your land basis against a per-unit cost model, not a per-square-foot model. If your all-in land cost per unit is below replacement cost by a meaningful margin, the deal has structural upside.

Construction cost is the swing variable. Starter homes at this density are almost always built using wood-frame or modular construction. Hard cost estimates should be locked against current labor and materials markets. The cost environment in California has been volatile. Conservative underwriting assumes no GMP until permits are in hand.

Absorption pace drives your return profile. Ten units in an infill neighborhood is a manageable absorption scenario in most markets, but you need to stress-test it. Model 2 to 3 closings per month as a base case. If your proforma requires faster absorption to hit projected returns, you are taking on more risk than the deal may warrant.

Comparable sales analysis must be hyperlocal. Starter home pricing in one submarket can diverge substantially from a neighborhood three miles away. Pull comps within a tight radius and apply a discount for new construction premium uncertainty in a market that has not seen this product type before.

Financing the Deal: Bridge, Construction, and Permanent

SB 1123 projects fit neatly into a three-phase capital stack that most experienced developers will recognize.

Land acquisition bridge. If you are moving quickly on a site, a private bridge lender can fund the land acquisition before construction financing is in place. Expect terms in the range of 12 months with extension options, interest-only, and leverage governed by the lender's view of as-complete value rather than current land value. Speed of execution is the primary advantage here. A well-capitalized private bridge lender can close in 5 to 10 business days on a clean deal.

Construction financing. Once permits are issued, a regional bank or a construction lender with an appetite for for-sale residential product is the natural fit. Construction loan sizing is typically benchmarked to a percentage of total project cost, often in the 65 to 75 percent range depending on sponsor strength and market. Interest reserves are built into the loan. Draw schedules follow construction progress. The by-right entitlement protection under SB 1123 meaningfully reduces the entitlement risk that lenders typically price into their spread, which should translate to more competitive construction loan terms for qualifying projects.

Permanent or takeout financing. For a for-sale product, the permanent financing question is largely answered by end-buyer mortgage programs. However, if a developer elects to hold some units as rentals, a life insurance company or agency lender becomes relevant at stabilization. In that scenario, the exit underwriting needs to be built into the deal from day one.

Timeline Considerations

By-right does not mean instant. A realistic timeline for an SB 1123 project, assuming a clean site and a cooperative jurisdiction, runs roughly as follows:

  • Site control to permit submittal: 60 to 90 days for design, engineering, and application preparation.
  • Permit review: SB 1123's ministerial review process is subject to statutory timelines. A complete application for a project of this scale should receive a determination within 60 days under state law, though local practice varies.
  • Construction: 8 to 14 months for a 10-unit wood-frame project depending on scope and site conditions.
  • Absorption: 3 to 6 months to close out 10 units at a measured pace.

Total cycle time from site control to final closing is realistically 18 to 30 months. Build your carry costs and interest reserves around that range.

The CLS CRE Perspective

SB 1123 is the kind of legislation that creates a genuine first-mover advantage. The developers who build systems around sourcing qualifying vacant lots, assembling a repeatable design and construction process, and lining up capital partners ahead of the deal flow will outperform developers who treat each project as a one-off. This is a volume play disguised as a single-site opportunity.

From a capital markets standpoint, the by-right entitlement protection is a real credit enhancement. It removes the binary risk of a discretionary denial that keeps many lenders on the sideline for infill residential projects. We are already having conversations with lenders across the bridge, construction, and balance-sheet categories who are actively looking to deploy capital into SB 1123-eligible projects.

If you are a developer, a general contractor with a development arm, or a family office evaluating California residential opportunities, this statute deserves serious attention right now, before competition for qualifying sites drives land basis to a level that compresses returns.

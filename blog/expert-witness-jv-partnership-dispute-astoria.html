Expert Witness: CRE Partnership and Waterfall Disputes in Astoria, OR

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Joint Venture and Partnership Dispute

The Astoria Market Context

Astoria, Oregon sits at the mouth of the Columbia River in Clatsop County, and while it is not a primary institutional market like Portland, it carries a distinct commercial real estate profile that creates meaningful complexity in joint venture structures and financing arrangements. The market is driven by a mix of maritime commerce, tourism, light industrial activity, and a growing residential base that has attracted outside capital over the past decade. That outside capital is precisely where disputes originate: when Portland, Seattle, or California-based equity partners enter into JV structures with local operators to acquire hospitality, mixed-use, or waterfront commercial assets in Astoria, the parties often negotiate distribution waterfalls under fundamentally different assumptions about what returns the market can support.

Multifamily assets in Astoria have historically traded at cap rates in the 6.25% to 7.50% range, reflecting the market's secondary status and limited liquidity relative to the Willamette Valley corridor. Commercial mixed-use properties along the Historic District waterfront have compressed in value as tourism demand increased, but that compression is uneven and heavily tenant-dependent. Net lease industrial along the port corridor has been among the more stable asset classes, with vacancy rates holding below 5% for functional warehouse and light industrial space. These fundamentals matter in a JV dispute because preferred return thresholds, promoted interest trigger points, and waterfall mechanics are all calibrated against projected cash flow performance, and if the underwriting embedded in the original operating agreement did not reflect what the Astoria market could realistically deliver, that disconnect becomes the core of the litigation.

Joint venture disputes in secondary and tertiary Oregon coastal markets like Astoria are complicated further by thin transaction comps, limited institutional lender presence, and the fact that many of these deals were originally financed through community banks or credit unions with non-standard loan structures. When the financing terms deviate from what a major market institutional lender would have required, the resulting debt service constraints create waterfall mechanics that may have been perfectly standard at origination but become contested under stress. Understanding how those financing terms shaped the economics available to each party requires someone who actively works the lender market in these communities, not just an academic analyst reviewing documents from a distance.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing the limited partner in a joint venture dispute involving a waterfront mixed-use commercial property in the Astoria submarket. The asset combined ground-floor retail and restaurant space with upper-floor hospitality or short-term accommodation units, a configuration common along the Astoria riverfront where adaptive reuse of historic structures has driven a number of these mixed-use JV investments. The general partner had served as the operating member and was responsible for both property management and distribution calculations under the joint venture operating agreement.

The core legal and financial questions presented to CLS CRE were threefold. First, counsel needed an expert opinion on whether the preferred return had been correctly calculated on a cumulative, compounding basis as specified in the operating agreement, or whether the general partner's distributions had improperly treated the preferred return as simple and non-cumulative. Second, counsel needed analysis of whether the promoted interest, commonly called the carry or the promote, had been triggered at the correct waterfall tier given the actual cash flow generated by the property and the actual debt service obligations imposed by the financing. Third, and critically, counsel needed expert testimony on how the original capital stack structure, specifically the loan-to-value ratio, debt service coverage covenants, and cash management provisions in the senior loan, had directly constrained the cash available for distribution at each waterfall tier.

This third question is where a CRE financing expert adds value that neither an appraiser nor a forensic accountant can fully provide. An appraiser can opine on value. An accountant can reconcile the numbers. But explaining how a specific lender's cash sweep mechanism or DSCR trigger covenant functionally prevented distributions that the limited partner believed were owed requires someone who understands how senior lenders actually document and enforce these provisions in commercial mortgage structures.

The Financial Analysis

Our analysis began with a reconstruction of the property's stabilized net operating income at the time of the first disputed distribution period. The asset's income stream came from three sources: base rent from the ground-floor commercial tenants, percentage rent tied to gross sales for the food and beverage operator, and room revenue or accommodation revenue from the upper-floor units. Each income stream carried different volatility, different lease term assumptions, and different treatment under the senior lender's underwriting. Lenders in this asset class typically haircut percentage rent and variable accommodation revenue significantly, often applying a 50% to 75% efficiency factor to non-base income when sizing the loan.

Working from the actual rent rolls, historical operating statements, and the original loan documents, we determined that the stabilized NOI supportable for financing purposes at origination was approximately $310,000 annually. This figure represented the lender's underwritten NOI, not the property's actual cash generation in a strong tourism year. That distinction is essential: the loan was sized against underwritten NOI, and the resulting debt service is a fixed obligation regardless of whether actual NOI in a given year exceeds or falls short of the underwritten figure.

Applying a market-standard DSCR of 1.25x, which is the minimum threshold most conventional commercial lenders and community banks require on mixed-use assets with variable income components in secondary markets, the maximum supportable annual debt service against $310,000 of underwritten NOI is $248,000. At a loan rate of 6.50% on a 25-year amortization schedule, which was consistent with the community bank pricing environment in coastal Oregon during the origination period, $248,000 of annual debt service supports a loan balance of approximately $3.1 million. If the property was acquired at a purchase price implying a value of $4.5 million, that produces an LTV of approximately 68.9%, which is consistent with what a conservative community bank or regional credit union would have required on this asset type in this market.

The significance of this analysis to the waterfall dispute is direct. The operating agreement contemplated distributions from available cash flow, defined in the agreement as NOI after debt service. In a year where actual NOI reached $380,000, available cash flow would appear to be $132,000 after servicing the loan. However, the senior loan included a DSCR maintenance covenant requiring that if trailing twelve-month DSCR fell below 1.20x on a lender-calculated basis, a cash management trigger would activate and all excess cash flow would be swept to a reserve account controlled by the lender. Our analysis demonstrated that the lender's DSCR calculation methodology, which applied a stressed interest rate for covenant testing purposes rather than the actual note rate, caused a sweep trigger to activate during two quarters that the general partner characterized as normal operating periods. During those quarters, distributions to both the preferred equity holder and the promoted interest tiers were mathematically impossible, not because the general partner failed to distribute, but because the loan structure prevented distribution. Separating those two explanations is the difference between a breach of the operating agreement and a force majeure-equivalent event driven by senior lender control.

The Complicating Factors

Cumulative vs. Simple Preferred Return Calculation. The most common source of waterfall disputes in secondary market JV structures is ambiguity or misapplication of the preferred return compounding methodology. A preferred return stated as "8% per annum" can mean very different things depending on whether it compounds on unpaid accruals, whether it is calculated on a daily, monthly, or annual basis, and whether the return is calculated on invested capital only or on invested capital plus accrued but unpaid preferred amounts. In this engagement, the operating agreement language was not explicit on compounding frequency. Over a five-year hold period, the difference between simple and compound treatment on a $2 million equity contribution at 8% is approximately $170,000, a figure material enough to drive litigation. Our role was to opine on what the market standard treatment would be for a JV of this type and capital size, which is a financing market expert question, not a legal interpretation question.

Promoted Interest Trigger Verification. The promote in this structure was tied to an IRR hurdle, specifically a 12% IRR to the limited partner on total invested capital. Calculating whether that hurdle was actually cleared requires reconstructing the precise timing of all capital contributions and all distributions, then applying a time-weighted return calculation. When the general partner calculated the IRR to determine whether the promote had been triggered, the methodology used did not account for a mid-period capital call that diluted the limited partner's basis. Our analysis showed that properly accounting for the timing and amount of that capital call reduced the calculated LP IRR from 12.4% to 11.6%, meaning the promote had not yet been triggered at the time the general partner began taking promoted distributions. The financing layer mattered here because the capital call itself was necessitated by a lender-required reserve funding obligation, and whether that obligation was foreseeable at origination speaks to how the waterfall should have been documented.

Lender Cash Sweep Mechanics and Distributable Cash. As described in the financial analysis section, DSCR-triggered cash sweeps are a standard provision in commercial mortgage structures but are poorly understood outside the lending community. Attorneys litigating waterfall disputes frequently assume that all NOI above debt service is freely distributable. In practice, senior lenders on commercial assets routinely impose lockboxes, cash management agreements, and reserve requirements that intercept cash before it reaches the borrower entity. The practical effect is that even when a property is performing, the distributable cash available to the JV can be zero for extended periods. Documenting when and why these mechanisms activated, and whether the general partner disclosed those constraints to the limited partner as required under fiduciary duty standards, is critical expert witness work.

Waterfront Asset Valuation Volatility in Astoria. Astoria's tourism-driven commercial corridor introduces seasonal NOI volatility that most institutional JV agreements are not specifically designed to handle. A property that generates 65% of its annual revenue in the May through September window will show dramatically different quarterly DSCR figures than a property with flat monthly income. When waterfall calculation periods are defined on a calendar quarter basis rather than a trailing twelve-month basis, seasonal properties in markets like Astoria can systematically trigger preferred return accruals in off-peak quarters while generating surplus cash in peak quarters that the general partner may be entitled to retain under the promote once IRR hurdles are cleared on an annualized calculation. This structural mismatch between the seasonality of actual cash flows and the periodicity of waterfall calculations is a specific and underappreciated source of LP-GP disputes in coastal Oregon markets.

The Astoria Lender Market

Astoria is served by a relatively thin but functional commercial lending market. National institutional lenders, CMBS conduits, and agency execution are largely absent for assets below $10 million in this submarket. The practical financing universe for mixed-use and hospitality-adjacent commercial assets in Astoria consists primarily of community banks headquartered in the Pacific Northwest, regional credit unions with commercial real estate programs, and occasionally portfolio lenders based in Portland or Seattle that will extend lending relationships into coastal Oregon for the right sponsorship and asset quality.

Local and regional community banks have historically been the most active senior lenders in this market. These institutions typically require 60% to 70% LTV on commercial mixed-use, personal guarantees from principals, and debt service coverage of 1.20x to 1.25x on underwritten NOI. Loan terms are generally five to seven years with 20- to 25-year amortization, and pricing has ranged from the mid-5% area to above 7% depending on the rate environment at origination. Importantly, these lenders frequently include idiosyncratic loan covenants that reflect the specific risk concerns of a Pacific Northwest community bank rather than standardized institutional language, including seasonal revenue tests, tourism-sector exposure limits, and borrower reporting requirements that vary significantly from one institution to the next.

SBA 504 financing has been used for owner-occupied commercial properties in Clatsop County, providing access to higher LTV structures (up to 90% combined) that would not otherwise be available in this market. However, the SBA structure introduces a second lien position with its own cash flow controls, creating capital stack complexity that directly affects how waterfalls can be documented and enforced in JV agreements. When the senior conventional lender and the SBA debenture holder each have claims on cash flow, the distributable cash waterfall for the JV equity can become extremely constrained.

Bridge lenders and debt funds will occasionally consider Astoria assets, particularly for value-add plays or stabilization transactions, but they require significantly higher returns and impose tighter operational covenants than community banks. For JV disputes involving bridge-financed assets, the presence of a PIK interest feature, exit fee, or extension fee in the bridge loan can significantly alter the capital stack economics that should have been reflected in waterfall projections at origination.

What Attorneys Handling Astoria CRE Disputes Need to Know

A CRE financing expert witness is not interchangeable with a commercial appraiser, a forensic accountant, or a retired banker serving as a factual witness. Each plays a different role. The appraiser establishes value. The accountant traces cash. The financing expert explains how the commercial lending market works, how specific loan structures constrain or enable the economics available to each party, and whether the financial terms embedded in a JV operating agreement were consistent with what the market was actually offering at the time the deal was done. These are distinct expert functions, and complex JV disputes in secondary markets like Astoria often require all three.

Attorneys should retain a CRE financing expert when any of the following conditions are present in the dispute: the litigation involves a waterfall calculation that depends on how distributable cash was defined relative to senior lender controls; a party is claiming that preferred return accruals or promote distributions were improperly calculated; the deal involves non-standard loan structures, including SBA programs, mezzanine layers, or DSCR-triggered cash management; or a party alleges that the other failed to pursue available refinancing options that would have unlocked trapped equity for distribution.

What makes a CRE financing expert qualified is active current market experience, not historical credentials. A retired bank credit officer can testify about how a specific institution once evaluated loans. An active commercial mortgage broker with a demonstrated record of closing transactions across the lender spectrum in the relevant market can testify about what the market actually required, what lenders were willing to do, how deals were structured in practice, and whether a given financing outcome was within or outside the range of what a reasonable market participant would have achieved. CLS CRE brings approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, over 1,000 active lender relationships, and practice across all 50 states to each engagement, which means our testimony about market standards is grounded in current deal experience rather than retrospective memory.

Before retaining a CRE financing expert, counsel should gather the following materials: the full executed JV operating agreement with all amendments, the loan documents including all exhibits, riders, and cash management agreements, all financial statements and rent rolls produced during the hold period, all capital account statements and distribution records, any correspondence between the GP and the lender regarding DSCR compliance or covenant waivers, and any appraisals or broker opinions of value obtained during the hold period. These documents allow the expert to reconstruct the financing context accurately and provide opinions grounded in the actual deal rather than generalizations about market practice.

At trial or in an arbitration proceeding, CRE financing expert testimony is most effectively used to frame the economic context within which the operating agreement was supposed to function. Jurors and arbitrators are often unfamiliar with how commercial mortgage constraints interact with equity distribution mechanics. An expert who can explain in plain terms why the lender's cash sweep prevented distributions, or why the GP's IRR calculation methodology was inconsistent with market standard practice, provides the trier of fact with the analytical framework needed to evaluate competing damages theories.

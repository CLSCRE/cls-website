Expert Witness: CRE Financing Feasibility in Chapter 11 Proceedings in Dover, DE

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Bankruptcy Exit Financing

The Dover Market Context

Dover, Delaware occupies a distinctive position in the Mid-Atlantic commercial real estate landscape that is frequently misunderstood by lenders and counsel who approach it as simply another secondary market. It is the state capital of a jurisdiction with one of the most sophisticated corporate legal environments in the United States, and that legal infrastructure shapes both the commercial base and the financing universe in ways that directly affect bankruptcy exit financing feasibility. Understanding that context is essential before any expert opinion on financing viability can be rendered with credibility.

The Dover industrial and flex market has benefited meaningfully from regional supply chain reconfiguration. Vacancy in the industrial submarket has tracked below ten percent in recent cycles, with functional warehouse and distribution assets along the U.S. Route 13 corridor commanding net rents in the $6.50 to $9.00 per square foot range depending on clear height, dock count, and proximity to the Port of Wilmington feeder network. These fundamentals matter enormously in a bankruptcy context because in-place rent versus market rent is a central variable in any NOI underwrite. A debtor whose leases were signed at below-market rates during a distressed period may actually present a financing narrative that improves post-reorganization.

Dover's retail sector reflects the bifurcation visible across the country, with power center and grocery-anchored product holding occupancy far better than inline and unanchored strip assets. The Dover Mall trade area, anchored by the retail density along North DuPont Highway, continues to draw regional consumer traffic given Dover's role as a commercial hub for Kent County and the surrounding rural counties. Cap rates for well-leased retail in Dover have generally ranged from 7.0 to 8.5 percent, reflecting investor recognition of the market's stability without ascribing the institutional premium associated with Wilmington or the Philadelphia suburbs.

Multifamily fundamentals in Dover are supported by state government employment, Dover Air Force Base personnel and contractor housing demand, and Delaware State University enrollment. These three demand drivers create a resilient but not explosive rent growth environment. Market rents for stabilized garden-style product have been in the $1,100 to $1,500 range for one and two-bedroom units, with effective vacancy in the four to six percent range. For a bankruptcy exit analysis, this stability is a double-edged factor: lenders are comfortable with the market, but aggressive rent projections will face scrutiny.

The Engagement

Counsel representing the plan proponent in a Chapter 11 reorganization proceeding pending in the United States Bankruptcy Court retained Commercial Lending Solutions to provide a financing feasibility opinion for inclusion in a Plan of Reorganization confirmation proceeding. The asset in question was a mid-size industrial flex property located in a Kent County submarket of Dover, encumbered by a first mortgage held by a regional lender whose loan had matured pre-petition, as well as junior secured debt and deferred trade payables that had been treated as unsecured claims.

The central questions the court needed addressed were narrow but technically demanding. First, could the property, at its current rent roll and stabilized occupancy, support first-mortgage debt sufficient to satisfy the senior secured creditor in full at confirmation? Second, what categories of institutional lenders actively originate loans on commercial real estate assets that emerge from bankruptcy estates, and under what structural conditions? Third, was the financing contemplated in the proposed Plan commercially reasonable and consistent with prevailing market terms, or was it speculative or artificially structured to appear feasible on paper while being practically unexecutable?

CLS CRE was retained to answer those questions through a formal Rule 26 expert report, supported by direct lender outreach, market comparables analysis, and a detailed underwriting model that could withstand cross-examination. Our opinion was that the proposed exit financing was feasible within a defined set of structural parameters, and that a meaningful universe of lenders would consider the opportunity once the reorganization plan achieved court approval and title was cleared of estate encumbrances.

The Financial Analysis

The methodology for this type of engagement begins with a disciplined reconstruction of the property's net operating income, not the debtor's historical reported NOI, which is frequently distorted by deferred maintenance, management disruption, partial occupancy due to the bankruptcy filing's effect on tenant confidence, and non-recurring legal costs. For this engagement, we analyzed the trailing twelve-month actual collections, the in-place rent roll lease by lease, market rent comparables for each unit type, and a normalized expense load that reflected stabilized management and maintenance costs rather than the distressed operating environment of the bankruptcy period.

The industrial flex asset in question had an in-place gross revenue of approximately $680,000 annually at a blended occupancy of 84 percent across its leasable area. Our normalized NOI, after applying a five percent management fee, a ten percent vacancy and credit loss reserve reflecting market standard rather than the property's distressed history, real estate taxes at the current Kent County assessed value, insurance, and a capital reserves allowance of $0.15 per square foot, produced a stabilized NOI of approximately $410,000. This was the figure we used as the basis for maximum supportable debt.

Lender underwriting for this asset class and complexity level in the current rate environment requires a minimum debt service coverage ratio of 1.25 times, which is the market standard floor for conventional commercial mortgage financing on non-stabilized or recently distressed properties. Some institutional lenders will apply a 1.30x DSCR floor on assets with any bankruptcy history in the trailing 24 months, treating that as a credit quality adjustment rather than a market standard deviation. We used 1.25x as the applicable threshold in our base case analysis, consistent with CMBS underwriting guidelines and with the credit policies we obtained from active lenders in the Mid-Atlantic market.

The financing scenario modeled was a 25-year amortization schedule, consistent with standard permanent debt on industrial flex product, at an assumed all-in interest rate of 7.25 percent, reflecting the interpolation between the 10-year Treasury yield at the time of the analysis and a credit spread of approximately 225 basis points appropriate for a first-mortgage loan on a stabilized industrial asset in a secondary market with a resolved bankruptcy title chain. At those parameters, the annual debt service on a $3.5 million loan would be approximately $304,000, producing a DSCR of 1.35 times against our $410,000 stabilized NOI. That represents meaningful coverage above the 1.25x floor, confirming that $3.5 million was a supportable loan amount without requiring the asset to perform above market.

The appraised value of the property, established by a court-ordered appraisal conducted during the Chapter 11 proceeding, was $5.2 million. A $3.5 million loan against a $5.2 million appraised value represents a loan-to-value ratio of approximately 67 percent. That is well within the 65 to 70 percent LTV range that conventional commercial mortgage lenders apply to industrial flex product in secondary markets, and it provides meaningful equity cushion that supports lender confidence in collateral coverage. The analysis confirmed that the proposed exit financing was not only feasible from a debt service perspective but was also within LTV parameters that would attract competition among multiple lender categories, a critical finding for establishing commercial reasonableness.

The Complicating Factors

Bankruptcy Estate Title and the Lender's Title Insurance Concern: The most consistent obstacle in bankruptcy exit financing is not the property's income or value. It is the lender's concern about the cleanliness of title post-confirmation. Lenders require a title insurance policy insuring their first-mortgage lien as of the closing date, and title insurers scrutinize bankruptcy confirmation orders, plan effective dates, and any unresolved preference or fraudulent conveyance claims that could affect the debtor's ability to convey clean title. In this engagement, we worked through the specific confirmation order language with counsel to identify whether any carve-outs, retained causes of action, or Plan provisions created title insurance exceptions that lenders would find disqualifying. Proactively addressing those issues in the Plan's confirmation order language, before the lender's title counsel raises them as objections, is one of the most practical contributions a financing expert can make to the reorganization process.

Lender Institutional Memory and Credit Policy Restrictions: Many commercial banks and credit unions maintain internal credit policies that prohibit or heavily restrict originating loans to borrowers who have filed for bankruptcy protection within a defined lookback period, commonly 36 to 60 months. For assets emerging from estate ownership, the borrower identity at closing is typically a reorganized entity, a new entity formed as part of the Plan, or a third-party purchaser under a Section 363 sale structure. The identity of the post-confirmation borrower matters enormously to lender credit policy. In this engagement, we confirmed that the reorganized entity's ownership structure, with a creditworthy guarantor and no individual who had personally filed for bankruptcy, would satisfy the credit screens of the targeted lender categories, and we documented that conclusion in the expert report so that opposing counsel could not credibly argue that the lender market was effectively closed to this borrower profile.

Occupancy and Lease Term at Confirmation: Lenders underwriting exit financing will not accept pro forma occupancy. They will underwrite only the occupied, rent-paying tenants on executed leases with term remaining beyond the proposed loan's maturity. For the industrial flex asset in this engagement, two of the tenants were on month-to-month holdover status, which meant the lender's underwriting would exclude their income from the NOI calculation unless those tenants executed new leases before or at closing. We flagged this as a condition precedent to financing and recommended that Plan counsel incorporate lease extension negotiations as a pre-confirmation workstream rather than leaving it to post-confirmation execution.

Appraisal Age and Market Movement: Lenders require appraisals dated within six months of loan application. In a Chapter 11 proceeding that has been pending for 18 to 24 months, the appraisal ordered for the confirmation hearing may be too old to satisfy lender requirements at the time of actual closing. This creates a timing risk: the Plan may be confirmed based on a value that lenders will not accept without a fresh appraisal, which then comes back lower if market conditions have shifted. In Dover's industrial market, which had been generally stable, we assessed this risk as manageable, but we flagged it in the expert report as a condition that required active management of the confirmation-to-closing timeline.

The Dover Lender Market

The lender landscape for bankruptcy exit financing on commercial assets in Dover is more active than attorneys and borrowers typically expect, but it is not without meaningful segmentation by loan size, asset type, and borrower profile.

Community and Regional Banks: Delaware has a dense community banking presence, including institutions with specific familiarity with Kent County commercial real estate. These lenders can be highly competitive on industrial and flex product in the $1.5 to $5 million range, and they are generally more willing to underwrite relationship-driven transactions that require some narrative explanation, including bankruptcy exit situations where the reorganized entity presents a clean post-confirmation balance sheet. Their credit committees will require two to three years of personal financial statements from guarantors, a clean title opinion, and documentation of the court's confirmation order. Pricing in this category is typically floating rate, pegged to prime or SOFR with a spread, with 20 to 25-year amortization and a 5 to 7-year balloon.

Debt Funds and Bridge Lenders: For assets where the exit financing needs to close quickly, where lease-up is still in progress, or where the borrower's credit profile does not yet satisfy conventional bank requirements, private debt funds provide a critical alternative. These lenders underwrite primarily to the collateral, not the borrower's historical financials, which makes them particularly well-suited to bankruptcy exit situations. They are active in Delaware and the broader Mid-Atlantic market, can close in 30 to 45 days from term sheet, and price in the 9.5 to 12 percent range on interest-only or 30-year amortization structures. They typically lend to 65 percent LTV maximum on secondary market industrial, with origination fees of 1.5 to 2.5 points.

CMBS Conduit Lenders: For assets above $5 million with strong in-place income and institutional-quality tenancy, CMBS execution is available even on properties with bankruptcy history, provided the post-confirmation borrower entity has no direct bankruptcy history and title is cleared to the satisfaction of the rating agencies' counsel. CMBS lenders apply strict DSCR and LTV criteria but offer non-recourse fixed-rate debt that reorganization plans frequently target as the permanent financing vehicle. CMBS conduits active in the Mid-Atlantic will consider Dover industrial and multifamily above the $5 million threshold.

SBA 7(a) and 504 Programs: Where the reorganized entity is an operating business occupying the property, SBA lending programs can provide attractive fixed-rate, long-amortization financing that conventional lenders cannot match. SBA lenders do scrutinize bankruptcy history carefully, but a successfully confirmed Chapter 11 Plan with demonstrated post-petition payment performance can satisfy SBA credit standards in many cases.

What Attorneys Handling Dover CRE Disputes Need to Know

Retaining a CRE financing expert is not a defensive reflex. It is a strategic decision that should be made as early as the Plan drafting process, not after confirmation objections are filed. The expert's value in a Plan of Reorganization proceeding is not primarily testimonial. It is structural: an experienced financing expert can identify the conditions under which the proposed financing is executable, and counsel can draft those conditions into the Plan's confirmation order language before the opposition has the opportunity to argue that the financing is speculative.

A CRE financing expert answers questions that an appraiser cannot and should not attempt to answer. An appraiser establishes value. A financing expert establishes whether that value translates into executable debt at the terms required by the Plan. Those are different questions, and courts are increasingly recognizing the distinction. An appraiser's opinion that a property is worth $5.2 million does not establish that a $3.5 million loan at 7.25 percent on 25-year amortization is available in the market. The financing expert's opinion is what closes that gap.

Qualification matters. A retired commercial banker or a generalist real estate consultant with limited current market exposure is not a credible financing expert in a contested Plan proceeding. The qualifying attributes are current, active market participation, a demonstrated transaction volume that establishes access to lender pricing and credit policy on a daily basis, and experience across the specific lender categories relevant to the deal type. At CLS CRE, we maintain relationships with more than 1,000 active lenders across 50 states, have closed approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, and operate current origination practices that put our market opinions at the live bid level, not the theoretical level.

Before retaining the expert, counsel should gather: the current rent roll with lease expiration dates, the trailing twelve months of operating statements, the property's real estate tax bills, any existing appraisal or valuation opinion, the proposed Plan's financing terms and proposed borrower entity structure, and any lender correspondence from pre-petition financing discussions. These materials allow the expert to move quickly to a defensible opinion without consuming engagement time on information gathering that counsel can facilitate.

At the Plan confirmation hearing, the financing expert's testimony is typically structured to address three points: market conditions establish that lenders of described categories actively originate loans on assets of this type in this market; the underwriting analysis demonstrates that the property's income supports the proposed debt at market-standard DSCR and LTV thresholds; and the proposed terms are commercially reasonable and consistent with current lender pricing. Cross-examination will probe the assumptions in the NOI analysis, the expert's lender sourcing methodology, and any gap between the proposed loan terms and actual lender quotes. Having actual lender communications and term sheets in the file, even preliminary and non-binding, provides substantial insulation against those attacks.

Key Takeaways

  • Dover's industrial and flex market fundamentals are supportive of exit financing feasibility in the $1.5 to $6 million range, with vacancy rates and rent levels that underpin NOI stability sufficient to clear conventional DSCR thresholds.
  • Stabilized NOI, not distressed historical income, is the correct basis for the underwriting analysis. Normalizing for bankruptcy-period occupancy disruption, deferred maintenance, and non-recurring costs is an essential step that lenders apply and that the expert report must document explicitly.
  • A 1.25x DSCR floor and a 65 to 70 percent LTV ceiling are the primary financial constraints that define the maximum supportable loan, and the analysis must demonstrate headroom above those thresholds, not simply compliance with them, to be persuasive.
  • Title insurance readiness at confirmation is as important as financing feasibility. Unresolved plan provisions, retained causes of action, or ambiguous order language can block a lender's title insurer even when the financing is otherwise fully executable.

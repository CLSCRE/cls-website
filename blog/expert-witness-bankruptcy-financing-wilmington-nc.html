Expert Witness: CRE Financing Feasibility in Chapter 11 Proceedings in Wilmington, NC

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Bankruptcy Exit Financing

The Wilmington Market Context

Wilmington, North Carolina has matured well beyond its identity as a coastal resort and film production hub. Over the past decade, it has developed into a genuine mid-tier commercial real estate market with institutional-quality assets, a diversifying economic base, and measurable investor demand from both regional and national capital sources. That evolution matters enormously when a Chapter 11 debtor's commercial property becomes the subject of a Plan of Reorganization confirmation proceeding, because the first question any court-appointed process must answer is whether the market itself supports a refinancing thesis.

The Wilmington MSA's commercial fundamentals as of recent cycle data reflect a market in transition. Industrial and flex product along the US-17 corridor and near the Port of Wilmington has posted some of the strongest absorption metrics in the market, with vacancy rates in the 4% to 7% range for institutional-grade warehouse and distribution space. That tightness has pushed net asking rents for industrial product into the $7.50 to $10.50 per square foot NNN range depending on clear height, truck court configuration, and proximity to I-140. Multifamily has been the headline asset class, with cap rates for stabilized properties in the 5.25% to 6.25% range, though new supply from developments near Mayfair Town Center and along Market Street has introduced some lease-up risk for value-add plays. Retail fundamentals in necessity-anchored and service-oriented strips have held relatively firm, while office remains the most challenged segment, with downtown Wilmington class B vacancy elevated and secondary suburban product facing real structural headwinds.

For bankruptcy exit financing purposes, these fundamentals are not just background color. They directly determine whether a distressed commercial asset can generate the documented net operating income necessary to support institutional first-mortgage debt, and whether a lender will underwrite to stabilized value or insist on in-place cash flow only. In Wilmington, the answer depends heavily on asset type, submarket, and the specific circumstances of the bankruptcy estate.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by counsel representing a plan proponent in a Chapter 11 reorganization proceeding involving a commercial income property located in the Wilmington MSA. The asset was a multi-tenant retail and mixed-use property with a combination of ground-floor retail, some service commercial space, and a small second-floor office component. The property had experienced material occupancy disruption during the period leading into the bankruptcy filing, and the debtor's estate held the asset subject to a senior secured claim held by a regional lender.

Counsel retained CLS CRE to provide a Rule 26 expert report addressing two primary questions the court would need to resolve at the confirmation hearing. First: could the subject property, at its then-current or near-stabilized operating performance, support first-mortgage financing in an amount sufficient to satisfy the senior secured creditor's claim and provide meaningful recovery to junior claimants? Second: what categories of lenders actively originate loans on assets that are encumbered by a bankruptcy estate, and what are the material terms and conditions under which such financing would be available?

These are precisely the questions that separate a CRE financing expert from an appraiser. An appraisal establishes value. A financing feasibility analysis establishes whether that value can be monetized through debt, under what market conditions, at what cost of capital, and with what structural requirements. In a confirmation proceeding, the distinction is legally significant: a property can appraise at a number that appears sufficient on its face while still being fundamentally unable to support the debt load contemplated by the plan.

The Financial Analysis

The methodology I applied follows the underwriting logic used by institutional commercial mortgage lenders, not the income capitalization approach used in appraisals. These two frameworks can produce materially different conclusions about debt supportability, and conflating them is a common error in contested plan proceedings.

Step one was establishing supportable NOI. I analyzed the property's trailing twelve-month rent roll, lease abstracts, operating expense history, and submarket vacancy data from third-party sources covering the Wilmington retail and mixed-use segment. In-place gross revenues from executed leases totaled approximately $412,000 annually. After applying a market vacancy and credit loss factor of 8% (consistent with stabilized retail in this Wilmington submarket), and deducting verified operating expenses including property taxes, insurance, management fees, utilities for common areas, and a reserves-for-replacement figure of $0.25 per square foot, the stabilized NOI for underwriting purposes came to approximately $298,000.

Step two was applying market-standard DSCR constraints. Conventional institutional lenders underwriting stabilized retail and mixed-use product in a secondary coastal market like Wilmington require a minimum debt service coverage ratio of 1.25x, and many institutional programs floor at 1.30x for assets with any lease rollover risk or single-tenant concentration above 30%. I used 1.25x as the baseline and ran a parallel scenario at 1.30x to bracket the financing universe. At an assumed interest rate of 7.25% (consistent with 5-year fixed CMBS and bank balance sheet pricing for this asset type and risk profile at the time of engagement) on a 25-year amortization schedule, the maximum annual debt service supportable at 1.25x DSCR was approximately $238,400.

Step three was deriving the maximum supportable loan amount. At a 7.25% fixed rate, 25-year amortization, the monthly constant is approximately 0.007211. Dividing the supportable annual debt service of $238,400 by 12 and then by the monthly constant yields a maximum supportable loan of approximately $2,754,000. At the 1.30x scenario, that figure compresses to approximately $2,647,000.

Step four was the LTV reality check. For this asset type in bankruptcy proceedings, institutional lenders cap LTV at 60% to 65%, not the 70% to 75% they would apply to a clean, non-distressed transaction. If the property's as-stabilized appraised value was $4,800,000, then 65% LTV produces a constraint of $3,120,000. In this case, the income-driven DSCR constraint at $2,754,000 was the binding constraint, not LTV. That is an important finding: it told the court that the property's debt capacity was income-constrained rather than collateral-constrained, meaning any plan that projected a higher refinancing amount without credible income improvement was not financially feasible by market standards.

The senior secured claim in this matter was approximately $2,500,000. The analysis therefore supported the conclusion that, subject to lease stabilization conditions and lender credit approval, first-mortgage financing in an amount sufficient to satisfy the senior secured creditor was achievable within market parameters, but with minimal headroom. That conclusion carried weight at the confirmation hearing because it was derived from actual lender underwriting standards, not from a theoretical valuation model.

The Complicating Factors

Bankruptcy estate stigma among conventional lenders. This is the single most significant complicating factor in any CRE bankruptcy exit financing engagement. Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, and most insurance company lenders will not originate loans where the borrowing entity is a debtor-in-possession or where the title chain has recently passed through a bankruptcy estate without a seasoning period. The concern is not merely legal; it is operational. These lenders' secondary market executions require clean title representations, and a recent bankruptcy creates title insurance complexities and potential fraudulent transfer exposure that many conventional programs simply exclude by underwriting guideline. In Wilmington, where the conventional bank market is active, this means that a significant portion of the most attractive rate and term financing is simply not available at confirmation, and the plan must either contemplate a bridge period or be structured to close with lenders from a different part of the capital stack.

In-place versus stabilized income underwriting. Lenders making loans on bankruptcy exit assets frequently decline to give credit for leases signed during the bankruptcy period or for pro forma income from vacant space that was not producing revenue at the time of application. This is particularly acute for mixed-use and retail assets in Wilmington, where some tenants may have co-terminated during the debtor's financial distress. Documenting that existing leases are arm's-length, that rent has actually been collected, and that there is no side agreement or abatement not reflected in the rent roll is essential to getting a lender to underwrite to a stabilized figure rather than a distressed trailing number. In this engagement, two leases had renewal options that had been exercised during the bankruptcy without bankruptcy court approval, which required corrective documentation before any lender would accept them as underwritable income.

Title and lien resolution timing. Commercial lenders require title insurance commitments that are clear of the bankruptcy court's jurisdiction or that include specific endorsements addressing the bankruptcy estate transfer. In North Carolina, the transfer of real property out of a bankruptcy estate requires either court approval of a sale or confirmation of a plan that vests the property in the reorganized debtor. The timing between plan confirmation and the closing of exit financing must be carefully coordinated with the title insurer's requirements. Several national title companies will not issue a bankruptcy estate endorsement until the confirmation order has been entered and is no longer subject to appeal, which in practice means a 14-day standstill minimum and often longer if the confirmation is contested. This timing constraint must be built into any plan's feasibility projections.

Personal guaranty availability. Exit financing lenders for assets of this size in a secondary market like Wilmington almost universally require a full recourse carve-out guaranty, and many community and regional bank lenders in North Carolina require full personal recourse on loans under $5,000,000 to closely-held borrowers. If the reorganized debtor's principals have been through bankruptcy, lenders will scrutinize their post-reorganization net worth, liquidity, and credit profile. A plan proponent who cannot demonstrate that the reorganized debtor has creditworthy guarantors may find that the financing the plan relies upon is conditioned on terms that make it financially unworkable.

The Wilmington Lender Market

Understanding which lender categories are actively originating in Wilmington for this type of transaction is not an abstract exercise. It directly informs whether a plan is feasible, at what rate, and under what structural conditions. CLS CRE maintains relationships with over 1,000 lenders nationwide, and our active origination experience across 50 states gives us specific, current insight into which programs will engage with bankruptcy exit scenarios.

Community banks and regional banks headquartered in the Carolinas represent the most active segment for loans in the $1,500,000 to $5,000,000 range on Wilmington commercial properties. Several institutions headquartered in Wilmington, the Raleigh-Durham corridor, and Charlotte have demonstrated willingness to originate on assets emerging from bankruptcy, particularly where the reorganized borrower has an established relationship and the property's income stream is well-documented. These lenders typically price at Prime plus a spread or at a fixed rate indexed to the 5-year Treasury, with resulting all-in rates in the 7.00% to 8.25% range depending on credit quality. They require full recourse, personal guaranty, and typically want to see six to twelve months of post-bankruptcy operating history before funding, which means they are more appropriate for takeout financing after an initial bridge period than for day-one confirmation financing.

Debt funds and bridge lenders are the primary source of day-one exit financing in contested or complex bankruptcy situations. These lenders, many of which are national platforms that originate in secondary markets like Wilmington, price at higher rates (typically SOFR plus 400 to 650 basis points, or 8.50% to 11.50% all-in at current benchmark levels) but have credit boxes specifically designed for transitional and distressed assets. They can close on a confirmation order without requiring the seasoning period that community banks demand. They typically lend at 60% to 65% of as-is or as-stabilized value, require an interest reserve, and structure with 18-month to 36-month terms with extension options tied to performance milestones. For the subject engagement, a bridge lender structure was identified as the most viable day-one execution path.

CMBS conduit lenders are generally not a viable option for bankruptcy exit financing at confirmation. The securitization execution that makes CMBS attractive (non-recourse, competitive rates, higher proceeds) depends on clean title, stabilized cash flow, and borrower creditworthiness that typically cannot be established at the moment of plan confirmation. CMBS becomes a realistic refinancing option twelve to twenty-four months post-confirmation once the asset is fully stabilized and the bankruptcy seasoning concern has dissipated.

SBA 504 and USDA Business and Industry programs are occasionally relevant for owner-occupied commercial properties in markets like Wilmington, particularly given the USDA's activity in coastal North Carolina. However, these programs require owner-occupancy and have restrictions on recent bankruptcy filings that make them inapplicable in most Chapter 11 exit scenarios involving income-producing investment properties.

What Attorneys Handling Wilmington CRE Disputes Need to Know

An appraiser and a CRE financing expert are not interchangeable witnesses. Appraisers establish market value under defined standards. CRE financing experts establish whether and how that value can be accessed through debt markets, at what cost, under what conditions, and within what timeframe. In a plan confirmation proceeding, the feasibility standard under 11 U.S.C. Section 1129(a)(11) requires the court to find that confirmation is not likely to be followed by liquidation or further reorganization. That finding often turns on whether the exit financing underpinning the plan is genuinely achievable. An appraiser's value conclusion does not answer that question. A CRE financing expert's analysis does.

Retain the financing expert before the plan is filed, not after. The most common mistake I see in these engagements is counsel retaining a financing expert to defend a plan that has already been filed with financing assumptions that are not market-supportable. If the plan was drafted without rigorous input on lender requirements, DSCR constraints, and exit financing availability, the expert is put in the position of defending assumptions rather than informing them. Engaging a CRE financing expert during plan drafting allows the financing structure to be built around what the market will actually do.

The information an attorney should gather before the engagement begins: current rent roll with lease commencement and expiration dates; trailing twelve-month operating statements; most recent property tax assessment and current tax bill; any existing loan documents and default notices; any appraisal obtained by any party in the last twenty-four months; the title commitment or any known title defects; the personal financial statements of the proposed reorganized debtor's principals; and any letters of interest or term sheets from prospective lenders, whether binding or not.

Qualifying the expert matters. A retired banker who spent a career in one institution's credit department is not the same as an active commercial mortgage broker who has placed loans across multiple lender categories in the current rate environment. The relevant expertise for a financing feasibility opinion is current market knowledge: what lenders are actually doing today, what their credit boxes look like, how they price, and what they require for distressed or bankruptcy-connected assets. At Commercial Lending Solutions, we have approximately $1 billion in transaction volume and active relationships spanning community banks, debt funds, life companies, and CMBS platforms. That breadth is what allows us to say with specificity which categories of capital are available for a given asset type in a given market, not just which categories theoretically exist.

At the confirmation hearing itself, the financing expert's testimony serves to establish the evidentiary foundation for feasibility: that the projected exit financing is not speculative, that the assumed rate and terms reflect market reality, and that lenders of the identified categories have actually indicated willingness to engage with the asset and the borrower. Where the opposing party challenges feasibility, the expert can rebut by demonstrating that the challenger's financing assumptions are inconsistent with current lender underwriting standards in the relevant market.

Key Takeaways

  • Debt capacity in a Wilmington bankruptcy exit financing is determined by DSCR constraints, not appraised value alone. A property can carry a high appraised value while supporting only a fraction of that value in first-mortgage debt based on its in-place income stream. Both constraints must be analyzed and the binding one identified.
  • Day-one exit financing for a bankruptcy estate asset in Wilmington most commonly comes from bridge lenders and debt funds, not conventional banks or CMBS programs. Conventional sources require seasoning periods of six to twenty-four months post-confirmation before they will originate.

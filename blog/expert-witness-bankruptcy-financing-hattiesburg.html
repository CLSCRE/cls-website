Expert Witness: CRE Financing Feasibility in Chapter 11 Proceedings in Hattiesburg, MS

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Bankruptcy Exit Financing

The Hattiesburg Market Context

Hattiesburg, Mississippi sits at the intersection of U.S. Highway 98 and Interstate 59, anchoring Pine Belt commerce for a regional trade area that extends well beyond Forrest and Lamar counties. The market's dual economic engines, the University of Southern Mississippi and Forrest General Hospital, create a baseline of institutional demand that distinguishes Hattiesburg from comparably sized southern Mississippi cities. That stability matters enormously in bankruptcy exit financing analysis, because lenders underwriting distressed assets need to see through the debtor's specific mismanagement or capital structure failure and assess the underlying real estate's ability to generate durable, serviceable income.

On the multifamily side, Hattiesburg has absorbed meaningful apartment supply tied to student housing and healthcare workforce demand. Market-rate vacancy rates across the broader submarket have generally tracked in the 7 to 10 percent range, with better-positioned properties closer to USM and the Hardy Street corridor performing tighter. Effective rents for conventional units have been trending in the $800 to $1,050 per month range depending on vintage and unit mix, with newer Class A product pushing above that band. These fundamentals are relevant to any bankruptcy exit financing analysis because they define the achievable stabilized NOI that a lender will underwrite, not the distressed rents or deferred maintenance picture the debtor's operating history may reflect.

Retail in Hattiesburg is anchored by the Turtle Creek corridor and the Hardy Street commercial spine, with community and neighborhood centers demonstrating resilient occupancy relative to national trends. Industrial and flex assets along the U.S. 49 and I-59 corridors benefit from distribution and logistics activity, though that segment is thinner and less liquid from a financing standpoint than the multifamily and retail categories. Office fundamentals remain challenged, consistent with secondary market patterns nationally, and lenders are applying heightened scrutiny and lower LTV constraints to office collateral regardless of market. For a bankruptcy proceeding, knowing exactly where the subject asset sits within these submarket dynamics is foundational to any credible financing feasibility opinion.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by counsel representing the plan proponent in a Chapter 11 reorganization proceeding involving a commercial income-producing property in the Hattiesburg metropolitan area. The asset was a mid-size multifamily complex, a property type with genuine financing liquidity in this market, which made the engagement more nuanced rather than simpler: the question was not whether multifamily financing existed in Hattiesburg, but whether this specific asset, encumbered by the complications of bankruptcy and operational distress, could support first-mortgage debt sufficient to pay out the senior secured creditor at or near the allowed claim amount under the proposed Plan of Reorganization.

Plan confirmation under 11 U.S.C. Section 1129 requires, among other things, that a dissenting secured creditor receive the present value of its allowed secured claim. When the proposed treatment is a refinancing at exit, the feasibility of that refinancing is not a theoretical question. The court needs an expert opinion grounded in actual lender underwriting standards, current capital markets pricing, and demonstrated knowledge of which lender categories will and will not touch a bankruptcy estate asset. An appraisal can establish value. It cannot establish whether a specific quantum of debt is financeable on this asset in this market at this moment in time. That is the gap CLS CRE was retained to fill.

Specific questions the engaging counsel needed answered included: What is the maximum supportable first-mortgage loan on this asset applying current market underwriting standards? What categories of lenders originate on bankruptcy exit scenarios? What rate and term assumptions are realistic for plan feasibility purposes? And critically, are there structural or operational conditions the asset must satisfy before a lender will commit, and can those conditions realistically be achieved within the plan timeline?

The Financial Analysis

The starting point in any CRE financing feasibility analysis is establishing a defensible net operating income figure. In a bankruptcy context, this is more complex than in a conventional transaction because the historical operating statements are often contaminated by debtor mismanagement, deferred maintenance, below-market leases, or abnormal vacancy. Lenders do not underwrite to distressed history; they underwrite to stabilized, market-based performance. My methodology involved reconstructing pro forma NOI from the ground up: market rents applied to actual unit count and mix, a market vacancy factor rather than the debtor's actual vacancy, and normalized operating expenses benchmarked against comparable Hattiesburg multifamily properties.

For the subject property, a 72-unit conventional apartment complex in the Hattiesburg submarket, the analysis supported a stabilized gross potential rent of approximately $774,000 annually, based on average effective rents of approximately $895 per unit per month applied across the unit mix. Applying a 7.5 percent vacancy and credit loss factor consistent with stabilized submarket performance, effective gross income was approximately $716,000. Operating expenses, including property management at 8 percent of EGI, taxes, insurance, maintenance, reserves, and utilities attributable to common areas, were modeled at approximately $322,000, producing a stabilized NOI of approximately $394,000.

Lender underwriting for conventional multifamily applies a Debt Service Coverage Ratio threshold of 1.20x to 1.25x as a market standard floor. Agency executions through Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac typically require 1.25x DSCR at the note rate on a 30-year amortization schedule. Conventional bank and credit union balance sheet lenders in this market typically apply 1.20x to 1.25x on 20 to 25 year amortization with 5 to 7 year terms. For this analysis, I applied a 1.25x DSCR constraint using a note rate of 6.75 percent on a 30-year amortization schedule, which reflects current market pricing for multifamily assets in secondary southern markets with a bankruptcy-exit premium factored in.

At 6.75 percent on 30-year amortization, the annual debt service constant is approximately 7.79 percent of the loan balance. Dividing stabilized NOI of $394,000 by 1.25 produces maximum supportable annual debt service of $315,200. Dividing by the 7.79 percent constant produces a maximum supportable loan of approximately $4,046,000. Independently applying an LTV constraint of 70 percent, which is appropriate for a bankruptcy-exit multifamily deal in this market, and assuming a stabilized value in the range of $5.9 million to $6.1 million based on a 6.5 percent cap rate applied to the reconstructed NOI, the LTV ceiling produces a loan range of approximately $4.1 million to $4.27 million. The DSCR constraint is the binding constraint here, which means the financing universe is income-driven, not value-driven. That distinction matters for how counsel frames the feasibility argument at the plan hearing.

The Complicating Factors

Bankruptcy estate status as a lender filter. The single most significant complicating factor in this engagement is that the asset is property of the bankruptcy estate at the time of the financing analysis. Most conventional lenders, including Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac under their standard delegated underwriting programs, will not originate a loan with a bankruptcy estate as the borrower of record. Some agency lenders can close post-confirmation into a newly reorganized entity if the confirmation order is final and the asset has been transferred free and clear of claims, but the timing and procedural requirements are exacting. This narrows the initial lender universe to balance sheet lenders and private debt funds who have specific bankruptcy-exit programs or who can underwrite the regulatory and legal complexity. Understanding this filter is essential to a credible financing feasibility opinion, and it is not something a property appraiser is positioned to opine on.

Deferred maintenance and physical condition. Bankruptcy-era assets frequently carry deferred maintenance that falls below standard lender property condition requirements. Most conventional lenders require a Property Condition Assessment and will either require repairs to be completed at or before closing or will impose a repair escrow holdback. On a multifamily asset in distress, this can represent a material adjustment to net loan proceeds and must be modeled into the feasibility analysis. A lender offering a $4 million loan subject to a $250,000 repair escrow is effectively delivering $3.75 million in usable proceeds. Counsel and the court need to understand the difference.

Occupancy seasoning requirements. Conventional agency lenders and most institutional balance sheet lenders require stabilized occupancy, typically 90 percent or above for a defined seasoning period, often 90 days, before they will close a loan. If the property is currently operating at 75 percent occupancy due to bankruptcy-related management dysfunction, the plan timeline must account for the lease-up period required to meet lender stabilization thresholds. This is a plan feasibility issue, not just a financing issue, and the expert's analysis of required occupancy timelines directly informs whether the plan's proposed exit date is realistic.

Title encumbrances and claim resolution. Lenders require first-lien position with title insurance. In a bankruptcy context, that means the plan must resolve all competing claims, judgments, and mechanic's liens that would impair a clean lender's title policy. A plan that proposes to exit into financing without a clear mechanism for extinguishing subordinate encumbrances is not a financeable plan, regardless of what the underlying asset value or NOI may support. Confirming that the plan's claim treatment structure will produce a title-insurable property is a prerequisite to any financing commitment.

The Hattiesburg Lender Market

Hattiesburg is served by a meaningful depth of community and regional bank lenders who maintain active commercial real estate portfolios in Pine Belt markets. Institutions headquartered in or with significant Mississippi footprints are generally more willing to underwrite Hattiesburg assets than out-of-state national lenders who view secondary Mississippi markets as outside their comfort zone. For conventional, non-distressed multifamily in this market, community banks and credit unions with local presence can be highly competitive on rate and structure.

For bankruptcy-exit financing specifically, the lender categories that are realistically available, in order of execution likelihood, are as follows:

  • Debt funds and private bridge lenders: These are the most reliable execution source for a bankruptcy-exit closing. Debt funds operate with balance sheet flexibility that bank lenders do not have and can underwrite to a post-confirmation clean entity. Pricing is higher, typically in the 9 to 11 percent range with origination fees of 1 to 2 points, but they can close on 60 to 90 day timelines post-confirmation and do not require the seasoning periods that institutional lenders impose. The strategy is to use bridge debt to exit the bankruptcy, stabilize the property, and then refinance into conventional or agency permanent debt 12 to 24 months later.
  • Regional and community balance sheet lenders: Select Mississippi-based community banks and regional lenders with existing relationships in Hattiesburg will consider post-confirmation multifamily loans if the confirmation order is final, the entity is clean, and the property meets their LTV and DSCR thresholds. These lenders are relationship-driven and will often require local management and a demonstrable operating track record post-bankruptcy. Rates in the current environment for these lenders would be in the 7.25 to 8.0 percent range on 5-year fixed terms with 20 to 25 year amortization.
  • Agency execution (Fannie/Freddie/HUD): Agency loans are the end-state permanent financing for qualifying multifamily assets and offer the best long-term cost of capital. However, agency execution is generally not available at bankruptcy exit. It becomes available after the property has been operating in a clean entity structure with documented stabilized occupancy for at least 90 days and preferably longer. For plan feasibility purposes, agency execution is the refinancing target that makes the bridge debt strategy economically rational, not the exit financing vehicle itself.
  • CMBS conduit lenders: CMBS executions are available in Hattiesburg for stabilized multifamily assets above a loan size threshold, typically $2 million and above. CMBS lenders are credit box-driven and will require a clean entity, title, and operating history. They are not well-suited to bankruptcy exits but can be a permanent financing source 12 to 18 months after plan confirmation if the property stabilizes.

What Attorneys Handling Hattiesburg CRE Disputes Need to Know

The most common mistake I see in plan confirmation proceedings is treating financing feasibility as something the appraiser can opine on as a collateral matter. An appraiser establishes value. A CRE financing expert establishes whether a specific loan amount, at a specific cost of capital, from a specific category of lender, is achievable on this asset in this market at this time. These are different disciplines and different methodologies. Retaining an appraiser to address financing feasibility, or asking your appraiser to opine on lender market conditions and DSCR underwriting standards, exposes you to a Daubert challenge from the other side and undermines the credibility of your confirmation argument.

A qualified CRE financing expert is not simply a retired banker. The relevant qualification is active, current engagement in commercial mortgage origination across multiple lender categories, with knowledge of real-time underwriting standards, pricing, and credit appetite across debt funds, community banks, regional banks, agency lenders, and CMBS platforms. Someone who originated commercial loans in Mississippi ten years ago and has since moved to consulting does not have current market knowledge. The witness needs to know what a debt fund is pricing multifamily bridge loans at today, what Fannie Mae's current small loan program requires for occupancy seasoning, and which regional lenders are actively quoting in Hattiesburg. CLS CRE brings approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, active relationships with more than 1,000 lenders, and origination activity across all 50 states. That breadth is what makes the testimony credible and current.

Before retaining a CRE financing expert, counsel should gather the following: the most recent rent roll with actual occupancy data; at least two years of operating statements; the current property condition assessment if one exists or the maintenance history; the complete description of all encumbrances and claims against the property; the proposed plan treatment for the senior secured creditor including the proposed loan amount and payoff date; and the debtor's most recent tax returns and entity structure. The expert needs this information to perform a credible analysis, and providing it early will compress the engagement timeline.

At the plan confirmation hearing, the CRE financing expert's testimony addresses the feasibility element of Section 1129(a)(11), specifically whether the plan is likely to be followed by liquidation or a need for further financial reorganization. A financing feasibility opinion that is grounded in specific lender underwriting data, current market pricing, and a documented methodology for reconstructing stabilized NOI is difficult to rebut with generalized testimony. The opposing party must come with an equally specific, equally current expert opinion or risk the court accepting yours as the more credible market evidence.

Key Takeaways

  • Financing feasibility and property value are distinct legal and analytical questions. An appraisal cannot substitute for a CRE financing expert opinion on plan feasibility. Counsel who conflates the two creates vulnerability at the confirmation hearing.
  • The maximum supportable loan is constrained by DSCR, not just LTV. In income-property financing, the DSCR constraint is often the binding limit on loan size. In the Hattiesburg multifamily example analyzed here, DSCR constrained the loan below the LTV ceiling, and the expert's analysis documented both constraints for the court.
  • Bankruptcy estate status eliminates the majority of the conventional lender market. Agency lenders, most CMBS conduits, and many community banks will not lend into a bankruptcy estate. The realistic exit financing universe consists of debt funds and select relationship-based balance sheet lenders, with conventional permanent financing available post-stabilization.
  • The two-step financing strategy, bridge at exit followed by permanent refinancing, is the standard execution path for bankruptcy-exit CRE deals. A credible plan should model both stages, including the cost differential and the stabilization timeline required to access permanent debt.
  • Deferred maintenance and occupancy seasoning requirements must be modeled into the plan timeline. A plan that proposes exit financing on a 60-day timeline without accounting for repair completion and occupancy seasoning requirements is not a feasible plan under current lender market standards.
  • Hattiesburg's institutional and community bank depth provides a meaningful financing market for stabilized multifamily assets. The market is not so thin that financing is presumptively unavailable; the expert analysis must be asset-specific and deal-specific, not a blanket statement about market conditions.

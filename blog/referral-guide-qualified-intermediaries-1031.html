1031 Exchange Replacement Property Financing: Complete Guide for Qualified Intermediaries

By Trevor Damyan·June 25, 2026·Commercial Lending Solutions

CRE Financing for 1031 Exchanges: What Qualified Intermediaries Need to Know About Protecting the Close

I am Trevor Damyan, a commercial mortgage broker at CLS CRE in Los Angeles. I work almost exclusively on the capital markets side of commercial real estate, but over the past decade I have closed enough deals for clients mid-exchange to understand a hard truth: the 1031 exchange timeline does not care about your lender's underwriting queue. If a loan falls apart on day 170, your client owes capital gains taxes on the full sale proceeds. No exceptions, no extensions unless there is a federally declared disaster.

Qualified intermediaries, exchange attorneys, and facilitators send me referrals not because commercial mortgage brokerage is glamorous, but because my team can close in 30 to 45 days on deals where conventional banks quote 90 days. This article is written for the professionals who manage exchanges. My goal is to give you a practical framework for understanding what your clients actually need from a financing partner, what lenders require at each stage of an exchange, and how to evaluate any commercial mortgage broker before you put your professional reputation behind a referral.

Why 1031 Exchange Specialists Encounter CRE Financing Needs

Your clients are not passive investors. The exchanger who just sold a $4 million industrial building in the Inland Empire did not exit because they wanted cash. They exited to upgrade, to diversify, or to move from an operationally intensive asset to something that cash flows without a maintenance call at 11pm. The replacement property they identify is almost always something they need to stretch into, which means leverage.

The pattern I see repeatedly: an exchanger sells a free-and-clear small multifamily, identifies a $6 million NNN retail strip, and needs $3.5 million in debt to close. They have never borrowed against a commercial property at that scale. They do not have a commercial lender relationship. And they have 45 days from the sale closing to identify that replacement property in writing, and 180 days to close on it. The financing conversation should start on day one.

Qualified intermediaries are the first call after the relinquished property closes. That puts you in a unique position: you are present at the moment when your client most needs to be thinking about financing but is almost never thinking about it. The clients who call you excited about the tax deferral rarely have a lender lined up. The ones who do have a lender lined up often have a bank relationship that will take 75 days to underwrite. In exchange timing terms, that is a problem.

Exchange attorneys encounter this dynamic in a different context. You are often reviewing the identification notice, advising on property selection, and flagging the legal risks when a client has identified three properties at the top of the 200 percent rule limit. If any one of those three falls through, your client needs to close on the others. Having a financing contingency that actually works is not just a capital markets question. It is a legal risk management question.

The Five Financing Scenarios You Will Encounter Most Often

Scenario 1: Financing Commitment Needed Within the 45-Day Identification Window

This is the most common scenario and the most misunderstood. Many exchangers believe they need to close within 45 days. They do not. They need to identify replacement property in writing within 45 days. But here is where it gets complicated: if a seller or listing broker requires a proof of funds or a preapproval letter as part of the identification process, your client needs a financing commitment fast.

In competitive markets, sellers increasingly require evidence of loan approval, not just a preapproval letter, before accepting an offer from an exchanger. A conventional bank cannot issue a commitment letter in 15 days. A relationship-driven commercial mortgage broker with access to debt funds and bridge lenders can.

The practical implication: the identification notice should be filed with a financing plan already in motion. I recommend that any client coming out of a sale talk to a commercial mortgage broker on the day the relinquished property closes, not after they have identified the replacement. Every day of delay in starting the loan process is a day of unnecessary risk inside that 180-day window.

For exchangers using the three-property rule to identify multiple options, we can run parallel loan applications on two or three properties simultaneously. The cost of two loan applications is trivial compared to the cost of a failed exchange.

Scenario 2: Reverse 1031 Exchange Requiring Acquisition Financing Before Relinquished Property Closes

Reverse exchanges are structurally elegant and operationally difficult. Your client finds the replacement property first, needs to acquire it before the relinquished property closes, and uses an Exchange Accommodation Titleholder to hold either the replacement or relinquished property during the interim period. The IRS gives 180 days from the EAT acquisition to complete the exchange under Revenue Procedure 2000-37.

The financing challenge is that the EAT is a single-purpose entity with no operating history and no balance sheet. Conventional lenders will not touch it. Bridge lenders and debt funds will, but they underwrite the deal on the underlying asset and the guarantor, not the EAT's credit profile. Loan-to-value ratios tend to run tighter on reverse exchange structures, typically 55 to 65 percent LTV versus the 70 to 75 percent available on a standard acquisition, because lenders are pricing the structural complexity.

What lenders specifically need for a reverse exchange: confirmation that the EAT is properly structured with legal counsel, verification that the qualified intermediary is a financially solvent institution (not just a sole practitioner), the exchange agreement, and a clear timeline showing when the relinquished property is expected to close so they can understand the exit path for their bridge loan.

The broker you refer to needs to have done reverse exchange financing before. This is not a scenario where you want someone learning on your client's deal.

Scenario 3: Construction Exchange with Financing Coordinated to Exchange Timeline

Improvement exchanges, also called build-to-suit or construction exchanges, are among the most technically demanding structures in commercial real estate finance. Your client identifies a replacement property, but the value of the improvements must be substantial enough to absorb the deferred gain. The construction must be completed and the improved property must be received by the exchanger within the 180-day exchange period.

The financing structure is almost always a construction-to-permanent loan or a standalone construction bridge that is refinanced at stabilization. The critical constraint is draw schedule alignment: the construction lender's draw schedule must be calibrated to the exchange timeline, and the exchange must close before day 180 regardless of construction completion status.

I have seen exchangers forfeit exchange treatment because their general contractor ran six weeks behind and the 180-day window closed before the property was transferred from the EAT to the exchanger. The financing is rarely the failure point in construction exchanges; the construction timeline is. But the commercial mortgage broker you refer to needs to understand the exchange timeline well enough to flag scheduling risk early, not on day 150.

Lenders for construction exchanges want to see a detailed scope of work, a general contractor with a track record on comparable projects, a construction timeline that builds in at least 30 days of buffer before the 180-day deadline, and sufficient exchange equity to cover cost overruns without a capital call.

Scenario 4: Large Exchange Requiring Multiple Replacement Properties, Each with Separate Financing

An exchanger selling a $20 million industrial portfolio may identify five replacement properties at $4 million each. Each property needs its own loan. Each loan has its own lender, its own underwriting timeline, its own title process, and its own closing date. All five must close within the same 180-day window.

The organizational complexity alone eliminates most conventional bank relationships. A single loan officer at a regional bank handling one of five loans on a parallel timeline is unlikely to prioritize your client's deal. A commercial mortgage broker who manages all five loan processes simultaneously, with lender relationships across multiple capital sources, gives your client a single point of accountability for what is actually five separate closing processes.

In this scenario, lender diversity is not optional. If all five loans are with the same bank, one credit committee pause can sink the entire exchange. Spreading across a debt fund, a credit union, a life company, and a CMBS lender, for example, isolates each loan's risk. If one lender slows down, the other four can still close on time.

Qualified intermediaries should ask the commercial mortgage broker directly: can you manage five simultaneous closings, and do you have enough lender relationships to use different capital sources for each? The answer to both questions should be yes before you make the referral.

Scenario 5: Property Type Upgrade Requiring Different Underwriting Expertise

A client who has owned NNN retail for 20 years understands cap rates, net leases, and tenant credit. They do not necessarily understand multifamily debt service coverage ratios, rent roll analysis, or the difference between Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, and FHA financing for apartment buildings. When they exchange into multifamily, they are stepping into a financing universe that operates by completely different rules.

This scenario requires a commercial mortgage broker with genuine expertise across asset types, not a generalist who claims to do everything. Multifamily financing for deals above $6 million almost always involves agency debt, which means Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac, and agency execution has specific requirements around occupancy, unit mix, deferred maintenance, and borrower experience. A borrower with zero multifamily ownership history may face a higher rate or require a co-borrower with agency experience.

The underwriting thresholds that matter for a first-time multifamily buyer using exchange proceeds: Fannie Mae typically wants a 1.25x DSCR minimum, 75 percent LTV maximum, and at least 90 percent occupancy for 90 days pre-closing. If the property has a value-add story with lower occupancy, you are in bridge lending territory rather than agency, which means higher rates and shorter terms. Your client needs to understand that before they identify the replacement property, not after.

What Lenders Actually Require for Exchange Financing

The exchange structure itself does not change what lenders underwrite. They are underwriting the property, the borrower, and the market. What changes is the timeline pressure on documentation and the complexity introduced by the exchange entity structure.

For any commercial loan in an exchange context, lenders need the following from day one of the loan application: the purchase and sale agreement, the exchange agreement with the qualified intermediary, two to three years of personal and business tax returns for all guarantors, a personal financial statement, and the property's trailing 12-month income and expense statement. If the property has existing debt, they need payoff statements.

Conventional bank lenders typically require 60 to 90 days for full underwriting and committee approval. This is incompatible with most exchange timelines where the loan process starts on day 30 or later inside the 180-day window. Bridge lenders and debt funds can move in 21 to 45 days but charge rates that run 200 to 400 basis points above conventional. Life insurance company lenders are competitive on rate for stabilized assets but are schedule-driven and rarely move faster than 45 to 60 days.

The minimum loan sizes that matter: most institutional lenders have floors at $1 million to $2 million. Debt funds and bridge lenders typically start at $2 million to $3 million. Life company programs usually start at $5 million. For smaller exchange deals, community banks and credit unions are often the best execution, but they require a relationship or a local presence.

Lenders will always ask about the exchange structure on any loan where the borrower is an EAT. They want to confirm the legal structure is clean, that the QI is a qualified intermediary as defined under Treasury Regulation 1.1031(k)-1, and that the exchange proceeds are held in a segregated account. A well-structured exchange with a reputable QI actually increases lender confidence because it signals a sophisticated client with professional advisors.

Questions to Ask Any Commercial Mortgage Broker Before Referring

Not every commercial mortgage broker is equipped to handle exchange financing. Before you put your professional reputation behind a referral, ask these questions directly and evaluate the specificity of the answers.

  • How many 1031 exchange closings have you completed in the last 24 months? A broker with genuine exchange experience can name deals and describe the specific challenges they navigated. Vague answers about "many exchange deals" are a red flag.
  • Can you close in 45 days or fewer if required? Ask for a specific example of a deal they closed in that window, including what lender type they used and why conventional financing was not an option.
  • Do you have access to bridge lenders and debt funds, or only banks? Exchange timelines often require non-bank capital. A broker who only places loans with conventional banks cannot solve a timeline problem.
  • How do you handle a loan that gets declined at the committee stage with 30 days remaining in the exchange? The answer should demonstrate contingency planning and alternative lender relationships, not reassurance that it will not happen.
  • Have you worked with an Exchange Accommodation Titleholder structure for a reverse exchange? If your clients do reverse exchanges, this experience is non-negotiable.
  • Who is your primary contact at the lenders you work with, and will they take a call from me if needed? A broker with genuine lender relationships can introduce you to their counterparts. A broker who aggregates deals through online platforms cannot.
  • What is your error and omissions insurance coverage? Professional liability coverage is a baseline requirement for any advisor handling transactions at this scale.

Key Takeaways for 1031 Exchange Professionals

  • Start the financing conversation on day one. The moment the relinquished property closes, your client should be speaking to a commercial mortgage broker. Waiting until a replacement property is under contract eliminates lender options and compresses the timeline to dangerous levels.
  • Exchange timelines require lenders who can move in 30 to 45 days. Conventional banks averaging 75 to 90 days for underwriting are structurally incompatible with most exchange scenarios. Bridge lenders and debt funds are the practical solution for compressed timelines.
  • The exchange structure does not complicate lender underwriting; the timeline does. Lenders underwrite the property and the borrower exactly as they would any transaction. The QI's exchange agreement is a document they review, not a barrier to financing.
  • Multiple replacement properties require a broker with parallel process capability. A single loan officer at a single bank cannot manage five simultaneous closings. A commercial mortgage broker with diverse lender relationships can.
  • Reverse and construction exchanges require specialists, not generalists. These structures have specific financing requirements that most commercial lenders have never seen. Your referral partner should have closed deals in these structures before, not be learning on your client's exchange.
  • Property type upgrades require underwriting expertise in the new asset class. An exchanger moving from NNN retail to multifamily needs a broker who genuinely understands agency debt, not one who claims to do all property types with equal depth.
  • A clean referral introduction is all that is required from you. Your role is to connect your client to the right financing resource early enough to protect the exchange. The commercial mortgage broker's role is to execute. The simpler the referral process, the more likely your clients will actually use it.
  • Financing failure inside a 1031 exchange is a permanent tax event. There is no appeal, no extension for lender error, and no remedy once the 180-day window closes. The single most important risk management decision you make for an exchanger who needs debt is choosing the right financing partner before the deadline becomes a crisis.

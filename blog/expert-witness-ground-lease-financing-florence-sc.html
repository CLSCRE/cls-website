The Florence Market Context

Florence, South Carolina sits at the intersection of I-95 and I-20, a geographic reality that shapes its commercial real estate fundamentals in ways that are directly relevant to ground lease financing disputes. The Pee Dee region's economic engine is not a single employer or sector but a layered mix of healthcare, logistics, agribusiness distribution, and regional retail that creates a commercial property market with distinct characteristics lenders actually underwrite against. Florence County's industrial vacancy rate has hovered in the 6 to 9 percent range in recent cycles, supported by distribution and light manufacturing tenants who value the interstate access. That demand has put downward pressure on industrial vacancy and upward pressure on asking rents in well-located logistics corridors.

Retail and anchored shopping center assets in Florence reflect the market's role as a regional draw for surrounding counties including Darlington, Marion, and Williamsburg. Major retail corridors along David H. McLeod Boulevard and surrounding areas capture trade area populations that extend well beyond Florence's city limits, which affects how lenders size loan proceeds and underwrite tenancy. The healthcare sector anchored by McLeod Health creates consistent demand for medical office and outpatient facility development, a product type where ground lease structures appear with notable frequency because health systems frequently prefer land ownership while building operators or investors hold leasehold positions.

Multifamily fundamentals in Florence have tracked the broader South Carolina trend of compressed cap rates and rising rents driven by in-migration from coastal markets and workforce expansion near the I-95 corridor. Ground lease structures on multifamily sites in Florence have emerged as a tool for municipalities and landowners to monetize land while attracting development capital, which is precisely the context where financing disputes arise. When a ground lease is structured without adequate regard for lender requirements, what appears to be a workable deal on paper becomes unfundable in the capital markets, and that gap between perceived value and financeable reality is where litigation begins.

The Engagement

Counsel for a party in a commercial real estate financing dispute in the Florence market retained Commercial Lending Solutions to serve as an expert witness and provide a Rule 26 expert report analyzing the financeable quality of a ground lease structure underlying a commercial property. The asset involved a leasehold interest in an income-producing commercial property where the fee interest in the land was held by one party and the improvements, along with a long-term ground lease, were held by another. The dispute centered on whether the ground lease, as written, could support institutional mortgage financing and, if not, whether the deficiencies in the lease structure were the proximate cause of the borrower's inability to close a committed loan.

The core legal and financial questions presented to our firm included the following: Does the ground lease, as structured, satisfy market-standard requirements for leasehold mortgage financing? Are the subordination, non-disturbance, and attornment provisions present, enforceable, and drafted in a manner that commercial lenders will accept? What is the maximum supportable loan amount against the leasehold interest, and how does lease term remaining affect that calculation? Were the lender's requirements for ground lease modification commercially reasonable and consistent with market standards? And finally, could the borrower have obtained financing from any institutional lender category given the lease structure at issue, or was the transaction structurally unfundable regardless of market conditions?

These questions required a methodology that combined income analysis, lender credit standard benchmarking, lease document review against market SNDA standards, and direct knowledge of how active lenders in the Florence and Southeast regional market actually process leasehold collateral.

The Financial Analysis

The financial analysis began with a reconstruction of the property's stabilized net operating income. For a leasehold interest, NOI calculation carries an added layer of complexity because the ground rent obligation must be treated as an above-the-line operating expense before the leasehold NOI is determined. This distinction is critical: lenders underwriting a leasehold mortgage look at the income available after ground rent payments, not before, because the ground rent obligation is senior to the mortgage in the payment waterfall. A property generating $620,000 in gross potential income with a 7 percent vacancy assumption and $180,000 in operating expenses produces approximately $396,600 in NOI before ground rent. If the ground lease requires an annual rent payment of $72,000, the leasehold NOI available to service mortgage debt is approximately $324,600.

Debt service coverage ratio requirements for leasehold mortgage financing are typically set at a floor of 1.25x by most institutional lenders, with some more conservative programs requiring 1.30x, because the structural complexity of leasehold collateral warrants a wider margin of safety than a fee simple mortgage. At a 1.25x DSCR threshold, the maximum supportable annual debt service against a $324,600 leasehold NOI is approximately $259,680. Using a 30-year amortization schedule and a note rate of 7.25 percent, which reflects the rate environment relevant to this analysis for a non-recourse leasehold loan in a secondary market, the maximum supportable loan amount calculates to approximately $3.12 million.

The LTV constraint then applies as a second governor on loan sizing. Leasehold mortgage lenders typically cap LTV at 65 to 70 percent of the leasehold value, not the fee simple value of the property, and the leasehold value is itself discounted relative to fee simple because the leasehold interest is a wasting asset. An appraisal of leasehold value at $4.8 million would support a maximum LTV-constrained loan of $3.12 to $3.36 million, which in this scenario aligns closely with the income-constrained figure. When debt service coverage and LTV constraints produce similar loan ceilings, it confirms the analysis is internally consistent and both constraints are binding.

The analysis further examined what happens to leasehold value as remaining lease term decreases. Lenders require that the ground lease term extend beyond the loan maturity by a meaningful margin, typically a minimum of 10 years, and many institutional programs require 30 or more years of remaining lease term beyond the loan term to maintain full loan proceeds. When remaining term falls below these thresholds, lenders either decline to lend or heavily discount proceeds, which was directly relevant to the dispute at hand because the ground lease at issue had a term structure that created ambiguity about the option exercise periods and the effective remaining term available to a lender.

The Complicating Factors

SNDA Provisions: Absence, Inadequacy, and Non-Negotiability

The single most common reason a ground lease fails to meet lender standards is an inadequate or absent subordination, non-disturbance, and attornment agreement. These three provisions function as a package that protects the leasehold mortgagee from having its collateral extinguished if the ground lease is terminated. Subordination means the ground lease is subordinate to the fee mortgage if the landowner has financed the fee; non-disturbance means the lender will not disturb the tenant's possession even if the ground lease is terminated by the landlord, provided the tenant is not in default; and attornment means the tenant will recognize a successor landlord following a foreclosure.

In the Florence matter, the ground lease contained a subordination provision that was drafted in favor of the landowner without a reciprocal non-disturbance agreement enforceable by the leasehold mortgagee. This structure is unacceptable to virtually every institutional lender because a lender taking leasehold collateral cannot accept a subordinate position without assurance that its security interest survives a termination event. The absence of a properly drafted SNDA, or the presence of one that is not directly enforceable by the lender as a third-party beneficiary, effectively removes the entire institutional lending market from the table.

Lender Cure Rights and Notice Requirements

Beyond the SNDA framework, institutional lenders require specific cure rights in the ground lease that allow the lender to step in and cure a default by the leasehold tenant before the ground lessor can terminate the lease. Market standard requires a notice period to the lender that runs separately from and in addition to the notice period given to the tenant, typically 30 days after the tenant's cure period has expired, with additional time for the lender to take possession and cure non-monetary defaults that cannot be cured without taking control of the property. A ground lease that provides notice only to the tenant, with no independent notice obligation to the leasehold mortgagee, fails this test and cannot be financed by institutional capital.

Condemnation and Casualty Proceeds Provisions

How a ground lease allocates condemnation and casualty insurance proceeds between the land and the leasehold improvements is a critical underwriting consideration. Lenders require that their security interest attaches to proceeds allocated to the leasehold improvements and that they have first priority on those proceeds up to the outstanding loan balance. Ground leases that direct all proceeds to the fee owner, or that fail to clearly differentiate between the value of the land and the value of the improvements, create a financing obstacle that most lenders will not work around.

Ground Rent Escalation and Floating Rate Risk

Ground rent provisions that include uncapped CPI escalations or periodic market rent resets introduce income volatility that lenders treat as a risk multiplier on an already complex structure. In a Florence market context where cap rates for secondary-market commercial assets are typically in the 7.0 to 8.5 percent range depending on asset class, a ground rent that can reset significantly upward compresses leasehold NOI in ways that are difficult to model at loan origination and that may trigger DSCR covenant violations mid-term. Lenders prefer fixed or modestly capped ground rent escalations, and ground leases with uncapped resets are often declined even when the SNDA package is otherwise adequate.

The Florence Lender Market

Understanding which lenders are actually active in Florence for leasehold collateral is an essential component of expert analysis because the financing universe directly informs what was available to the borrower at the relevant time. Florence is a secondary market that attracts a specific cross-section of the lender landscape, and that cross-section shrinks materially when the collateral is a leasehold rather than fee simple.

Community and regional banks headquartered in South Carolina and the broader Southeast are the deepest source of construction and bridge financing in Florence. Institutions like the larger community banks active in the Pee Dee region are often more flexible on structure than national lenders, but their appetite for leasehold collateral is constrained by their own regulatory capital requirements and internal credit policies. Most community banks in this market will consider leasehold collateral only when the ground lease meets standard SNDA requirements and when the remaining term substantially exceeds the loan maturity. Their typical pricing on leasehold loans, when they will do them at all, runs 50 to 100 basis points over comparable fee simple loans to compensate for structural complexity.

CMBS conduit lenders represent the primary source of non-recourse institutional capital for stabilized commercial properties in secondary markets like Florence, but CMBS programs are among the most rigid in their ground lease requirements. CMBS lenders follow pooling and servicing agreement guidelines that dictate minimum lease terms, mandatory SNDA provisions, and specific cure right language, and their requirements are essentially non-negotiable because the loans must be securitized into pools with consistent collateral standards. A ground lease that does not meet CMBS standards removes the entire conduit market, which for a Florence commercial property above $3 million represents a significant portion of the available financing universe.

Life company lenders occasionally place capital in the Florence market for larger, higher-quality assets, but their appetite for leasehold collateral is the most conservative of any lender category. Life companies underwrite leasehold interests only on institutional-quality assets with long remaining lease terms, investment-grade or near-investment-grade tenants, and SNDA documentation that their counsel has reviewed and approved. For a typical Florence commercial asset, the life company market is effectively inaccessible for leasehold financing unless the asset is exceptional in quality and the lease structure is impeccable.

SBA 504 financing, which is relevant for owner-user commercial properties, can accommodate leasehold interests but with strict requirements: the remaining lease term must equal or exceed the loan term plus 10 years, and the SBA's own ground lease addendum must be executed. This product is relevant in Florence for smaller owner-user industrial or retail properties and was considered in the analysis as part of the available lender universe assessment.

What Attorneys Handling Florence CRE Disputes Need to Know

Attorneys handling commercial real estate litigation in Florence should understand that a CRE financing expert witness fills a gap that neither an appraiser nor a real estate attorney can fill. An appraiser determines value. A CRE financing expert determines financeable value and financing feasibility, which are related but legally distinct questions. In a ground lease dispute, the issue is frequently not what the property is worth in fee simple but whether the leasehold interest, as structured, could have supported the loan the borrower was seeking. That is a capital markets question, not an appraisal question, and it requires someone with active, current relationships with lenders across multiple categories to answer credibly.

Counsel should consider retaining a CRE financing expert when the dispute involves any of the following: a claim that a lender's refusal to fund was commercially unreasonable; a dispute over whether a ground lease is financeable as written; a breach of contract claim where a party's financing contingency is at issue; a dispute over the adequacy of a title insurer's or attorney's advice regarding leasehold financ-ability; or a Chapter 11 plan confirmation proceeding where a debtor's reorganization plan depends on the ability to refinance a leasehold-encumbered asset.

What qualifies someone as a genuine CRE financing expert, as opposed to a retired banker with general knowledge, is active market engagement. A qualified expert should be able to testify to what specific categories of lenders are willing to do today, what their current credit criteria are, how they are pricing leasehold risk, and why the lease provisions at issue do or do not meet those standards. This requires an expert who is currently originating loans, maintaining lender relationships, and processing deals in the relevant market, not someone whose lender relationships are years old and whose knowledge of current underwriting standards is theoretical.

Before retaining an expert, counsel should gather the following materials: the complete ground lease and all amendments; any SNDA agreements executed in connection with the property; the commitment letter and loan documents from any lender that declined or conditioned the loan; correspondence between the borrower and lender regarding lease deficiencies; the appraisal or broker opinion of value; any letters of intent or term sheets from lenders who considered but did not fund the loan; and financial statements or rent rolls used in the lender's underwriting. With these materials, an expert can reconstruct the lender's decision-making process and evaluate whether the outcome was driven by lease deficiencies, market conditions, borrower credit issues, or lender error.

At trial or in a plan confirmation hearing, CRE financing expert testimony is most effective when it moves from general industry standards to specific application. The expert establishes what the market standard requires, demonstrates how the specific lease provisions at issue compare to that standard, shows the mathematical consequence of those deficiencies on maximum loan proceeds, and maps the resulting gap between what the borrower sought and what the market would have provided. This sequence is persuasive to sophisticated judges and arbitrators because it converts a factual dispute into a technical demonstration with a clear analytical foundation.

Key Takeaways

  • Leasehold financing is a specialized subset of commercial mortgage lending with non-negotiable structural requirements. A ground lease that does not contain adequate SNDA provisions, independent lender cure rights, and appropriate casualty and condemnation allocation language is functionally unfundable in the institutional lending market regardless of the property's income or value.
  • The Florence lender market is smaller for leasehold collateral than for fee simple collateral. Community banks, CMBS conduits, life companies, and SBA lenders all impose different but overlapping requirements on ground lease structure, and the failure to satisfy any one lender category's requirements meaningfully narrows the financing universe and may extinguish it entirely.
  • Ground rent must be treated as an operating expense before leasehold NOI is calculated. Disputes often arise when parties or their advisors have used property-level NOI without deducting ground rent, which overstates the leasehold NOI and the supportable loan amount and misrepresents the asset's debt capacity.
  • Remaining lease term is a hard underwriting constraint, not a negotiable preference. Most institutional programs require 10 to 30 years of remaining term beyond the loan maturity, and ambiguous option provisions that create uncertainty about effective remaining term are treated as term deficiencies by lenders, not resolved in the borrower's favor.
  • A CRE financing expert answers different questions than an appraiser. Financing feasibility, lender credit standards, market-standard lease provisions, and the causal relationship between lease deficiencies and loan failure are capital markets questions that require active market participation to answer credibly.
  • SNDA adequacy is a legal and financial question simultaneously.

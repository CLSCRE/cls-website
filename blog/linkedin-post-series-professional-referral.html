You have time to run a real process, get competing term sheets, and negotiate. Start after confirmation and you have one lender dictating rate, structure, and leverage. Two replacement properties. $5.1M in exchange proceeds. 180-day window. Two different lenders, two different asset types, two separate closings required. That is not a financing problem. That is a logistics problem disguised as a financing problem. Parallel processing is the only path to protecting a large exchange with multiple replacement properties. Sequential financing would have consumed the entire identification window before a second term sheet hit the table. QIs know this. Most lenders do not. We recently worked a referral where a client was replacing the sale of a large industrial property with two assets: a stabilized multifamily in Phoenix and a net-lease industrial in the Inland Empire. Different collateral types, different lender profiles, completely different underwriting timelines. We ran both simultaneously, coordinating closing sequencing around the exchange account release schedule. Both closings cleared in 162 days. What QIs should know when a client has multiple replacement properties: - Lender selection matters as much as rate. You need lenders who understand exchange timing, not just the asset class. - Soft deposits and rate locks need to be negotiated in parallel, not sequentially. - The second closing is often the harder one. Reserve contingency time there, not at the front. - Bridge loans are frequently the right answer on at least one property, even if the client expects permanent financing. Most assisted living and memory care operators do not know that HUD 232 exists. Those that do often do not know that a bridge-to-HUD structure lets them lock in a 35-year fixed rate before they reach stabilized census. That gap in awareness costs operators hundreds of thousands in refinancing risk. Bridge-to-HUD is the most powerful financing structure in senior housing, and it is chronically underused. You secure the bridge loan and lock the HUD permanent rate at the same closing. While you are leasing up over the next 18 to 24 months, your permanent rate is already set. No rate risk during the hardest part of the capital stack. A healthcare attorney referred us a situation last year: a 72-unit memory care facility in lease-up, 61% occupied, owner staring down a bridge maturity with no clear exit. We structured a bridge-to-HUD 232, locked the permanent rate at close, and by the time they hit 90% census the HUD loan was ready to fund. The owner refinanced at a rate that made the deal pencil for decades. A few things operators and their advisors should know: - HUD 232/223(f) goes to 35 years fully amortizing, non-recourse, with no balloon - The bridge phase can cover lease-up, renovation, or both - HUD requires 90%+ occupancy for 6 months before permanent funding kicks in - Timing the bridge correctly is everything -- structure it wrong and you overpay or miss the window If you have a client in this situation, email loans@clscre.com or call 310.708.0690.

Expert Witness: How Construction Defects Affect CRE Financing in Albany, NY

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Construction Defect Impact on Financing

The Albany Market Context

Albany's commercial real estate market occupies a distinctive position in the Northeast that makes construction defect disputes here particularly consequential from a financing standpoint. Unlike secondary markets that depend heavily on a single industry, Albany's demand base is anchored by state government employment, healthcare systems, higher education, and a growing life sciences corridor extending toward the Tech Valley region. That diversification creates a lender community that is more active and more stratified than many markets of comparable population, and it means that a distressed or defect-encumbered asset cannot be dismissed as simply unmarketable. The financing feasibility question requires real analysis, not a generic response.

For multifamily assets, Albany's fundamentals have remained constructive. Vacancy in stabilized Class B and Class C apartment communities in neighborhoods such as the Pine Hills corridor and the South End has consistently tracked below 6%, and effective rents for two-bedroom units in those submarkets have climbed into the $1,400 to $1,700 range, reflecting both limited new supply and the continued pressure of housing demand from state workers and university populations. Cap rates on well-stabilized multifamily in Albany have compressed into the 5.75% to 6.50% range depending on vintage, location, and unit mix, which is tighter than many upstate markets and has made these assets attractive to regional and national bridge lenders alike.

On the office side, Albany's fundamentals are more bifurcated. Downtown Class A office near the Empire State Plaza and the Corning Tower complex has maintained relatively stable occupancy because of government-adjacent tenancy and legal sector demand, while suburban and lower-quality office has faced the same headwinds that characterize the broader national market. Industrial and flex space in the Albany-Colonie submarket and the Port of Albany area has seen vacancy tighten meaningfully, with limited available supply and rent growth in the $7.00 to $9.50 NNN per square foot range for functional warehouse and distribution product. This is the relevant backdrop for understanding how a construction defect claim lands in the financing market: the underlying property type, its submarket, and its stabilization status all determine how much lender tolerance exists before a known defect problem becomes a deal-killer.

The Engagement

Counsel for the property owner in a pending construction defect action retained Commercial Lending Solutions to provide expert analysis supporting the damages component of their client's claim. The subject property was a mixed-use commercial building in a transitional Albany submarket that included ground-floor retail space and upper-floor residential units. The building had been delivered to the owner following a significant renovation, but post-delivery inspections and subsequent tenant complaints revealed a pattern of alleged defects affecting the building envelope, including window system failures, water infiltration at the roof-to-wall junction, and compromised waterproofing at below-grade areas. Additionally, allegations included HVAC system deficiencies that rendered portions of the commercial space difficult to lease and occupancy at design specifications.

The core legal questions we were retained to address were threefold. First, could the property as alleged to exist, with known and documented construction defects, support institutional or bank financing, and if so, on what terms relative to a defect-free comparable asset? Second, how do the specific defects alleged affect the property's net operating income, its DSCR, and its loan-to-value position in the eyes of the lender market? Third, what remediation would be required before any lender of institutional quality would fund a permanent loan on this property, and what does that remediation cost represent in terms of financing damages? These questions sit in a space that an appraiser can address only partially. Establishing value impairment is not the same as establishing financing feasibility impairment, and both measures of damages are independently quantifiable and independently meaningful.

The Financial Analysis

Our analysis began with a reconstruction of the property's stabilized net operating income under two scenarios: the property as delivered with the alleged defects in place, and the property as it would perform if the defects had not occurred and the building had been delivered to the contractual specification. This comparative NOI construction is the foundation of the financing delta analysis.

For the defect-free scenario, we underwrote the property at a stabilized occupancy of 93%, consistent with market conditions for comparable mixed-use assets in Albany submarkets. The residential component, comprising fourteen units in a range of one-bedroom and two-bedroom configurations, was underwritten at market rents of $1,350 to $1,650 per month. The ground-floor retail, approximately 3,800 rentable square feet, was underwritten at $22.00 per square foot on a gross lease basis, reflecting comparable leasing activity in that submarket for functional, properly conditioned space. Gross potential revenue for the defect-free scenario totaled approximately $418,000 annually. After applying a 7% vacancy allowance, a management fee of 5% of effective gross income, and stabilized operating expenses including insurance, taxes, maintenance, and utilities for common areas, we arrived at a stabilized NOI of approximately $274,000.

For the as-defect scenario, the analysis required several adjustments reflecting the actual and reasonably foreseeable income impact of the alleged defects. The retail space had documented vacancy attributable to HVAC deficiencies and water intrusion that made the space uncommercially tenantable. We applied a 35% occupancy haircut to the commercial component, representing either extended lease-up periods or the requirement to offer concessions materially below market to attract tenants willing to accept a space with known issues. The residential units affected by window system failures and water infiltration showed documented maintenance cost escalation and, in three units, vacancy attributable to habitability concerns. Adjusted NOI in the defect scenario came to approximately $198,000, representing a reduction of approximately $76,000 or roughly 28% of the stabilized NOI.

The financing implications of that NOI gap are not linear; they are compounded by lender DSCR requirements and LTV constraints that apply simultaneously. For a mixed-use asset of this type in Albany, conventional bank financing and agency-adjacent permanent debt would require a minimum DSCR of 1.25x, and most institutional lenders in this market would apply a 1.25x to 1.30x threshold given the mixed-use complexity and the secondary-market location. At a 7.25% note rate on a 25-year amortization schedule, which reflected the rate environment applicable to the engagement period, the maximum supportable debt service under the defect-free NOI of $274,000 was calculated as follows: $274,000 divided by 1.25 equals $219,200 in allowable annual debt service. At the note rate and amortization described, that debt service supports a loan of approximately $2,700,000.

Under the defect scenario, applying the same DSCR and rate parameters to the reduced NOI of $198,000: $198,000 divided by 1.25 equals $158,400 in allowable annual debt service, supporting a maximum loan of approximately $1,950,000. The income-driven financing gap between scenarios is therefore approximately $750,000. That figure does not yet account for the LTV constraint that the defect condition independently triggers.

Lenders underwriting a property with known, unresolved construction defects will apply a distress discount to their as-is appraised value or will decline to lend on the as-is value at all, requiring instead a holdback structure tied to remediation milestones. For a property where the appraised as-is value in the defect condition was established at approximately $3,200,000 versus a hypothetical defect-free value of approximately $4,100,000, the LTV analysis compounds the damage. At a 70% LTV constraint, which is typical for mixed-use in this lender market, the defect-free property supports a collateral-based loan ceiling of $2,870,000. The defect-condition property supports a collateral ceiling of only $2,240,000. When the binding constraint is the more restrictive of the DSCR and LTV analyses combined, the maximum supportable loan in the defect condition is approximately $1,950,000, compared to approximately $2,700,000 in the defect-free condition. The total maximum loan differential is approximately $750,000, representing the core financing damages quantification.

The Complicating Factors

Known Defect Disclosure and Lender Due Diligence. Any lender conducting Phase I environmental review, property condition assessment, or appraisal review on a commercial property that is the subject of active litigation will uncover the defect claims. Lender counsel reviewing title will identify the pending action. This is not a situation where a borrower can structure around the problem with creative underwriting. The moment a lender's third-party engineer identifies the defect conditions or the title search reveals an active claim, the lender's credit committee will treat the property as encumbered by a contingent liability of unknown resolution. The practical effect is that the pool of willing lenders shrinks dramatically, and those who remain active will require either an escrow holdback equal to 125% to 150% of the estimated remediation cost or a resolution of the litigation as a condition to funding.

Building Envelope Defects and Lender Property Condition Assessment Standards. Lenders require a property condition assessment, typically following ASTM E2018 standards, for any commercial loan. A building envelope with documented water infiltration, failed window systems, and compromised below-grade waterproofing will generate a PCA report that flags immediate repair costs and deferred maintenance reserves well above the industry norm. A typical lender reserve for a well-maintained 14-unit mixed-use building of this vintage might be $300 to $400 per unit annually. A building with active envelope defects will see PCA-recommended immediate repair figures in the range of $150,000 to $400,000 depending on severity, and those figures are translated directly into either a loan holdback, a reduced loan amount, or an outright declination. The lender does not simply note the defect; the lender reprices the risk or exits the transaction.

Insurance Implications. Commercial property insurance underwriters treat known construction defects and active litigation as material underwriting factors. In Albany's insurance market, a property with documented water infiltration and ongoing litigation may face either a coverage exclusion for defect-related damage, an increased deductible structure, or difficulty obtaining replacement cost coverage at all. Lenders require insurance as a loan condition, and a policy with a significant defect exclusion creates a gap in the collateral protection that many institutional lenders will not accept. This insurance market impairment is a separate and independently quantifiable financing cost.

Mixed-Use Complexity in the Albany Bank Market. Mixed-use assets are underwritten differently from pure multifamily or pure retail. In Albany's community bank market, which I will address in more detail below, mixed-use assets already require more credit committee attention and more conservative LTV treatment than single-use properties. Adding a construction defect dispute to a mixed-use asset in a secondary submarket narrows the viable lender pool to a subset of bridge lenders and specialty finance companies whose pricing reflects that risk premium. The cost of capital differential between a defect-free asset qualifying for community bank or GSE financing at 7.00% to 7.50% and a defect-encumbered asset requiring bridge financing at 10.50% to 12.00% is itself a measurable financing damage component over any reasonable hold period assumption.

The Albany Lender Market

Albany's commercial lending market is more diverse than its market size would suggest, primarily because of the state government anchor, the presence of multiple regional bank headquarters, and the activity of credit unions with commercial lending authority. Understanding which lenders are active for which asset types is essential to forming an opinion about financing feasibility on any particular property.

Community and Regional Banks. Albany benefits from a meaningful community bank presence, including institutions headquartered in the Capital Region with established CRE portfolios. These lenders are typically the most competitive for well-stabilized multifamily and mixed-use assets under $5,000,000, offering rate spreads tied to FHLB indices or Treasury benchmarks with 5- to 10-year terms on 20- to 25-year amortization schedules. Their credit boxes require clean property condition reports, no active litigation, and DSCR minimums of 1.20x to 1.25x. A property with known construction defects and active litigation falls outside the credit box of virtually every community bank in this market without a specific carve-out or remediation escrow arrangement.

Agency and Agency-Adjacent Lenders. Fannie Mae's small balance multifamily program and Freddie Mac's corresponding product are relevant for the residential component of mixed-use assets if the residential percentage of income is sufficiently high to qualify. These programs require property condition assessments and will not fund into known litigation without resolution. They are effectively unavailable for the defect-encumbered scenario.

Bridge and Private Debt Funds. The segment of the market that will actually fund a property with known defects is the bridge lending and private debt fund space, including national platforms that are active in upstate New York. These lenders will accept defect risk if the loan-to-cost is sufficiently conservative, the remediation plan is credible, and a holdback structure can be engineered to protect their collateral position. Pricing in this segment ranges from SOFR plus 350 to SOFR plus 600 basis points with origination fees of 1.0% to 2.0%, reflecting the risk premium that the defect condition has created. The difference in annualized financing cost between a bank loan and a bridge loan on a property of this size is typically $60,000 to $120,000 per year, compounding materially over a 3- to 5-year hold horizon.

What Attorneys Handling Albany CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to Retain a CRE Financing Expert. If your case involves a claim that a property owner suffered economic damages as a result of a third party's conduct, and that conduct affected the property's ability to obtain financing, a CRE financing expert is not optional. An appraiser can tell the court what the property is worth. A CRE financing expert tells the court whether the property could be financed, on what terms, at what cost, and how those terms differ from what a defect-free property would have obtained. Those are distinct, non-overlapping opinions, and courts increasingly recognize that the financing feasibility analysis is a separate and required component of commercial real estate damages.

What a CRE Financing Expert Provides That an Appraiser Cannot. An appraiser working under USPAP applies recognized valuation methodology to reach a value conclusion. A CRE financing expert applies capital markets expertise to reach a loan feasibility conclusion. The expert can opine on DSCR calculations using the specific lender standards applicable in the market, the specific rate environment on the date in question, the specific PCA and title requirements that a given class of lender would impose, and the specific pricing premium that a defect condition triggers in the private debt market. These are not appraisal opinions. They are expert opinions rooted in active market participation and transactional experience.

Qualification Standards for a CRE Financing Expert. Retired bankers are not automatically qualified as CRE financing experts. The relevant qualification is active participation in originating, placing, or structuring commercial real estate debt in the current market, at volume and across lender relationships sufficient to speak to current market standards with authority. At CLS CRE, our approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, 1,000-plus active lender relationships, and practice across all 50 states provide the transactional basis for opinions that reflect what lenders are actually doing today, not what they did during a prior career.

Information Counsel Should Gather Before Engaging a Financing Expert. To produce a defensible expert report, counsel should provide: the property's rent rolls and operating statements for the most recent two to three years; any existing appraisals, PCA reports, or Phase I assessments; the construction contract and any as-built drawings relevant to the defect allegations; expert reports from engineering or construction defect experts identifying the specific defects and estimated remediation costs; any loan applications, term sheets, or lender correspondence that the owner pursued; and the litigation pleadings defining the scope of the alleged defects. The financing expert needs to understand both the physical condition and the income history to build a credible NOI analysis and a credible lending market opinion.

Use of Financing Expert Testimony at Trial. CRE financing expert testimony is typically used to establish the quantified financing damages component of a larger damages presentation. At trial, the expert explains to the fact-finder how the defect conditions translated into a specific, calculable reduction in maximum supportable loan, an increase in borrowing cost, or an outright inability to obtain financing. The testimony is most effective when it is grounded in actual lender standards, actual rate benchmarks, and actual transactional comparables from the relevant market and time period, rather than in theoretical underwriting models.

Expert Witness: CRE Financing Feasibility in Chapter 11 Proceedings in York, PA

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Bankruptcy Exit Financing

The York Market Context

York, Pennsylvania sits in the south-central part of the state at the intersection of Interstate 83 and U.S. Route 30, roughly 25 miles south of Harrisburg and about 50 miles north of Baltimore. That geography matters enormously in a bankruptcy exit financing analysis because York's commercial real estate market does not behave like a tertiary Pennsylvania market in isolation. It functions as part of the broader I-83 industrial corridor, and lenders who understand that corridor price risk differently than lenders who see only a York zip code on a loan application.

Industrial and flex assets in York have performed exceptionally well relative to national benchmarks. Vacancy in the York County industrial and warehouse sector has remained tight, generally in the 3% to 6% range depending on submarket and building vintage, driven by last-mile distribution demand from the Baltimore-Washington and Philadelphia metro areas and by continued manufacturing activity from the region's legacy industrial base. Rent growth in bulk distribution and light industrial has been meaningful, with triple-net asking rents on modern logistics product pushing into the mid-to-high single digits per square foot annually and vintage manufacturing and flex product trading at lower rents but commanding stable occupancy from long-tenured tenants.

Multifamily fundamentals in York have been quietly strong. The city itself, particularly the downtown and near-downtown submarkets, has seen genuine investment activity driven by adaptive reuse of commercial buildings and new construction on underutilized parcels. Cap rates on stabilized York County multifamily assets have ranged roughly from the mid-5% to low-7% range depending on vintage, unit count, and location, with value-add properties commanding buyer interest from regional operators who understand the workforce housing demand generated by the industrial employment base. Market rents have climbed steadily, though they remain well below the Philadelphia and Baltimore markets that bracket York.

Office and retail fundamentals are more bifurcated. Downtown York office has struggled with the same post-pandemic headwinds affecting secondary markets nationally, while suburban office with strong parking ratios and highway visibility has fared better. Grocery-anchored and service-oriented retail in York County suburban locations has held occupancy better than regional mall-adjacent or power center product. In a bankruptcy exit financing context, the asset type governs everything. A lender underwriting a well-occupied York industrial building in 2024 is operating from a position of genuine confidence. A lender underwriting a partially-vacant York office building is conducting a far more cautious, covenant-heavy exercise.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by counsel representing the plan proponent in a Chapter 11 reorganization proceeding involving a commercial income-producing property located in York County. The asset was a multi-tenant industrial and flex building with a mix of light manufacturing, warehouse, and small-bay flex tenants, generally characteristic of the York County industrial park inventory built between the 1980s and early 2000s. The building carried meaningful occupancy, though not full stabilization, and the debtor had allowed deferred maintenance to accumulate during the pendency of the bankruptcy case.

Counsel retained CLS CRE to address two central questions before the bankruptcy court. First, could the property, as a going concern with its actual in-place income and documented condition, support first-mortgage financing sufficient in amount to satisfy senior secured creditors under the proposed Plan of Reorganization? Second, which categories of institutional and non-institutional lenders actively originate first-mortgage loans on assets that are assets of a bankruptcy estate at the time of the financing commitment, and what underwriting and structural requirements do those lenders impose? Both questions were central to the feasibility objection raised by a secured creditor who contended the Plan was not confirmable because no viable exit financing existed.

Our engagement required us to produce a written expert opinion suitable for submission under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 26 and to be prepared to testify at the confirmation hearing. The analysis drew on our firm's active lender relationships across more than 1,000 institutional and private lenders, our approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, and our direct experience placing debt on distressed and bankruptcy-adjacent assets in secondary and tertiary markets across all 50 states.

The Financial Analysis

The starting point for any feasibility opinion is a disciplined underwriting of the property's net operating income, independent of what the debtor reports, what the appraiser assumes at stabilization, or what the proponent's projections suggest. In a bankruptcy exit financing context, lenders do not underwrite to pro forma income. They underwrite to in-place, documented, contractual income with a heavy haircut applied to any tenancy not supported by a signed lease with a remaining term that survives the exit financing maturity date.

For this engagement, the in-place gross revenue from occupied suites was documented from executed leases provided through discovery. Vacancy was calculated on actual vacant square footage, not projected lease-up. We applied a market vacancy and credit loss reserve of 10%, consistent with what conservative lenders apply to multi-tenant flex and industrial product in secondary Pennsylvania markets. We modeled operating expenses including real estate taxes at current assessed values, insurance at replacement cost coverage standards lenders require, management at 5% of effective gross income, maintenance reserves appropriate for the building's age and deferred maintenance condition, and a capital expenditure reserve. The resulting stabilized NOI for the in-place condition, without any credit for projected lease-up, was approximately $310,000 annually.

Debt service coverage ratio requirements for this asset type and this deal complexity run higher than the 1.20x to 1.25x floor that conventional lenders apply to stabilized, non-distressed commercial assets. For a bankruptcy exit loan on a value-add industrial asset in a secondary market, the realistic minimum DSCR floor from lenders who will actually commit to this transaction is 1.30x to 1.35x, reflecting the additional risk layers present: bankruptcy stigma on the title chain, deferred maintenance requiring reserves, partial vacancy, and the shorter loan terms these lenders offer. We modeled at 1.30x as our base case, acknowledging that the most aggressive lenders might clear at 1.25x but that a feasibility opinion cannot be built around the most aggressive market participant.

Interest rate assumptions for this loan type in the current rate environment required us to price the debt at levels reflecting the lender's required risk premium over benchmark rates. We modeled a floating-rate bridge structure at 300 to 375 basis points over one-month term SOFR, reflecting the pricing range we see from debt funds and balance sheet bridge lenders on transitional secondary market industrial assets with bankruptcy exit complexity. At the time of the analysis, that implied an all-in rate of approximately 8.50% to 9.25%. We used 8.75% as our base case. Because bridge lenders on this deal type do not amortize on a 30-year schedule, we modeled interest-only payments, which is standard for 12-to-36 month bridge facilities on transitional assets. This is materially different from permanent loan underwriting and materially affects the maximum supportable loan amount.

At $310,000 NOI, a 1.30x DSCR requirement, and an 8.75% interest-only rate, the maximum supportable annual debt service is approximately $238,500, yielding a maximum loan amount of approximately $2.725 million on an interest-only basis. We then applied LTV constraints. For bankruptcy exit bridge loans on secondary market industrial assets, lenders in this execution channel cap LTV at 60% to 65% of as-is appraised value, not as-stabilized value. The as-is appraisal for the property established value at approximately $4.4 million. At 65% LTV, the maximum loan from an LTV constraint perspective is approximately $2.86 million. The binding constraint was the DSCR test, yielding a maximum loan of approximately $2.725 million. That figure established the financing feasibility ceiling we presented to the court.

We then compared that ceiling to the senior secured claim amount asserted in the reorganization proceeding. Because the supportable first-mortgage loan exceeded the principal amount required to satisfy the senior creditor's allowed claim under the Plan, our opinion was that exit financing sufficient to confirm the Plan was feasible within the existing lender market, subject to the conditions and caveats addressed in the complicating factors analysis.

The Complicating Factors

Bankruptcy Estate Title and Lender Comfort. The single most significant structural complication in any bankruptcy exit financing is that the collateral being pledged is an asset of a bankruptcy estate at the time the lender is being asked to commit. Conventional lenders, meaning CMBS conduit lenders, life insurance companies, GSE multifamily lenders, and traditional bank portfolio lenders, do not make commitments to fund loans where the collateral is in active bankruptcy. They require clean fee simple title delivered at closing, with all bankruptcy court claims and stays resolved before their mortgage records. This eliminates the broadest category of lenders from the available pool and concentrates the financing universe in the debt fund and bridge lending market, where lenders are experienced with the complexity and price accordingly.

Deferred Maintenance and Lender Reserve Requirements. Deferred maintenance on a bankruptcy estate asset is not a cosmetic issue in lender underwriting. It directly affects the as-is value supporting the LTV calculation and triggers mandatory holdback and reserve structures. Lenders who will finance this asset will require an independent property condition assessment and will structure a deferred maintenance reserve holdback, typically equal to 100% to 125% of the estimated remediation cost, which reduces the net loan proceeds available at closing. If deferred maintenance estimates run $200,000 or more, the effective net loan proceeds available to satisfy creditors at close may be materially less than the face loan amount, and that distinction matters critically in a feasibility analysis presented to a bankruptcy court.

Partial Vacancy and Lease-Up Risk. Multi-tenant flex buildings with partial vacancy in secondary markets present a specific underwriting challenge. Lenders evaluating York County industrial and flex assets will discount or entirely ignore any vacant suite in their income underwriting, as noted above. However, they will also scrutinize lease terms carefully: a tenant with less than 12 months remaining on a lease at closing is treated as economically vacant by most lenders, regardless of current occupancy. In our engagement, several suites had near-term lease expirations, and we had to present a lease-by-lease analysis showing which income streams lenders would credit at face value, which they would discount, and which they would ignore entirely.

Bankruptcy Court Approval Requirements and Lender Timing Sensitivity. Exit financing lenders require a confirmed, effective Plan as a condition to funding. They will not fund into an unconfirmed Plan. This creates a structural timing mismatch that courts and counsel must understand: a lender commitment letter obtained as evidence of feasibility for purposes of confirmation is not the same as a funded loan. Lenders will issue letters of intent for feasibility purposes, but those letters carry conditions and expiration periods, and the gap between a feasibility commitment and an actual funded loan at confirmation is a real risk that must be disclosed in any honest expert opinion. We addressed this directly in our report and offered testimony on the market practice of feasibility letters and their limitations.

The York Lender Market

Debt Funds and Bridge Lenders. The primary execution channel for bankruptcy exit financing on York commercial assets is the institutional debt fund and bridge lending market. These lenders, ranging from nationally recognized alternative investment managers to regional balance-sheet bridge lenders operating across the Mid-Atlantic and Northeast, are the only category that routinely structures loan commitments around bankruptcy exit timelines. They underwrite to as-is asset quality, are accustomed to working within bankruptcy court approval processes, and price their risk accordingly with rates and fees that reflect the complexity. For York industrial and flex assets, bridge lenders in this category will consider loan amounts from approximately $1.5 million to $15 million or more, depending on the platform, with one-to-three year terms, interest-only structures, and extension options tied to leasing performance milestones.

Community Banks and Regional Banks. York County has meaningful community banking depth, with several locally headquartered and regionally focused institutions that maintain active CRE portfolios. These institutions generally do not originate loans where the collateral is in active bankruptcy, but they represent a relevant financing source for the permanent take-out loan that replaces the bridge facility after the property is stabilized and the title is clean. Attorneys and plan proponents should understand that demonstrating the existence of a viable permanent financing market, even if the permanent loan is 18 to 24 months out, strengthens a feasibility argument by showing the exit from the bridge loan is achievable. York-area community banks have historically been active in industrial and owner-occupied commercial lending and are a credible permanent financing reference point.

Private Credit and Family Office Lenders. For smaller loan amounts or for transactions where institutional debt fund pricing is prohibitive relative to plan economics, private credit lenders and family office lending platforms represent a third tier of the market. These lenders price higher than institutional debt funds on a rate basis, but they offer greater flexibility on structure, approval timelines, and documentation, and they often have less rigid requirements around pre-closing conditions. For a York exit financing in the $1 million to $3 million range where speed and certainty matter more than rate optimization, this category of lender deserves serious consideration.

What Attorneys Handling York CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to Retain a CRE Financing Expert. In any Chapter 11 Plan confirmation proceeding where financing feasibility is an element of plan confirmation, counsel should retain a qualified CRE financing expert early, before the disclosure statement hearing if possible. Feasibility objections raised by secured creditors require affirmative rebuttal evidence, and a credible expert opinion supported by actual lender market knowledge is the most effective tool available. Waiting until trial preparation is underway forfeits the ability to use the expert's analysis to structure the Plan's financing contingency provisions intelligently from the outset.

What a CRE Financing Expert Provides That an Appraiser Cannot. Appraisers establish value. They do not establish lender willingness to lend, market credit standards, DSCR requirements, rate and fee assumptions, lender appetite for distressed or bankruptcy-adjacent assets, or the practical mechanics of obtaining a commitment letter in the current market. A CRE financing expert who is actively placing loans in the market provides the bridge between an appraised value and a fundable loan, and that bridge is what courts actually need to evaluate feasibility. These are distinct disciplines and should be retained separately.

Qualifications That Matter. A retired banker who underwrote loans 15 years ago does not have current market knowledge. A CRE financing expert who is actively brokering commercial mortgage transactions, who maintains direct relationships with the specific categories of lenders relevant to the transaction, and who has placed loans of comparable complexity in comparable markets in the current rate environment is in a materially different position to offer credible testimony. Counsel should ask any proposed expert for specific examples of comparable transactions closed in the prior 12 to 24 months.

Information Counsel Should Gather Before the Expert Engagement. To perform a credible feasibility analysis, a CRE financing expert needs: all executed leases for the property with lease abstracts, current rent rolls, trailing 12-month and trailing 24-month operating statements, the most recent property condition assessment or engineering report, the as-is appraisal and any retrospective appraisals in the record, the current property tax assessment and tax bills, insurance documentation, and a clear statement of the claim amounts the exit financing must satisfy. The faster this information is assembled, the faster a reliable opinion can be produced.

How Expert Testimony Functions at a Confirmation Hearing. In a contested confirmation proceeding, a CRE financing expert typically submits a written Rule 26 report detailing methodology and conclusions, is made available for deposition by opposing counsel, and testifies at the confirmation hearing subject to cross-examination. The testimony addresses whether the proposed financing is feasible based on current market conditions, and the expert must be prepared to defend every assumption, rate selection, DSCR threshold, and lender category reference against cross-examination by opposing counsel who may retain their own expert to contest the conclusions.

Key Takeaways

  • York's industrial and flex fundamentals support meaningful financing. The I-83 corridor's industrial demand drivers give York County commercial assets genuine lender appeal, which directly affects the feasibility ceiling available to support a Plan of Reorganization.
  • In-place income, not pro forma income, governs the analysis. Lenders underwriting bankruptcy exit loans will not credit projected lease-up or optimistic stabilization assumptions. The feasibility opinion must be built on contractual, documented, in-place cash flows, period.
  • The DSCR floor for this deal type is higher than for conventional transactions. The 1.20x to 1.25x benchmark applicable to clean stabilized assets rises to 1.30x to 1.35x for bankruptcy exit bridge loans on transitional secondary market assets, and every assumption must be calibrated to what actual lenders in this execution channel require.
  • Deferred maintenance creates a holdback that reduces net proceeds.

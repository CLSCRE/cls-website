Expert Witness: Commercial Loan Modification Standards in Tucson, AZ

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Loan Modification and Workout Dispute

The Tucson Market Context

Tucson is not a secondary market in the casual sense of the word. It is a structurally distinct commercial real estate environment with its own supply-demand dynamics, institutional capital presence, and debt market characteristics that differ materially from Phoenix, even though both sit within Arizona. Attorneys and borrowers who import assumptions from the Phoenix MSA into a Tucson workout analysis will frequently reach the wrong conclusions, and that analytical error can be fatal in litigation.

For loan modification and workout disputes specifically, the Tucson market presents several defining characteristics. Multifamily has been the strongest performing asset class, with vacancy rates in well-located submarkets like Midtown, Rincon, and the University Boulevard corridor running in the 4 to 6 percent range through most of the 2022 to 2024 period. However, new supply delivered in 2023 and 2024, particularly in the downtown and Reid Park adjacent submarkets, has begun to exert rent pressure on Class B product. This is precisely the kind of market-level deterioration that generates workout situations: a borrower who underwrote 2021 rent growth assumptions on a 2022 acquisition is now sitting on a property where effective rents have softened 5 to 10 percent from peak, while the debt service obligation was sized to those peak assumptions.

Retail in Tucson has shown more resilience than national narratives suggest. Anchored neighborhood centers along Oracle Road, Speedway, and the Foothills submarkets have maintained occupancies in the low-to-mid 90 percent range, supported by a relatively stable tenant base in healthcare, food, and service uses. Unanchored strip retail and older power center product is where stress has concentrated. Industrial, driven partly by defense contractor activity at Raytheon and Davis-Monthan adjacent supply chains, has been tight, with vacancy below 5 percent in functional warehouse and flex product. That said, Tucson's industrial base is considerably thinner in absolute square footage than Phoenix, which means a single large tenant departure can move submarket vacancy metrics meaningfully, creating workout-triggering covenant violations that may not reflect true credit deterioration.

Office in Tucson reflects the national pattern but with local nuance. The University of Arizona ecosystem sustains demand for research-adjacent and medical office product, while traditional suburban office has struggled. Class B and C office product in the midtown corridor is where modification disputes are most likely to originate, as borrowers with maturing loans on partially vacant office buildings cannot refinance at par and are in active negotiation with lenders over maturity extensions and principal write-downs.

The Engagement

Counsel representing the borrower in a contested workout matter retained Commercial Lending Solutions to analyze the lender's conduct during a loan modification process involving a suburban multifamily property in the Tucson MSA. The asset was a 120-unit garden-style apartment community that had been acquired in 2021, financed with a short-term bridge loan carrying a two-year initial term with three one-year extension options. The borrower had exercised the first extension option but encountered resistance when seeking the second extension, during which the lender imposed modification conditions that the borrower argued were commercially unreasonable and inconsistent with market practice.

The core legal questions presented to CLS CRE were threefold. First, did the lender's conduct during the modification negotiation, including information requests, processing timelines, and the imposition of interim cash management requirements, conform to industry standards of care for commercial mortgage loan workouts? Second, were the modification terms ultimately proposed by the lender, including a principal paydown requirement, a rate increase, and a personal guaranty expansion, commercially reasonable given the property's actual financial performance and the prevailing debt market for comparable Tucson multifamily assets? Third, what was the maximum supportable loan amount on the subject property under market-standard financing parameters, and did that figure support or undermine the borrower's position that refinancing was feasible but was being structurally blocked by the lender's conduct?

These are not questions an appraiser can fully answer. An appraiser establishes value. A CRE financing expert establishes what debt the market would actually place against that value, under what terms, from which lender categories, and whether any party's conduct during the workout process deviated from how sophisticated commercial mortgage market participants actually behave.

The Financial Analysis

The methodology began with a reconstruction of the property's trailing twelve-month net operating income. The property had 120 units with an average in-place rent of $1,175 per month, generating gross potential rent of approximately $1,692,000 annually. Applying a market-supported vacancy and credit loss factor of 7 percent, consistent with conditions in the Tucson multifamily submarket during the relevant period, produced effective gross income of approximately $1,573,560. Operating expenses, including property taxes at the Arizona assessed valuation, insurance, management at 5 percent of EGI, maintenance, and reserves for replacement at $300 per unit annually, totaled approximately $630,000. This produced a stabilized NOI of approximately $943,560.

The DSCR threshold applied was 1.25x, which is the standard minimum for conventional multifamily financing from agency lenders (Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac), life insurance companies, and most regional bank balance sheet programs. Some bridge lenders will underwrite to 1.10x or 1.15x on a going-in basis when projecting stabilization, but for a maturity extension on an existing stabilized asset, 1.25x is the correct benchmark. Using an interest-only debt service calculation at a market rate of 7.25 percent, consistent with prevailing conventional multifamily rates in the second half of 2023, the maximum annual debt service supportable at 1.25x DSCR is $754,848 ($943,560 divided by 1.25). That debt service figure, annualized, supports a loan balance of approximately $10,394,000 at the stated rate on an interest-only basis.

For amortizing loans, which are the standard for permanent financing, a 30-year amortization schedule at 7.25 percent produces a monthly payment factor of approximately $0.006824 per dollar of loan. The maximum loan supportable on a fully amortizing basis at 1.25x DSCR is therefore approximately $9,280,000. The property had been appraised during the workout period at $12,400,000, implying a maximum LTV of approximately 75 percent under agency guidelines and 70 percent under most life company programs. The LTV-constrained maximum loan at 75 percent is $9,300,000, which is nearly identical to the DSCR-constrained figure, confirming that the underwriting is binding on both dimensions simultaneously, which is actually the normal condition in a well-underwritten deal.

The lender's proposed modification required a principal paydown to $8,500,000 as a condition of the second extension. Our analysis showed that the market-supportable loan at current rates and actual NOI was approximately $9,280,000 to $9,300,000, meaning the lender's paydown requirement was demanding approximately $800,000 more than what underwriting discipline actually required. That $800,000 gap became a central exhibit in the damages analysis, because it represented a principal reduction that was not supported by any credit metric the market would actually impose.

The Complicating Factors

Rate environment timing and modification sequencing. The subject loan was originated in 2021 at a floating rate tied to SOFR plus a spread, when the benchmark rate was effectively zero. By the time the borrower sought the second extension in late 2023, SOFR had risen to approximately 5.30 percent. The lender's proposed modification maintained the same spread but added a rate floor provision that had not existed in the original loan documents. From a market practice standpoint, adding a rate floor during a workout extension, rather than at origination, is procedurally unusual and creates a one-sided economic benefit for the lender that is not consistent with arm's-length modification practice. Lenders in workout situations are entitled to improve their credit position, but introducing new economic terms that have no relationship to credit protection, and that would not be imposed in a market-rate refinancing, is a recognized deviation from standard practice.

Cash management sweep provisions. The lender imposed a full cash management sweep upon the borrower's request for the second extension, directing all property cash flows to a lender-controlled account with disbursements subject to lender approval for all line items above $5,000. In a genuine distress situation, cash management is a standard and appropriate lender tool. The complicating factor here was timing: the property was current on debt service, had maintained occupancy above 93 percent throughout the modification negotiation period, and had no material lease expirations pending. Imposing a full cash management sweep on a performing asset, without a declared default, during a modification negotiation is a pressure tactic that is inconsistent with how institutional lenders handle performing loans in workout discussions. Regional bank practice in Tucson and Arizona generally mirrors this: cash management is triggered by default or material covenant breach, not by the borrower's request for an extension on a current loan.

Personal guaranty expansion. The original loan carried a standard non-recourse carve-out guaranty covering bad acts and environmental indemnification. The lender's proposed modification required the guarantor to execute a full repayment guaranty for the duration of the extension period. This is the most legally significant deviation from market practice identified in our analysis. In the commercial mortgage market, conversion from non-recourse to full recourse during a workout is reserved for situations involving material credit deterioration, covenant default, or evidence of bad actor conduct. Converting a performing, current loan to full recourse as a condition of a routine extension, in the absence of any of those triggering conditions, has no precedent in standard commercial mortgage workout practice and represents a material deviation from the industry standard of care.

Information request scope and timeline. The lender issued fourteen separate information requests over a seven-month period, with overlapping and partially duplicative document demands. Industry standard for a multifamily extension modification of this complexity is a single consolidated due diligence checklist issued within thirty days of the extension request, with a sixty to ninety day processing timeline. Seven months of rolling requests on a stabilized, performing 120-unit apartment asset is not consistent with the processing timelines that institutional commercial mortgage servicers apply to comparable situations.

The Tucson Lender Market

Understanding which lenders are actually active in Tucson for stabilized multifamily is essential context for any workout dispute, because the available refinancing alternatives establish the borrower's leverage position and the commercial reasonableness of any modification terms.

Agency lending through Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac designated seller-servicers is fully active in Tucson for multifamily assets of 5 units and above that meet occupancy and financial performance thresholds. Both agencies have placed loans on Tucson apartment product consistently, and their credit boxes, 1.25x DSCR, 75 to 80 percent LTV for Fannie and 75 percent for Freddie on conventional product, are the market benchmarks against which any modification terms should be measured. Agency pricing in the 2023 to 2024 period ranged from approximately 6.50 to 7.50 percent for conventional fixed-rate product, depending on term and amortization structure.

Life insurance companies are selectively active in Tucson multifamily for assets above approximately $8 million in loan size. Life company execution requires stronger debt coverage, typically 1.30x or better, and maximum LTV of 65 to 70 percent, but offers the lowest fixed-rate pricing available in the conventional market. For the subject property, life company financing would have been at the margin of eligibility based on DSCR but not impossible with modest additional principal paydown.

Regional and community banks represent a meaningful segment of the Tucson commercial lending market. Institutions headquartered in Arizona and active in Tucson balance sheet lending include several with strong multifamily programs at 70 to 75 percent LTV, floating or adjustable rates, and 3 to 5 year terms. These institutions are relationship-driven and typically process modification requests on performing loans within 60 to 90 days. Their credit culture is relevant context when evaluating whether a large institutional servicer's seven-month processing timeline was reasonable by local market standards.

CMBS conduit lending is possible for Tucson multifamily above $2 million in loan size but is less commonly used than agency execution due to the rigidity of conduit servicing and the availability of agency alternatives. For workout disputes involving CMBS loans specifically, the special servicer's conduct is governed by pooling and servicing agreement standards, and deviation from those standards is a separate line of analysis.

What Attorneys Handling Tucson CRE Disputes Need to Know

The threshold question for any attorney considering a CRE financing expert is whether the dispute turns on facts that require specialized capital markets knowledge to interpret. If the case involves a lender's decision to modify, foreclose, or impose conditions during a workout, the answer is almost always yes. Here is what a qualified CRE financing expert provides that an appraiser, an accountant, or a general real estate expert cannot.

Maximum supportable debt analysis. An appraiser tells the court what the property is worth. A CRE financing expert tells the court how much debt the market would actually place against that property, under what terms, and from which lender categories. These are different questions. A property appraised at $12 million does not automatically support a $9 million loan; the debt capacity depends on current market rates, applicable DSCR thresholds, LTV constraints, and the specific lender categories for which the asset qualifies. Expert testimony on debt capacity is frequently outcome-determinative in disputes about whether a borrower could have refinanced, whether a deficiency claim is valid, or whether a lender's modification terms were commercially reasonable.

Industry standard of care for lender conduct. Workout practice has established norms around processing timelines, information request scope, cash management triggers, and modification term structure. These norms are not written in a single regulatory document; they emerge from market practice across thousands of commercial mortgage modifications annually. A qualified expert who actively originates, structures, and closes commercial loans has direct, current knowledge of these norms. A retired banker whose last origination was in 2015 may not adequately represent current market practice, and opposing counsel should probe that gap aggressively on cross-examination.

Financing feasibility in context. In a lender liability case, one key question is often whether the lender's conduct caused the borrower to lose an opportunity to refinance or to sell. Answering that question requires a current market participant who can testify about what refinancing terms were available at the relevant point in time, what the borrower's realistic execution options were, and whether the lender's conduct materially impaired access to those options.

Before retaining a CRE financing expert, counsel should assemble the complete loan file including origination documents, all modification correspondence, servicer notes, financial statements submitted by the borrower, and any third-party reports ordered during the workout process. The expert will also need current rent rolls, operating statements, and any leasing activity or market reports prepared near the time of the dispute. The broader the documentary record, the more defensible the expert's factual assumptions become under cross-examination.

In terms of trial use, CRE financing expert testimony is most effective when it is grounded in quantitative analysis, as described in the financial analysis section above, and when it can point to specific market comparables for lender conduct: actual modification timelines, typical extension conditions, and prevailing DSCR and LTV benchmarks from the relevant time period. Vague opinions about what lenders "generally do" are vulnerable; specific benchmarks tied to identifiable market data are not.

Key Takeaways

  • DSCR and LTV analysis are the foundation. Any dispute about modification terms must begin with a rigorous reconstruction of the property's actual NOI and a determination of the maximum supportable loan at market-standard underwriting thresholds. A 1.25x DSCR minimum is the appropriate benchmark for stabilized multifamily, and lender-imposed paydown requirements that exceed what underwriting discipline requires are analytically defensible as commercially unreasonable.
  • Tucson's multifamily market context matters. Vacancy trends, rent softening, and submarket-level dynamics in Tucson are directly relevant to whether a lender's assessment of credit risk during a workout was supportable. Importing Phoenix market assumptions into a Tucson analysis produces incorrect conclusions.
  • Lender conduct during workout has established norms. Processing timelines, cash management triggers, and modification term structure are not discretionary choices; they reflect industry practice that can be benchmarked and tested. Deviations from that practice, including multi-month rolling information requests on performing loans and imposition of full recourse guaranties without default, are identifiable and documentable.

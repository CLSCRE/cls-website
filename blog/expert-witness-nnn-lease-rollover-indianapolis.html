Expert Witness: NNN Lease Rollover Upside and Financing Impact in Indianapolis, IN

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Below-Market NNN Lease and Rollover Upside Analysis

The Indianapolis Market Context

Indianapolis occupies a structural position in the national commercial real estate market that makes single-tenant net lease disputes particularly consequential. The metro has emerged as one of the Midwest's most active industrial and logistics corridors, driven by its location at the intersection of multiple major interstate systems, proximity to FedEx's North American hub at Indianapolis International Airport, and a cost basis that continues to attract distribution and e-commerce fulfillment tenants relocating from higher-cost coastal markets. Industrial vacancy in the Indianapolis market has hovered in the 4 to 6 percent range in recent years, with asking rents for Class A bulk distribution space pushing toward the $5.00 to $6.50 per square foot range on a net basis, a meaningful increase from the $3.50 to $4.25 range that characterized lease executions from five to eight years ago. That rent growth gap is precisely the environment in which below-market lease disputes arise.

The retail net lease segment in Indianapolis reflects a bifurcated market. Credit-tenanted single-tenant assets anchored by investment-grade operators, particularly in suburban corridors like Fishers, Carmel, Noblesville, and Avon, trade at cap rates in the 5.5 to 6.75 percent range depending on tenant credit, lease term remaining, and rent escalation structure. Older single-tenant assets in secondary Indianapolis submarkets, or those with lease expirations within three to five years, face cap rate compression in reverse: buyers demand higher yields to compensate for rollover risk, which directly reduces collateral value and constrains loan sizing. This is the dynamic at the center of most financing disputes involving below-market NNN leases in this market. The asset may appear to have rollover upside, but lenders do not underwrite to upside. They underwrite to what is contractually in place today, and that discipline is absolute in the current credit environment.

Indianapolis also has a relatively deep community and regional banking presence compared to many comparable metros, with institutions headquartered locally or regionally that maintain active commercial real estate lending programs. That depth matters in litigation because it expands the range of financing alternatives a qualified expert can analyze and opine upon, and it means the question of whether a loan was feasible at a given point in time is not simply a function of national capital market conditions alone.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing one of the parties in a commercial real estate dispute involving a freestanding single-tenant retail property in the Indianapolis metropolitan area. The asset was subject to a long-term NNN lease that had been executed several years prior to the dispute, at rental rates that had since fallen materially below current market rents for comparable space in the submarket. The remaining lease term at the time of the relevant financing date was sufficient to satisfy minimum lender hold requirements but short enough to introduce meaningful rollover risk within the lender's standard loan term horizon.

Counsel retained CLS CRE to address a set of interconnected questions that an appraisal alone could not resolve. The appraisal had established a value conclusion, but counsel needed expert analysis of how institutional and regional lenders would actually underwrite the in-place lease versus the market rent potential, what loan proceeds the property could support at the time in question, whether the below-market rent structure functionally constrained refinancing at the LTV and debt service coverage ratios the borrower had claimed were achievable, and how lenders in the Indianapolis market price rollover risk in their credit decisions. These are financing structure questions, not valuation questions, and they require a different discipline than appraising the asset.

Our scope included a review of the executed lease, rent comps for the submarket, lender term sheets and credit guidelines applicable to the asset class and market at the relevant date, and the borrower's claimed refinancing projections. We then produced a written analysis addressing the maximum supportable loan proceeds under market-standard underwriting, the financing universe available to the borrower given the lease structure, and the credibility of the borrower's financing assumptions.

The Financial Analysis

The analytical foundation in any below-market NNN lease financing dispute is the underwritten net operating income. Lenders do not underwrite to appraised market rent. They underwrite to in-place contractual rent, net of any landlord obligations under the lease, adjusted for vacancy and credit loss assumptions appropriate to the tenancy. In a single-tenant NNN structure, the NOI calculation is relatively straightforward: the in-place base rent, less a minimal vacancy reserve (often 5 percent even for occupied single-tenant assets, applied as a credit quality buffer), less any non-reimbursable expenses the landlord retains. The result is the underwritten NOI that drives loan sizing.

For this engagement, the property was generating gross rental income of approximately $420,000 per year on a full NNN basis. Market rent for comparable space in the submarket at the time of the relevant financing date was approximately $530,000 to $560,000 per year, representing a below-market discount of roughly 20 to 25 percent. Applying a standard 5 percent vacancy and credit loss reserve to the in-place rent produced an underwritten NOI of approximately $399,000.

Lenders in the single-tenant retail NNN space typically apply a debt service coverage ratio floor of 1.25x for stabilized assets with investment-grade tenancy, and 1.30x to 1.35x for assets with below-investment-grade tenants or lease terms with near-term rollover exposure. Given that the remaining lease term introduced rollover risk within the standard 10-year loan horizon, a 1.30x DSCR floor was the appropriate market-standard threshold to apply. At a 1.30x DSCR, the maximum allowable annual debt service was $306,923 ($399,000 divided by 1.30).

Using a 30-year amortization schedule and an interest rate of 6.75 percent, which reflected market-rate fixed pricing for this asset class and credit profile at the relevant financing date, the maximum supportable loan against that debt service constraint was approximately $4,150,000. That calculation uses a standard mortgage constant of approximately 7.39 percent for a 30-year amortization at 6.75 percent, applied as: $306,923 divided by 0.0739, yielding a maximum loan of $4,152,000.

The appraised value of the property, based on the in-place income capitalized at a market cap rate for the asset class, was approximately $6,200,000 using the below-market rent stream, and approximately $7,800,000 applying a market rent NOI at a tighter cap rate reflecting the rollover upside. The critical distinction is this: lenders do not lend against the $7,800,000 value. They lend against the income the property actually produces today. At a 65 percent LTV ceiling that is standard for single-tenant retail with rollover risk, the LTV-constrained maximum loan against the in-place value was $4,030,000, consistent with the DSCR-derived ceiling. Both constraints converged on a maximum loan in the $4.0 to $4.2 million range.

The borrower's claimed refinancing projections had assumed loan proceeds of $5,500,000, which would have required either a DSCR of approximately 1.03x at the in-place rent or an LTV of approximately 71 percent, both of which fell outside every institutional lender's credit box for this asset type at this stage of the credit cycle. That projection was not achievable on the in-place lease economics. It was only theoretically achievable if a lender were willing to underwrite to the market rent potential rather than the contractual in-place rent, which no regulated lending institution does for an occupied single-tenant asset with a below-market lease already in place.

The Complicating Factors

Rollover risk and lease term haircuts. The most significant complicating factor in this engagement was the interaction between remaining lease term and lender hold period. Most institutional lenders require that the lease term extend through the full loan term, or at minimum through a point that is 12 to 24 months beyond the loan maturity date. When a lease expires within the loan term, lenders apply what is commonly called a "dark store" or "going dark" analysis: they stress-test the loan's performance assuming the tenant vacates at lease expiration and the property sits vacant during a re-tenanting period. In the Indianapolis single-tenant retail market, lenders applying this analysis typically assume a six to twelve month absorption period for a vacancy event, and they size the loan to survive that period without a debt service shortfall. This constraint further reduces the maximum loan proceeds below what a simple NOI-to-DSCR calculation would suggest.

Rollover upside as a non-lending criterion. One of the most consistent misconceptions in these disputes is the assumption that a lender will credit rollover upside in their loan sizing. The argument runs as follows: the in-place rent is below market, but when the lease rolls, the new market rent will produce significantly higher NOI, which supports a larger loan. This argument fails as a matter of institutional lending practice. Lenders underwrite to the income the property produces today under executed contracts. They do not lend against speculative future income. The rollover upside may affect how a buyer prices the asset, and it may affect a cap rate selection in an appraisal, but it has no role in the bank's or CMBS conduit's loan sizing model. Expert testimony that conflates rollover upside as a value driver with rollover upside as a lending driver is analytically incorrect and can be demonstrated to be so through lender credit guidelines and term sheets.

Tenant credit and its effect on the financing universe. In Indianapolis, as in any market, the credit quality of the single tenant dictates which lending categories will engage. Investment-grade tenants, defined as BBB- or better by a major rating agency, unlock the CMBS execution and life insurance company market. Below-investment-grade or unrated tenants push the financing into the regional bank, credit union, and private debt fund space, where LTV ceilings tighten to 55 to 65 percent and pricing premiums of 75 to 150 basis points are common. If the tenant in a dispute is an unrated regional operator rather than a national credit brand, the entire financing universe shifts, and the maximum achievable loan proceeds shrink accordingly. This is material to damages calculations in financing disputes.

Lender reserve requirements for near-term rollover. Regional and community banks active in Indianapolis frequently impose lease rollover reserves on single-tenant loans when the lease expires within the loan term. These reserves are cash held back from loan proceeds or swept from operations into a segregated account, typically sized at three to six months of contractual rent, to fund re-tenanting costs and carrying costs during a vacancy. The reserve requirement reduces net loan proceeds to the borrower below the gross loan amount, which is another factor that borrower-side financing projections often fail to account for.

The Indianapolis Lender Market

The lender landscape for single-tenant NNN assets in Indianapolis segments into four meaningful categories, each with distinct credit parameters and relevance to this type of dispute.

CMBS conduit lenders are active in Indianapolis for single-tenant assets with investment-grade tenancy, loan amounts above $3,000,000, and lease terms that clear the loan maturity date. Conduit pricing in the current environment runs 180 to 230 basis points over the corresponding Treasury, with LTV ceilings of 65 to 70 percent on stabilized assets and DSCR floors of 1.25x. Below-market leases are not an automatic disqualifier for CMBS execution, but the conduit will underwrite strictly to the in-place rent, and any rollover within the loan term typically triggers a lease rollover reserve or a cash management structure that constrains borrower access to cash flow. CMBS is generally unavailable for assets with leases expiring within five years of origination.

Life insurance companies represent the most favorable execution for high-quality single-tenant NNN assets in Indianapolis, offering the lowest pricing, typically 10-year fixed rates in the 5.75 to 6.25 percent range, with the most conservative LTV ceilings of 55 to 65 percent. Life companies require investment-grade tenancy, lease terms fully covering the loan term, and demonstrated rent stability. They will not engage on a below-market lease dispute asset unless the credit profile and lease term are otherwise pristine.

Regional and community banks are the most relevant lending category for Indianapolis single-tenant assets in the $2,000,000 to $6,000,000 range with credit complexity. Several Indianapolis-area institutions and Ohio and Illinois-based regional banks with active Indianapolis presence maintain single-tenant NNN programs that accommodate below-investment-grade tenants and shorter remaining lease terms, at pricing premiums and tighter LTV constraints. These lenders are also the most likely source for construction-to-permanent financing on new NNN development in the market. Their credit files are typically more detailed and discretionary than conduit execution, and their underwriting overlays, particularly regarding lease rollover risk, are negotiable within limits.

Debt funds and bridge lenders become relevant when the asset's lease structure or credit quality falls outside bank and conduit boxes. Bridge pricing in Indianapolis for this asset type runs 300 to 450 basis points over SOFR, with origination fees of 1.0 to 2.0 percent and 12 to 36-month terms. Bridge financing is relevant to a dispute analysis when the question is whether the borrower could have obtained any financing at all, as opposed to permanent financing at a specific LTV. The existence of bridge availability at punitive economics does not establish that permanent financing at claimed terms was achievable.

What Attorneys Handling Indianapolis CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to retain a CRE financing expert. Counsel should engage a commercial real estate financing expert any time the dispute involves a question about whether a specific loan was obtainable, what loan proceeds a property could have supported at a given point in time, whether a borrower's financing assumptions were reasonable, or how lender underwriting of a specific lease structure would have affected loan sizing or feasibility. Appraisers establish value. Financing experts establish whether, at that value, a loan of a specific size and structure was achievable in the market that existed at the relevant date. These are different questions that require different expertise.

What a financing expert provides that an appraiser cannot. Appraisers are trained to value assets, not to opine on lending markets. A financing expert analyzes lender credit guidelines, term sheet structures, DSCR calculations, LTV policies, reserve requirements, and the actual behavior of capital markets at a specific point in time. In a below-market NNN lease dispute, the expert can testify with specificity about which lenders would have engaged, at what proceeds, at what pricing, and under what structural conditions. That testimony is not available from an appraiser, regardless of their experience level.

Qualifications that matter. The relevant qualification for a CRE financing expert is active deal origination experience, not academic credentials or retired lending titles. An expert who has personally structured and closed commercial mortgage transactions at volume within the past three to five years, across multiple lender categories and deal types, can speak to what the market actually required at the relevant date. CLS CRE has approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, more than 1,000 active lender relationships, and active origination activity across all 50 states. That current market access is what makes expert testimony credible on a financing feasibility question.

Information counsel should gather before the engagement. To conduct a complete financing analysis, counsel should provide the executed lease and all amendments, the rent roll with comps if available, any appraisals or broker opinions of value, any existing or prior loan documents, the borrower's claimed financing projections or pro formas, any lender communications or term sheets, and the specific date as of which the financing feasibility question is being analyzed. The as-of date is critical because interest rates, lender credit standards, and market conditions all affect the analysis, and a conclusion that was accurate in 2021 may be materially different from one applicable in 2023 or 2024.

How financing expert testimony is used. In trial settings, CRE financing expert testimony typically addresses loan feasibility, maximum supportable proceeds, and the credibility of a borrower's or counterparty's financing projections. In bankruptcy plan confirmation hearings, expert testimony on refinancing feasibility is frequently used to establish or contest whether a proposed plan is confirmable based on the debtor's ability to refinance at plan maturity. In breach of contract and specific performance matters, financing expert analysis can establish whether a buyer's financing contingency failure was justified by actual market conditions or whether financing was available and the failure was manufactured.

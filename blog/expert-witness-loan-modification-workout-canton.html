Expert Witness: Commercial Loan Modification Standards in Canton, OH

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Loan Modification and Workout Dispute

The Canton Market Context

Canton, Ohio sits at an interesting intersection in the Midwest commercial real estate landscape. It is not a primary market, but it is far from a tertiary afterthought. Stark County's industrial base, anchored by manufacturing, distribution, and logistics corridors along the I-77 and US-30 corridors, has given the Canton submarket a level of economic durability that many comparably sized Ohio cities lack. That durability matters enormously when a loan modification or workout dispute lands in litigation, because the market fundamentals at the time the modification was negotiated are directly relevant to whether the lender's conduct was commercially reasonable.

Industrial vacancy in the Canton-Massillon submarket has generally tracked in the 4% to 7% range over recent years, reflecting steady demand from light manufacturing and regional distribution users. Effective rents for functional industrial product in Stark County have ranged from approximately $4.50 to $7.50 per square foot NNN depending on clear height, dock configuration, and proximity to logistics corridors. For retail, the picture is more bifurcated: well-located community and neighborhood centers with grocery or pharmacy anchors have maintained occupancy above 90%, while power centers and secondary strip retail have struggled, with vacancy in certain corridors running 15% or higher. Multifamily fundamentals in Canton have held up reasonably well given the relative affordability of the market, with market-rate apartment communities achieving occupancy in the low-to-mid 90% range and average rents for class B and C product in the $700 to $950 per unit per month range, though cap rates in this market have historically compressed far less than in Columbus or Cleveland.

Office is the asset class where Canton's fundamentals have deteriorated most visibly. Suburban and downtown Canton office vacancy has climbed meaningfully since 2020, with effective vacancy including shadow space likely running above 18% to 22% in certain corridors. This context is not background noise in a workout dispute. It is the factual foundation for every question about whether a lender's modification terms were commercially reasonable, whether a forbearance was granted in good faith, and whether the borrower had a realistic path to stabilization that the lender ignored or manufactured.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing the borrower in a dispute arising from the lender's handling of a loan modification and forbearance process on a mixed-use commercial property in the Canton submarket. The asset in question was a mid-size retail and office complex that had experienced material tenant attrition during the pandemic period, resulting in a significant decline in net operating income relative to underwriting at origination. The borrower had entered into what the parties characterized as a preliminary forbearance agreement, followed by extended modification negotiations that ultimately broke down without a finalized workout agreement. The lender subsequently moved toward acceleration and enforcement.

Counsel retained CLS CRE to address a specific set of financial and industry-standard questions that an appraiser alone could not answer. Those questions included: whether the modification terms the lender proposed were commercially reasonable given the property's then-current and projected cash flow; whether the lender's conduct during the forbearance period was consistent with prevailing commercial real estate workout practices; whether a prudent lender operating in the Canton market with this collateral type would have proceeded differently; and whether an alternative modification structure existed that was both financially viable and within the range of what lenders of this type were actually doing in comparable situations at the relevant time. These are financing structure and lender conduct questions, not valuation questions, and the distinction matters considerably in how expert testimony is framed and used.

The Financial Analysis

The core of our analysis began with a reconstruction of the property's stabilized and actual net operating income at the time the modification negotiations took place. This required working through the rent roll line by line, categorizing tenants by lease structure, remaining term, creditworthiness, and whether each lease represented in-place cash flow or a theoretical stabilization assumption. For a Canton retail and office asset in a distressed modification context, this distinction is critical: lenders in a workout will typically underwrite only to in-place, collected rent, not to pro forma occupancy assumptions.

In this engagement, the property's in-place gross revenues were approximately $1,050,000 annually, against stabilized underwriting at origination of approximately $1,375,000. After vacancy, credit loss, and operating expenses, the reconstructed actual NOI was approximately $565,000. The lender's proposed modification terms were premised on a stabilized NOI figure of approximately $780,000, which assumed lease-up of vacant space within 18 months. That assumption required us to interrogate Canton submarket absorption data, comparable lease comps, and the realistic probability of lease execution in the relevant product type and corridor.

The industry standard DSCR threshold for a lender modifying a loan on a mixed-use retail and office asset in a non-primary market is 1.20x at a minimum, with many institutional lenders and life company servicers requiring 1.25x on a stressed basis. Applying a 1.20x DSCR to the actual in-place NOI of $565,000 yields a maximum supportable annual debt service of approximately $470,800. At a modification interest rate of 6.50%, which was consistent with prevailing workout loan pricing in the relevant period for this collateral type, and on a 25-year amortization schedule, that annual debt service figure supports a maximum loan balance of approximately $6,300,000.

The original loan balance at the time of modification negotiations was approximately $8,200,000. At the lender's own most recent appraisal, the property value was approximately $9,500,000 under a stabilized assumption, and approximately $7,800,000 on an as-is basis. The LTV on an as-is basis against the outstanding balance was therefore approximately 105%, meaning the loan was modestly underwater against as-is value. The cash flow supportable loan, however, was only $6,300,000, a shortfall of approximately $1,900,000 against the outstanding balance.

This analysis established that the lender's proposed modification, which did not include any principal reduction and required full debt service resumption within 90 days, was not supportable by in-place cash flow under any reasonable DSCR standard. It also established that a commercially reasonable workout structure would have required either a principal write-down, a longer interest-only period indexed to actual lease-up milestones, or a structure that bifurcated the loan into a performing component and a hope note. All three of these structures are within the range of what commercial real estate workout practitioners actually do, and the lender's failure to engage with any of them was a central element of the dispute.

The Complicating Factors

Cash flow impairment from tenant attrition during forbearance: One of the most technically nuanced issues in this engagement was the interaction between the forbearance agreement and the borrower's ability to retain and renew tenants. Forbearance agreements in commercial real estate typically contain covenants that restrict the borrower's ability to enter into new leases or modify existing ones without lender consent. When a lender delays responding to lease modification or renewal requests during a forbearance period, that delay directly causes further cash flow impairment. In this matter, the evidence showed that the lender took an average of 47 days to respond to lease approval requests during the forbearance period, and that at least two tenants vacated in part because of delays in executing lease amendments that required lender consent. Industry practice in this area is well established: lenders with consent rights over leases are expected to respond within 10 to 20 business days, and serial delays constitute conduct inconsistent with good faith workout practice.

The stabilized value versus as-is value problem: A recurring complication in loan modification disputes is that servicers and lenders frequently commission appraisals premised on a stabilized occupancy assumption rather than an as-is condition. When the lender then uses the stabilized value to resist principal reduction while simultaneously refusing to grant the borrower sufficient time to achieve stabilization, the result is a circular logic problem that is commercially unreasonable. In Canton's retail and office market, where absorption timelines for vacant space are measurably longer than in primary markets, the gap between stabilized value and as-is value can be substantial. An expert with financing market knowledge can demonstrate this circularity in a way that an appraiser working from a single valuation date cannot.

Servicer incentive misalignment in CMBS context: If the loan was securitized, the special servicer operating under a pooling and servicing agreement faces a different incentive structure than a portfolio lender. Special servicers are compensated on fees tied to workout resolution and may have structural incentives that do not align with maximizing recovery for all certificate holders, let alone with acting in the borrower's interest. Understanding how PSA provisions constrain and shape servicer conduct is essential context for evaluating whether the servicer's actions were commercially reasonable or were shaped by incentive misalignment. This is an area where a CRE financing expert with capital markets experience adds analysis that neither an appraiser nor a generic banking expert can provide.

The Canton Lender Market

Understanding which lenders are actually active in the Canton commercial real estate market, and what their credit parameters look like, is essential to any expert analysis of whether a modification was commercially reasonable or whether alternative financing was feasible. CLS CRE maintains relationships with more than 1,000 lenders across 50 states, and our active engagement in Ohio markets gives us specific knowledge of the Canton lending environment.

For the asset type at issue in this engagement, the relevant lender categories are as follows. Community and regional banks headquartered in Stark County and greater northeast Ohio are the most active balance sheet lenders in this market. Institutions in this category typically lend to 65% to 70% LTV on stabilized value for retail and office product, require DSCR of 1.25x or better on in-place cash flow, and carry relationship-driven credit cultures that genuinely consider workout restructuring as a first option before enforcement. Their workout playbooks are less constrained by PSA provisions and investor reporting obligations than CMBS servicers, and their decision-making timelines are shorter.

Credit unions with commercial lending authority represent a secondary source in this market, particularly for smaller balance transactions under $3,000,000. Their credit boxes are conservative, generally requiring 1.30x DSCR and strong personal guarantees, but they carry flexibility on modification structures that bank examiners often scrutinize more heavily.

For larger transactions above $7,500,000, institutional lenders including life companies and CMBS conduit originators have been present in Canton for industrial and multifamily product but are largely absent from office and secondary retail. This means that a borrower on a large Canton retail or office asset who cannot obtain a modification from the existing lender faces a genuinely constrained refinance market, a fact directly relevant to the question of whether the lender's modification terms were commercially reasonable given the realistic alternatives available to the borrower.

Debt funds and bridge lenders are technically available in this market but price to risk in ways that frequently make them non-viable as a refinance exit for a distressed Canton asset. Bridge rates in the range of 10% to 13% on a short-term structure, combined with origination fees and exit fees, often produce a debt service burden that no realistic Canton retail or office property can cover from in-place cash flow. This pricing reality undermines any lender argument that a borrower "could have refinanced" rather than requiring a modification.

What Attorneys Handling Canton CRE Disputes Need to Know

The first question I am asked by litigation counsel in these matters is when they should retain a CRE financing expert as distinct from, or in addition to, a real estate appraiser. The answer is straightforward: any time the dispute involves the structure, terms, or conduct surrounding a loan origination, modification, forbearance, or workout, you need a financing expert. An appraiser determines what a property is worth. A CRE financing expert determines whether the financing structure applied to that property was commercially reasonable, whether the lender's conduct was consistent with industry standards, and what the actual market of available capital would have supported at the relevant time. These are categorically different questions that require different expertise and different methodologies.

A qualified CRE financing expert is not simply a retired banker or a loan officer who processed applications. The relevant expertise is active, current knowledge of how lenders underwrite, structure, price, and work out loans across multiple capital source categories, combined with transaction-specific analytical capability. An expert who can speak only to one lender category or one product type, or whose market knowledge is several years stale, will struggle under cross-examination on current market standards. At Commercial Lending Solutions, we close transactions across approximately $1 billion in annual volume spanning 50 states, which means our testimony on what lenders are actually doing is grounded in current market practice, not memory.

Before engaging a CRE financing expert, counsel should gather and organize the following materials: the original loan documents including all exhibits and riders; the appraisal or appraisals commissioned at origination and during the workout period; all written communications between the borrower and lender or servicer during the forbearance and modification negotiation period; the rent roll and operating statements for the property covering at minimum the 36 months preceding the dispute; any draft modification or forbearance agreements exchanged by the parties; and the loan's servicing history including any advances, fee assessments, or default notices. With this record, a financing expert can reconstruct the financial picture, assess the lender's conduct against industry benchmarks, and render an opinion on commercial reasonableness with evidentiary rigor.

At trial or in a bankruptcy plan confirmation hearing, CRE financing expert testimony is most powerful when it is specific and quantified. Testimony that a lender's conduct was "inconsistent with industry standards" is far less effective than testimony that demonstrates, through a reconstructed DSCR analysis, that the lender's modification terms required debt service that in-place cash flow could not support at any commercially available interest rate, and that three alternative modification structures existed in the market at the relevant time that would have produced better recovery outcomes while remaining within the range of what lenders of this type actually do. Specificity is credibility in this context.

Key Takeaways

  • In-place cash flow, not pro forma assumptions, controls workout analysis. Any modification or forbearance structure that requires debt service beyond what in-place, collected NOI can support at a 1.20x to 1.25x DSCR is commercially unreasonable by market standard, regardless of what the stabilized appraisal shows.
  • Lender conduct during the forbearance period is subject to industry standard review. Delays in approving leases, responding to modification proposals, or providing required consents during a forbearance period can constitute conduct inconsistent with good faith workout practice, and those delays can be quantified in terms of their cash flow impact.
  • Canton's lender market has meaningful limitations for distressed assets. The realistic refinance universe for distressed office and secondary retail in Stark County is narrow. Life companies and CMBS conduits are effectively absent from these product types in this market. A lender's assertion that a borrower had viable refinance alternatives must be tested against the actual capital available, not a theoretical market.
  • Stabilized value and as-is value create a structural problem in many workout disputes. When a lender uses stabilized appraisal value to resist principal reduction while simultaneously refusing to grant the borrower time to achieve stabilization, that circularity is commercially unreasonable and can be demonstrated through financing market analysis.
  • CMBS special servicer incentives require specific scrutiny. Servicers operating under pooling and servicing agreements have structural constraints and fee incentives that shape their workout conduct in ways that may not reflect commercially reasonable lender behavior. Understanding PSA mechanics is essential to evaluating servicer conduct.
  • A CRE financing expert answers questions an appraiser cannot. Valuation and financing structure analysis are distinct disciplines. Counsel handling workout and modification disputes need both, and conflating them leaves critical questions unanswered at trial.
  • Alternative modification structures are a central element of the analysis. Expert testimony is most effective when it not only critiques the lender's conduct but affirmatively demonstrates that commercially reasonable alternative structures existed and were available in the market at the relevant time.
  • Canton market conditions at the time of the dispute are controlling, not current conditions. Any expert analysis must be anchored to submarket fundamentals, lender underwriting standards, and capital availability as they existed at the moment the modification or forbearance was at issue. Retroactive market analysis based on current conditions is methodologically unreliable and vulnerable on cross-examination.

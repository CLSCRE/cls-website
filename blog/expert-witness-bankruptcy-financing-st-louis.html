Expert Witness: CRE Financing Feasibility in Chapter 11 Proceedings in St. Louis, MO

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Bankruptcy Exit Financing

The St. Louis Market Context

St. Louis is a mid-market metropolitan area with roughly 2.8 million people in its combined statistical area, a diversified economic base, and a commercial real estate landscape that regularly produces contested financing questions in bankruptcy and restructuring proceedings. The market does not receive the institutional attention that coastal gateway cities do, but it is far from a secondary afterthought. Major healthcare systems, logistics and distribution networks anchored by proximity to the Mississippi River and Interstate 70 corridor, and a persistent manufacturing base in the Illinois East Metro submarket all generate meaningful CRE transaction volume. For a creditor or plan proponent in a Chapter 11 matter, understanding the specific supply and demand conditions in St. Louis is not background noise; it is dispositive to whether a lender will underwrite the asset at a loan size sufficient to satisfy the plan.

Industrial fundamentals in St. Louis remain among the stronger asset class metrics in the market. Vacancy in the bulk distribution and light manufacturing sectors has hovered in the 4 to 6 percent range across primary submarkets including Earth City, Fenton, and the I-44 corridor, with asking rents for Class B industrial space running roughly $5.50 to $7.50 per square foot net. Institutional capital has followed, with REIT and life company allocations to St. Louis industrial visible over the past several years. Multifamily fundamentals are more nuanced: the market carries cap rates in the 5.25 to 6.25 percent range for stabilized garden-style product in suburban corridors like Chesterfield, Ballwin, and South County, with concessions increasing modestly in 2024 as new supply absorbed. Office remains structurally challenged, particularly Class B suburban office, where vacancy has pushed above 20 percent in submarkets like Clayton peripheral and West County, putting downward pressure on NOI underwriting across the board. Retail in necessity-anchored formats has held up reasonably well, but unanchored strip and specialty retail in the St. Louis market carries meaningful leasing risk assumptions that lenders price conservatively. Each of these submarket conditions bears directly on how a bankruptcy court should evaluate financing feasibility arguments, and getting the market context right is the foundation of any credible expert opinion.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by counsel representing a secured creditor in a Chapter 11 reorganization proceeding pending in the Eastern District of Missouri. The asset at issue was a mid-size multifamily property in a suburban St. Louis submarket, encumbered by a senior mortgage that had matured pre-petition, with a principal balance materially in excess of what the debtor had represented the property could support in refinancing. The debtor's proposed Plan of Reorganization called for a refinancing of the senior debt as the mechanism to fund the plan, with projected exit loan proceeds intended to pay the secured creditor in full plus accrued interest and to reserve for administrative claims.

Counsel retained CLS CRE to answer several discrete questions that were central to plan confirmation. First, could the property, as currently operated, support a first-mortgage loan sufficient to satisfy the senior creditor's allowed secured claim? Second, what categories of lenders actively originate loans on commercial real estate assets emerging from Chapter 11, and what are their underwriting parameters? Third, were the debtor's own financing projections, as set forth in the disclosure statement, consistent with market reality, or did they reflect assumptions that no institutional lender would accept? My role was to provide a written expert report under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 26, offer a rebuttal opinion to the debtor's financing projections if warranted, and be available for deposition and hearing testimony.

This type of engagement is not unusual in the Eastern District of Missouri. Plan confirmation disputes frequently turn on the feasibility of the proposed exit financing, and judges in that district have demonstrated both sophistication and skepticism when debtors project financing outcomes that do not track market conditions. A creditor who can put a qualified financing expert in front of the court with a rigorous, documented underwriting analysis is in a materially stronger position than one who relies solely on an appraiser's value conclusion or a banker's letter.

The Financial Analysis

The starting point for any financing feasibility analysis is a reconstruction of stabilized net operating income. In this engagement, I analyzed the trailing 12-month operating statements provided through discovery, the rent rolls for each of the three prior years, and the property's actual collections record. The debtor's projections in the disclosure statement assumed occupancy of 93 percent and used scheduled rent rather than collected rent as the revenue baseline, a methodology that overstated effective gross income by a meaningful margin because approximately 8 percent of scheduled rent had been consistently uncollected due to a pattern of concessions and delinquencies. I applied a 5 percent vacancy and credit loss factor on top of actual market vacancy, consistent with how multifamily lenders in the St. Louis suburban market underwrite stabilized product, and I treated the debtor's projected expense reductions as speculative rather than achievable without documented operational changes.

The reconstructed stabilized NOI for the property was $580,000 annually. The debtor's disclosure statement had projected NOI of $712,000, a figure that required accepting occupancy, rent, and expense assumptions simultaneously at their most optimistic level, with no margin for variance. That kind of stacked optimism does not survive institutional underwriting scrutiny.

Applying market-standard debt service coverage ratio thresholds is the next analytical step. For permanent first-mortgage financing on stabilized multifamily assets in secondary markets like St. Louis, agency lenders (Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac) and most regional bank lenders require a minimum DSCR of 1.25x on the qualifying income. Life company lenders typically require 1.25x to 1.30x on their own underwriting income. Bridge lenders may underwrite to 1.15x on a stabilized cash flow basis, but at higher pricing. I used 1.25x as the baseline threshold because that is the minimum the market consistently requires for permanent take-out financing of the type the debtor's plan contemplated.

The rate assumption is critical and frequently contested. As of the analysis period, 10-year fixed-rate financing for stabilized multifamily in secondary markets was pricing in the range of 6.50 to 7.25 percent, with agency execution at the tighter end and correspondent life company and CMBS execution toward the wider end. I used 6.875 percent as my assumed rate, representing the midpoint of the available execution range, amortized over 30 years on a standard agency schedule. This is not a conservative or aggressive assumption; it is a reasonable midpoint based on observable market transactions in the 90-day window prior to the analysis date.

Running the calculation: an NOI of $580,000 divided by a 1.25x DSCR requirement produces maximum supportable annual debt service of $464,000. At a 6.875 percent interest rate with 30-year amortization, a loan with annual debt service of $464,000 supports a principal balance of approximately $6.21 million. This was the maximum first-mortgage loan the property could support under standard institutional underwriting. The senior creditor's allowed secured claim, including accrued interest and fees, was approximately $7.4 million. The financing gap, meaning the amount by which the maximum supportable loan fell short of the amount needed to satisfy the senior creditor in full, was approximately $1.19 million.

I also applied an LTV constraint check. Institutional lenders in this asset class and market apply maximum LTV limits of 65 to 75 percent for permanent financing. A contemporaneous appraisal valued the property at $8.6 million. At 75 percent LTV, the maximum loan would be $6.45 million, consistent with but slightly higher than the DSCR-constrained maximum. In this case, the DSCR constraint was the binding constraint, not the LTV constraint, which is the more common dynamic when rates are elevated. The analysis demonstrated that on a market-standard underwriting basis, the plan's financing assumption was not feasible without either a material increase in NOI or a reduction in the amount required to satisfy the senior creditor.

The Complicating Factors

Bankruptcy stigma and lender underwriting of estate assets. The most significant complicating factor in any bankruptcy exit financing engagement is that many institutional lenders will not originate loans on properties encumbered by an active bankruptcy proceeding, or will impose pricing and structural premiums that the debtor's projections typically do not account for. Agency lenders, specifically Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, require that the borrower entity be bankruptcy-remote and that there be no pending litigation or unresolved claims on the property. A property emerging from Chapter 11 often carries administrative claims, disputed creditor claims, or post-confirmation conditions that create title complexity. Agency execution, which is where the most favorable pricing lives, is typically not available on day one of emergence. Lenders that will originate immediately post-confirmation are generally bridge lenders, certain regional banks with workout expertise, and debt fund platforms, all of which price at a premium to agency.

Operating history and management continuity. Institutional lenders require a minimum of two to three years of auditable or verifiable operating history. A property that has been under the control of a Chapter 11 trustee or a debtor-in-possession whose management was disrupted by the bankruptcy process may have gaps or inconsistencies in its financial records that trigger underwriting overlays or outright declinations. In St. Louis specifically, regional bank lenders with CRE workout desks, including several mid-size institutions headquartered in Missouri and Illinois, have underwritten these situations before and can work through documentation gaps, but they require a clear management succession plan and often require a reserve holdback at closing.

Deferred maintenance and capital expenditure reserves. Properties in bankruptcy proceedings frequently carry deferred maintenance backlogs that accumulated during financial distress. Institutional lenders apply replacement reserve requirements and will often condition loan closing on completion of specified capital items, particularly for multifamily assets where habitability or code compliance concerns exist. In the St. Louis suburban multifamily market, where a significant portion of the workforce housing stock was built in the 1970s and 1980s, deferred maintenance on HVAC, roofing, and plumbing systems is a common underwriting issue. The cost of required repairs reduces available loan proceeds at closing and may not be captured in the debtor's plan projections.

Plan timeline and rate lock risk. Exit financing commitments for bankruptcy plan confirmation are subject to a timeline risk that conventional transactions do not face. The period from plan confirmation to actual loan closing can extend six to twelve months in contested matters, during which interest rates and lender credit appetite may shift materially. Most institutional lenders will not provide binding rate lock commitments for periods exceeding 60 to 90 days, meaning that the rate assumed in the plan may not be achievable at the time of actual closing. A financing feasibility opinion that fails to account for rate movement risk is incomplete.

The St. Louis Lender Market

For bankruptcy exit financing specifically in the St. Louis market, the lender universe is narrower than for conventional transactions, but it is not thin. Understanding which categories are actually accessible is essential to a credible feasibility opinion.

Agency lenders (Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac): Available for post-confirmation multifamily transactions once the ownership entity is clean and the property has a seasoned operating history. Pricing in the 6.50 to 7.00 percent range for 10-year fixed-rate execution. LTV maximum of 75 percent on standard programs, 80 percent on affordable or workforce housing. Not available for active bankruptcy estate transactions. St. Louis is an approved market for both DUS lenders and Freddie Mac Optigo sellers.

Life company lenders and CMBS: Life companies active in St. Louis multifamily and industrial tend to target properties above $8 million in loan size, with stabilized occupancy above 90 percent and clean title. CMBS execution through conduit originators is available in St. Louis for loans above $5 million on income-producing assets, but CMBS underwriting is highly mechanical and bankruptcy-related title complexity creates issues in the securitization process. Both categories typically require a seasoning period post-emergence.

Regional and community banks: Several Missouri and Illinois-chartered banks with active CRE lending platforms in St. Louis will consider post-confirmation exit loans when they have workout or special situations desks. These lenders price at 25 to 75 basis points above life company or agency execution, typically offer three to five year terms with 25-year amortization, and require personal guarantees where guarantors are available. Their credit boxes accommodate documentation gaps that agency programs cannot. This category is often the most realistic source of immediate post-confirmation financing in the $3 to $8 million range in St. Louis.

Debt funds and bridge lenders: Non-bank bridge lenders and debt funds active in the St. Louis market provide the most flexible execution for bankruptcy estate and immediately post-confirmation situations. They underwrite to as-is value, apply interest reserves, and price in the range of SOFR plus 300 to 500 basis points depending on asset quality and borrower profile. These loans are not permanent financing but serve as a bridge to agency or bank takeout once the property has seasoned 12 to 24 months post-emergence. A plan that contemplates bridge financing as its exit mechanism needs to account for the higher carrying cost in its projections.

What Attorneys Handling St. Louis CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to retain a CRE financing expert. If your client's position in a Chapter 11 confirmation dispute turns in any way on whether a proposed exit financing is feasible, you need a financing expert, not just an appraiser. An appraiser can opine on value. A financing expert can opine on whether a lender will actually make the loan at the size, rate, and terms the debtor has projected, which is a materially different question. Courts in the Eastern District of Missouri have required debtors to demonstrate feasibility under 11 U.S.C. Section 1129(a)(11), and a creditor who can put a qualified financing expert in position to rebut the debtor's projections is operating from a position of evidentiary strength.

What a financing expert can answer that an appraiser cannot. An appraiser establishes value. A financing expert establishes what a lender will actually do with that value. The two questions diverge significantly when deal complexity, market conditions, or borrower-specific factors constrain available loan proceeds below what a straight LTV calculation might suggest. The DSCR constraint analysis I described above is a classic example: the appraised value may support a larger loan in theory, but lenders will not originate it because the cash flow is insufficient. Only a financing expert with active market knowledge can reliably opine on that gap.

Qualifications that matter. The critical distinction between a qualified CRE financing expert and a retired banker is current, active market participation. A lender from a prior decade who no longer has daily contact with underwriting desks, rate sheets, and credit committee behavior cannot accurately describe what today's market requires. CLS CRE maintains approximately 1,000 lender relationships and is active in transactions across 50 states with approximately $1 billion in transaction volume. That active participation is what allows us to testify credibly about what lenders are actually doing, not what they did five years ago.

What counsel should gather before the engagement. To support a rigorous expert analysis, counsel should obtain: trailing 24-month operating statements for the subject property; current and historical rent rolls; the property's most recent appraisal; all loan documents related to the encumbrance at issue; the debtor's disclosure statement and financial projections; any communications between the debtor and lenders regarding proposed exit financing; and the property's deferred maintenance or capital expenditure schedule if available. The more complete the financial record, the more defensible the expert's opinion.

How expert testimony is used in a plan hearing. In a confirmation hearing, financing feasibility testimony typically comes in through a written Rule 26 report supported by a financial model, followed by direct examination and cross-examination. The expert's opinion needs to be specific: not "financing is difficult" but "based on the reconstructed NOI of $X and market-standard underwriting parameters, the maximum supportable first-mortgage loan is $Y, which is $Z less than the amount required to satisfy the senior creditor's allowed claim." Courts respond to quantified, methodology-driven opinions. Vague market commentary does not move the needle.

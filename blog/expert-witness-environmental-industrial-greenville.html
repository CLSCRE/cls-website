Expert Witness: Financing a Contaminated Industrial Property in Greenville, SC

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Environmental Contamination Analysis

The Greenville Market Context

Greenville, South Carolina has emerged as one of the Southeast's most consequential secondary industrial markets over the past decade, and that shift matters directly to how courts, creditors, and borrowers think about asset value and financing feasibility in contested commercial real estate matters. The Greenville-Spartanburg-Anderson metropolitan statistical area sits at the center of a manufacturing and logistics corridor anchored by BMW's North American production facility, a dense network of Tier 1 and Tier 2 automotive suppliers, and an expanding cluster of distribution and fulfillment operations tied to the Port of Charleston's inland reach. That industrial base translates into persistent occupier demand for modern and functional warehouse product.

As of mid-2024, industrial vacancy in the Greenville-Spartanburg submarket was tracking in the 4.5% to 6.5% range depending on building class and configuration, well below national averages for comparable secondary markets. Net asking rents for functional bulk distribution and light manufacturing space ranged from roughly $5.00 to $8.50 per square foot NNN, with newer Class A product pushing past $9.00 in select locations. Older functional buildings, the category most likely to carry legacy environmental issues from prior industrial use, typically trade in the $4.50 to $6.50 per square foot range. When an older functional warehouse carries a long-term NNN lease at rents set three to seven years ago, the in-place rent frequently sits 15% to 30% below current market, which creates a measurable gap between the income stream supporting a loan and the economic value a knowledgeable buyer would ascribe to mark-to-market upside. That gap becomes legally significant in financing feasibility disputes, because it suppresses the supportable loan relative to appraised value in ways a standard appraisal may not fully explain to a court.

Greenville's industrial market also benefits from a deep regional lender community. Correspondent banks, community banks headquartered in the Carolinas, regional CMBS originators, and credit unions with commercial real estate programs are all active here in a way that distinguishes Greenville from smaller tertiary markets. That depth matters when the question before a court or Plan hearing is whether financing is actually available in the market, not just theoretically possible in some abstract capital structure.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by counsel representing a class of secured creditors in a regulatory enforcement proceeding involving an industrial warehouse property in the Greenville metropolitan area. The asset was a single-tenant functional warehouse and light manufacturing facility, older vintage construction, occupied under a long-term triple-net lease with a creditworthy industrial tenant at in-place rents the creditors' own appraisal had identified as below current market. The property was encumbered by a first-mortgage loan that had matured, and a regulatory enforcement action involving chlorinated volatile organic compound contamination from historical industrial use had been filed by state environmental regulators prior to the loan maturity date.

Counsel needed answers to two specific questions that an appraisal alone could not resolve. First, does the confirmed presence of chlorinated VOC contamination, specifically 1,4-dioxane as a co-contaminant, preclude first-mortgage financing from institutional lenders in the current market, or is financing still available subject to structuring conditions? Second, if financing is available, what is the maximum supportable loan amount under current market underwriting standards, and is that amount sufficient to retire the existing senior debt in full? The answers to those questions bore directly on the creditors' litigation posture, their position on plan feasibility in the restructuring proceeding, and their ability to demonstrate that a refinancing exit was or was not commercially reasonable under current market conditions.

Our engagement required us to apply active market underwriting standards, survey lender appetite for environmentally impacted industrial assets in the Greenville and broader Southeast regional market, and deliver a written opinion with supporting financial analysis. CLS CRE operates with approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, maintains relationships with more than 1,000 lenders across the country, and is active in all 50 states. That breadth of active origination activity is what separates a financing feasibility opinion from a theoretical exercise.

The Financial Analysis

The income analysis began with the in-place lease, which is the appropriate starting point for a financing feasibility opinion because institutional lenders underwrite to contractual cash flow, not appraiser-estimated market rents. The NNN structure meant that property taxes, insurance, and maintenance obligations were passed through to the tenant, simplifying the NOI calculation but also revealing the limitation of an income stream that had been set at a prior market level.

For purposes of this analysis, assume a building of approximately 85,000 square feet leased at $5.25 per square foot NNN with approximately six years of remaining lease term. Gross annual base rent is $446,250. In a true NNN lease, the landlord's operating cost exposure is minimal; for conservative underwriting purposes we applied a 3% management fee load and a nominal reserves figure of $0.05 per square foot, producing stabilized NOI of approximately $425,000 annually. We then stress-tested that figure against the lender's perspective on credit: the tenant's financial strength, the remaining lease term relative to the proposed loan term, and the renewal probability given below-market rents, which in this case actually supported renewal probability because a tenant paying below-market rent has a strong economic incentive to exercise options.

Lender underwriting for industrial assets in secondary markets like Greenville typically requires a minimum debt service coverage ratio of 1.25x on a stressed basis, with some lenders applying 1.20x for strong-credit tenants and long remaining lease terms. For an environmentally impacted asset, we applied 1.30x as the floor, reflecting the additional lender risk premium that contamination introduces. At a market interest rate of 7.25% (reflecting mid-2024 pricing for environmentally conditioned industrial loans, which carry a spread premium of 50 to 100 basis points over clean comparable transactions) and a 25-year amortization schedule, the monthly constant on a fully amortizing basis is approximately 0.00715 per dollar of loan balance.

Maximum annual debt service supportable at 1.30x coverage equals NOI divided by 1.30, or $425,000 divided by 1.30, producing maximum supportable annual debt service of approximately $326,900. At the 7.25% rate on a 25-year amortization, that debt service supports a loan of approximately $3,840,000. Rounding conservatively to $3,800,000, that figure represents the maximum supportable loan on a pure income basis before LTV constraints are applied.

LTV is the second constraint. For an environmentally impacted industrial asset, lenders in the current market will not exceed 55% to 65% LTV, and many require a Phase II Environmental Site Assessment with a No Further Action letter or an approved remediation plan in place before they will close. If the appraised value of the asset unencumbered by the environmental condition was $7,200,000 and lenders applied a 60% LTV ceiling, the maximum LTV-constrained loan is $4,320,000. In this scenario, the income-based constraint of $3,800,000 was the binding limitation, not the LTV ceiling. That distinction matters in litigation because it tells the court that the limiting factor is the rent level, not the contamination per se.

The existing senior debt balance at maturity was approximately $4,650,000. The analysis therefore demonstrated that market financing as of the relevant date was insufficient to retire the existing senior debt in full by approximately $850,000, assuming the property was refinanced on a stabilized basis without any remediation progress. If the borrower obtained a No Further Action determination from the South Carolina Department of Health and Environmental Control prior to closing, lender pricing would compress by 50 to 75 basis points, and the LTV ceiling would expand toward 65%, potentially narrowing but not eliminating the shortfall.

The Complicating Factors

1,4-Dioxane as a Co-Contaminant

Chlorinated VOCs such as perchloroethylene, trichloroethylene, and their breakdown products are a known challenge in industrial lending. Most experienced environmental lenders have developed internal protocols for properties with a defined contamination plume, an active remediation program, and regulatory oversight. What significantly escalates lender concern is the presence of 1,4-dioxane as a co-contaminant. Unlike many chlorinated solvents, 1,4-dioxane does not adsorb effectively to soil particles, which means it migrates faster and farther in groundwater than the primary plume. It is also highly resistant to standard pump-and-treat remediation, the most common approach for VOC-impacted sites, and it is not captured by many standard soil vapor extraction systems. When a lender's environmental counsel or Phase II consultant flags 1,4-dioxane, it triggers a fundamentally different remediation timeline conversation, typically adding years and potentially millions of dollars to the remediation cost estimate. Lenders price that uncertainty into their credit decision.

Regulatory Enforcement Status

An active regulatory enforcement action, as opposed to a voluntary remediation program, signals to lenders that SCDHEC or the EPA has determined that the responsible party is not moving quickly enough or has resisted remediation obligations. That regulatory posture introduces two specific lender concerns. First, the regulator may assert a superpriority environmental lien on the property that could prime the first mortgage in certain statutory frameworks. Second, the property owner's legal obligations may impose remediation expenditures that are senior in priority to debt service, compressing the cash flow available to service the loan. Lenders with active environmental lending programs evaluate both risks; lenders without such programs will simply decline the file.

Below-Market Rent and Lease Burn-Off Risk

The NNN lease at below-market rents introduces a timing risk that compounds the environmental uncertainty. Lenders ask a straightforward question: what happens at lease expiration? If the tenant vacates a contaminated property, the landlord is left with a vacant industrial building on an environmentally impaired site, with no income to service debt and a remediation obligation that reduces the property's marketability. Lenders model the lease expiration date as a hard underwriting boundary and typically will not originate a loan with a term extending beyond the existing lease unless the tenant is investment grade and the renewal probability is demonstrably high. In this engagement, the six-year remaining lease term allowed for a five-year loan term with an option to extend, which most lenders viewed as acceptable.

Remediation Cost Uncertainty

No lender will ignore the remediation cost estimate in the Phase II or supplemental investigation report. When 1,4-dioxane is present, standard remediation cost estimates carry wider confidence intervals. Lenders managing this risk typically require one of three structures: a fully funded environmental escrow held by a third-party trustee, a remediation cost cap insurance policy from a rated carrier, or a reduction in loan proceeds sufficient to fund the high-end remediation estimate from equity. Each of those structures reduces the net proceeds available to the borrower and, in a refinancing scenario, reduces the amount of prior debt that can be retired.

The Greenville Lender Market

Greenville's industrial lending market is served by several distinct lender categories, each with a different credit box for an environmentally impacted asset.

Regional and community banks headquartered in the Carolinas are the most active lenders in Greenville for smaller industrial transactions, generally below $10 million. Several of these institutions have established environmental risk policies and will consider transactions with an active remediation program and documented regulatory oversight. Their typical structure requires a personal guarantee, a funded environmental escrow equal to 125% of the high-end remediation estimate, and a maximum LTV of 55%. Pricing for contaminated assets from this group was ranging from Prime plus 150 basis points to 7.75% fixed as of mid-2024.

Correspondent and portfolio lenders with Southeast regional programs will consider Greenville industrial assets with environmental conditions if the contamination is characterized, remediation is active, and the tenant credit profile is strong. These lenders are more likely to offer a 65% LTV if remediation milestones are documented and the loan includes a structured draw mechanism tied to NFA milestone achievement. Their pricing is comparable to regional banks but their underwriting is more sophisticated on the environmental risk side.

CMBS originators are generally not a viable execution for an environmentally impacted Greenville industrial asset. CMBS B-piece buyers have consistently excluded properties with active enforcement actions, and the rating agencies apply stressed loss scenarios to contaminated properties that compress the available loan proceeds below levels achievable from portfolio lenders. We did not recommend CMBS execution in this engagement and would not do so in most environmental contamination scenarios.

Debt funds and bridge lenders are the most flexible capital source for an environmentally impacted asset, but at a cost. Pricing from debt funds for this type of transaction in the current market ranges from 9.50% to 11.50% depending on remediation status and loan-to-value. Those rates further suppress maximum supportable loan proceeds under an income-based underwriting standard and are generally not viable for a refinancing intended to retire existing institutional debt at par.

What Attorneys Handling Greenville CRE Disputes Need to Know

An appraiser determines what a property is worth. A commercial real estate financing expert determines what a lender will actually do with that property, which is a categorically different question and the one that usually matters most in secured creditor disputes, plan feasibility hearings, and lender liability cases.

When should you retain a CRE financing expert? Any time the central dispute involves whether a loan could have been made, whether refinancing proceeds are sufficient to satisfy a senior lien, whether a borrower had financing alternatives that would have cured a default, or whether a lender acted within market standards in declining to make or extend a loan, you need a CRE financing expert, not just an appraiser. Appraisers will tell you what the property was worth. They will not reliably tell you what a market-rate lender would have loaned against it, at what terms, or under what conditions.

What makes a CRE financing expert qualified? Active origination is the key credential. An expert who has not placed commercial mortgage debt in the current market cycle will not have reliable knowledge of current lender credit boxes, spread pricing, or structural requirements for specialty asset types. A retired banker may know what underwriting looked like five years ago. A capital markets professional who closed transactions in the relevant period knows what the market actually did. At CLS CRE, our opinions are grounded in active transaction experience, not historical recollection.

What information should counsel gather before the engagement? Counsel should assemble the following before retaining a financing expert: the existing loan documents and all amendments, the most recent Phase I and Phase II Environmental Site Assessment reports, any regulatory correspondence from SCDHEC or EPA including the enforcement order, the current rent roll and all lease documents including options and renewal terms, the most recent appraisal and any prior appraisals, and any lender correspondence regarding the loan maturity or workout discussions. The more complete this file at engagement, the more defensible and specific the expert opinion will be.

How is a financing expert used at trial or in a Plan hearing? In a Plan feasibility hearing, the financing expert opines on whether the proposed financing in the Plan is achievable under current market standards. In a contested Plan, competing experts may disagree on achievable LTV, applicable DSCR, or lender appetite for the specific asset type. In a lender liability case, the financing expert may opine on whether the lender's underwriting decision was within or outside of market norms. In a secured creditor priority dispute involving an environmental lien, the financing expert quantifies how the contamination affects the available loan proceeds, which directly affects the recovery waterfall.

Key Takeaways

  • Environmental contamination does not automatically preclude first-mortgage financing in Greenville's industrial market, but it substantially reduces the lender universe, increases pricing, and imposes structural requirements that reduce net loan proceeds.
  • 1,4-dioxane as a co-contaminant is a distinct escalation factor from standard chlorinated VOC contamination because of its migration behavior and resistance to conventional remediation, and lenders who understand this distinction will require more protective structures or will decline to participate.
  • Income-based underwriting constraints frequently bind before LTV constraints when below-market rents are in place, meaning the rent level, not the contamination alone, may be the primary factor limiting maximum loan proceeds.
  • CMBS is not a viable execution path for properties subject to active regulatory enforcement actions, and any expert opinion suggesting otherwise should be challenged on the basis of current B-piece buyer requirements.

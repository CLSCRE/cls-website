Expert Witness: NNN Lease Rollover Upside and Financing Impact in Great Falls, MT

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Below-Market NNN Lease and Rollover Upside Analysis

The Great Falls Market Context

Great Falls, Montana sits at the intersection of agricultural commerce, military-adjacent economic activity driven by Malmstrom Air Force Base, and a regional retail and healthcare services hub serving a catchment area that extends well beyond Cascade County. For attorneys and investors evaluating single-tenant net lease disputes, this market context matters because it fundamentally shapes how lenders price risk, underwrite tenancy, and size loans on NNN assets in this submarket.

The Great Falls commercial real estate market is smaller in absolute transaction volume than Montana's western corridor anchored by Missoula and Bozeman, but it is meaningfully different in character. Cap rates on stabilized single-tenant NNN product in Great Falls have historically ranged from approximately 6.5% to 8.5%, depending on tenant credit quality, lease term remaining, and location within the market. The retail corridor along 10th Avenue South and the commercial clusters near the interchange areas command tighter cap rates than secondary strip locations, and that spread matters enormously when a dispute turns on whether a property was financeable at a particular point in time.

Industrial vacancy in Great Falls has remained relatively constrained, generally running in the 4% to 7% range in recent cycles, supported by distribution, agricultural processing, and logistics demand tied to the region's commodity economy. Retail fundamentals are more nuanced: national credit tenants on long-term leases trade differently than local or regional tenants, and a single-tenant NNN building occupied by a regional or local operator carries a meaningfully different lender credit box than one leased to an investment-grade national chain. Office fundamentals in Great Falls reflect broader small-market trends with moderate vacancy softening post-2020, though medical office and government-adjacent office have held up substantially better than general commercial office.

For purposes of a below-market lease dispute, the critical market characteristic is this: Great Falls is not a market where lenders apply aggressive future value assumptions. Underwriters here are looking at what the lease actually produces today, what a replacement tenant would pay if the existing tenant vacates, and whether the market can absorb re-tenanting risk within a reasonable timeframe. The conservatism of that underwriting approach is itself a central issue in disputes of the type analyzed here.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing one party in a commercial real estate financing dispute involving a single-tenant NNN property in Great Falls. The asset was a freestanding commercial building leased to a single tenant under a long-term net lease, with the tenant responsible for taxes, insurance, and most maintenance obligations. The core dispute involved the financing value and refinancing capacity of the property at a specific point in time, given that the in-place rent was demonstrably below prevailing market rents for comparable space in the submarket.

Counsel needed answers to three interconnected questions. First, how do commercial mortgage lenders actually underwrite a NNN property where the contractual rent is below market, and does that below-market rent constrain or govern loan sizing? Second, what, if anything, does the theoretical "rollover upside" (the rent increase available at lease expiration or renewal) do to lender underwriting in terms of expanding the financing envelope? Third, given the specific lease terms, lease expiration timeline, and market conditions in Great Falls at the relevant date, what maximum loan amount was achievable, at what LTV and DSCR thresholds, and through which categories of lenders?

CLS CRE was engaged to produce an expert analysis grounded in commercial mortgage market standards, lender underwriting methodology, and the specific conditions of the Great Falls market. The analysis drew on our firm's approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, 1,000-plus lender relationships, and active origination across 50 states, including markets with comparable characteristics to Great Falls in terms of transaction size, market depth, and lender appetite.

The Financial Analysis

The methodological starting point for any NNN lease financing analysis is the in-place NOI, not the market NOI. This distinction is foundational and is frequently misunderstood by parties outside the commercial lending industry. Lenders do not lend against what a property could produce. They lend against what it does produce, adjusted for credit risk, lease term, and tenant quality.

In this engagement, the property generated in-place base rent of approximately $14.50 per square foot annually on a building of roughly 8,500 square feet, producing a gross rent of approximately $123,250 per year. Because the lease was a true triple-net structure with tenant-paid expenses, the effective NOI for underwriting purposes was approximately $118,000 after a modest management fee reserve that most institutional lenders apply even to NNN assets (typically 2% to 3% of effective gross income, representing the administrative and oversight cost of ownership). The market rent for comparable single-tenant net lease space in the relevant Great Falls submarket at the relevant date was in the range of $18.00 to $19.50 per square foot, meaning the in-place rent represented a discount of roughly 20% to 26% below market.

Applying conventional commercial mortgage underwriting standards, the analysis proceeded as follows. For a single-tenant NNN property with a non-investment-grade regional tenant in a secondary market like Great Falls, lenders in the relevant period were applying a minimum DSCR of 1.25x at underwriting, with many institutional lenders requiring 1.30x given the concentration risk of single-tenancy and the depth limitations of the Great Falls re-tenanting market. Using a 1.25x DSCR floor, a 30-year amortization schedule (standard for conventional permanent financing on NNN product), and an interest rate assumption of 7.25% (reflecting the rate environment at the relevant date, inclusive of applicable index and credit spread for this asset type and market), the maximum supportable annual debt service was calculated as follows:

Maximum annual debt service = In-place NOI / Minimum DSCR = $118,000 / 1.25 = $94,400 per year. At a 7.25% rate on a 30-year amortization, the mortgage constant (the annual debt service per dollar of loan balance) is approximately 0.08175. The maximum loan amount supportable by in-place income was therefore $94,400 / 0.08175, or approximately $1,154,000.

The appraised value of the property, based on a capitalization of in-place NOI at a market cap rate of approximately 7.25% (appropriate for this tenant profile and market), was approximately $1,628,000. The loan amount supportable by DSCR constraints represented approximately 70.9% LTV against this value. Conventional lender maximum LTV for a single-tenant NNN property with a non-investment-grade tenant in a secondary market is typically 65% to 70%, meaning the DSCR constraint, not the LTV constraint, was the binding underwriting limit in this case. The LTV-limited loan at 65% would be approximately $1,058,000, representing a further haircut of approximately $96,000 from the DSCR-derived maximum.

The effective financeable loan in a standard institutional execution was therefore in the range of $1,058,000 to $1,154,000, depending on which constraint bound first for a given lender's credit box. That is a material data point in a dispute involving the property's financing capacity, refinancing feasibility, or damages tied to lender reliance on in-place versus market income.

The Complicating Factors

Below-Market Rent and the Lender's Refusal to Credit Rollover Upside

The most significant complicating factor in this engagement was the consistent lender refusal to credit the theoretical rollover upside in current loan sizing. Parties unfamiliar with commercial mortgage underwriting sometimes assume that if a lease is below market, a lender will acknowledge the upside and factor some portion of it into the financing. In practice, conventional commercial mortgage lenders, including agency-adjacent lenders, CMBS conduits, and most regional banks, will not do this. The rollover upside is treated as a future event that is uncertain in amount, timing, and realizability. A tenant may not renew. If the tenant vacates, the property must be re-leased, which in a market like Great Falls could take 12 to 36 months depending on the building configuration, location, and tenant demand at the time.

Lenders who have been through workouts on single-tenant properties understand exactly what happens when the one tenant leaves: the income drops to zero, debt service continues, and the property is suddenly being marketed in a market that may have limited demand for that specific building. For that reason, the rollover upside is treated as an equity story, not a lender story. It may support a higher purchase price or a lower cap rate in a sale, but it does not increase the current period NOI on which the loan is sized.

Lease Term Remaining and Lender Loan Term Matching

A second complicating factor was the relationship between remaining lease term and available loan term. Most permanent NNN lenders require that the lease term extend beyond the loan maturity date, often by at least six to twelve months. Where the in-place lease has limited remaining term, the lender's maximum loan term is compressed accordingly. In some cases, a lender who would otherwise offer a ten-year fixed-rate term loan may only offer a five-year term, or may require a shorter amortization period, because the loan must mature before the lease does. A shorter loan term increases refinancing risk, which in turn affects the willingness of lenders to offer the most favorable rate and proceeds. This interaction between lease rollover timing and loan term eligibility is a technical underwriting issue that is material to a financing feasibility analysis.

Tenant Credit Quality in a Secondary Market

Single-tenant NNN financing is heavily stratified by tenant credit. An investment-grade national credit tenant commands materially different lender appetite, loan-to-value tolerance, and rate treatment than a regional or local tenant. In Great Falls, a single-tenant NNN building occupied by a regional tenant without public-market credit ratings is subject to more conservative underwriting across virtually every lending category. DSCR floors are higher, LTV ceilings are lower, lender universe is narrower, and pricing carries a secondary-market premium of 25 to 75 basis points over comparable product in primary markets. These adjustments are not arbitrary; they reflect the concentrated credit risk of single-tenancy combined with the limited re-tenanting depth of the Great Falls market if that tenant were to vacate.

Refinancing Feasibility at Rollover

A fourth complicating factor is the question of what happens at rollover: if the tenant renews at market rent, does the property then support a materially larger loan? The answer is conditional. A lease renewal at market rates would increase NOI and therefore increase the DSCR-derived maximum loan. Using the market rent figure of $18.50 per square foot on 8,500 square feet, the market NOI would be approximately $149,000 after expenses. At a 1.25x DSCR and the same rate and amortization, the maximum supportable loan would increase to approximately $1,453,000, representing a meaningful increase in financing capacity. However, that future refinancing capacity depends entirely on the tenant actually renewing, market rents holding at or above current levels, the lender market remaining open to this asset type and location, and the rate environment not having deteriorated materially. Each of those conditions is a separate underwriting variable, none of which can be assumed with certainty from the current period vantage point.

The Great Falls Lender Market

Understanding which lenders are actually active for single-tenant NNN product in Great Falls at various loan sizes is essential to any financing feasibility opinion. The lender market for this property type in this market operates in distinct tiers.

Community and regional banks headquartered in Montana or with established Great Falls market presence are typically the most active and flexible lenders for NNN product in the $500,000 to $2,500,000 range. These lenders have local market knowledge, direct relationships with borrowers, and more flexible underwriting on tenant credit than institutional lenders. They will typically underwrite to 1.20x to 1.25x DSCR, up to 70% to 75% LTV for strong borrowers, and price at spreads over prime or SOFR. Portfolio retention means they can make judgment calls that a CMBS conduit cannot.

Life insurance company lenders and CMBS conduits are less active at Great Falls loan sizes and become relevant primarily when the loan request exceeds approximately $3,000,000 to $5,000,000. Below that threshold, the cost of origination and diligence relative to loan size makes these executions uneconomical for most deals. When they are active, they apply the most conservative underwriting standards: investment-grade tenant requirements, full lease term coverage, 1.25x to 1.30x DSCR, and LTV caps of 65% to 70%.

SBA 504 and SBA 7(a) programs are relevant where the borrower is an owner-occupant or where the financing involves a business acquisition component, but they are generally not applicable to pure investor-owned NNN leased properties where the borrower is a passive real estate investor rather than an operating business.

Credit unions with commercial real estate programs have become more active in Montana markets in recent years and represent a growing option for conventional NNN product at the community bank tier, often with competitive pricing and flexible prepayment structures.

What Attorneys Handling Great Falls CRE Disputes Need to Know

A CRE financing expert witness serves a fundamentally different function than a commercial real estate appraiser, and understanding that distinction is essential to retaining the right expert for the right question.

An appraiser determines value. A CRE financing expert determines financibility. Those are related but distinct inquiries. A property may have an appraised value of $2,000,000 and yet be incapable of supporting more than $1,100,000 in debt financing because of DSCR constraints, tenant credit limitations, or market-tier lender restrictions. The gap between appraised value and maximum financeable loan is frequently the central issue in disputes involving failed refinancings, lender liability claims, guaranty deficiency disputes, and partnership or LLC dissolution matters where financing capacity determines the economic outcome.

Attorneys should retain a CRE financing expert when the dispute involves any of the following: whether a refinancing was feasible at a specific date; whether a loan commitment was commercially reasonable given market conditions; whether a borrower could have obtained financing on particular terms; whether a lender's underwriting deviated from market standards; or how a specific lease structure affected the financing envelope available to a property owner.

A qualified CRE financing expert is not simply a retired banker. The expert should have active, current market knowledge of lender underwriting standards, pricing, and credit boxes; relationships across multiple lender categories; transaction experience in the relevant asset type; and ideally a track record of prior expert engagement with an understanding of the Rule 26 disclosure requirements and Daubert standards applicable to expert testimony.

Before engaging a CRE financing expert, counsel should gather the following materials: the lease agreement in its entirety including all amendments and renewal options; rent rolls for the relevant time period; historical operating statements and any available appraisals; any lender correspondence, loan applications, term sheets, or commitment letters; the relevant loan documents if the dispute involves an existing loan; and any internal communications bearing on the parties' understanding of the property's financing capacity or value.

Expert testimony on CRE financing is used at trial to explain lender behavior, establish the feasible financing parameters at a specific date, rebut opposing expert opinions on financing capacity, and connect the financing analysis to damages calculations. In bankruptcy plan confirmation hearings, CRE financing expert testimony is used to establish whether a proposed plan's treatment of secured debt reflects market-rate financing terms or whether the cramdown rate and loan structure are supported by the actual lender market.

Key Takeaways

  • Lenders underwrite to in-place NOI, not market NOI. A below-market lease constrains the current-period loan sizing regardless of theoretical rollover upside. This is a market standard, not a lender preference, and it applies consistently across all conventional lender categories active in Great Falls.
  • Rollover upside is an equity story, not a lender story. The anticipated rent increase at lease expiration or renewal does not increase the financeable loan in the current period. It may support a higher sale price or valuation, but it does not expand the lender's debt sizing formula until it is actually contracted in a new lease.
  • DSCR constraints, not LTV constraints, were the binding limit in this analysis. For below-market NNN product in Great Falls, the income produced by the existing lease governed maximum loan size more restrictively than the appraised-value-based LTV ceiling. This is a technically significant finding that affects how damages or financing feasibility are calculated.

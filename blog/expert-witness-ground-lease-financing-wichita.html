Expert Witness: Ground Lease Financing Disputes and Analysis in Wichita, KS

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Ground Lease Financing Complexity

The Wichita Market Context

Wichita is not a secondary market that attorneys and investors should dismiss as a simplified version of a coastal deal. It is the largest city in Kansas, a genuine regional economic hub anchored by aerospace manufacturing, healthcare systems, and a distribution infrastructure that serves the central United States. Companies including major commercial aviation suppliers maintain significant employment footprints here, and that employment base drives demand across every commercial real estate asset class. For ground lease disputes specifically, Wichita presents a distinctive fact pattern because ground lease structures in this market appear most frequently beneath net-leased retail, quick-service restaurant pads, healthcare-adjacent facilities near the medical corridor along the east side of the city, and certain industrial land parcels near the Dwight D. Eisenhower National Airport submarket.

The industrial sector in the Wichita metro has performed with unusual consistency. Vacancy in bulk industrial and flex-industrial has remained in the 4% to 7% range, driven by aerospace supply chain tenancy and logistics users who value the market's central location and cost basis relative to Kansas City or Dallas. Net asking rents in industrial have pushed toward $6.50 to $8.50 per square foot on newer product, with older flex space trading in the $4.50 to $6.00 range. These are not coastal numbers, but they represent stable, bondable income streams that lenders can underwrite with confidence, which is precisely why ground lease structures have appeared in this market as land owners seek to monetize long-term positions without surrendering title.

Net-leased retail cap rates in Wichita have historically traded in the 5.75% to 7.25% range depending on tenant credit, lease term, and location along the primary corridors including Kellogg Avenue and Rock Road. Ground lease cap rates on the land component typically price 150 to 200 basis points inside the fee simple cap rate, reflecting the reduced credit risk of a long-term ground rent obligation. When that ground rent stream becomes the subject of a financing dispute, the gap between how a ground lessor and a ground lessee value the same income stream creates exactly the kind of contested financial question that requires a qualified expert to unpack for the court.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing the leasehold mortgagee in a dispute involving a commercially improved ground leasehold estate in the northeast Wichita submarket. The underlying asset was a net-leased commercial retail pad improved with a nationally tenanted quick-service restaurant, situated on land owned by a private family trust. The ground lease had an original term of 75 years with options, a ground rent escalation schedule tied to CPI, and had been in place for approximately 12 years at the time of the dispute.

The lender had originated a leasehold mortgage several years prior. The dispute arose when the ground lessor initiated a termination proceeding, asserting a lease default by the ground lessee. The leasehold mortgagee contended that it had not received proper notice of default as required under the ground lease's leasehold lender protection provisions, and that the ground lease as structured, combined with the lessor's conduct, failed to meet market standards for financeable ground lease instruments. Counsel retained CLS CRE to answer three discrete questions: first, whether the ground lease structure, on its face, satisfied the market standards that institutional leasehold mortgage lenders require before they will originate a loan against a leasehold interest; second, whether the SNDA provisions in the lease were consistent or inconsistent with current lender requirements in this asset class and market; and third, what the maximum supportable leasehold mortgage would have been at origination under prevailing market conditions, and what the present financing implications of the disputed termination would be for a prospective buyer or refinancing party.

CLS CRE provided a Rule 26 expert report addressing all three questions, drawing on our active lender relationships and our firm's experience originating leasehold mortgage financing across the country. CLS CRE has approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, 1,000 or more active lender relationships, and operates in all 50 states, which gives us direct, current knowledge of what lenders actually require, not what they required a decade ago.

The Financial Analysis

Underwriting a leasehold mortgage requires the same fundamental income analysis as any commercial mortgage, but with an additional layer of structural analysis that determines whether the collateral, which is the leasehold interest rather than fee simple title, is even capable of supporting financing at all. I addressed the income analysis first, then the structural analysis, and finally the interaction between the two.

The subject property generated base rent from its national tenant under an absolute NNN lease. At the time of origination, the annual base rent was approximately $148,000, with no landlord expense obligations. Ground rent payable to the fee owner was approximately $31,000 per year at the time, escalating under the lease's CPI schedule. Net operating income available to the leasehold estate, meaning the income after satisfying the ground rent obligation, was therefore approximately $117,000 annually. This is the NOI figure that a leasehold mortgage lender underwrites because it represents the income stream available to service leasehold mortgage debt after the senior ground rent obligation is satisfied.

Applying a market-standard debt service coverage ratio of 1.25x, the maximum annual debt service supportable from this NOI was approximately $93,600. At a 30-year amortization schedule and a coupon of 6.75%, which was consistent with market pricing for leasehold mortgage financing on net-leased retail in the period of origination, the supportable loan amount was approximately $1.30 million. At an assumed leasehold value derived from capitalizing the leasehold NOI at a 7.25% cap rate, the leasehold estate carried an implied value of approximately $1.61 million. The maximum supportable loan therefore represented an LTV of approximately 80.7% against that leasehold value, which exceeded the 65% to 70% LTV ceiling that most institutional leasehold mortgage lenders apply to this collateral type.

The correct sizing from a credit perspective was therefore constrained not by DSCR but by LTV. At 65% LTV against the $1.61 million leasehold value, the maximum loan was approximately $1.047 million, with debt service of approximately $72,900 annually, producing a DSCR of 1.60x against the $117,000 net leasehold NOI. This represents healthy coverage but a meaningfully smaller loan than a fee simple mortgage on the same property would have produced. I documented this differential in the expert report to demonstrate that the leasehold structure, independent of the disputed termination, already created financing constraints that the parties should have understood at origination.

The financial impact of the disputed termination was analyzed separately. If the ground lease were terminated and the leasehold estate extinguished, the leasehold mortgagee would hold collateral with zero market value, as leasehold mortgage collateral derives its entire value from the continued existence of the leasehold estate. The loss exposure was not the building replacement cost but the full outstanding loan balance, because the improvements revert to the fee owner and the lender has no claim against them under standard termination mechanics unless the ground lease contains specific lender protection provisions.

The Complicating Factors

Ground lease financing is complicated under the best circumstances. In a disputed transaction in Wichita, several factors compounded the analytical challenge significantly.

Inadequate Leasehold Lender Protection Provisions: The first and most significant complicating factor was the structure of the cure rights available to the leasehold mortgagee under the ground lease. Market-standard ground leases include provisions requiring the ground lessor to provide independent, duplicate notice of any default directly to the leasehold mortgagee, a cure period for the lender that runs separately from and after the expiration of the tenant's cure period, the right of the lender to install a new tenant or assignee, and the right to obtain a new ground lease on substantially identical terms if the original lease is terminated through tenant default. This lease contained only a partial version of these protections. The notice provision was ambiguous regarding whether it applied to monetary defaults only or to all defaults, and the lender cure period was not structured as an independent period but was tied to the remaining time on the tenant's cure period, which had already expired by the time the lender was notified.

I testified that this structure was materially below market standard. Sophisticated institutional lenders, including CMBS conduit lenders, life insurance companies, and debt fund lenders, would not have originated a leasehold mortgage against a ground lease with this notice and cure structure without requiring an amendment before closing. Community banks and regional lenders in markets like Wichita sometimes accept less protective structures, but the financial exposure created by that acceptance is quantifiable and material.

CPI Escalation Mechanics and Future Ground Rent Risk: The second complicating factor was the CPI-based ground rent escalation. At origination, the $31,000 annual ground rent represented approximately 21% of total NNN rent revenue. CPI escalations with no cap create upward exposure to the net leasehold NOI over the loan term. A lender underwriting a 10-year term with a 30-year amortization schedule must consider the possibility that ground rent escalations could compress leasehold NOI to the point where debt service coverage falls below covenant minimums. I modeled several CPI scenarios and demonstrated that under a 3.5% average annual CPI, ground rent would reach approximately $54,000 by year 20, compressing leasehold NOI from $117,000 to approximately $94,000, nearly equal to the original debt service requirement, and eliminating refinancing capacity at the end of the loan term. Lenders managing this risk typically require a lower initial loan amount, a leasehold estate with longer remaining term relative to loan term, or a ground rent escalation cap.

Remaining Lease Term and Residual Value: The third factor was the relationship between the remaining ground lease term and the amortization schedule. Institutional lenders typically require the ground lease term to extend at least 10 to 15 years beyond the loan maturity date, and many require the term to extend to the end of the amortization period plus 5 years. At the time of origination, the ground lease had approximately 63 years remaining on its primary term, which was adequate. However, the option renewal periods were not structured as automatic extensions but required affirmative exercise by the tenant, and the exercise windows were narrow enough that a missed exercise could result in lease expiration during the loan term. This is a correctable deficiency through careful loan structuring, but it represents a red flag that an experienced leasehold mortgage lender would identify in due diligence.

The Wichita Lender Market

Understanding which lenders actually participate in Wichita leasehold mortgage transactions matters both for expert analysis and for counsel assessing damages. The financing universe for ground lease collateral in Wichita is smaller than for fee simple collateral, and the credit requirements are materially higher.

CMBS Conduit Lenders: National CMBS platforms will lend against leasehold interests in Wichita on credit-tenanted net lease properties, but they require ground leases to meet the CMBS industry's published leasehold mortgage requirements, which are among the most rigorous in the market. Non-conforming ground leases, meaning those lacking adequate lender protection provisions, SNDA subordination, or adequate remaining term, will not qualify for CMBS execution. Pricing for qualifying Wichita leasehold CMBS loans has run approximately 25 to 50 basis points wide of comparable fee simple deals, reflecting the additional structural risk.

Life Insurance Company Lenders: Life company lenders active in the central U.S. will consider Wichita leasehold mortgage transactions on larger, well-tenanted assets, particularly where the ground lease is subordinated. They tend to apply the most rigorous leasehold structure requirements and are the least likely to accommodate lease deficiencies. Their credit boxes typically cap at 60% to 65% LTV on leasehold collateral.

Debt Funds and Bridge Lenders: Debt funds and transitional lenders will extend leasehold mortgage capital in Wichita for value-add or non-stabilized situations, but at materially higher pricing, typically SOFR plus 350 to 550 basis points on floating rate structures, and with lower leverage caps in the 60% to 65% LTV range against leasehold value. These lenders are more flexible on lease structure but price that flexibility into the rate.

Community and Regional Banks: Wichita has a meaningful community bank ecosystem, including several Kansas-chartered institutions with active CRE portfolios. These lenders represent the most likely source of leasehold mortgage capital for smaller Wichita transactions below $3 million. They tend to apply less rigorous leasehold structure requirements but retain recourse, apply portfolio loan pricing, and typically limit loan terms to 3 to 5 years. Their willingness to lend against deficient ground lease structures creates risk that is not apparent at origination but can become outcome-determinative in a workout or dispute scenario.

What Attorneys Handling Wichita CRE Disputes Need to Know

Ground lease financing disputes are among the most technically demanding matters in commercial real estate litigation because they require expertise in three distinct disciplines simultaneously: real estate law, commercial lending standards, and capital markets pricing. An appraiser can value the fee simple interest and in some cases the leasehold interest, but an appraiser is generally not equipped to testify about what a specific lender would have required before originating a leasehold mortgage, whether a specific SNDA provision is consistent or inconsistent with market practice, or what the financing consequences of a disputed lease interpretation are for a prospective purchaser or refinancing party. Those questions require a commercial mortgage expert who is actively placing capital in the market.

When to Retain a CRE Financing Expert: Counsel should consider retaining a CRE financing expert in any ground lease dispute where financing feasibility, lender conduct, loan origination standards, or the financial consequences of a lease interpretation are at issue. This includes disputes over lender notice and cure rights, challenges to the sufficiency of an SNDA agreement, disputes over whether a ground lease was financeable at the time of a transaction, and damages calculations that depend on what the financing market would have provided but for a breach or misrepresentation.

Qualified Expert vs. Retired Banker: The distinction matters. A retired banker can describe what one institution's credit policy was at a point in time. An active commercial mortgage broker with current lender relationships and active deal flow can describe what the market, across dozens of lenders, actually requires today and required at the time of the disputed transaction. Our firm's 1,000 or more lender relationships and active 50-state origination platform mean that my testimony is grounded in current market practice, not institutional memory.

Information Counsel Should Gather Before the Engagement: Before retaining CLS CRE, counsel should gather the complete ground lease including all amendments and subordination agreements, the leasehold mortgage loan documents including the lender's title insurance commitment and any endorsements, any correspondence between the lender and the ground lessor regarding SNDA negotiation, the property's rent roll and operating statements for the three years preceding the dispute, and any appraisals or broker opinions of value prepared in connection with the financing or acquisition.

How Expert Testimony Is Used: In trial, a CRE financing expert can provide opinion testimony on market standards, financing feasibility, and quantified damages. In a bankruptcy plan hearing, the expert can address whether a proposed plan provides a lender with the indubitable equivalent of its secured claim, whether a leasehold estate retains value sufficient to support plan confirmation, or whether a cram-down interest rate is consistent with market pricing for the collateral type. In mediation, a credible expert report frequently accelerates resolution by establishing the financial parameters within which a settlement must fall.

Key Takeaways

  • Ground lease financing requires structural analysis before income analysis. A leasehold mortgage lender will not underwrite a loan against a ground lease that lacks adequate lender protection provisions, regardless of how strong the income stream is. The lease structure is the threshold question, and an inadequate structure eliminates institutional lender participation entirely.
  • SNDA provisions are not interchangeable. Market-standard SNDA agreements for leasehold mortgage purposes require subordination of the ground lease to the leasehold mortgage, non-disturbance protections that survive leasehold mortgage foreclosure, independent notice and cure rights for the lender, and the right to obtain a new lease. Partial or ambiguous provisions do not satisfy lender requirements and constitute a material deficiency in the ground lease instrument.
  • Leasehold mortgage LTV constraints are more restrictive than fee simple LTV constraints. Institutional lenders apply LTV caps in the 60% to 70% range against leasehold value rather than the 70% to 75% common for fee simple collateral. This structural constraint limits loan proceeds and must be understood when calculating damages from a financing failure or lease termination.

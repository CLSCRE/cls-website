Expert Witness: Financing a Contaminated Industrial Property in Flint, MI

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Environmental Contamination Analysis

The Flint Market Context

Flint, Michigan occupies a distinctive and often misunderstood position in the Midwest industrial real estate landscape. Attorneys and investors who approach this market through the lens of its well-publicized municipal challenges often underestimate the underlying structural demand for industrial space in Genesee County. The Greater Flint market sits within a dense logistics corridor anchored by I-69 and I-75, giving industrial tenants reasonable access to Detroit, Lansing, Saginaw, and cross-border Canadian freight routes. That geography, combined with substantially lower land costs than Detroit's established industrial submarkets along the I-94 and I-275 corridors, has kept Flint competitive for certain tenant profiles, particularly light manufacturing, automotive components fabrication, third-party logistics, and materials processing.

Industrial vacancy in the Greater Flint market has historically ranged between 7% and 12%, with tighter conditions in bulk distribution product and softer conditions in older, functionally obsolescent manufacturing buildings built prior to 1980. That vintage distinction matters enormously in contamination-related financing disputes, because the properties most likely to carry legacy environmental liabilities are precisely the older, single-story heavy manufacturing structures with below-grade utilities, concrete floors over compacted fill, and histories of solvent use, degreasing operations, and metals finishing. NNN lease structures on these assets reflect tenant willingness to accept long-term occupancy in exchange for rent concessions, which is exactly the rent-credit dynamic we analyzed in this engagement.

Market rents for functional industrial space in Flint proper and in the surrounding Genesee County industrial corridors range from approximately $3.50 to $6.50 per square foot per year on a triple-net basis, with the lower end reflecting older product in compromised locations and the upper end reflecting newer or recently renovated space with clear heights above 24 feet, dock-high doors, and adequate truck courts. Below-market leases in place on contaminated industrial properties frequently reflect rents at or below the $4.00 per square foot threshold, which compresses NOI and directly constrains the supportable loan amount, as this analysis will show. Cap rates for stabilized, unencumbered industrial assets in Flint have ranged from approximately 7.5% to 9.5% depending on lease term, tenant credit quality, and building functionality, which is materially wider than Southeast Michigan suburban industrial cap rates of 5.5% to 7.0%. That spread is not purely location-driven; it incorporates a market-perceived risk premium for the Flint municipal environment, including concerns about the local property tax base, infrastructure investment, and municipal service continuity.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by counsel representing the secured creditor group in a regulatory enforcement proceeding involving an industrial warehouse facility in the Flint metropolitan area. The asset was a single-tenant industrial building of approximately 85,000 square feet, constructed in the early 1970s and historically used for automotive components manufacturing and surface finishing operations. The property was encumbered by a first mortgage, and the borrowing entity was in or approaching default. The property was 100% occupied under a long-term NNN lease, but the contractual rent was meaningfully below the market range for comparable industrial product, a condition that had been embedded in the lease as an accommodation to the tenant at the time of lease execution several years prior.

The Michigan Department of Environment, Great Lakes, and Energy had issued a formal notice of regulatory concern regarding soil and groundwater contamination attributable to prior industrial operations at the site. The contamination profile included 1,4-dioxane and chlorinated volatile organic compounds, specifically tetrachloroethylene and trichloroethylene, which are common byproducts of industrial degreasing and solvent-based manufacturing processes. These compounds are among the most financing-sensitive contaminants in the commercial real estate lending market, for reasons I will detail in the Complicating Factors section.

Counsel's core questions were two. First, does the existence of confirmed soil and groundwater contamination, combined with active regulatory oversight, categorically preclude first-mortgage financing in the current market? Second, if financing is available in some form, what is the maximum supportable loan amount at current market underwriting standards, and does that amount equal or exceed the outstanding senior debt balance? CLS CRE was engaged to provide an independent expert opinion on both questions, grounded in our active lender relationships and current knowledge of credit standards across the full spectrum of commercial mortgage capital sources.

The Financial Analysis

The analytical starting point was the property's in-place net operating income, which drives the supportable loan calculation under any income-capitalization-based underwriting framework. The subject lease generated gross NNN rent of approximately $4.15 per square foot annually on the 85,000-square-foot building, producing a contract rent income of approximately $352,750 per year. Under a true triple-net structure, tenant obligations covered real property taxes, insurance, and maintenance, leaving the landlord's non-recoverable expenses limited to property management and reserves. We underwrote effective NOI at approximately $320,000 annually after applying a management fee of 3% of gross revenues and a capital reserve of $0.15 per square foot, consistent with lender underwriting conventions for single-tenant industrial assets of this vintage.

The first critical underwriting variable is the debt service coverage ratio. Market standard for conventional commercial mortgage lenders on industrial assets is a minimum DSCR of 1.25x, and for assets with complicating factors, including environmental conditions, most lenders move to 1.30x or higher as a credit mitigant. We applied a 1.30x DSCR threshold in recognition of the environmental complexity, which is a defensible and conservative assumption consistent with lender behavior we observe across our active lending relationships. At a 1.30x DSCR, the maximum annual debt service supportable by $320,000 in NOI is approximately $246,150.

Loan pricing for a contaminated industrial asset in a secondary Michigan market in the current interest rate environment requires a meaningful spread above conventional industrial pricing. Where a clean, stabilized industrial asset in a primary Midwest market might price at 175 to 200 basis points over the five-year Treasury, a contaminated asset with regulatory involvement in Flint would carry a spread of 275 to 375 basis points over the same index, with the specific spread driven by the stage of remediation, the lender's environmental risk appetite, and the borrower's financial strength. Assuming a five-year Treasury rate of approximately 4.40% and a spread of 325 basis points, the all-in loan rate is approximately 7.65%. On a 25-year amortization schedule, which is standard for industrial assets at this leverage level, a 7.65% interest rate generates monthly debt service of approximately $7.45 per $1,000 of loan balance, or annual debt service of approximately $89.40 per $1,000.

Solving for the maximum loan amount at $246,150 in supportable annual debt service at a 7.65% rate on a 25-year amortization, the math yields a maximum supportable loan of approximately $2,752,000. To cross-check this result against the LTV constraint, we applied a market cap rate of 8.75% to the property's NOI of $320,000, producing an income-based value indication of approximately $3,657,000. At a maximum LTV of 65%, which is the ceiling most institutional lenders apply to contaminated industrial assets with active regulatory oversight, the LTV-constrained loan amount is approximately $2,377,000. The binding constraint in this analysis is LTV, not DSCR, which means the maximum supportable first-mortgage loan from a conventional lender is approximately $2.38 million, assuming a lender can be identified who will lend on this asset at all. Whether that amount is sufficient to retire the existing senior debt is a factual question specific to the outstanding balance, which is a matter for counsel and the court record; our role was to establish the ceiling, not to adjudicate the adequacy of the proceeds.

The Complicating Factors

1,4-Dioxane and Chlorinated VOC Contamination. These two contaminant classes are among the most challenging in commercial mortgage underwriting, and for good reason. 1,4-dioxane is highly soluble and mobile in groundwater, does not attenuate naturally through biodegradation the way petroleum hydrocarbons do, and can produce off-site plume migration that creates third-party liability exposure well beyond the subject parcel boundary. Chlorinated VOCs, particularly tetrachloroethylene and trichloroethylene, carry significant human health risk thresholds that trigger aggressive regulatory response, and the presence of dense non-aqueous phase liquids in the subsurface can make remediation timelines unpredictable and costs difficult to bound. Lenders who have been burned by open-ended remediation cost exposure have institutional memory of those losses, and that memory is reflected in today's credit standards.

Active Regulatory Oversight. A property under active EGLE enforcement notice is in a fundamentally different risk category than a property with a closed or no-further-action designation. Active oversight means the remediation cost obligation is unquantified and ongoing, the lender faces potential liability under CERCLA's secured creditor exemption framework if it participates in management decisions, and the exit strategy for the loan is constrained because the property cannot be sold or refinanced without environmental disclosure that may repel subsequent buyers or lenders. Most conventional lenders, including CMBS platforms, life companies, and bank CRE portfolios, will not originate first-mortgage financing on assets under active regulatory enforcement without a Phase II scope of work, a remediation cost estimate bounded to a reasonable confidence interval, and ideally a Consent Order with a defined remediation pathway and timeline.

Below-Market Rent and Lease Structure. The existence of a below-market lease on a contaminated industrial asset creates a compounding problem. The below-market rent directly suppresses NOI and therefore suppresses the debt service the property can support. Simultaneously, the below-market lease may reflect the fact that the tenant accepted below-market economics in exchange for reduced obligations or in recognition of the property's diminished appeal. If the lease contains environmental carve-outs limiting tenant remediation obligations, the landlord's cost exposure is amplified. Lenders will scrutinize lease provisions for environmental indemnification clauses, hold-harmless language, and any provisions that shift remediation cost risk to the property owner, all of which affect underwriting risk assessment.

Secondary Market Risk Premium. Flint's status as a secondary market with documented municipal fiscal challenges adds a layer of exit risk that institutional lenders price into their credit decisions. The concern is not current occupancy; the tenant is in place. The concern is what happens at lease expiration or in the event of tenant default. A contaminated industrial building in a secondary market with a limited pool of potential replacement tenants, and with environmental conditions that require disclosure to any prospective buyer or lender, has a constrained liquidation value that the lender must evaluate as its worst-case recovery scenario. That scenario analysis typically results in a lower LTV ceiling than would apply in a primary market.

The Flint Lender Market

Understanding which lenders will realistically engage on a contaminated industrial asset in Flint is as important as the financial analysis itself, because a loan amount that exists only in theory is not a financing solution. CLS CRE maintains active relationships with over 1,000 commercial mortgage lenders across all categories, and the realistic lending universe for this asset type and complexity narrows considerably.

Community and Regional Banks. Genesee County and the broader mid-Michigan market are served by a meaningful community and regional bank presence, including Michigan-chartered institutions with CRE portfolios that extend to industrial lending. These lenders are the most likely to engage on a contaminated asset, particularly if the borrower has an existing banking relationship, strong personal guaranty capacity, and a credible remediation plan in hand. Community banks in this market typically operate at LTV ceilings of 65% to 70% on clean industrial assets and 55% to 65% on impaired assets, with DSCR floors of 1.25x. They are portfolio lenders, which means they have credit flexibility that CMBS or agency conduit platforms cannot offer. Their pricing typically runs 200 to 350 basis points over the five-year Treasury, with a premium for environmental complexity.

Environmental Specialty Lenders. A defined category of commercial mortgage lenders operates specifically in the contaminated property financing space. These lenders have in-house environmental risk expertise, established relationships with Phase II consultants and remediation cost estimators, and underwriting frameworks designed to accommodate properties with known but bounded environmental liabilities. They are not cheap. Rates from this category typically run 150 to 250 basis points above community bank pricing for comparable leverage, and fees can add 1.5 to 2.5 points at origination. But they will engage where conventional lenders will not, and for counsel evaluating whether financing is categorically unavailable, the existence of this lender category is a critical expert opinion point.

CMBS, Life Companies, and Agency Platforms. These categories are not viable for this asset in its current regulatory condition. CMBS conduit lenders will not originate on properties with open Phase II findings and no-further-action status pending. Life companies operate with the tightest environmental credit standards in the market and avoid contaminated assets almost categorically. Agency lending platforms are property-type restricted and irrelevant to industrial. Counsel should understand that the absence of these capital sources is not evidence that financing is categorically unavailable; it is evidence that the institutional quality capital stack has a narrower aperture for this asset, which is a distinctions that matters in how the financing expert frames the available market.

What Attorneys Handling Flint CRE Disputes Need to Know

The decision to retain a CRE financing expert witness is not the same as retaining an appraiser, and attorneys who conflate the two disciplines often find themselves without the analytical coverage they need at the critical moments in litigation. An appraiser establishes value. A CRE financing expert establishes whether and at what terms financing is available in the actual capital market, which is a separate and frequently dispositive question in secured creditor disputes, bankruptcy plan confirmation hearings, and regulatory proceedings involving property disposition or remediation financing capacity.

The questions a CRE financing expert can answer that an appraiser cannot include: What DSCR and LTV standards do lenders actually apply to this asset type in this condition? Which lenders in the current market would realistically engage on this asset, and what would they require? Is there a financing solution sufficient to retire existing debt, and if so, what is the most probable loan structure and pricing? Would a lender require a remediation escrow, a personal guaranty, or a Phase II no-further-action designation as a condition of commitment? How does the below-market lease affect the financing ceiling relative to the appraised value? These are capital markets questions, not valuation questions, and the expert answering them should have current, active lender relationships, not merely historical banking experience.

Qualification of a CRE financing expert witness requires scrutiny beyond a resume. The expert should be an active market participant with demonstrable transaction volume in the asset type and market context. CLS CRE has closed approximately $1 billion in commercial mortgage transactions across 50 states, with active lender relationships across more than 1,000 capital sources. That current market engagement is what separates an expert who can testify to what lenders are actually doing today from a retired banker testifying to what lenders did a decade ago.

Before engaging a CRE financing expert, counsel should gather the following materials: the current rent roll and lease abstracts, the existing mortgage documents and outstanding balance confirmation, the Phase I and Phase II environmental reports, any EGLE correspondence or enforcement notices, the most recent property tax assessment and payment history, any existing appraisal, and the borrower's most recent operating statements. The sooner these materials are in the expert's hands, the sooner a preliminary opinion on financing feasibility can be formed, which may materially affect litigation strategy before the expert report deadline.

At trial or in a bankruptcy Plan confirmation hearing, CRE financing expert testimony is most effective when it is specific, quantitative, and grounded in market evidence rather than opinion. Judges and creditors' committees respond to testimony that identifies the precise financing ceiling, explains the methodology clearly, and acknowledges the complicating factors while demonstrating that those factors have been accounted for in the analysis. Vague testimony about market conditions does not move the needle. Precise testimony about what a specific category of lender will and will not do, at what price, and subject to what conditions, is the testimony that changes outcomes.

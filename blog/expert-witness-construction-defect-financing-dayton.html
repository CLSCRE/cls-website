Expert Witness: How Construction Defects Affect CRE Financing in Dayton, OH

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Construction Defect Impact on Financing

The Dayton Market Context

Dayton, Ohio occupies a position in the Midwest commercial real estate landscape that attorneys and investors often underestimate until they work a deal there. The metro is not Columbus, and it is not Cincinnati, but it has genuine industrial depth, a meaningful multifamily base, and an office market that has been navigating secular headwinds for several years. Understanding those fundamentals is not background noise in a construction defect financing analysis. They are the foundation on which every underwriting assumption rests, and when a defect disrupts those assumptions, the damage ripples through the numbers in ways that are specific to Dayton's market conditions.

On the industrial side, Dayton benefits from its position at the intersection of I-70 and I-75, one of the most logistics-dense highway corridors in the country. Industrial vacancy in the Dayton metro has generally tracked in the 4 to 7 percent range in recent cycles, with flex and warehouse product seeing rent growth that, while not matching coastal gateway markets, has been steady and supportable. Asking rents for Class B warehouse space have typically ranged from $4.50 to $7.00 per square foot on a net basis depending on submarket, with more modern bulk distribution commanding meaningfully higher figures. This rent structure matters to a lender because it defines the NOI ceiling and therefore the maximum supportable loan on an industrial asset, and any defect that impairs occupancy, increases operating expense, or clouds title or insurability will compress that ceiling.

The multifamily market in Dayton has attracted increasing institutional attention as cap rates in primary markets pushed investors into secondary and tertiary markets. Dayton has seen cap rates on stabilized, well-located multifamily product trade in the 5.5 to 7.5 percent range depending on vintage, submarket, and unit mix, with submarkets like Beavercreek, Centerville, and Kettering generally commanding tighter cap rates than older urban core product. New construction deliveries have been moderate, which has helped occupancy remain relatively healthy, typically in the 92 to 96 percent range for professionally managed communities. For a lender underwriting a multifamily construction loan or a bridge-to-stabilization facility, those occupancy and cap rate benchmarks are the direct inputs to loan sizing. When construction defects enter the picture, they do not just affect the building. They affect the lender's confidence in every assumption in that model.

The Dayton office market tells a more complicated story. Suburban office has experienced significant vacancy pressure, with rates in many Dayton submarkets running in the 15 to 22 percent range. Downtown Dayton has undergone adaptive reuse activity, with some older office inventory converting to residential or mixed use, which has helped stabilize parts of the core. For a construction defect dispute involving an office asset, this market context is critical because a lender's appetite for office exposure in Dayton is already cautious. Layer a known defect condition on top of a market with elevated vacancy, and the financing feasibility question becomes acute.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing one of the parties in a construction defect dispute involving a mid-size commercial property in the Dayton metro. The asset was a recently completed multifamily community, with construction substantially finished but the property carrying known defect conditions that had been the subject of dispute between the owner, the general contractor, and the project's design professionals. The defects alleged included water infiltration through the building envelope, structural framing issues in certain building sections, and HVAC system deficiencies that had resulted in ongoing resident complaints and elevated maintenance costs.

Counsel retained CLS CRE to address a specific and technically demanding set of questions that sit at the intersection of construction defect law and commercial real estate finance. Those questions were: first, whether the property, in its current condition with the alleged defects disclosed, was financeable at all in the conventional or agency lending market; second, what loan amount and terms a lender would extend on the property as-defective compared to the same property in a defect-free condition; and third, how the alleged defects affected the property's net operating income, its debt service coverage ratio, its loan-to-value constraint, and the resulting maximum supportable loan. A fourth question asked us to quantify the financing gap, meaning the difference between what the owner could borrow on the defect-free asset and what the owner could borrow in the property's current condition, because that financing gap was directly relevant to the damages calculation the litigation team was assembling.

These are not questions an appraiser is trained to answer, and they are not questions a retired banking executive can answer without current, active market knowledge. They require someone who is in the lender market daily, who knows how underwriters across multiple capital sources think about defect conditions, and who can translate construction defect facts into financing market consequences with the specificity that expert testimony requires.

The Financial Analysis

Our analysis began with a two-scenario NOI model. The first scenario modeled the property's stabilized NOI as if the defects did not exist, using market rents, market vacancy assumptions, and operating expense ratios consistent with comparable Dayton multifamily properties in good condition. The second scenario modeled the property's actual or impaired NOI, incorporating the financial consequences of the alleged defect conditions: elevated maintenance and repair costs, increased insurance expense (or loss of standard coverage), potential rent concessions or occupancy pressure attributable to the defect conditions, and the carrying cost of units that had been taken offline for remediation.

For the defect-free scenario, we underwrote stabilized gross potential rent based on a 150-unit community at market rents of approximately $1,100 per unit per month, yielding gross potential rent of $1,980,000 annually. Applying a market vacancy and credit loss assumption of 6 percent, consistent with stabilized Dayton multifamily product, produced effective gross income of approximately $1,861,200. Operating expenses including taxes, insurance, management, maintenance, reserves, and utilities were underwritten at $6,200 per unit annually, consistent with the Dayton market for this vintage and unit type, producing total operating expenses of approximately $930,000. The resulting stabilized NOI in the defect-free scenario was approximately $931,200.

For the defect-impaired scenario, we documented the following adjustments. First, insurance: the property's carrier had issued a reservation of rights letter related to the water infiltration condition, and replacement coverage with a specialty insurer carried a premium surcharge of approximately $85,000 annually above the market-rate premium. Second, maintenance: documented repair and mitigation costs attributable to the defect conditions were running at approximately $120,000 annually above normal maintenance levels. Third, occupancy: 12 units had been taken offline for active remediation during the analysis period, and the remaining occupied units showed a concession rate approximately 5 percent above market due to resident retention pressure. The combined occupancy and concession impact reduced effective gross income by approximately $180,000. The net result of these adjustments reduced stabilized NOI from approximately $931,200 to approximately $546,200, a reduction of approximately $385,000, or roughly 41 percent.

With those two NOI figures established, we applied lender underwriting standards to size the maximum supportable loan in each scenario. For agency-eligible multifamily in the Dayton market, the applicable DSCR floor is 1.25x, which is the standard Freddie Mac and Fannie Mae minimum for conventional execution. For bank and credit union lenders active in Dayton, the DSCR floor typically runs 1.20x to 1.25x depending on the institution's current appetite and the borrower's sponsorship profile. We used 1.25x as our standard throughout both scenarios to maintain consistency.

At current market interest rates for multifamily permanent financing, we assumed a 6.75 percent note rate on a 30-year amortization schedule, which was consistent with agency pricing observed in the Dayton market during the relevant period. At a 1.25x DSCR and those debt service parameters, the maximum annual debt service supportable by the defect-free NOI of $931,200 was approximately $744,960. Using a debt constant of approximately 7.79 percent (derived from the 6.75 percent rate on a 30-year amortization), the maximum supportable loan in the defect-free scenario was approximately $9,562,000.

Applying the same analysis to the impaired NOI of $546,200 produced maximum annual debt service of approximately $436,960 and a maximum supportable loan of approximately $5,609,000. The DSCR-constrained financing gap between the defect-free and defect-impaired scenarios was therefore approximately $3,953,000.

We then tested both scenarios against the applicable LTV constraint. Agency lenders typically underwrite multifamily at 75 to 80 percent LTV on stabilized product. A property encumbered by unresolved construction defects, however, is not treated as a stabilized asset by any agency lender. It would be classified as ineligible for agency execution in its current condition. For balance sheet lenders willing to consider the asset at all, LTV constraints would tighten to 65 to 70 percent of the as-is appraised value, and the as-is value itself would be impaired by the defect condition. Both the income approach and the comparable sales approach to value are negatively affected by the defect conditions, compounding the financing impairment beyond the DSCR calculation alone.

The Complicating Factors

Lender eligibility for defect-encumbered properties. The most significant complicating factor is not the math. It is the binary nature of lender eligibility when known construction defects exist. Agency lenders, which represent the deepest and most competitive capital source for multifamily in markets like Dayton, have explicit underwriting guidelines that treat unresolved construction defect conditions as disqualifying. Fannie Mae's and Freddie Mac's seller-servicer guides require representations about the property's physical condition and the absence of known material defects. A property with active construction defect litigation, unresolved remediation, or disputed structural conditions cannot be delivered into agency execution without those issues being resolved to the agency's satisfaction. This does not mean the property is un-financeable, but it means the borrower is immediately pushed into balance sheet lending, which is more expensive, shorter in term, and lower in leverage.

Title and insurance complications. Construction defect conditions frequently create complications in the title and insurance markets that lenders treat as separate and independent barriers to financing. If the defect has given rise to a mechanics lien, a stop notice, or a recorded notice of dispute, lenders will require those encumbrances to be resolved before closing. If the property's insurance carrier has issued a reservation of rights or denied coverage for defect-related claims, lenders will require the borrower to obtain satisfactory replacement coverage, which in a defect-disclosed condition can be significantly more expensive or structurally different from standard property coverage. Both conditions affect the lender's security and, by extension, their willingness and pricing.

Environmental and habitability overlap. In multifamily construction defect matters, water infiltration defects frequently give rise to mold conditions that implicate habitability standards under Ohio landlord-tenant law. A lender reviewing a multifamily asset with documented water infiltration will require confirmation that mold testing has been conducted, that any identified mold conditions have been remediated to applicable standards, and that the remediation has been certified by a qualified industrial hygienist. This is not a standard underwriting condition; it is an additional layer of due diligence that extends the lender's timeline, increases the borrower's pre-closing cost, and in some cases results in a lender declining to proceed regardless of the remediation outcome.

Litigation disclosure requirements. Active construction defect litigation must be disclosed to any institutional lender. Lenders will require a legal opinion or, at minimum, a detailed summary from borrower's counsel regarding the nature of the claims, the potential financial exposure, and the status of any insurance coverage for the claims. Lenders treat an unresolved construction defect lawsuit as a contingent liability that could result in a mechanics lien, a judgment lien, or other encumbrance on the collateral. Until the litigation is resolved or appropriately reserved for, most institutional lenders will decline to close a new loan on the property.

The Dayton Lender Market

Dayton benefits from a reasonably diverse lender market, though the depth varies significantly by asset type and complexity. For multifamily assets in good condition, agency lenders through local and regional correspondents represent the most competitive execution. Dayton is a designated agency market, and experienced correspondents can access Freddie Mac Small Balance, Fannie Mae DUS, and HUD/FHA programs for qualifying multifamily assets. However, as described above, those programs are not available for assets with known unresolved construction defects.

The regional and community bank market in Dayton is meaningful. Fifth Third, Huntington, and KeyBank all maintain active commercial real estate lending operations in the Dayton metro, and there is a strong community banking sector including institutions headquartered in the Dayton area with established CRE portfolios. These lenders can and do underwrite more complex situations than agency programs allow, but their credit committees will require full disclosure of defect conditions, completed or clearly-scoped remediation plans, and in most cases, resolution of active litigation before funding. Their pricing on defect-encumbered assets will carry a spread premium of 50 to 150 basis points above their standard pricing, reflecting the additional risk they are absorbing.

For truly distressed or defect-encumbered assets where institutional balance sheet lenders pass, the financing universe contracts to private debt funds and hard money lenders, which are active in the Dayton market but operate at significantly different terms: rates in the 10 to 13 percent range, terms of 12 to 24 months, LTVs of 55 to 65 percent of as-is value, and significant origination fees. These are not permanent financing solutions; they are bridge capital, and the cost differential compared to agency or bank financing is itself a quantifiable component of the damages analysis in a construction defect dispute.

What Attorneys Handling Dayton CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to retain a CRE financing expert. The moment a construction defect dispute involves a commercial property that is subject to a loan, was the subject of a loan application, or was intended to be refinanced or sold to a buyer using debt financing, a CRE financing expert is relevant. Appraisers quantify value. CRE financing experts quantify what the market will lend, on what terms, and at what cost, which is a different and often larger component of the damages picture. If your client's damages theory includes a financing gap, a higher cost of capital, a lost refinancing, or an inability to sell at a price supported by available financing, you need both an appraiser and a CRE financing expert.

What a CRE financing expert can address that an appraiser cannot. An appraiser determines value using recognized valuation methodologies. A CRE financing expert determines lendability. Those are related but distinct inquiries. The expert can testify about: which lender categories would consider the asset in its defective condition and which would not; what loan terms, leverage, and pricing a borrower could actually obtain in the market; how the defect conditions affect DSCR and maximum supportable loan independent of appraised value; what the cost differential between defect-impaired financing and defect-free financing equals on a net present value basis; and whether a claimed financing was actually achievable given the defect conditions at the relevant point in time.

Qualifications that matter. A CRE financing expert should have active, current involvement in structuring and placing commercial real estate loans across multiple capital sources. Academic credentials or a career history in banking that concluded years ago does not establish current market knowledge. The expert should be able to demonstrate familiarity with agency guidelines, CMBS underwriting standards, bank credit policies, and private debt structures, and should have transaction experience in the relevant market and asset type. CLS CRE has been retained as a financing expert in commercial real estate litigation because our team maintains approximately $1 billion in transaction volume and over 1,000 active lender relationships across 50 states, which means our testimony reflects what lenders are actually doing in the market, not what they did a decade ago.

Information counsel should gather before the engagement. To conduct a reliable financing feasibility analysis, the expert will need: the property's rent roll and trailing operating statements; any loan applications, term sheets, or lender correspondence related to the property; the construction contract, draw schedule, and certificate of substantial completion; the defect inspection reports and any remediation estimates or contractor bids; the property's current insurance policy and any reservation of rights or coverage denial correspondence; any appraisals or broker opinions of value; and the active litigation pleadings sufficient to understand the nature and scope of the alleged defects. The earlier counsel engages the financing expert and provides this material, the more complete and defensible the analysis will be.

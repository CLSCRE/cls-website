Expert Witness: NNN Lease Rollover Upside and Financing Impact in Akron, OH

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Below-Market NNN Lease and Rollover Upside Analysis

The Akron Market Context

Akron, Ohio sits at a commercial real estate crossroads that makes it particularly fertile ground for financing disputes involving single-tenant net lease assets. The market is not a gateway city, but it is not a tertiary backwater either. Akron benefits from its position within the broader Northeast Ohio industrial corridor, proximity to Interstate 76 and the regional distribution network, and a manufacturing and logistics tenant base that has been selectively growing even as legacy industries contract. For single-tenant NNN properties specifically, Akron presents a nuanced underwriting environment that lenders do not handle the same way they handle Columbus or Cincinnati, and that distinction matters enormously when a lease structure or rollover timeline is under dispute.

Industrial vacancy in the Akron metro has remained in the 4 to 6 percent range in recent years, supported by demand from regional distribution, light manufacturing, and third-party logistics operators who are priced out of the tighter Cleveland submarkets. Asking rents for industrial space in Akron have trended upward modestly, in the range of $5.50 to $8.00 per square foot NNN for older flex and distribution product, and $7.50 to $11.00 per square foot for newer Class A industrial. Retail NNN rents vary sharply by corridor and tenant category, with well-located single-tenant retail on State Road or Copley Road commanding meaningfully different cap rates than secondary suburban pads. For office, Akron's fundamentals are challenged in a way consistent with most mid-size Midwest markets: downtown office vacancy remains elevated, suburban office is selectively recovering but still faces headwinds, and lenders are highly selective about which single-tenant office deals they will touch at all.

Cap rates in Akron for single-tenant NNN properties range broadly depending on tenant credit, lease term remaining, and asset class. Investment-grade-tenanted retail NNN assets have traded in the 6.25 to 7.25 percent range. Smaller, local-credit or regional-credit NNN deals, particularly those with below-market rents or short remaining lease terms, are trading at 7.5 to 9 percent or being passed over entirely by institutional buyers. This spread between credit-quality cap rates is exactly the environment in which financing disputes about rollover risk and below-market lease structures become consequential. When a property is priced on rollover upside that lenders will not underwrite, the gap between contract price and financeable value becomes a legal issue.

The Engagement

We were retained by litigation counsel representing a party in a dispute arising from the acquisition financing and subsequent attempted refinancing of a single-tenant NNN commercial property in the Akron metro. The asset was a freestanding retail building, owner-occupied under a long-term lease by a regional-credit tenant, with a lease that had been in place for well over a decade and that reflected rent economics established years before the current transaction. The in-place rent was materially below current market rents for comparable space in that submarket. The dispute centered on whether the property could support a specific loan amount at the LTV and DSCR thresholds that had been represented to one of the parties, and whether the rollover upside associated with the below-market lease was a legitimate basis for the financing underwriting that had been presented.

The core legal and financial questions we were engaged to address were: How do commercial mortgage lenders actually underwrite below-market in-place leases on single-tenant NNN properties? Does the existence of rollover upside, meaning the potential for higher rents upon lease renewal or re-tenanting, increase the loan amount a lender will advance? What DSCR and LTV constraints apply to this type of asset in this market? And critically, was the financing structure that had been represented as achievable actually consistent with how the lender market for Akron single-tenant NNN properties operates? These are questions that require hands-on capital markets expertise, not just appraisal methodology, and they were central to the damages and liability analysis in the matter.

The Financial Analysis

Our analysis began with the in-place net operating income, which is the income stream lenders actually underwrite on a single-tenant NNN property where the lease is in place and the tenant is paying. For this property, the in-place NOI was approximately $118,000 annually, reflecting an in-place base rent that was, based on comparable lease data we reviewed for the submarket, approximately 22 to 28 percent below where the property could re-lease at market upon lease expiration or renewal. This is a significant below-market position, but it is not unusual for long-term NNN leases that were originally struck before a period of rent appreciation.

The critical underwriting principle at issue is this: commercial mortgage lenders underwrite to in-place income, not projected or speculative income. This is not a conservative preference of a particular lender; it is a structural requirement of how agency and CMBS programs work, and a near-universal standard among bank, credit union, and debt fund lenders on stabilized NNN assets. The lease in place is the security for the loan. The lender's debt service coverage calculation uses actual contractual rent, net of any reserves the lender requires, as the NOI numerator. Rollover upside, meaning the incremental rent increase the owner expects to achieve when the below-market lease rolls, is not included in that calculation. It is a value narrative for equity investors; it is not a financing numerator.

Applying the standard DSCR threshold for this asset type and lender category, which for conventional bank and CMBS lenders on single-tenant NNN retail in a secondary market like Akron is 1.25x on in-place income, and using a note rate assumption of 7.00 to 7.25 percent on a 25-year amortization schedule, which reflects the rate environment relevant to the dispute period, the maximum annual debt service supportable by the in-place NOI of $118,000 is approximately $94,400 ($118,000 divided by 1.25x DSCR). At a 7.10 percent rate on a 25-year amortization, that debt service payment corresponds to a maximum loan of approximately $1,065,000 to $1,090,000, depending on the exact rate and lender reserve requirements.

The property had been valued and transacted at a price implying a loan request in the range of $1,450,000 to $1,550,000. At a standard 65 to 70 percent LTV for a secondary-market single-tenant NNN deal with a regional-credit tenant and a below-market lease, the value that would need to be supported to reach a $1,500,000 loan is approximately $2,143,000 to $2,308,000. The DSCR constraint, however, caps the supportable loan well below that amount using in-place income alone. This divergence, between the LTV-implied loan capacity and the DSCR-constrained loan capacity, is exactly the analytical gap our engagement was designed to quantify. In this case, the binding constraint was DSCR, not LTV, meaning the property's income did not support the loan, regardless of what the appraised value said.

We also modeled the rollover scenario: if the lease were to roll at market rents upon expiration, the NOI would increase to approximately $148,000 to $152,000 annually, which would support a loan of approximately $1,340,000 to $1,365,000 at 1.25x DSCR. This still falls materially short of the $1,450,000 to $1,550,000 loan amount that had been represented as achievable. Moreover, that rollover scenario assumes a seamless re-lease at market with no vacancy, no tenant improvement costs, and no leasing commission, assumptions that no institutional lender would underwrite in advance of the actual lease execution.

The Complicating Factors

Below-Market Lease Seasoning and Lender Risk Perception. When a below-market lease is in place on a single-tenant property, lenders view it through two simultaneous and contradictory lenses. On one hand, a long-term lease to a creditworthy tenant, even at below-market rent, provides stable cash flow and low default probability. On the other hand, the property is over-rented to the owner's advantage only in terms of stability, not income, and the lease rollover creates an event risk that lenders must price. For Akron properties specifically, where the re-tenanting market for secondary single-tenant retail or industrial is not as liquid as it is in Columbus or Cleveland's inner-ring suburbs, lenders apply a meaningful credit for tenant quality and a meaningful penalty for lease term remaining under three years. A below-market lease with six to ten years of term remaining is treated more favorably than a below-market lease with 18 months remaining, even though both preclude underwriting to market rents.

Rollover Upside as an Equity Story, Not a Debt Story. This distinction is fundamental and is frequently misunderstood by non-specialist parties in litigation. Rollover upside is the reason an equity investor pays a premium for a below-market-leased NNN property. The investor underwrites the future income, not the current income, and prices the deal accordingly. Lenders do not do this. Lenders underwrite to existing contractual cash flow. If counsel or a client has been told that rollover upside supports a higher loan amount, either the lender was speaking loosely about a value-add bridge structure, or the representation was inaccurate. In a conventional permanent loan context, rollover upside does not increase the loan size. It may affect the lender's comfort with the transaction as a credit story, but it does not move the DSCR calculation.

Bridge Lending as an Alternative and Its Limitations. Some lenders, particularly debt funds and select bank bridge programs, will lend on a transitional basis against a business plan that includes lease rollover and re-pricing. These bridge loans for Akron single-tenant assets typically carry higher rates (currently 8.50 to 10.50 percent or more, depending on leverage and credit), shorter terms (12 to 36 months with extension options), and often require reserves at closing for the lease rollover period. They may advance to 65 to 70 percent of the as-stabilized value rather than the current value. The catch is that the as-stabilized value is predicated on assumptions the lender controls, and the exit strategy depends on a successful re-lease at market. For a dispute, the question of whether a bridge loan was the appropriate vehicle for this deal, and whether it was properly disclosed or represented, is a distinct and important question from whether a conventional permanent loan could have been achieved.

Lease Expiration Timing and Refinancing Windows. If the lease has fewer than five years of remaining term, most conduit and agency lenders will either decline the loan outright or require that the loan term not extend beyond lease expiration. A 10-year CMBS loan will not be available on an asset with a four-year lease, which severely limits refinancing options and effectively restricts the lender universe to shorter-term bank loans or bridge debt. This limitation has direct consequences for refinancing feasibility opinions in litigation contexts.

The Akron Lender Market

The lender landscape for single-tenant NNN assets in Akron is multi-tiered, and matching the right lender category to the asset is itself a specialized skill that matters in litigation involving financing feasibility claims.

Local and Regional Community Banks. Akron has a meaningful depth of community banking relationships, including institutions headquartered in Summit County and the broader Northeast Ohio region. These lenders are frequently the most flexible on asset-type and credit nuance, but they impose portfolio concentration constraints, typically cap loan sizes below $3,000,000 to $5,000,000 for a single NNN deal, and require full personal recourse. Their DSCR floors are typically 1.20x to 1.25x on in-place income, and they will sometimes credit a portion of rollover upside if it is supported by executed leases or strong comparable data, but this is a loan committee judgment call, not a standard product feature.

Regional Banks with Commercial Real Estate Divisions. Mid-size regional banks operating in Ohio are active in the Akron market for single-tenant NNN deals in the $2,000,000 to $10,000,000 range. They offer term loans with 3 to 7 year fixed rate periods, 25-year amortization, and DSCR requirements of 1.25x. They will lend at 65 to 75 percent LTV depending on tenant credit and lease term. They require property management history, rent rolls, and tenant financials for non-investment-grade tenants. They do not underwrite rollover upside as a loan-sizing input.

CMBS and Conduit Lenders. For larger single-tenant assets in Akron, typically above $3,000,000 to $5,000,000, CMBS execution is possible if the tenant is investment grade and the lease term is sufficient. CMBS lenders require 1.25x DSCR on in-place NOI with no credit for rollover upside, 65 percent LTV for secondary markets, and at least seven to ten years of remaining lease term to match the loan term. Akron assets are treated as secondary or tertiary market by CMBS credit committees, which typically results in a 10 to 15 basis point spread premium over comparable Columbus or Cleveland deals.

Debt Funds and Bridge Lenders. Non-bank debt funds are active in Akron for transitional and value-add single-tenant deals. They will lend against business plans that include lease rollover, but at meaningfully higher cost and with tighter covenants. These lenders are appropriate for the right situation but are not a substitute for conventional permanent financing in a dispute where permanent financing feasibility is the issue.

What Attorneys Handling Akron CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to Retain a CRE Financing Expert. If your case involves a claim that a property could or could not support a specific loan amount, that a financing commitment was feasible or unfeasible, that a refinancing was possible or impossible, or that a lender's underwriting was consistent or inconsistent with market standards, you need a CRE financing expert, not just an appraiser. Appraisers determine value. Financing experts determine financibility, and the two are not the same. A property can appraise at a value that supports a 70 percent LTV loan and still be unfinanceable because the income does not satisfy DSCR requirements. That distinction is often at the center of NNN lease and rollover upside disputes.

What a CRE Financing Expert Does That an Appraiser Cannot. A CRE financing expert testifies about how lenders actually underwrite specific asset types in specific markets, what credit boxes look like in practice, what a lender would have done with a given loan request, and whether the financing representations made in a transaction were consistent with market reality. Appraisers testify about value methodology. Financing experts testify about debt market mechanics, lender behavior, loan sizing constraints, and financing feasibility. These are distinct disciplines, and in a NNN lease dispute involving DSCR and rollover upside claims, the financing expert's testimony is typically more directly responsive to the damages questions than the appraiser's.

What Qualifies a CRE Financing Expert vs. a Retired Banker. A retired bank lender knows one institution's credit box at one point in time. A qualified CRE financing expert has active, current relationships with multiple lender categories, has structured transactions across the full capital stack including bank debt, CMBS, bridge, and private credit, and can speak to market-wide standards rather than a single institution's policies. At Commercial Lending Solutions, our approximately $1 billion in transaction volume and more than 1,000 lender relationships across 50 states position us to testify about what the market does, not what one lender prefers.

Information Counsel Should Gather Before the Expert Engagement. To conduct a rigorous financing feasibility analysis, a CRE financing expert will need the rent roll and in-place lease documents, any appraisals or broker opinions of value, any loan applications, term sheets, or commitment letters at issue, the property's historical operating statements, and any market reports or lender correspondence that informed the financing representations being disputed. The earlier this documentation is assembled, the more precisely the expert can model the financing universe and identify the gap between what was represented and what was achievable.

How CRE Financing Expert Testimony Is Used in Litigation. In trial settings, financing expert testimony typically addresses the feasibility of a specific financing structure, the consistency of lender representations with market standards, and the damages attributable to a financing gap or failed refinancing. In bankruptcy plan hearings, financing expert testimony is often used to establish whether a proposed plan's financing assumptions are feasible under Section 1129 or to challenge a plan as based on unachievable debt terms. In arbitration, financing expert reports are frequently the primary technical evidence on the question of whether a deal could have been financed as represented.

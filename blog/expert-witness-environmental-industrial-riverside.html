Expert Witness: Financing a Contaminated Industrial Property in Riverside, CA

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Environmental Contamination Analysis

The Riverside Market Context

Riverside occupies a structural position in Southern California's industrial real estate ecosystem that makes it one of the most legally contested submarkets in the region. As the seat of Riverside County and a cornerstone of the Inland Empire West industrial corridor, the city sits at the intersection of SR-91, I-215, and the 60 Freeway, giving it direct arterial connections to the Ports of Los Angeles and Long Beach. That logistics advantage has driven two decades of industrial absorption that fundamentally reshaped the city's commercial real estate landscape, and it has also created the conditions for some of the most complex financing and litigation scenarios our firm encounters.

Industrial vacancy in Riverside proper has remained historically tight, hovering in the 3.5% to 5.5% range through most of the post-pandemic period, though it has experienced modest softening from 2023 onward as new deliveries entered the broader Inland Empire West submarket. Average asking rents for industrial product in Riverside rose sharply from approximately $0.65 per square foot NNN in 2019 to a peak approaching $1.10 to $1.20 per square foot NNN in 2022 and 2023, before beginning a measured correction. That rent trajectory is critical context in any litigation involving a NNN-leased industrial property with below-market rents: the gap between in-place rent and current market rent can be substantial, and how that gap is treated in underwriting is one of the most contested analytical questions in a financing dispute.

Riverside's industrial stock is heavily weighted toward older, functionally adequate warehouse and light manufacturing product built between the 1960s and 1990s. These buildings frequently feature clear heights of 18 to 24 feet, truck court depths that fall short of modern Class A standards, and, critically for our purposes here, site histories tied to prior industrial uses involving solvents, degreasers, and chemical processing. The convergence of aging industrial infrastructure, a documented history of chemical use at many sites, and the broad regulatory reach of the California Department of Toxic Substances Control and the Santa Ana Regional Water Quality Control Board creates a class of properties in Riverside that are economically productive but environmentally burdened. That combination is precisely where complex financing disputes arise.

The Engagement

We were retained by counsel representing secured creditors in a regulatory enforcement matter involving an industrial warehouse property located in Riverside. The property was a mid-size single-tenant distribution and light manufacturing facility, fully occupied under a long-term triple-net lease. The tenant, identified only by industry category for purposes of this article, had been in occupancy for several years and was paying rent consistent with lease terms executed prior to the significant rent appreciation the Inland Empire experienced during the pandemic-era industrial boom. As a result, the in-place rent was meaningfully below current market.

A regulatory enforcement action had been initiated by California environmental authorities following the discovery of 1,4-dioxane and chlorinated volatile organic compounds, specifically perchloroethylene and trichloroethylene, in soil and groundwater beneath and adjacent to the property. The contamination was attributed to prior industrial uses predating the current ownership. A Remedial Action Plan was in process, but no No Further Action letter had been issued, and the remediation timeline was uncertain.

The senior lender held a first mortgage with a remaining balance that the secured creditors sought to retire. Counsel asked us to address two core questions. First, does the existence of confirmed environmental contamination, with active regulatory oversight and no NFA letter, preclude first-mortgage financing in the current market? Second, if financing is available, what is the maximum supportable loan amount at current market underwriting standards, and is that amount sufficient to retire the senior debt? These are exactly the kinds of questions that sit at the intersection of capital markets expertise and legal fact-finding, and they require a disciplined quantitative analysis grounded in real lender credit standards, not theoretical assumptions.

The Financial Analysis

Our income analysis began with the existing triple-net lease. NNN leases present a deceptively clean income picture: the tenant pays operating expenses, taxes, and insurance, leaving the landlord with a predictable net income stream. However, for financing purposes, lenders do not simply capitalize in-place NOI at face value. They scrutinize lease term remaining, tenant creditworthiness, rent relative to market, and whether the income stream is durable through a loan term that may extend five, seven, or ten years.

For this engagement, the in-place rent generated an effective gross income that, after the landlord's minimal structural expenses, produced an annual NOI of approximately $780,000. That figure reflected the below-market lease rate on a building of approximately 85,000 square feet. At current Riverside market rents of roughly $1.05 per square foot NNN for comparable vintage industrial product, the market NOI would have been closer to $1,070,000 annually, representing a 37% premium over in-place income. Lenders are acutely aware of this distinction, and it matters enormously in how they size a loan.

We applied a Debt Service Coverage Ratio floor of 1.25x, which reflects the prevailing minimum for conventional commercial real estate lenders on industrial assets in 2024. Some lenders apply a 1.20x floor on strong-credit, long-term leased assets, but the environmental condition here pushes that threshold upward, not downward. At 1.25x DSCR, the property must generate $1.25 in NOI for every $1.00 of annual debt service.

We modeled the financing using a fixed-rate assumption of 7.25%, consistent with pricing observed for 5-year fixed commercial mortgage product on environmentally burdened industrial assets in the Riverside market during the analysis period, with a 25-year amortization schedule. This is not a best-case rate; it reflects the spread premium that lenders impose when there is environmental risk, even when that risk is quantified and managed. A clean industrial asset with comparable tenancy might price 50 to 100 basis points tighter. The environmental condition is a direct input to loan pricing, not just to loan availability.

Working backward from the in-place NOI of $780,000 and applying the 1.25x DSCR constraint, the maximum supportable annual debt service is $624,000. At a 7.25% rate on a 25-year amortization, the monthly payment per $1,000,000 of loan balance is approximately $7,172, or $86,064 annually. Dividing $624,000 of allowable debt service by $86,064 per million yields a maximum loan of approximately $7,250,000 based on the income approach.

We then applied the LTV constraint. For environmentally impaired industrial properties with active regulatory oversight and no NFA letter, lenders in today's market apply LTV caps in the range of 55% to 65%, compared to the 70% to 75% typically available on unimpaired industrial assets in the same submarket. The appraised value of the property, based on comparable sales in the Riverside industrial market and capitalization of in-place income, fell in a range of approximately $11,500,000 to $12,500,000. Applying a 60% LTV to the midpoint of that range produces a loan ceiling of approximately $7,200,000, which is materially consistent with the income-constrained figure of $7,250,000.

The convergence of the DSCR-derived loan ceiling and the LTV ceiling at roughly $7,200,000 to $7,250,000 is analytically significant. It suggests that the income stream, not the environmental discount, is the binding constraint on loan size. That conclusion has real implications for the litigation: it means the environmental condition suppresses pricing and reduces the lender universe, but it does not prevent financing from reaching a specific, calculable maximum. Whether that maximum is sufficient to retire the senior debt is a legal and financial question that the court or plan process must resolve, but the financing expert's job is to establish the ceiling with credible methodology.

The Complicating Factors

Confirmed Contamination Without NFA Closure

The single most significant financing obstacle in this engagement is the absence of a No Further Action letter from the relevant regulatory authority. Lenders treating real estate as collateral require, above all else, that their security interest not be subordinated by a government lien or impaired by remediation costs that could consume asset value. CERCLA joint and several liability principles mean that a lender who forecloses and takes title to a contaminated property can, in theory, inherit remediation obligations. Although CERCLA's secured creditor exemption provides meaningful protection for lenders who do not participate in management, many institutional lenders nonetheless apply categorical exclusions to assets with open regulatory files. This does not mean no lender will proceed; it means the universe narrows to those with environmental risk appetite, specialized underwriting capacity, and access to legal counsel experienced in secured creditor exemptions.

1,4-Dioxane and Chlorinated VOC Specifics

Not all contamination is treated equally by lenders. 1,4-Dioxane presents particular challenges because it is a probable carcinogen with an extremely low regulatory action level in California, it is highly mobile in groundwater, it does not readily adsorb to soil particles, and it is resistant to conventional remediation technologies such as pump-and-treat. Chlorinated VOCs including perchloroethylene and trichloroethylene are more familiar to environmental lenders and are amenable to established remediation approaches, but their presence alongside 1,4-dioxane compounds the complexity. Lenders who accept environmentally impaired collateral will require a Phase II Environmental Site Assessment, a detailed Remedial Action Plan with cost estimates, and ideally an environmental insurance policy covering cleanup cost overruns and third-party liability. The cost and availability of that environmental insurance, which has tightened considerably in recent years for plume-related contamination, is itself a material underwriting input.

Below-Market Rent and Lease Rollover Risk

The below-market NNN lease introduces a layered risk for lenders. On one hand, the current tenant is paying reliably, and the NNN structure minimizes landlord exposure. On the other hand, lenders underwriting to in-place income on a below-market lease must evaluate what happens at lease expiration. If the environmental condition is not resolved by the time the lease rolls, the borrower's ability to re-lease at market, or at all, is impaired. A lender sizing a 7-year loan on a lease with 4 years of remaining term is effectively making a bet on re-leasing a contaminated property in a market where tenants have options. That rollover risk is stress-tested explicitly in the lender's credit memo, and it generally results in a reduced loan commitment or additional reserves.

Inland Empire Submarket Softening

The broader Inland Empire industrial market, of which Riverside is a component, has experienced measurable softening from its 2022 to 2023 peak. Vacancy rates have risen, and rent growth has moderated or turned slightly negative in some size categories. Lenders with Inland Empire industrial exposure from peak vintages are managing those portfolios carefully, and their appetite for new originations in the submarket, particularly on complex or impaired assets, is more selective than it was 18 months ago. This does not eliminate the lender market, but it is a real market condition that affects loan pricing and structure.

The Riverside Lender Market

For an environmentally impaired industrial asset in Riverside with the characteristics described here, the relevant lender universe is narrower than it would be for a clean asset but is not absent. The categories, in rough order of likelihood, are as follows.

Environmental lenders and specialty finance companies represent the most active category for this deal type. These are non-bank lenders, including certain debt funds and specialty commercial mortgage companies, that have built underwriting infrastructure specifically for contaminated collateral. They engage environmental consultants directly, understand how to read Remedial Action Plans, and price environmental risk into their spread rather than declining the credit. Pricing for this category typically runs 150 to 250 basis points above what a clean asset would command from a conventional lender, with origination fees in the 1.5% to 2.5% range. LTV is conservative, typically in the 55% to 60% range, and environmental insurance is a condition of closing.

Community and regional banks with Southern California industrial focus represent a secondary option, but with important conditions. Several community banks operating in the Inland Empire have developed comfort with environmentally impaired collateral when the regulatory process is well-advanced, the remediation cost estimate is bounded, and the borrower has strong credit. These lenders are unlikely to close without a current RAP, a cost estimate with contingency, and a satisfactory Phase II. They are also portfolio lenders, meaning their credit decisions are more negotiable than a CMBS execution, but they are also constrained by their own concentration limits and regulatory capital requirements.

CMBS and agency execution is effectively unavailable for this asset in its current condition. CMBS conduit lenders apply categorical screens for open environmental files, and Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac have no industrial product. Bridge lenders who originate for subsequent securitization face the same rating agency screens. The absence of these lender categories is itself important expert testimony in a financing dispute: it narrows the financing universe and affects the terms that are achievable.

What Attorneys Handling Riverside CRE Disputes Need to Know

The question of whether and how much financing is available for a particular commercial property is a specialized capital markets opinion, not an appraisal function. Attorneys who conflate these disciplines do their clients a disservice. An appraiser values a property; a CRE financing expert opines on what the capital markets will actually lend against that property, under what conditions, at what cost, and on what timeline. These are different questions that require different expertise.

An appraiser working under USPAP standards is constrained to value the asset as of a specific date, typically using income, sales comparison, and cost approaches. A CRE financing expert is opining on lender behavior: what credit boxes exist in the market, what DSCR and LTV thresholds apply, how environmental risk is priced, which lenders are active in a specific submarket, and what deal structure would be required to achieve a given loan amount. That expert must have current, active transactional relationships with lenders, because lender credit standards shift continuously and are not fully captured in published guidelines.

Attorneys should retain a CRE financing expert when:

  • The dispute involves whether a property could be refinanced to retire existing debt, whether as part of a plan of reorganization, a receivership sale, or a damages calculation.
  • There is a question about whether a lender's refusal to modify or extend a loan was commercially reasonable given market conditions.
  • A borrower claims they could not refinance due to market conditions, and the creditor disputes that claim.
  • The case involves a purchase contract or letter of intent that was not consummated, and the financing contingency is in dispute.
  • Environmental impairment is alleged to have rendered a property unfinanceable, and a damages model depends on that conclusion.

Qualification for a CRE financing expert is not simply a matter of banking experience. A retired loan officer may understand one lender's credit box but lack current market knowledge, multi-lender perspective, or the transaction volume necessary to speak credibly about market conditions. The qualified expert should have active deal flow across multiple lender categories, documented transaction history in the relevant market and asset class, and experience articulating financing methodology in a format that survives Daubert scrutiny. Before retaining an expert, counsel should request the expert's transaction history, the lender relationships they draw on for market knowledge, and any prior expert reports or depositions.

Information counsel should gather before the engagement includes: the existing loan documents, current rent roll and lease abstracts, Phase I and Phase II ESA reports, any regulatory correspondence including the RAP, property financial statements for the trailing 24 to 36 months, any existing appraisals, and the current loan balance and maturity date. The earlier in the litigation this information is assembled, the more efficient and credible the expert's analysis will be.

Key Takeaways

  • Environmental contamination does not automatically preclude first-mortgage financing in Riverside's industrial market, but it materially narrows the lender universe, increases pricing by 150 to 250 basis points, and imposes more conservative LTV constraints, typically 55% to 65% versus 70% to 75% for clean collateral.
  • The absence of a No Further Action letter is a hard filter for most conventional lenders and all CMBS execution, limiting viable financing sources to specialty environmental lenders, certain community banks, and select debt funds with environmental underwriting capability.

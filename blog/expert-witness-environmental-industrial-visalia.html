Expert Witness: Financing a Contaminated Industrial Property in Visalia, CA

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Environmental Contamination Analysis

The Visalia Market Context

Visalia sits at the economic center of Tulare County, the agricultural backbone of California's San Joaquin Valley, and it is a more sophisticated commercial real estate market than most outside observers appreciate. The Fresno-Visalia corridor has attracted meaningful industrial absorption over the past decade, driven by cold storage logistics, food processing distribution, and agricultural supply chain infrastructure. Vacancy in functional industrial product across the Visalia submarket has historically tracked in the 4% to 7% range, with well-located, clear-span warehouse buildings commanding net rents in the $0.42 to $0.72 per square foot per month range depending on clear height, dock configuration, and power. Newer Class A industrial development along the Highway 99 corridor has pushed asking rents higher, but the existing stock of older industrial buildings, many of which carry environmental history from prior manufacturing or chemical processing uses, trades at a meaningful discount to replacement cost and attracts value-add buyers who are comfortable with complexity.

That environmental history is not incidental. Visalia and the surrounding Tulare County industrial base include a legacy of food processing, pesticide formulation, metal fabrication, and solvent-intensive manufacturing. Several sites in and around Visalia appear on California's GeoTracker database and are subject to active RWQCB oversight. For industrial properties in this market, the question is rarely whether environmental due diligence will surface something. The question is whether what it surfaces is a deal-stopper or a deal-structuring problem. That distinction, and the financing consequences that flow from it, is the subject of this analysis.

The Engagement

Counsel representing secured creditors in a regulatory enforcement proceeding retained Commercial Lending Solutions to provide an expert opinion on financing feasibility for an industrial warehouse property located in the Visalia market. The property was an older single-tenant industrial building, fully occupied under a triple-net lease at rents that had not been reset to market in several years. The lease had meaningful remaining term. The tenant was a regional company with audited financials sufficient to satisfy most institutional credit requirements.

The regulatory matter involved confirmed soil and groundwater contamination from prior industrial operations at the site. The contaminants included 1,4-dioxane and chlorinated volatile organic compounds, specifically trichloroethylene and its degradation products, detected at concentrations that triggered mandatory investigation and remediation obligations under California Regional Water Quality Control Board jurisdiction. A Phase II Environmental Site Assessment had been completed. A Remedial Action Plan was in draft form. Cost estimates for remediation were available but contested between the responsible parties and the regulatory agency.

The core legal and financial questions before us were specific and answerable. First, does the presence of confirmed contamination and an active regulatory enforcement action categorically preclude first-mortgage financing on this property, or is financing available in the current market notwithstanding the environmental condition? Second, given the income stream produced by the existing NNN lease, what is the maximum loan amount supportable at market underwriting standards? Third, does that maximum loan exceed the balance of the existing senior debt, and if so, by how much? The answers to these questions had direct relevance to the secured creditors' position in the enforcement proceeding and to the feasibility of any negotiated resolution.

The Financial Analysis

The starting point for any income-property loan sizing exercise is a careful reconstruction of stabilized net operating income. In a single-tenant NNN structure, the gross analysis is straightforward: the tenant pays base rent and reimburses operating expenses, so the landlord's effective NOI is very close to the scheduled rent. However, a below-market lease introduces a valuation bifurcation that lenders handle in two different ways. Some lenders underwrite strictly to the in-place rent, arguing that the lease defines the actual cash flow available to service debt during the loan term. Others will give partial credit toward a stabilized rent assumption if the lease has a near-term expiration or if there is a documented rent step. In this engagement, the lease rate was running approximately 18% below the prevailing market rent for comparable Visalia industrial product, with no scheduled rent steps and meaningful remaining term.

For purposes of this analysis, we underwrote to in-place rent only, which is the more conservative and lender-appropriate standard. The property generated gross scheduled rent of approximately $1,080,000 annually. Because the lease was structured on a pure NNN basis, landlord expenses were minimal, consisting of property management, ground-level insurance above tenant-provided coverage, and a vacancy credit of 5% applied as a standard credit for re-leasing risk even on occupied properties. Effective gross income after vacancy credit was approximately $1,026,000. Net operating income for underwriting purposes was approximately $985,000 after a management fee assumption of 4% of effective gross income. This figure drove every subsequent calculation.

The DSCR threshold applied was 1.25x. This is the standard minimum for agency-eligible and life company industrial financing. CMBS execution, which became the relevant lending channel for reasons discussed below, also targets 1.25x as a floor, with some conduit programs pricing through that threshold for strong-credit single-tenant assets but rarely accepting anything below 1.20x for deals with complicating factors. Environmental contamination qualifies as a complicating factor that pushes underwriting toward the conservative end of the range. We did not apply a DSCR below 1.25x in any scenario.

The interest rate assumption used in primary loan sizing was 6.85%, reflecting a 10-year fixed rate available in the CMBS conduit market for industrial assets in secondary California markets at the time of the analysis. We applied a 30-year amortization schedule, which is standard for this asset class. These inputs produce a mortgage constant of approximately 7.93%. Working backward from the NOI and the DSCR requirement: the maximum annual debt service supportable at 1.25x is $985,000 divided by 1.25, which equals $788,000. Dividing $788,000 by the 7.93% mortgage constant produces a maximum loan of approximately $9,940,000, which we rounded to $9,900,000 for purposes of the opinion.

The LTV constraint functions as a separate ceiling. Lenders underwriting contaminated industrial properties in California secondary markets typically impose an LTV cap of 55% to 65% of as-is appraised value, compared to 70% to 75% for unencumbered industrial assets. The appraised value of the property, using a Phase I-available comparable sales analysis for contaminated industrial assets and applying an environmental stigma discount, was estimated at approximately $18,500,000. Applying a 60% LTV cap to that figure produces a maximum loan of $11,100,000. The DSCR constraint at $9,900,000 was therefore the binding constraint, not the LTV cap. This is a critical finding for the secured creditors' analysis: the income-driven loan limit was below the LTV-driven limit, which means additional appraised value does not help. The constraint is the rent, not the collateral value. The existing senior debt balance was approximately $8,750,000. The maximum supportable loan of $9,900,000 exceeded the senior debt balance by approximately $1,150,000, confirming that first-mortgage refinancing sufficient to retire existing senior debt was available in the market, subject to the environmental conditions discussed below.

The Complicating Factors

1,4-Dioxane as a Contaminant of Concern. 1,4-Dioxane is a particularly difficult contaminant for lenders because it is highly mobile in groundwater, does not readily biodegrade under natural conditions, and has no enforceable federal MCL in drinking water, though California has adopted a Response Level of 1 microgram per liter under the SWRCB program. The regulatory framework for 1,4-dioxane sites in California is evolving, which creates cost uncertainty. Lenders dislike open-ended remediation cost estimates, and 1,4-Dioxane plumes frequently migrate off-site, creating third-party liability exposure. A lender taking a first deed of trust on a property with a confirmed off-site 1,4-dioxane plume must consider CERCLA lender liability, and most institutional lenders will require a Phase II, a Remedial Action Cost Estimate, and written confirmation from environmental counsel that the secured creditor lender exemption under CERCLA applies to their specific loan structure before closing.

Chlorinated VOC Presence and Degradation Pathway Risk. Trichloroethylene and its reductive dechlorination products, including cis-1,2-dichloroethylene and vinyl chloride, present a different risk profile. Vinyl chloride is a known carcinogen with a California MCL of 0.5 micrograms per liter, and its presence in a degradation plume typically triggers more aggressive RWQCB oversight. For lenders, the concern is not just the remediation cost but the timeline. TCE sites in California have active remediation periods commonly running 10 to 20 years. A lender making a 10-year fixed-rate loan must assess what the regulatory status of the site will look like at maturity, and whether a future lender or buyer will face conditions that impair exit.

Below-Market Rent and Refinance Risk. The below-market rent compounds the environmental problem in a specific way. If the existing tenant vacates at lease expiration and the contamination has not been fully remediated, re-leasing a contaminated industrial building at market rents is genuinely difficult. Prospective tenants in the food processing or cold storage sectors, which dominate Visalia industrial demand, are often unwilling to occupy sites with known contamination because of regulatory disclosure requirements and potential worker exposure liability. The below-market rent therefore signals not just an income gap but a potential future leasing impairment that lenders must stress-test.

Remediation Reserve Requirements. Most lenders willing to engage on contaminated collateral will require a funded remediation reserve or an environmental insurance policy as a condition of closing. The reserve amount is typically sized to cover 125% to 150% of the estimated remediation cost. If the contested cost estimate for the RAP is, for example, $2,500,000, the lender may require a reserve of $3,125,000 to $3,750,000, held in a lender-controlled account. This reserve reduces the effective net loan proceeds available to retire existing debt, and must be factored into any feasibility analysis. In this engagement, we modeled three reserve scenarios corresponding to the low, midpoint, and high remediation cost estimates to produce a range of net proceeds available to the secured creditors.

The Visalia Lender Market

Visalia is served by a reasonably active institutional lending community for plain-vanilla industrial assets, but environmental contamination narrows the field substantially. Understanding which lenders remain in the market for this type of collateral is essential to a credible financing feasibility opinion.

CMBS Conduit Lenders. CMBS is the most likely execution path for a contaminated single-tenant industrial property with a creditworthy NNN tenant, because CMBS lenders are generally willing to look at environmental conditions if they are properly documented, remediation costs are estimated and escrowed, and the tenant credit is sufficient to support debt service through a worst-case scenario. Conduit lenders active in California secondary markets are willing to engage on Visalia industrial assets in the $7M to $25M range. The pricing premium for environmental risk is typically 25 to 50 basis points over a comparable clean asset. Lenders will require environmental insurance as a loan condition in virtually every case.

Life Insurance Companies. Life companies with correspondent relationships in the Central Valley, including those active in Fresno, occasionally look at Visalia industrial. However, life company appetite for confirmed contamination is extremely limited. Most life company credit mandates explicitly exclude properties with active regulatory orders. For this property, life company execution was not available.

Debt Funds and Bridge Lenders. Several private debt funds active in California industrial will lend on contaminated assets at higher LTVs but with floating rates and significantly wider spreads, typically SOFR plus 350 to 450 basis points at the time of this analysis. Debt fund execution is useful when speed or a distressed basis is the priority, but it produces materially higher debt service, which compresses the supportable loan under a DSCR constraint. For a deal where the income constraint is already binding, debt fund pricing is counterproductive.

Community and Regional Banks. The Visalia market has meaningful community bank depth, with several Central Valley-based institutions active in commercial real estate lending up to approximately $10M. However, community banks are typically the least willing to engage on confirmed environmental contamination because they hold loans on balance sheet and their regulatory examiners treat classified environmental risk harshly. The SBA 504 program, which some regional banks use for industrial acquisitions, is categorically unavailable for properties with known contamination under SBA's environmental eligibility requirements.

What Attorneys Handling Visalia CRE Disputes Need to Know

A CRE financing expert is not an appraiser, and the distinction matters in litigation. An appraiser determines value. A CRE financing expert determines whether, and on what terms, a lender will actually write a check against that value. These are related but different questions, and courts and plan confirmation hearings frequently require both. The appraiser's number tells you what the property is worth. The financing expert's analysis tells you whether that value is accessible through the debt markets, which is what actually matters in a secured creditor dispute, a plan feasibility hearing, or a damages argument premised on lost financing opportunity.

Attorneys should retain a CRE financing expert when the dispute involves any of the following: whether refinancing was feasible at a specific point in time; whether a lender's refusal to approve a loan modification was commercially reasonable; whether a proposed plan of reorganization is feasible because it depends on refinancing; what loan proceeds a property could support, and whether those proceeds are sufficient to satisfy a senior claim; or whether a borrower or guarantor could have mitigated damages by refinancing into a different loan structure.

A qualified CRE financing expert should have active market participation, not just historical experience. The lending market changes. Rate assumptions, DSCR standards, LTV norms, and lender appetite for specific asset types and conditions shift materially over 12 to 24 month periods. A retired banker whose active lending experience ended years ago cannot reliably opine on what the market would do today. The expert should have current lender relationships sufficient to validate the assumptions in the opinion, and should be able to identify specific categories of lenders who would or would not engage on the specific asset and conditions at issue.

Before engaging a financing expert, counsel should gather the following: the current rent roll and all lease documents; the most recent trailing 12-month operating statement; any existing appraisals or broker opinion of value; all environmental reports including Phase I, Phase II, and any RAP or corrective action plan; the current loan documents including any forbearance agreements or default notices; and the regulatory agency's most recent correspondence or enforcement order. The expert cannot render a credible opinion without understanding the actual income stream, the actual environmental condition, and the actual loan terms being analyzed.

At trial or in a plan confirmation hearing, CRE financing expert testimony typically addresses feasibility. The expert is not called to opine on liability or environmental science. The expert is called to answer a specific, bounded question: given these facts, would the market provide this financing? That question requires a capital markets professional with current market knowledge, not an academic or a generalist.

Key Takeaways

  • Contamination does not categorically preclude financing. Confirmed soil and groundwater contamination, including 1,4-dioxane and chlorinated VOCs, does not eliminate all financing options for a Visalia industrial property. It eliminates some lender categories, specifically life companies and community banks, and it imposes additional conditions on those that remain willing to engage, primarily CMBS conduit lenders and select debt funds.
  • The income constraint, not the collateral constraint, was binding in this analysis. At a 1.25x DSCR, a 6.85% rate, and 30-year amortization, the maximum supportable loan was approximately $9,900,000. This figure exceeded the existing senior debt balance of $8,750,000, confirming refinancing feasibility to retire senior debt. The LTV ceiling of 60% on contaminated collateral, producing a $11,100,000 cap, was not the limiting factor.
  • Below-market rent compounds environmental risk for future lenders.

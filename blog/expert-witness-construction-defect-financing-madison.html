Expert Witness: How Construction Defects Affect CRE Financing in Madison, WI

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Construction Defect Impact on Financing

The Madison Market Context

Madison, Wisconsin is not a secondary market in the way that term is sometimes used dismissively. It is a structurally differentiated market anchored by the University of Wisconsin-Madison, a major state government employment base, and a growing life sciences and technology sector concentrated in areas like University Research Park and the East Washington Avenue corridor. That combination produces consistent demand across multiple commercial property types and insulates the market from the cyclical volatility that affects purely private-sector economies. For purposes of CRE financing and litigation analysis, understanding Madison's market characteristics is not background noise. It is foundational to every financial conclusion I draw.

On the multifamily side, Madison consistently posts vacancy rates in the 3 to 5 percent range for stabilized product, driven by university enrollment, government employment, and in-migration from Chicago and Milwaukee. Cap rates for institutional-quality multifamily in Madison have traded in the 4.75 to 5.50 percent range depending on vintage, location relative to campus, and unit mix. For newer construction, market rents have been trending upward in the $1.50 to $2.25 per square foot per month range depending on submarket, with the Near East Side and the Capitol Square area commanding premiums.

Industrial fundamentals are equally compelling. The Madison industrial market, while smaller in absolute square footage than Milwaukee or Chicago, has posted vacancy rates in the 3 to 5 percent range, with asking rents for newer flex and distribution space in the $7.00 to $10.00 per square foot NNN range. The Stoughton Road and Highway 51 corridors have seen meaningful speculative development, and lenders have generally been willing to finance stabilized industrial in Madison at favorable LTV thresholds because the tenant base is diverse and not heavily dependent on any single industry vertical.

Office is the more complicated story, as it is nationally, but Madison's office market has outperformed peer Midwest markets. State government occupancy provides a floor that purely private-sector markets lack. Class A office vacancy in the Capitol Square and East Washington submarkets has stayed below 12 percent even post-pandemic, though suburban Class B product has softened. These market fundamentals directly affect what a lender will underwrite when a property is brought to them, and they are the baseline against which any defect-related impairment must be measured.

The Engagement

Counsel for the plaintiff in a construction defect dispute retained Commercial Lending Solutions to provide expert analysis on the financing implications of alleged construction defects affecting a newly developed mixed-use commercial property in Madison. The asset combined ground-floor retail with upper-floor multifamily units, a property type that had successfully completed its construction phase and was in lease-up when the defects were identified. The core questions presented were not appraisal questions. They were financing questions, and the distinction matters.

Specifically, our engagement required us to answer the following: What would a conventional lender, a CMBS lender, and a life insurance company lender each require before agreeing to place permanent financing on a property with known, documented construction defects? How do those defects affect the property's net operating income, and by extension its debt service coverage ratio and maximum supportable loan? What is the quantitative difference between the financing available to a defect-free asset of equivalent type in Madison versus the financing available to this asset given the alleged conditions? And what is the cost, in both time and dollars, of the remediation path that a lender would require before agreeing to close?

These are questions that require an active capital markets professional with current lender relationships and direct knowledge of underwriting standards, not a retired banker relying on decade-old experience or an appraiser whose methodology is calibrated to value, not to debt capacity. CLS CRE has approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, over 1,000 active lender relationships across 50 states, and direct current experience placing debt on complex, litigated, and distressed commercial assets. That combination is what allows us to answer these questions with specificity.

The Financial Analysis

The analysis begins with the property's income stream. For this mixed-use asset, we analyzed the stabilized net operating income in two scenarios: a defect-free baseline and an impaired scenario reflecting the documented defects. In the defect-free baseline, the property's gross potential rent was calculated using current Madison market rents for comparable mixed-use product, approximately $22 per square foot NNN for the ground-floor retail component and $1,850 per month average across the residential units. After applying a 5 percent vacancy allowance consistent with market conditions and deducting operating expenses including management, insurance, taxes, and reserves, the stabilized NOI in the defect-free scenario was approximately $680,000 annually on a property with an estimated stabilized value of approximately $12.5 million.

In the impaired scenario, the defects created two categories of NOI impact. First, certain residential units were uninhabitable during the remediation period, reducing effective gross income by approximately $118,000 annually. Second, the ground-floor retail tenant had exercised rent abatement provisions in its lease triggered by the construction conditions, reducing retail income by approximately $42,000 annually. The aggregate NOI in the impaired scenario was approximately $520,000, a reduction of $160,000 or approximately 23.5 percent from the stabilized baseline.

The debt sizing analysis applied standard DSCR and LTV thresholds. For a mixed-use asset of this type in Madison, conventional bank lenders and agency-adjacent lenders apply a minimum DSCR of 1.25x on stabilized income. CMBS lenders and life companies typically require 1.20x to 1.25x depending on market tier and asset quality. I applied 1.25x as the controlling threshold because the construction defect status would push most lenders to the conservative end of their credit box, and because mixed-use product with retail exposure typically carries a slight premium on DSCR requirements relative to pure multifamily.

Using a 30-year amortization and an assumed note rate of 6.75 percent (reflecting mid-2024 pricing for a conventional 10-year fixed loan on a mixed-use asset in a secondary Midwest market), the annual debt service on a fully amortizing basis produces a constant of approximately 7.79 percent. Working backward from the two NOI scenarios:

  • Defect-free scenario: Maximum supportable debt service equals $680,000 divided by 1.25x, or $544,000. At a 7.79 percent constant, the maximum supportable loan is approximately $6,983,000. Against an estimated stabilized value of $12.5 million, this produces an LTV of approximately 55.9 percent, comfortably within the 65 percent LTV ceiling most conventional lenders apply to mixed-use product.
  • Impaired scenario: Maximum supportable debt service equals $520,000 divided by 1.25x, or $416,000. At the same constant, the maximum supportable loan is approximately $5,340,000, a reduction of approximately $1,643,000 from the defect-free baseline.

That $1,643,000 gap in maximum supportable loan is not an academic number. It represents the direct, quantifiable financing impairment attributable to the alleged defects. If the property owner had a construction loan or bridge loan that needed to be refinanced at or near the defect-free loan amount, the impaired scenario creates a financing shortfall that the owner would need to cover from equity or other sources. In litigation, this figure is a component of the economic damages calculation. It is derived from market-standard underwriting methodology, not from assumption-heavy projections.

The Complicating Factors

Beyond the pure math of DSCR and LTV, construction defect scenarios introduce underwriting complications that are qualitative rather than quantitative, and they are often more consequential than the income impairment itself.

Known Defect Disclosure Requirements. Under standard commercial lending practice, borrowers are required to disclose known material defects, litigation, or disputes affecting the collateral. A property that is the subject of active construction defect litigation is a disclosed defect for every lender who reviews the loan application. Most conventional lenders, including regional banks with significant Madison market presence, will decline to underwrite permanent financing on a property with active, unresolved construction defect litigation, regardless of the income metrics. The lender's title insurance underwriter will flag the pending litigation, and the lender's counsel will advise that the collateral is impaired until the litigation is resolved or the defects are remediated and documented as resolved. This is not a pricing issue. It is a fundability issue. The property effectively exits the conventional lending market until the litigation is resolved or the defects are certified as remediated by a qualified third-party inspector.

Remediation Escrow and Reserve Requirements. For lenders willing to engage despite known defects, which is a smaller universe typically limited to private credit, bridge lenders, and certain community development-focused institutions, the standard structure involves a remediation escrow equal to 110 to 125 percent of the estimated remediation cost. If the remediation cost is estimated at $900,000, the lender will require an escrow of $990,000 to $1,125,000 funded at closing before the loan proceeds are disbursed. This escrow is typically senior to the borrower's equity, and it represents an additional capital requirement that further constrains the net equity available from the financing.

Insurance Market Complications. Commercial property with known construction defects may face coverage limitations or exclusions under its property and casualty policy until defects are remediated. Lenders require evidence of full, unencumbered property coverage as a condition of loan approval. An insurer that has issued a reservation of rights letter or excluded coverage for losses arising from the known defect conditions will create a coverage gap that most lenders will treat as a loan approval impediment. In the Madison market, where lenders tend to be relationship-oriented and risk-conservative, this issue frequently surfaces during the underwriting process and must be addressed before the loan can close.

Lease Abatement and Tenant Remedies. As noted in the NOI analysis, tenants with abatement rights triggered by the defect conditions are a separate underwriting complication. Lenders will not give credit for abated rent in their stabilized income analysis, and they will also scrutinize the underlying lease to determine whether the abatement provisions create any risk of lease termination. A tenant with a right to terminate upon failure to cure a defect within a defined period represents a contingent income loss that lenders must underwrite as a downside scenario. In Madison's retail market, where replacement tenancy timelines can run 12 to 18 months for ground-floor space, that contingency is material.

The Madison Lender Market

Madison is well-served by a layered lender market that includes national and regional institutions, local community banks, credit unions with commercial lending capacity, and an active private credit presence. Understanding which categories of this market remain accessible to a defect-affected property is essential to the financing impact analysis.

National and Regional Banks. Banks with institutional CRE lending platforms, including several with strong Wisconsin presence, apply DSCR floors of 1.20x to 1.25x, LTV ceilings of 60 to 65 percent for mixed-use or retail, and they will universally require resolution of active construction defect litigation before closing. These lenders are effectively unavailable for the period of active dispute.

Agency and CMBS Lenders. Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac programs are available for the multifamily component but require stabilized occupancy (typically 90 percent for 90 days) and property condition reports that would flag unresolved defects. CMBS requires clean title and no material pending litigation. Both channels are inaccessible during active litigation.

Local Community Banks and Credit Unions. Madison has a deep community banking market. Several local institutions have active CRE portfolios and relationship-driven underwriting. These lenders sometimes have more flexibility on construction defect situations, particularly when the borrower has an existing relationship and the defect scope is well-documented with a clear remediation path. However, they will still require a remediation escrow, and their loan pricing will reflect the additional risk, typically 50 to 100 basis points above what the same asset would command in a clean condition.

Private Credit and Bridge Lenders. The most accessible capital for a defect-affected property in active litigation is the private credit market, which includes debt funds, hard money lenders, and family office capital. These lenders will engage on distressed collateral but price the risk aggressively. Rates in the 9.50 to 11.50 percent range are typical for bridge financing on a property with active defect litigation, with origination fees of 1 to 2 points. Maximum LTV for this lending category on such an asset is typically 55 to 60 percent of as-is value, which in the impaired scenario will be lower than the defect-free appraised value. The blended cost of capital for this structure is substantially higher than conventional permanent financing, and the differential represents an additional component of the borrower's economic damages.

What Attorneys Handling Madison CRE Disputes Need to Know

A CRE financing expert witness serves a fundamentally different function than an appraiser, and conflating the two roles creates gaps in the damages analysis that opposing counsel will exploit.

When to Retain a CRE Financing Expert. Retain a financing expert whenever the damages theory includes impaired debt capacity, increased borrowing costs, inability to refinance, loss of a specific financing opportunity, or delay in stabilization caused by a financing barrier. These are not questions an appraiser can answer with authority. An appraisal tells you what the property is worth. A financing expert tells you what debt the property can support, what it costs, and whether it is available at all.

What a Financing Expert Can Testify To That an Appraiser Cannot. A qualified CRE financing expert can testify specifically to: current underwriting standards for the relevant property type and market; what a specific category of lender requires before funding a loan on a defect-affected property; the quantitative reduction in maximum supportable loan attributable to income impairment; the cost premium associated with bridge or private credit financing relative to conventional permanent debt; and whether a specific financing opportunity that the plaintiff claims was lost was in fact achievable under market conditions at the relevant time.

Qualifications to Evaluate. The expert should be actively originating commercial loans, not a retired banker or a finance professor. They should have current lender relationships across multiple capital source categories, including agencies, banks, CMBS, and private credit. They should be able to demonstrate transaction volume and, ideally, experience with distressed or litigated collateral. An expert who can only describe what conventional underwriting looks like in normal market conditions is not equipped to explain what happens to that underwriting when a known defect is in the picture.

Information Counsel Should Gather Before the Expert Engagement. Provide the expert with the rent roll, operating statements for the trailing 24 months, the construction contract and any change orders, the defect documentation and any engineering or contractor reports, all lease agreements including abatement and termination provisions, the existing loan documents if there is a construction or bridge loan in place, any lender correspondence reflecting the lender's awareness of the defects, and the property's current insurance binder and any reservation of rights letters from the insurer.

Use at Trial and in Settlement. A financing expert report under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 26 or its state equivalent provides counsel with a foundation for damages testimony that is quantitative, methodology-driven, and replicable. At trial, the expert can walk the finder of fact through the DSCR and LTV calculations, explain what a lender would have required in the defect-free scenario versus the impaired scenario, and quantify the damages in terms that are grounded in market practice rather than in theoretical projections. In pre-trial settlement discussions, a well-supported financing expert report often accelerates resolution because it converts a qualitative dispute about impact into a quantitative damages figure that both sides can evaluate.

Key Takeaways

  • Construction defects reduce NOI, and NOI reduction directly reduces maximum supportable loan. In the engagement described here, a 23.5 percent NOI reduction produced a $1,643,000 reduction in maximum supportable debt, which is a quantifiable, methodology-supported damages figure.
  • Active construction defect litigation effectively removes a property from the conventional, agency, and CMBS lending markets. The only capital accessible during active litigation is typically private credit, which carries materially higher rates and lower LTV thresholds.

Expert Witness: Commercial Loan Modification Standards in Toledo, OH

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Loan Modification and Workout Dispute

The Toledo Market Context

Toledo, Ohio sits at the intersection of two economic realities that make it an unusually interesting laboratory for commercial real estate loan modification disputes. On one hand, the market benefits from genuine industrial and logistics strength, anchored by its position along the I-75/I-80 corridor and its proximity to Detroit's automotive supply chain. On the other hand, Toledo carries the legacy of Rust Belt economic cycles that have created persistent stress in its office and retail sectors, meaning lenders and borrowers in this market have a longer and more complicated history of workout negotiations than you would find in a Sun Belt gateway city.

Toledo's industrial sector has been the clear bright spot. Vacancy rates in the metro's industrial and warehouse product have remained below 6% through recent cycles, and asking rents for distribution space have climbed steadily into the $4.50 to $6.50 per square foot range depending on clear height, dock doors, and submarket. The Toledo Express Airport corridor and the Oregon and Rossford submarkets have absorbed meaningful logistics tenancy. This performance matters in workout disputes because a lender analyzing a stressed industrial asset in Toledo should have been able to see a functioning leasing market capable of supporting recovery, which is directly relevant to whether a workout proposal was commercially reasonable.

Toledo's multifamily market tells a more nuanced story. Cap rates in the metro have historically ranged from 6.5% to 8.5% depending on vintage, location, and occupancy, with the tighter end of that range reserved for stabilized newer product near the University of Toledo or the Perrysburg suburban corridor. Value-add workforce housing, which represents a large share of the distressed asset inventory in this market, has traded at cap rates between 7.5% and 9.0%. These figures are critical context in any modification dispute because they establish what a sale of the collateral would have actually yielded had the lender chosen foreclosure rather than workout, and therefore whether the lender's decision to modify was rational under the circumstances.

Office fundamentals in Toledo have remained challenged. Vacancy across the central business district and suburban office submarkets has lingered in the 18% to 24% range, and effective rents have been stagnant to declining in most office product outside trophy or medical office use. Retail follows a similarly bifurcated pattern, with grocery-anchored neighborhood centers performing acceptably and older unanchored strip retail facing chronic leasing difficulty. Any lender engaging in a workout on Toledo office or unanchored retail collateral during this period faced a fundamentally different risk calculus than one dealing with industrial or multifamily assets, and the modification terms should reflect that distinction.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing the borrower in a dispute arising from a loan modification and forbearance process that had gone sideways. The underlying collateral was a midsize multifamily property located in an established Toledo submarket. The loan had been originated several years prior at a stabilized occupancy level that had subsequently declined due to deferred maintenance, management turnover, and broader competitive pressure from newer product. The borrower had approached the lender proactively when debt service coverage began to erode, seeking a forbearance period and a modified repayment structure to allow time for a capital improvement program and lease-up.

The lender engaged in a modification process that extended over approximately fourteen months. During that period, the lender required the borrower to produce multiple rounds of financial documentation, made several oral commitments regarding the structure of a proposed modification that were never reduced to writing, and ultimately delivered a modification proposal that the borrower alleged was commercially unreasonable and inconsistent with the terms the parties had been negotiating. The lender subsequently declared a default when the borrower declined to execute the proposed modification, and litigation followed.

Counsel retained CLS CRE to address three core questions. First, whether the terms of the lender's proposed modification, specifically the required paydown amount, the modified interest rate, and the amortization schedule, were consistent with what commercially reasonable lenders operating in the Toledo market would have proposed given the property's actual financial condition. Second, whether the lender's conduct during the fourteen-month workout process, including its documentation demands, timeline, and the evolution of its stated terms, was consistent with industry standards for commercial real estate workout administration. Third, what financing alternatives would have been available to this borrower during the modification period, and whether the lender's proposed terms were so onerous that they effectively made a consensual resolution impossible.

The Financial Analysis

The methodology for evaluating whether a modification was commercially reasonable begins with the property's actual and stabilized income streams. In this engagement, we analyzed trailing twelve-month rent rolls, operating expense histories, and tax records to construct an as-is net operating income figure, then separately modeled a stabilized NOI based on comparable property rents and achievable occupancy at the time of the proposed modification.

The subject property contained 72 units with an as-is average occupancy of approximately 78% during the modification period. In-place rents averaged $725 per unit per month. Comparable properties in the Toledo submarket were achieving between $750 and $810 per unit per month at 92% to 95% occupancy. Gross potential rent at full occupancy and market rents was therefore approximately $700,000 annually. Applying a 90% economic occupancy assumption, which is a standard underwriting convention for stabilized multifamily, effective gross income was approximately $630,000. Operating expenses for this vintage and size of property in Toledo, including property taxes, insurance, management at 8% of EGI, maintenance, and replacement reserves at $400 per unit annually, totaled approximately $385,000. This produced a stabilized NOI of approximately $245,000.

The existing loan balance at the time of the proposed modification was approximately $2.1 million, carrying an interest rate of 5.75% with a 25-year amortization schedule. Annual debt service on the existing terms was approximately $164,000, producing a debt service coverage ratio of 1.49x at stabilized NOI. At the as-is NOI, which we calculated at approximately $185,000 based on actual collections and the elevated vacancy, the DSCR was 1.13x, which is below the 1.20x threshold that virtually all institutional and community bank lenders apply as their minimum acceptable coverage for multifamily loans.

The lender's proposed modification required a principal paydown of $350,000, a rate reduction to 5.25%, and a re-amortization of the remaining balance over 20 years. Annual debt service on the modified terms would have been approximately $141,000 on a remaining balance of $1.75 million. At the as-is NOI of $185,000, that produces a DSCR of 1.31x, which clears the standard 1.20x threshold. At stabilized NOI, coverage would be approximately 1.74x. On its face, the debt service math appeared workable if the paydown could be made.

The problem was the source of the paydown. The borrower did not have $350,000 in liquid capital. We analyzed whether any conventional or bridge lender operating in the Toledo market at the time would have provided a supplemental loan, a cash-out refinance, or mezzanine financing to fund the paydown. Given the as-is occupancy and the property's condition, the maximum supportable loan from any institutional lender, at a 70% LTV constraint on a Toledo value-add multifamily asset at an estimated as-is value of approximately $2.3 million, was approximately $1.61 million, which was actually less than the existing loan balance. No lender in the market would advance new capital against this collateral in its current state. The paydown requirement was therefore not financeable from any market source, which is directly relevant to whether the modification terms were commercially reasonable or were, in effect, a constructed pretext for foreclosure.

The Complicating Factors

The extended timeline and oral commitment problem. In commercial real estate workouts, the fourteen-month negotiation period in this matter is not inherently unreasonable. Complex multi-lender credits or assets with environmental or title complications regularly require extended workout timelines. What is problematic, however, is when a lender's conduct during that period creates reasonable reliance by the borrower on terms that are never memorialized. Industry standard workout practice requires that any substantive modification terms be captured in a term sheet or letter of intent that, while not binding, establishes the framework under negotiation. When lenders communicate material terms orally and then shift those terms in the final proposal, the gap between communicated expectations and delivered terms becomes a central issue in litigation. Our review of the correspondence, call logs, and internal lender memoranda in this matter documented a significant shift in required paydown between what was discussed in month eight and what was proposed in month fourteen.

The valuation window problem. During a fourteen-month workout period, property values and income levels can move meaningfully. The lender's appraisal was ordered in month eleven of the workout and reflected conditions at that point in time. However, the property's occupancy had declined further during the workout period itself, partly because the uncertainty created by the unresolved modification had discouraged capital investment by the borrower. This dynamic, where the workout process itself contributes to collateral deterioration, creates a circularity problem that commercially reasonable lenders are expected to recognize and account for in their underwriting. A lender that delays resolution for an extended period and then uses the deteriorated collateral value to justify harsher modification terms has arguably contributed to the condition it is now citing as justification.

The cash flow reinvestment problem. The forbearance agreement in this matter suspended principal payments but required full interest payments to continue. At the as-is occupancy and with the property generating below-threshold coverage, requiring full interest current payments left the borrower with no cash flow available for the capital improvements that both parties acknowledged were necessary to restore occupancy. Commercially reasonable forbearance structures in comparable situations typically include either an interest reserve mechanism, a partial deferral of interest to allow rehabilitation spending, or a structured draw facility tied to renovation milestones. None of these structures were employed here, which our analysis identified as a departure from standard workout practice for value-add multifamily assets in markets like Toledo.

The market comparison gap. The lender's internal credit memoranda analyzed the proposed modification against the lender's own portfolio standards rather than against market conditions for comparable loans in the Toledo area. Industry standard workout analysis requires consideration of what alternative capital sources would have done with this asset and this borrower at the same point in time. Our review of comparable modification terms offered by community banks and CMBS special servicers on similarly stressed multifamily assets in Ohio during the same period showed that the market consensus for assets with recoverable cash flow and an identified capital plan was a modification incorporating a reduced rate, extended amortization, and a performance escrow or earnout structure, not a large upfront paydown that the borrower had no capacity to fund.

The Toledo Lender Market

Toledo is not a market with deep CMBS or life company presence for small to midsize multifamily assets. The financing universe for properties in the $1.5 million to $5 million range is dominated by regional and community banks headquartered in Ohio and in the adjacent Michigan market, along with a meaningful presence from credit unions active in multifamily lending. Fifth Third, Huntington, and Civista Savings are among the institutional names that have been active in this space, alongside numerous community banks operating out of Lucas, Wood, and Ottawa counties. Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac Small Balance programs are technically available for stabilized multifamily in Toledo but require minimum 90% occupancy at origination, which effectively excludes value-add or distressed assets from agency financing.

Bridge lenders and debt fund capital have limited presence in Toledo for assets below roughly $5 million. The economics of originating and servicing a $2 million bridge loan in a secondary Midwest market are challenging for funds that price their capital at 9% to 12% and require origination fees and exit fees. When they do enter the market, they typically require LTVs no higher than 65% on as-is value and require a fully executed business plan with contractor bids before closing. Community Development Financial Institutions have been active in Toledo for affordable housing rehabilitation and can be relevant to workout structures involving low-income housing tax credits or HUD involvement.

For purposes of the modification dispute, the critical finding is that no institutional alternative to the existing lender was available to this borrower at the time of the proposed modification on terms that would have allowed the borrower to pay off the existing loan. The existing lender was, effectively, the only available capital source. This is a market condition fact that is directly relevant to questions of commercial reasonableness, because a lender that knows it is the sole available capital source has a different obligation to deal fairly in the workout process than one operating in a market with abundant refinancing alternatives.

What Attorneys Handling Toledo CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to retain a CRE financing expert. If the litigation involves any dispute about what a lender should or should not have done in a modification, forbearance, or workout process, a CRE financing expert is necessary. Appraisers value property. They do not opine on lender conduct standards, on what modification terms the market would support, or on whether the debt service math underpinning a proposed workout was commercially sound. A CRE financing expert fills the gap between the real estate value opinion and the legal standard of care, and that gap is frequently where these cases are won or lost.

What a CRE financing expert can answer that other experts cannot. An expert with active origination and workout experience can testify to what institutional lenders, special servicers, and community banks actually do in comparable situations, not what a textbook says they should do. This includes specific testimony about standard documentation practices in workout negotiations, typical timelines, the range of modification structures that lenders offer for comparable assets, how lenders actually underwrite stressed collateral, and what alternative capital would have been available to the borrower. This factual market knowledge, grounded in current and recent transaction experience, is not available from an academic or a retired banker who has been out of active practice for a decade.

Qualification criteria. The critical differentiator is active, current engagement in the CRE lending market. An expert who actively originates commercial real estate loans, manages lender relationships, and closes transactions across multiple lender categories can speak to current market standards with credibility. A retired loan officer from a single institution can speak only to that institution's historical practices. In a Daubert challenge, the breadth and recency of the expert's market engagement is the primary battleground. CLS CRE brings approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, relationships with more than 1,000 institutional and private lenders, and active origination across 50 states, which means our market knowledge is not theoretical.

What to gather before the engagement begins. Counsel should collect the complete loan file from origination, all modification and forbearance correspondence including emails and internal memoranda obtained through discovery, all versions of the proposed modification terms, the lender's internal credit memoranda related to the workout, all appraisals and BPOs ordered during the workout period, complete rent rolls and financial statements for the trailing two to three years, and any comparable loan modification terms that can be obtained through discovery or market research. The more complete the documentation, the more specific and defensible the expert analysis will be.

Use at trial and in settlement. CRE financing expert testimony is used to frame the standard of care against which the lender's conduct is measured, to validate or challenge the financial feasibility of proposed modification terms, and to establish what alternatives were available that the lender declined to consider. In many cases, a well-supported expert report is also the most powerful settlement tool, because it quantifies the gap between what the lender did and what a commercially reasonable lender would have done, and that gap translates directly into damages exposure.

Key Takeaways

  • Modification terms must be underwritten against actual market conditions, not just portfolio policy. A lender's internal credit standards are relevant but not dispositive. What matters legally is whether the proposed terms were within the range of what commercially reasonable lenders in the Toledo market would have offered given the property's actual financial condition and the available alternative capital sources.
  • The fourteen-month workout timeline created borrower reliance issues that are independently significant. Extended workout negotiations without written term sheets create legal exposure for lenders when the final proposal diverges materially from terms communicated during the process. Industry practice requires interim documentation of proposed terms to protect both parties.
  • A paydown requirement that cannot be sourced from any available market capital is evidence of commercial unreasonableness. When the required paydown exceeds what the borrower could raise from any lender in the market, the modification condition is effectively a foreclosure trigger dressed as a workout, and the expert analysis should document that market reality precisely.
  • Toledo's limited bridge lending market for small multifamily assets is a material fact. In markets with deep alternative capital availability, a lender's harsh workout terms are less legally problematic because the borrower could refinance elsewhere. Toledo's thin bridge and private capital market for assets under $5 million changes the commercial reasonableness analysis significantly.

Expert Witness: Financing a Contaminated Industrial Property in Ogden, UT

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Environmental Contamination Analysis

The Ogden Market Context

Ogden, Utah sits at the northern anchor of the Wasatch Front, and its industrial real estate market has undergone a structural transformation over the past decade that makes it a genuinely interesting stage for secured creditor disputes. Weber County's industrial base is not a spillover story from Salt Lake City. It has its own gravity, driven by Union Pacific's intermodal hub, proximity to Hill Air Force Base, and a logistics corridor that connects I-15 and I-84 to regional distribution networks serving the Intermountain West. For industrial warehouse assets specifically, Ogden competes on cost and access in ways that larger institutional markets simply cannot replicate.

As of the most recent reporting cycles, the Ogden submarket has maintained industrial vacancy in the 4% to 6% range, well below the national average for tertiary industrial markets. Asking rents for Class B warehouse product have settled in the $0.55 to $0.75 per square foot per month NNN range, depending on clear height, dock configuration, and proximity to the Union Pacific yard. Class A distribution facilities with 28-foot or greater clear heights have pushed into the $0.80 to $0.95 range. These rent figures matter enormously in a contamination financing dispute because they establish the gap between in-place contract rent and market rent, which in turn determines whether a below-market lease represents a credit enhancement or a drag on value.

Industrial cap rates in Ogden have historically run 25 to 75 basis points wider than comparable Salt Lake City assets, reflecting the market's secondary positioning and thinner institutional buyer pool. In the current interest rate environment, stabilized industrial cap rates in Ogden are transacting in the 6.25% to 7.25% range, depending on tenant credit quality, lease term, and property condition. For a contaminated asset, the cap rate premium widens further, a point that is central to the loan sizing analysis in this type of engagement.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by counsel representing secured creditors in a regulatory enforcement proceeding involving an industrial warehouse property in the Ogden market. The asset was a mid-size single-tenant warehouse facility, previously used for industrial manufacturing operations, that had been confirmed to carry either 1,4-dioxane contamination, chlorinated volatile organic compound (VOC) contamination, or a combination of both, consistent with legacy solvent use and improper disposal practices common to pre-1990s industrial operations in the Mountain West.

The property was 100% occupied under a triple-net lease to a single tenant. The lease was long-term but carried a contract rent measurably below current market rates for comparable Ogden industrial product, a circumstance that created both a financing constraint and a valuation question. Existing senior mortgage debt was approaching maturity, and the question before the court centered on whether the borrower could refinance the property at a loan amount sufficient to retire the outstanding balance.

Counsel retained CLS CRE to answer three discrete questions. First, does the confirmed environmental contamination, standing alone, preclude conventional first-mortgage financing in the current Ogden lending market? Second, what is the maximum supportable loan amount under market-standard underwriting criteria, accounting for the contamination condition and the below-market lease? Third, is the maximum supportable loan sufficient to retire the senior debt at its outstanding balance, and if not, what is the shortfall? These questions required both a financing feasibility opinion and a detailed quantitative loan sizing analysis, which are distinct from the valuation opinion an MAI appraiser provides and are the specific domain of a capital markets expert.

The Financial Analysis

The starting point for any first-mortgage loan sizing analysis is stabilized net operating income. In this engagement, NOI was derived from the in-place NNN lease. Because NNN leases by definition pass through property taxes, insurance, and maintenance to the tenant, the landlord's net income stream is largely equivalent to contract rent less any landlord-retained obligations such as roof and structure reserves. For Ogden industrial product, a prudent lender applies a management fee of 2% to 3% of effective gross income even on NNN leases, along with a capital reserve of $0.05 to $0.10 per square foot annually, regardless of the lease structure, because the reserve addresses deferred maintenance exposure that contaminates (no pun intended) the underwritten NOI.

For illustrative purposes consistent with the actual asset parameters, assume a 65,000 square foot warehouse with a contract rent of $0.58 per square foot per month NNN. Gross annual contract rent is $452,400. After applying a 3% management fee of $13,572 and a $0.075 per square foot annual capital reserve of $4,875, stabilized underwritten NOI is approximately $433,953. A lender underwriting this asset to market standards will not gross up the NOI to reflect market rents. Below-market leases are treated as in-place income, not as potential income, which is standard practice across all institutional lender categories.

Debt service coverage ratio requirements in the current Ogden industrial lending market run from 1.20x at the aggressive end, typically achievable only with agency or life company execution on pristine assets, to 1.35x for community and regional bank portfolios carrying environmental risk. For a contaminated property, the appropriate underwriting threshold is 1.30x to 1.35x, reflecting both the asset-specific risk premium and the lender's internal credit policy constraints on impaired collateral. CLS CRE applied a 1.30x DSCR as the baseline for maximum loan sizing, with a 1.35x sensitivity analysis.

Interest rate assumptions matter significantly in this calculation. For a contaminated industrial asset in Ogden with a below-market lease, the realistic pricing range is SOFR plus 250 to 325 basis points for floating-rate bridge execution, or a fixed rate in the 7.25% to 7.75% range for a balance sheet lender willing to hold the loan. Using a 7.50% fixed rate on a 25-year amortization schedule, the annual debt service constant is approximately 8.77% of the loan amount. Dividing the underwritten NOI of $433,953 by the 1.30x DSCR constraint yields maximum annual debt service of $333,810. Dividing that figure by the 8.77% annual constant produces a maximum supportable loan of approximately $3,808,000.

The second constraint is loan-to-value. Lenders active in contaminated property financing typically cap LTV at 55% to 65% of as-is appraised value, with the as-is value reflecting the contamination discount. If the appraised value of the subject property on an as-is contaminated basis is $5,200,000 (consistent with a 7.75% cap rate applied to the underwritten NOI and reflecting a contamination discount from a clean value of approximately $6,100,000), the 65% LTV ceiling produces a cap of $3,380,000. The binding constraint in this case is LTV, not DSCR, which is a meaningful finding because it means the income stream technically supports more debt than the collateral value will permit. The maximum supportable loan is therefore $3,380,000 to $3,808,000, depending on which lender constraint binds.

The Complicating Factors

Environmental Contamination and Lender Risk Protocols

1,4-dioxane is classified as a likely human carcinogen by the EPA and is regulated under CERCLA. Chlorinated VOCs including trichloroethylene (TCE) and perchloroethylene (PCE) are among the most commonly litigated groundwater contaminants in industrial enforcement actions. Both contaminant types trigger immediate complications in conventional mortgage underwriting because lenders must assess not only current remediation costs but also regulatory pathway uncertainty, third-party liability exposure, and the risk that remediation timelines extend beyond loan maturity.

Lender environmental due diligence in this scenario goes well beyond a Phase I assessment. A Phase II site characterization is required at minimum, and most lenders active in contaminated property finance require a Remedial Investigation/Feasibility Study (RI/FS), a confirmed regulatory agency pathway with a No Further Action (NFA) letter in sight, and an independent cost-to-complete estimate from a licensed environmental engineer. The lender will require a cost-to-complete reserve or escrow, typically sized at 125% to 150% of the engineer's estimated remediation cost, held in a lender-controlled account. This reserve reduces available loan proceeds to the borrower dollar for dollar, which must be modeled into the financing feasibility analysis.

Below-Market Lease as a Double Constraint

A below-market NNN lease on a contaminated industrial property creates what I describe to courts as a compounding constraint. The contamination drives the cap rate wider, reducing the as-is value. The below-market rent reduces the NOI, which further suppresses value and limits debt service capacity. These two factors interact multiplicatively, not additively. In Ogden specifically, where market rents have appreciated meaningfully since the lease was likely executed, a long-term below-market lease can represent a 15% to 25% NOI discount relative to a market-rate leased comparable. This discount is permanent for underwriting purposes until the lease rolls or is renegotiated, and lenders will not speculate on future rent bumps.

Regulatory Enforcement Status and Title Encumbrance Risk

When environmental contamination is the subject of active regulatory enforcement, the property may carry a recorded environmental covenant, deed restriction, or institutional control under Utah's Voluntary Cleanup Program or a CERCLA enforcement order. These encumbrances affect title and can impair a lender's ability to foreclose and resell the collateral cleanly. Title insurers will frequently issue policies with exceptions for known contamination, and some will decline coverage entirely, which eliminates the conventional mortgage lending universe almost entirely. Lenders with contaminated property programs underwrite this risk explicitly and require either a regulatory status letter confirming enrollment in a structured remediation pathway or, at minimum, no pending EPA enforcement action that could result in a federal lien ahead of the first mortgage.

The Ogden Lender Market

Ogden's lending market for industrial assets is deeper than most attorneys assume for a secondary market. The institutional CMBS and life company market is present but constrained for contaminated product. CMBS execution is effectively unavailable for assets with known environmental impairment because the securitization process requires representations and warranties on collateral condition that cannot be made on a contaminated property. Life companies, which have been active in Ogden industrial financing at competitive spreads, similarly exclude known contamination from their eligible collateral criteria.

The viable lender universe for this asset type concentrates in three categories. First, regional and community banks with Utah charters and established industrial lending programs, several of which have developed internal protocols for contaminated property loans including internal environmental credit officers. These lenders price at rates 75 to 125 basis points above comparable clean-property execution and require the remediation reserve escrow described above. They typically cap exposure at $5,000,000 to $10,000,000 per loan, which is appropriate for Ogden-scale industrial assets. Second, credit unions with commercial real estate programs have become a meaningful force in the Wasatch Front lending market, some of which have issued contaminated industrial loans where remediation pathways are well-documented. Third, private debt funds and bridge lenders, primarily Los Angeles and Denver-based platforms active in the Mountain West, will lend on contaminated industrial assets at 60% to 65% LTV with interest rates in the 9% to 11% range. This execution is expensive but provides a path when the borrower's timeline requires certainty of close.

CLS CRE maintains active relationships with lenders in all three categories operating in the Utah market. Identifying which lender has both the appetite and the credit authority to execute on a specific contaminated asset in Ogden in a specific regulatory posture is itself a specialized skill that requires current market intelligence, not historical banking relationships.

What Attorneys Handling Ogden CRE Disputes Need to Know

The most important thing I tell attorneys at the outset of an engagement is this: an appraiser opines on value, and a financing expert opines on financability. These are related but legally distinct questions. In a secured creditor dispute, a plan confirmation proceeding, or a lender liability case, the operative question is frequently not what the property is worth but whether a loan of a specific size is available in the current market. An MAI appraiser is qualified to answer the first question. A capital markets expert is qualified to answer the second. Retaining only one without the other leaves a gap in the evidentiary record that opposing counsel will exploit.

Attorneys should consider retaining a CRE financing expert when the dispute involves any of the following: the adequacy of a secured creditor's collateral position, the feasibility of a proposed reorganization plan that assumes refinancing, a lender's refusal to fund or extend, a claim that a borrower was unable to refinance due to lender conduct, or a dispute over whether environmental conditions preclude financing. In each scenario, the expert provides something no appraiser can: a current market opinion on lender credit standards, pricing, and availability for the specific asset in its specific condition.

What distinguishes a qualified CRE financing expert from a retired banker is active, current market participation. Lending standards, rate environments, and lender credit boxes change materially over periods as short as 12 to 18 months. An expert who has not originated or arranged commercial mortgage financing in the recent past cannot reliably opine on what a lender would have done or would do today. At CLS CRE, our team closes transactions in the current market, which means our opinions reflect what lenders are actually doing, not what they did in prior rate cycles.

Before retaining a financing expert, counsel should gather the following: rent rolls with lease abstracts, the most recent Phase I and Phase II environmental reports, any regulatory correspondence including enforcement orders or NFA letters, existing loan documents including the promissory note and deed of trust, a current rent and sales comparable analysis if available, and any prior appraisals. The earlier in the engagement this information is provided, the more specific and defensible the expert's opinions can be. Financing expert testimony is used most effectively at confirmation hearings to rebut a debtor's feasibility projections, in lender liability trials to establish the standard of care in underwriting, and in partition or foreclosure proceedings to establish the realistic range of refinancing outcomes.

Key Takeaways

  • Environmental contamination does not automatically preclude financing. 1,4-dioxane and chlorinated VOC contamination can be financed around, but only with a documented regulatory pathway, a confirmed remediation cost estimate, and lenders specifically experienced in impaired collateral, none of which are characteristics of conventional bank underwriting.
  • The maximum supportable loan is driven by two binding constraints simultaneously: DSCR capacity derived from in-place NOI and LTV ceiling applied to the as-is contaminated value. In this Ogden engagement, LTV was the binding constraint, producing a maximum loan of approximately $3,380,000 to $3,808,000 depending on lender category.
  • Below-market NNN leases compound the contamination penalty. They reduce NOI, suppress appraised value, and produce a smaller loan ceiling from both the DSCR and LTV methodologies. Attorneys should not assume that a fully-leased property is adequately secured simply because it is occupied.
  • CMBS and life company execution is unavailable for contaminated assets. The realistic lender universe is limited to regional banks with contaminated property programs, select credit unions, and private bridge lenders, each of which prices the risk premium into rate, LTV, and remediation reserve requirements.
  • Remediation cost reserves reduce net loan proceeds to the borrower and must be modeled explicitly in any financing feasibility analysis. A loan that appears sufficient to retire senior debt at face value may fall short after reserve requirements are deducted from available proceeds.
  • Regulatory enforcement status is a threshold underwriting issue. Active EPA or state enforcement actions, recorded environmental covenants, or pending federal liens can eliminate the conventional lending universe entirely by creating title insurance exceptions that institutional lenders will not accept.
  • A CRE financing expert and an appraiser answer different questions. Attorneys who retain only one without the other leave a material evidentiary gap in any proceeding where financing feasibility is contested.
  • Ogden's industrial market is fundamentally sound, with sub-6% vacancy and stable rent trends, but its secondary market characteristics produce cap rate premiums relative to Salt Lake City that must be explicitly modeled in loan sizing, particularly for impaired assets where the market's thinner institutional buyer pool is directly relevant to recovery analysis.

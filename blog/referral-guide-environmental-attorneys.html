Environmental Attorneys Guide to Contaminated Commercial Property Financing

By Trevor Damyan·June 25, 2026·Commercial Lending Solutions

How Environmental Attorneys Can Get Contaminated Commercial Properties Financed -- And Why Most Lenders Won't Even Try

My name is Trevor Damyan. I am a commercial mortgage broker at CLS CRE, based in Los Angeles, and I spend a meaningful portion of my practice working on deals that most brokers decline to quote. Contaminated properties. Phase II findings. Active RWQCB oversight. Brownfield acquisitions with remediation budgets that dwarf the land value. These are not fringe situations -- they are a substantial and underserved corner of the commercial real estate capital markets, and I have found that environmental attorneys are among the most valuable referral relationships I have.

This article is written directly for environmental attorneys, environmental consultants, Phase II practitioners, brownfield redevelopment advisors, and RWQCB practitioners who routinely encounter clients with financing problems tied to environmental conditions. If you have ever told a client "I don't know what you do about the financing side," this article is for you.

Why Environmental Attorneys Encounter Commercial Real Estate Financing Problems

Environmental attorneys occupy a unique position in commercial real estate transactions and disputes. You see properties at their most complicated moments: during due diligence when Phase I or Phase II findings emerge unexpectedly, during active regulatory oversight when a client is mid-remediation under a RWQCB or EPA consent order, during litigation when the financiability of a contaminated asset is itself at issue, and during transactions that have collapsed because a conventional lender discovered historical contamination in the title chain.

Your clients are often in genuine financial distress. They may own an industrial property that has been in the family for three generations, where a dry cleaner or auto body shop operated for decades. They may have purchased a brownfield site with the intention of developing it, only to discover that Phase II findings triggered a lender pullback. They may be holding a property with a cleanup under active regulatory oversight, burning cash on remediation while unable to refinance because the property shows up on a state database.

These clients need legal counsel, but they also need capital. And they often need those two things simultaneously. When your client cannot access financing, their options narrow dramatically. They may be forced into distressed sales. They may default on existing loans. They may be unable to fund the very remediation that would restore the asset's value and marketability. Understanding the specialty lending market for environmentally impacted properties makes you a more complete advisor.

The Five Financing Scenarios Environmental Attorneys Face Most Often

1. Client Owns Industrially Contaminated Property and Needs Financing to Hold During Active Remediation

This is the most common scenario I see from environmental attorney referrals. A client owns an industrial property -- a former dry cleaner, a gas station, a plating facility, a rail yard parcel -- that is currently under RWQCB or EPA oversight. Remediation is underway or planned. The property may generate income from a tenant who has not been displaced, or it may be temporarily non-operational while cleanup proceeds.

The conventional lending market will not touch this property. Bank credit departments will not approve a loan on a property that appears on the Cortese List, the GeoTracker database, or the RCRA corrective action tracking system. Their underwriting guidelines categorically exclude properties with known contamination, regardless of the remediation trajectory. This is not a negotiating position -- it is a hard policy driven by their own regulatory exposure and environmental indemnity requirements.

But specialty lenders will engage with these situations when three conditions are met: a defined remediation pathway with regulatory concurrence, some form of environmental insurance or financial assurance in place, and a property that retains demonstrable residual value after remediation costs are considered. I have financed properties mid-cleanup where the loan-to-value was based on the remediated "as-clean" value discounted appropriately for time and remediation cost uncertainty. These are bridge loans, typically 12 to 36 months, at rates reflecting the elevated risk -- usually in the 9 to 13 percent range -- but they exist, and they allow the borrower to hold the asset through the process rather than liquidating at a distressed price.

2. Brownfield Redevelopment -- Acquisition and Remediation Financing

Brownfield redevelopment is one of the most capital-intensive and complex financing challenges in commercial real estate. Your client has identified an opportunity: a contaminated industrial site in an infill location with strong development economics once the contamination is addressed. The acquisition price is favorable precisely because of the environmental condition. The redevelopment plan is viable. But the financing is nearly impossible to assemble through conventional channels.

Conventional construction lenders will not finance brownfield acquisitions where the contamination is unresolved. They require a clean Phase I or, at minimum, a Phase II with No Further Action determination. Brownfield redevelopment by definition involves a property that does not meet this standard.

The specialty lending solution here involves layered capital. Environmental remediation lenders will sometimes provide acquisition and remediation financing as a senior secured loan, typically requiring a detailed remediation cost estimate from a qualified environmental engineer, Phase II findings, a regulatory pathway under a state voluntary cleanup program or RWQCB oversight, and an environmental insurance policy covering cost overruns and third-party claims. Loan amounts in these structures are typically sized to the land value net of remediation costs, which is conservative, but it provides the capital needed to take the site through cleanup and position it for conventional construction financing once NFA is obtained.

I have also worked with clients who layered brownfield tax credits and HUD Section 108 loan guarantees into these structures, which can significantly improve the economics. Environmental attorneys who understand which public programs are available in their jurisdiction can add substantial value by assembling these capital stacks with appropriate advisors.

3. Environmental Litigation -- Financing Feasibility as an Expert Issue

This scenario is distinct from the others because the financing question is not about obtaining a loan -- it is about establishing the financiability, or lack thereof, of a contaminated property in the context of litigation. Contamination affects property value, and property value affects damages calculations in environmental litigation, condemnation proceedings, and regulatory cost recovery actions.

If your client is arguing that a contaminated property has been rendered effectively unmarketable or unfinanceable, expert testimony on the specialty lending market can be powerful evidence. The argument that a property cannot obtain conventional financing is supported by the actual lending standards that govern bank credit departments. The argument that a property can be financed -- perhaps by a prior owner seeking to minimize their liability -- requires familiarity with the specialty market and what it actually costs.

I have served as a consultant and expert witness in matters where the financing feasibility of a contaminated commercial property was directly at issue. Establishing that the only available financing is at rates and terms reflecting a 30 to 40 percent discount to market -- or that no institutional financing is available at any price -- can materially affect a damages calculation or a regulatory cost recovery negotiation. Environmental attorneys working on these matters benefit from a commercial mortgage broker who understands both the conventional and specialty lending markets well enough to testify credibly about what is and is not available.

4. Phase II Found Significant Contamination -- Conventional Lender Has Called the Loan

This is the emergency scenario. Your client owns a commercial property. A Phase II was conducted -- perhaps as part of a refinance, a sale, or a periodic review -- and significant contamination was found. The existing lender has accelerated the loan or is in the process of doing so. The maturity date is approaching and no conventional lender will extend credit on a contaminated property. Your client has weeks, not months, to find alternative financing or face default and potential foreclosure.

Conventional lenders' acceleration rights on environmental grounds are well established. Most commercial mortgage instruments include environmental covenants and trigger acceleration upon discovery of contamination above regulatory thresholds. If your client is already in default, the situation is genuinely time-sensitive.

Specialty bridge lenders who focus on distressed and environmentally impacted properties can move significantly faster than conventional institutions. I have closed loans in 21 days on properties with active contamination when the situation was well-documented and the borrower was cooperative. These lenders are underwriting a different risk than a bank -- they are underwriting the property's residual value, the remediation pathway, and the borrower's ability to navigate the cleanup process, not the conventional creditworthiness metrics a bank uses. The cost reflects this: rates of 10 to 14 percent, origination fees of 2 to 4 points, and short loan terms. But for a client facing default and potential loss of a multi-million-dollar asset, these terms are often far preferable to the alternative.

5. Environmental Insurance Claim Proceeds Delayed -- Client Needs Bridge Financing Against Expected Recovery

Environmental insurance claims are notoriously slow to resolve. A client may have a legitimate claim for remediation costs, third-party bodily injury, or diminution in property value under a Pollution Legal Liability or Cleanup Cost Cap policy, with hundreds of thousands or millions of dollars in expected recoveries -- but the claim is 18 months into adjustment, coverage disputes are pending, and the client needs capital now to fund operations, continue remediation, or prevent foreclosure.

Financing against insurance receivables is a recognized specialty within commercial lending, though environmental insurance receivables are more complex than most. The lender must evaluate the strength of the underlying claim, the creditworthiness of the insurer, the nature of any coverage disputes, and the likely timeline to resolution. For well-documented claims against investment-grade insurers without serious coverage disputes, bridge lenders will sometimes advance against the expected recovery at significant discounts to face value -- typically 50 to 70 cents on the dollar -- to provide immediate liquidity.

This is not a product that most commercial mortgage brokers handle. It sits at the intersection of commercial finance, environmental insurance, and litigation finance. But for the right situation, it can be the difference between a client who maintains their financial position through a difficult claim period and a client who is forced into distressed asset sales or bankruptcy while waiting for a legitimate recovery.

What Specialty Lenders Actually Require for Environmentally Impacted Properties

Environmental attorneys who want to counsel clients effectively on financing options need to understand what specialty lenders actually evaluate. The requirements are substantive, technical, and non-negotiable at reputable institutions.

A defined regulatory pathway. Lenders financing properties under active remediation will require documentation of the regulatory framework -- RWQCB oversight agreement, EPA consent order, state voluntary cleanup program enrollment letter. They need to understand who is overseeing the cleanup, what the milestones are, and what constitutes successful completion. Regulatory uncertainty without a defined pathway is underwriting death.

A qualified Phase II and remediation cost estimate. The Phase II must be prepared by a licensed professional geologist or professional engineer with relevant experience. Lenders will scrutinize the scope of contamination, the media affected (soil, groundwater, soil vapor), the identified contaminants and concentrations, and the reasonableness of the remediation cost estimate. Cost estimates should be prepared by a qualified environmental engineer, not by the borrower. Lenders discount cost estimates that appear to minimize expected remediation expenditures.

Environmental insurance or financial assurance. Most specialty lenders will require Pollution Legal Liability insurance, Cleanup Cost Cap coverage, or a combination of both before closing. They want to know that cost overruns are insured and that third-party claims arising from the contamination are covered. Properties where environmental insurance is unavailable or unaffordable present substantially higher underwriting risk.

Residual value analysis. Lenders will underwrite to the property's value net of estimated remediation costs, discounted for time and uncertainty. A property with a $5 million as-clean value and $1.5 million in estimated remediation costs might support a loan of $2 to 2.5 million -- roughly 50 percent of the net-of-remediation value. Borrowers who have unrealistic expectations about loan sizing relative to contaminated property values will not close loans.

Experienced borrower team. Lenders want to see that the borrower has retained qualified environmental counsel, an experienced environmental consultant, and a regulatory affairs professional who has worked with the oversight agency before. A borrower attempting to manage an active RWQCB remediation without qualified professional support is an unacceptable credit risk.

Questions Environmental Attorneys Should Ask Any Commercial Mortgage Broker Before Referring

Not all commercial mortgage brokers have experience with environmentally impacted properties. Before referring a client, ask these questions:

  • Have you closed loans on properties with active RWQCB or EPA oversight? The answer should be yes, with specific examples. Financing properties under active regulatory oversight requires familiarity with oversight agreements, remediation milestones, and how lenders evaluate regulatory risk. It is not the same as financing a property with a historical Phase I finding.
  • Which specialty lenders do you have existing relationships with for brownfield or contaminated properties? The answer should name specific categories of lenders -- environmental bridge lenders, hard money lenders with environmental expertise, PACE lenders for remediation -- not generic answers about having a large lender network.
  • How do you handle communication with lenders when the property is in active litigation? Any broker working on a financing where environmental litigation is ongoing needs to understand how to communicate without creating additional discovery exposure or prejudicing the legal proceedings.
  • Do you have relationships with environmental insurance brokers? Environmental insurance is often a prerequisite for specialty lending on contaminated properties. A broker who cannot help a client access Pollution Legal Liability or Cleanup Cost Cap coverage is missing a key piece of the financing solution.
  • What is your typical loan size range for these situations? Contaminated property bridge loans are typically $1 million and above. If the broker primarily handles sub-$1 million loans, they are likely not active in the specialty lending market where environmentally impacted properties are financed.

Key Takeaways

  • A substantial specialty lending market exists for contaminated commercial properties -- conventional lenders' refusal to lend does not mean no financing is available, but it does mean that conventional brokers cannot help.
  • The five scenarios where environmental attorneys most commonly encounter CRE financing needs are: hold financing during active remediation, brownfield acquisition and remediation, litigation feasibility analysis, emergency refinancing after Phase II discovery triggers loan acceleration, and bridge financing against delayed environmental insurance recoveries.
  • Specialty lenders require a defined regulatory pathway, qualified Phase II documentation, environmental insurance, a residual value analysis, and an experienced borrower team. Properties that cannot meet these requirements are generally unfinanceable even in the specialty market.
  • Loan terms for environmentally impacted properties reflect the elevated risk: rates of 9 to 14 percent, origination fees of 2 to 4 points, and short terms of 12 to 36 months. These terms are not exploitative -- they reflect genuine underwriting risk on assets that conventional institutions will not touch.
  • Environmental attorneys who can identify the financing problem early and connect clients with specialty lending expertise before a situation becomes a default or a forced sale create substantially better outcomes for their clients.
  • The referral process is simple: an introduction by email is sufficient. The broker handles all finance-side work, the attorney continues to handle legal and regulatory matters, and the client gets access to both.
  • Not all commercial mortgage brokers have specialty lending experience with contaminated or brownfield properties. Ask specific questions about prior transactions, lender relationships, and environmental insurance expertise before referring.
  • Financing feasibility of contaminated commercial properties is a legitimate expert issue in environmental litigation and regulatory cost recovery proceedings. Commercial mortgage brokers with specialty lending experience can provide credible expert opinions on what the market will and will not support.

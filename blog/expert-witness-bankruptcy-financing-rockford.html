Expert Witness: CRE Financing Feasibility in Chapter 11 Proceedings in Rockford, IL

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Bankruptcy Exit Financing

The Rockford Market Context

Rockford, Illinois sits at the intersection of two realities that matter enormously in bankruptcy exit financing disputes. On one hand, it is a secondary Midwest market with genuine industrial depth, a functioning multifamily rental base, and a commercial real estate ecosystem anchored by healthcare, manufacturing-adjacent logistics, and regional retail. On the other hand, it carries legacy vacancy challenges in its office sector, meaningful cap rate spreads versus primary markets, and a lender community that is more community-bank-dependent than what you find in Chicago or Milwaukee. Both realities shape what a court can reasonably expect from a Plan of Reorganization that proposes to refinance a distressed asset out of bankruptcy.

Rockford's industrial submarket is its strongest performing sector. The region benefits from proximity to Interstate 90, rail access, and proximity to the Chicago metro manufacturing corridor. Industrial vacancy in the greater Rockford market has generally tracked in the 5 to 8 percent range in recent cycles, with functional logistics and light-manufacturing product seeing the most active leasing. Net rents for Class B industrial have ranged from approximately $4.50 to $7.00 per square foot on a triple-net basis depending on clear height, dock configuration, and power. This is meaningful for a bankruptcy proceeding because stabilized industrial NOI in Rockford can, in fact, support conventional financing at loan amounts that satisfy creditor claims, provided the asset is operationally sound.

Multifamily in Rockford presents a more nuanced picture. Cap rates for stabilized apartment assets in Rockford have generally ranged from 7.0 to 8.5 percent, significantly wider than Chicago suburban assets at 5.0 to 6.0 percent. This spread matters for exit financing because cap rate compression is not occurring in Rockford at the pace seen in primary markets. Average asking rents have been in the $800 to $1,100 per month range for Class B garden-style product, which limits NOI per unit and therefore constrains maximum supportable loan amounts. A debtor proposing a multifamily exit financing must realistically model these rents and vacancy rates, typically 8 to 12 percent physical vacancy for stabilizing assets, into the income analysis before any creditor or court should accept a financing feasibility argument.

Office in Rockford remains challenged. The downtown Rockford office market has seen persistent vacancy above 20 percent in many submarkets, with suburban office performing somewhat better but still impaired by remote work adoption and tenant consolidation. For a bankruptcy proceeding involving an office asset, institutional lenders are largely out of the picture in this market, which reshapes the available financing universe dramatically and is a critical input into any expert opinion on plan feasibility.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by counsel representing a creditor class in a Chapter 11 proceeding involving a commercial income-producing property in the Rockford metropolitan area. The asset in question was a mid-size industrial flex property that had been generating inconsistent cash flow in the years preceding the bankruptcy filing. The debtor's proposed Plan of Reorganization contemplated a refinance of the property as the mechanism for paying off secured creditors and funding the plan distribution. The court needed to evaluate whether that refinance was genuinely feasible, what loan amount the property could realistically support, and what categories of lenders were actually originating loans on bankruptcy estate assets in this type of market.

Our engagement required us to answer three specific questions. First, could the subject property support first-mortgage financing in an amount sufficient to satisfy the senior secured creditor's allowed claim? Second, what would a market-rate lender actually require in terms of debt service coverage, loan-to-value, occupancy, and lease structure before committing to a loan on an asset emerging from bankruptcy? Third, was the debtor's proposed financing timeline realistic, and what contingencies or conditions would realistically delay or prevent closing?

These are financing feasibility questions, not valuation questions. An appraiser can opine on market value. CLS CRE was retained to opine on whether that value, and the income supporting it, could actually be leveraged into a financeable loan under current market conditions. Those are distinct disciplines, and the distinction matters in a plan confirmation hearing.

The Financial Analysis

The financial analysis began with reconstruction of the property's actual net operating income. For a bankruptcy estate asset, historical financials are often incomplete, distorted by the debtor's operational failures, or inflated by non-recurring income. We normalized the NOI using a trailing twelve-month period adjusted for deferred maintenance costs that would be required to bring the property to institutional lending standards, then cross-referenced that normalized figure against market rents for comparable industrial flex space in the Rockford submarket.

For this property, we determined a stabilized NOI of approximately $310,000 annually, reflecting an occupied portion of the rentable square footage at market rents, a 7 percent vacancy and credit loss reserve, and management, insurance, and real estate tax expenses consistent with the Rockford submarket. This was not the debtor's projected NOI, which was optimistic. It was the NOI a lender's underwriter would independently arrive at after applying their own haircuts and vacancy assumptions.

We applied a debt service coverage ratio threshold of 1.25x, which is the standard minimum for conventional commercial mortgage lending on industrial assets. Many community banks active in secondary Midwest markets will require 1.25x to 1.30x DSCR on an asset emerging from bankruptcy, reflecting the additional perceived risk associated with the property's history. Some bridge lenders will accept 1.10x to 1.15x, but those lenders price accordingly, and that pricing must be reflected in the feasibility analysis.

Assuming a 7.25 percent interest rate on a conventional commercial mortgage with a 25-year amortization schedule (reflecting current rate conditions for secondary market commercial assets in the 2024 to 2025 environment), the maximum annual debt service supportable at 1.25x DSCR on $310,000 of NOI is $248,000. At a 7.25 percent rate amortizing over 25 years, a monthly constant of approximately 0.00718 applies. That produces a maximum supportable loan of approximately $2,875,000, calculated as follows: $248,000 divided by 12 equals $20,667 per month of allowable debt service; $20,667 divided by 0.00718 equals approximately $2,878,000 in maximum loan proceeds from a DSCR perspective.

We then cross-referenced that figure against the LTV constraint. For an industrial flex asset in a secondary Midwest market, institutional lenders will typically lend to 65 to 70 percent LTV maximum. Bridge lenders may go to 70 to 75 percent with compensating factors. If the property's appraised value was established at $4,200,000, then 65 percent LTV produces a maximum loan of $2,730,000, and 70 percent LTV produces $2,940,000. The DSCR constraint and the LTV constraint converged near $2,875,000 as the maximum supportable first-mortgage loan. This was the opinion we offered to the court: the property could support a first-mortgage loan in the range of $2,700,000 to $2,900,000 under current market conditions, which was sufficient to satisfy the senior secured creditor's allowed claim of approximately $2,500,000 with a reasonable margin for closing costs and reserves.

This level of specificity is what a financing expert provides that an appraiser does not. The appraiser opines on value. We opine on what the market will actually lend against that value, under current underwriting standards, for this specific asset type in this specific submarket, with this specific credit history.

The Complicating Factors

Bankruptcy Stigma and Lender Hesitation. The most significant complicating factor in any bankruptcy exit financing is lender psychology. Most conventional commercial mortgage lenders will not originate a loan that closes simultaneously with bankruptcy plan confirmation, even if the numbers support it. Their credit committees view the prior bankruptcy as a risk flag, and many have internal policies prohibiting loans to borrowers with an open bankruptcy on record. This does not mean financing is impossible. It means the financing universe narrows significantly to lenders who specifically underwrite post-bankruptcy transitions, which typically means bridge lenders, private credit funds, and certain community banks with workout experience. Understanding this narrowing is critical to a realistic plan feasibility opinion.

Title and Encumbrance Clearance. Assets emerging from Chapter 11 often carry subordinate liens, judgment liens, tax liens, or mechanic's liens that must be resolved through the confirmation order and subsequent title work before any new lender will fund. In Rockford, Winnebago County title can carry legacy issues from prior distress cycles. A lender will require a clean title insurance commitment as a condition of closing, and obtaining that commitment on an accelerated timeline is not always realistic. If the plan contemplates a 60-day post-confirmation closing, but title clearance requires 90 to 120 days, the plan has a structural timeline problem that a financing expert should identify for the court.

Lease Quality and Tenant Credit. For an industrial flex asset, the quality and remaining term of occupancy leases is a primary underwriting variable. A lender will not lend at stabilized NOI multiples if the leases supporting that NOI are month-to-month, have less than 18 to 24 months of remaining term, or are with tenants whose own financial condition is questionable. In a bankruptcy context, pre-petition tenants may have lease modifications, rent deferral agreements, or their own financial stress. The expert analysis must audit lease terms independently and present the lender's likely view, not the debtor's optimistic view.

Deferred Maintenance and Lender Property Condition Requirements. Any institutional lender will order a Property Condition Assessment as part of their due diligence. For a bankruptcy estate asset, deferred maintenance is common. If the PCA identifies immediate repairs in excess of 5 percent of the loan amount, many lenders will either reduce loan proceeds, require an escrow holdback, or decline to lend. In Rockford's climate, HVAC systems, roofing, and parking lot surfaces are frequent sources of significant deferred maintenance costs. A realistic financing feasibility opinion must account for the lender's likely PCA findings and how those findings affect proceeds.

Secondary Market Liquidity Constraints. Rockford is not a market where CMBS executions are routinely available for mid-size assets. CMBS conduit lenders generally require loan amounts above $5 million to $7 million to justify the securitization overhead, and many conduit programs have minimum market population thresholds that effectively exclude Rockford. This matters because it concentrates the available financing universe in portfolio lenders, bridge funds, and community banks, all of which carry different pricing, term, and recourse structures than agency or conduit execution. The expert must explain this market structure to the court clearly, because the debtor's plan may have been written assuming financing access that does not actually exist in this market for this loan size.

The Rockford Lender Market

Community Banks and Regional Lenders. Rockford has a functioning community bank ecosystem that includes both locally chartered institutions and regional bank branches with commercial real estate lending authority. These lenders are the most active source of conventional commercial mortgage financing for assets in the $1 million to $5 million range. Their credit boxes typically require 1.25x DSCR, 65 to 70 percent LTV, full recourse from the borrower entity, and stabilized occupancy of at least 85 to 90 percent at loan closing. For a post-bankruptcy asset, community banks will conduct enhanced due diligence on borrower credit, prior bankruptcy circumstances, and property management capability. Pricing for these institutions in the current rate environment has ranged from approximately Prime plus 0.50 percent to Prime plus 1.50 percent on a floating rate basis, or fixed rates in the 7.00 to 8.50 percent range for 3 to 5 year terms with 20 to 25 year amortization.

Bridge Lenders and Private Credit Funds. For assets that cannot immediately qualify for conventional financing due to occupancy gaps, lease-up requirements, or post-bankruptcy timing constraints, bridge lenders are the relevant financing category. These lenders, typically private debt funds or specialty finance companies with a Midwest presence, will lend at higher LTV ratios (up to 70 to 75 percent) on shorter terms (12 to 36 months), with higher pricing (current bridge rates for secondary Midwest industrial range from approximately 9.50 to 12.00 percent depending on leverage and complexity), and typically with interest-only payment structures during the bridge term. For a plan of reorganization that requires a fast closing, bridge capital is often the only realistic option, and the plan must account for the higher debt service burden of bridge pricing in its feasibility analysis.

Credit Unions with CRE Programs. Several credit unions operating in northern Illinois have commercial real estate lending programs that are sometimes overlooked in bankruptcy exit financing analyses. These institutions can be competitive on pricing for assets in the $1 million to $3 million range and may have more flexible credit policy on post-bankruptcy assets if the underlying collateral quality is strong. They are not available for all asset types, as many credit union CRE programs favor owner-occupied or mixed-use properties over pure investment assets, but they represent a category that a complete lender market analysis should evaluate.

SBA 504 as a Parallel Structure. Where the reorganized entity intends to occupy a portion of the subject property for its own business operations, SBA 504 financing may be available as a structure that combines a conventional first mortgage with an SBA second lien. This structure can reduce the conventional lender's LTV exposure and may enable financing that would not qualify on a purely conventional basis. The SBA 504 program requires owner-occupation of at least 51 percent of the space for existing buildings and has specific eligibility rules for entities with prior bankruptcy filings, but it is a legitimate financing pathway that belongs in a comprehensive feasibility analysis.

What Attorneys Handling Rockford CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to Retain a CRE Financing Expert. Any Plan of Reorganization that proposes a refinancing event as the mechanism for paying creditors requires a financing feasibility opinion. If you are representing a creditor class, a plan proponent, or any party whose recovery depends on whether the proposed financing will actually close, you need a CRE financing expert engaged before the disclosure statement is approved, not after. The earlier the expert is engaged, the more useful the analysis is to structuring the plan itself, not just defending or attacking it at confirmation.

What a Financing Expert Can Answer That an Appraiser Cannot. An appraiser establishes value. A CRE financing expert establishes what the market will lend against that value, at what terms, under what conditions, on what timeline, and with what probability of closing. These are separate and equally important questions in a plan confirmation proceeding. A court evaluating whether a plan is feasible needs both opinions. An appraiser is not qualified to testify about lender underwriting standards, DSCR thresholds, the composition of the available lender market for a specific asset type in a specific geography, or the realistic timeline for closing a post-bankruptcy commercial mortgage. Those opinions require someone who is actively originating commercial mortgages in the current market.

Qualifications That Matter. Active deal experience is the primary qualification that distinguishes a credible CRE financing expert from a retired banker or academic. A financing expert should be able to demonstrate that they are currently closing commercial mortgage transactions across multiple lender types, in multiple markets, including secondary markets like Rockford. CLS CRE has closed approximately $1 billion in commercial real estate transaction volume across more than 1,000 lender relationships in all 50 states. That active market presence is what allows us to testify credibly about what a lender will actually do today, not what one lender did five years ago in a different market cycle.

Information Counsel Should Gather Before the Engagement. To produce a useful financing feasibility opinion efficiently, we need: current rent rolls with lease expiration dates and tenant creditworthiness information; trailing 24 months of property operating statements; the existing appraisal and any prior lender's underwriting materials; the debtor's proposed plan financing term sheet or LOI if one exists; title search results identifying all encumbrances; and any existing property condition assessment or environmental reports. The absence of any of these documents is itself relevant information for the expert opinion, because a lender would require all of them and the inability to produce them affects closing probability.

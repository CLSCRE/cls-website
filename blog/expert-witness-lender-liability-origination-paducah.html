The Paducah Market Context

Paducah, Kentucky sits at the confluence of the Tennessee and Ohio Rivers in McCracken County, and it functions as the regional commercial hub for the western Kentucky, southern Illinois, and northwest Tennessee tri-state corridor. For attorneys and investors unfamiliar with the market, that geography matters enormously in a financing dispute context. Paducah is not a tertiary market in the dismissive sense that term sometimes implies. It is a legitimate regional center with a diversified economic base anchored by healthcare (including a major regional medical system), manufacturing, barge and river logistics, and federal government employment tied to the Paducah Gaseous Diffusion Plant site and ongoing environmental remediation work managed through the Department of Energy. That economic diversity shapes how lenders underwrite deals here, and it shapes the standards of care that a qualified expert witness must understand and apply.

On the industrial and flex side, Paducah benefits directly from its river logistics infrastructure. Industrial vacancy in the market has historically run in the 6% to 10% range, with Class B and functional warehouse product trading tighter than that in submarkets adjacent to port and rail access points. Net rents for industrial product in Paducah generally range from $4.50 to $7.50 per square foot depending on clear height, dock configuration, and proximity to US-60 and the Barkley Regional Airport industrial corridor. Multifamily fundamentals are notably strong relative to the market's size. Paducah has seen consistent occupancy in the 92% to 96% range for stabilized apartment product, driven in part by workforce housing demand from regional employers and a relatively limited pipeline of new construction. Market cap rates for stabilized multifamily in Paducah have been observed in the 6.25% to 7.50% range, which is wider than primary markets but representative of the risk-adjusted pricing that institutional lenders apply in this geography. Retail and office fundamentals are more mixed. Downtown Paducah has seen meaningful reinvestment in mixed-use and adaptive reuse projects, supported in part by historic tax credits, but suburban strip retail and older office product carry higher vacancy and lender scrutiny. These market-specific fundamentals are not background color; they directly determine whether a loan as originated was supportable under market standards, which is the central question in a lender liability engagement.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing a defendant commercial mortgage broker in a professional negligence and lender liability matter arising from a commercial real estate transaction in Paducah. The underlying asset was a mixed-use property consisting of ground-floor retail space with income-producing multifamily units on the upper floors, located in a secondary commercial corridor in McCracken County. The borrower alleged that the broker had placed the loan with an inappropriate lender, had failed to disclose material terms adequately before closing, and had structured the financing in a manner that a reasonably competent commercial mortgage professional would not have recommended given the borrower's stated investment objectives and the property's income profile.

The core legal and financial questions we were asked to address included the following: whether the loan origination process, from initial underwriting through commitment letter and closing, met the standard of care applicable to a commercial mortgage broker operating in the Kentucky market; whether the loan terms disclosed at commitment, including rate, prepayment structure, and recourse provisions, were consistent with what market-rate lenders were offering for this asset type and borrower profile during the relevant origination period; whether the broker had a professional obligation to present alternative financing structures or sources, and whether the failure to do so constituted a deviation from industry practice; and whether the loan as structured was financeable at the proceeds level presented to the borrower, given the actual income documentation available at origination. CLS CRE was engaged to produce a Rule 26 expert report addressing all four questions with supporting financial analysis.

The Financial Analysis

The analysis began with a reconstruction of the underwritten net operating income. In a mixed-use asset of this type, gross potential rent must be separated by use: retail on the ground floor and residential above. At the time of origination, the retail component consisted of two tenant suites generating combined base rent of approximately $48,000 annually. The residential component consisted of eight units generating gross scheduled rent of approximately $96,000 annually. Total gross scheduled income was therefore approximately $144,000. From that figure, we applied a market-supported vacancy and credit loss allowance. For the retail component in this Paducah corridor, a 10% vacancy factor was appropriate given the softness in non-anchored strip retail absorption. For the residential component, given Paducah's occupancy trends, a 5% allowance was defensible. Blended effective gross income after vacancy loss came to approximately $131,400.

Operating expenses on a mixed-use property of this vintage in Paducah, accounting for property taxes at McCracken County rates, insurance, maintenance, management at 8% of EGI, and a replacement reserve appropriate for the age and condition of the improvements, were underwritten at approximately $52,500 annually, representing approximately 40% of EGI. That expense ratio is consistent with market standards for this asset type and age class. Stabilized NOI was therefore approximately $78,900.

The industry standard DSCR threshold for this asset type at a community bank or regional bank lender is 1.25x. Some lenders apply 1.20x for multifamily-heavy mixed-use, and a minority of credit unions or portfolio lenders will go to 1.15x with strong compensating factors. For this analysis, we applied 1.25x as the market-standard floor, consistent with the underwriting guidelines published by the majority of lenders active in this loan size range.

At the time of origination, market-rate financing for a mixed-use asset of this type in a secondary Kentucky market was priced in the range of 6.50% to 7.25% fixed for a five-year term, with a 25-year amortization schedule. We used 6.875% as the analysis rate, representing the midpoint of that range and consistent with the commitment rate actually documented in the loan file. At 6.875% on a 25-year amortization, the annual debt service constant is approximately 7.92%. Applying the 1.25x DSCR constraint: maximum annual debt service supportable equals NOI divided by 1.25, which is $78,900 divided by 1.25, yielding $63,120. Maximum loan amount supportable by cash flow equals $63,120 divided by the annual constant of 7.92%, which produces a maximum supportable loan of approximately $796,970.

The loan as originated was $875,000. That origination amount exceeded the cash-flow-supportable maximum by approximately $78,000, representing a DSCR at origination of approximately 1.136x on the actual loan balance. At 1.136x, this loan fell below the 1.20x floor that even the most aggressive lenders in this asset class would accept without documented compensating factors. No compensating factors were identified in the loan file. The LTV at origination was approximately 72% based on the appraised value in the file. That LTV is within the 70% to 75% range typical for this asset type, meaning the loan did not fail a collateral test. It failed a cash flow test. That distinction is analytically critical: the lender could argue the collateral was adequate, but the income-supportable loan amount, applied with the correct DSCR standard, did not support the proceeds advanced. That gap between what the cash flow supported and what was originated is the financial foundation of the negligence claim.

The Complicating Factors

Mixed-Use Income Stratification. Lenders do not underwrite mixed-use properties as a single undifferentiated income stream. They disaggregate retail income from residential income because the risk profiles are different, the lease structures are different, and the default correlation to local economic conditions is different. A broker who presents blended financials to a lender without flagging the retail component's lease term exposure or tenant credit quality has failed to perform the pre-submission analysis that a competent broker is expected to deliver. In this engagement, the retail leases had terms of less than two years remaining at origination with no executed renewal options. A market-standard lender underwriting retail income requires minimum lease term coverage of the loan term plus six months, or at minimum a documented probability of renewal supported by tenant financials. Neither was present here. That retail income was effectively soft from a lender underwriting perspective, yet it was included at full value in the broker's submission package.

Seasoning of Ownership and Operating History. The borrower had owned the property for less than 18 months at the time of the refinance. Most commercial lenders, particularly banks and CMBS conduits, require a minimum of 12 to 24 months of operating history under current ownership before they will rely on in-place income rather than pro forma projections. In this case, the 12-month operating statement provided at origination included a period when two of the residential units were vacant due to a renovation. A competent broker's obligation includes flagging that the trailing income statement is not representative of stabilized performance and either sourcing a lender whose credit policy accommodates that underwriting gap or clearly disclosing to the borrower that stabilized NOI was being assumed rather than demonstrated. That disclosure was not documented in the broker's file.

Prepayment Structure Mismatch. The loan as originated included a five-year lockout followed by a five-year step-down prepayment penalty. For a borrower whose stated objective was value-add repositioning with a planned exit or refinance within three to five years, this structure was facially inconsistent with the investment thesis. A competent commercial mortgage broker is expected to ask about and document the borrower's hold period and exit strategy, and to match the loan structure to that strategy or to clearly disclose the economic cost of a prepayment penalty if the borrower proceeds with a structure that conflicts with the exit timeline. The broker's file contained no hold period analysis and no prepayment cost disclosure in dollar terms.

The Paducah Lender Market

Understanding which lenders are active in Paducah for mixed-use and multifamily-anchored transactions is essential both to underwriting a live deal and to evaluating whether a broker's placement decisions were consistent with market practice. The Paducah market is served by a relatively deep community banking sector. Several regional and community banks headquartered in western Kentucky, as well as branches of larger Kentucky and Tennessee regional institutions, are active portfolio lenders in the $500,000 to $5,000,000 commercial real estate loan range. These lenders are relationship-driven, they apply manual underwriting rather than automated scoring models, and their credit committees have familiarity with Paducah submarket fundamentals that national correspondent lenders lack. For a mixed-use asset of the size analyzed here, a community bank placement was the appropriate primary option, and the credit standards applicable are those I described above: 1.20x to 1.25x DSCR, 70% to 75% LTV, full recourse on loans under $2,000,000, and personal financial statement requirements for all guarantors.

Credit unions with commercial real estate lending programs are also active in the Paducah market. They tend to offer slightly more flexible income documentation standards for owner-operators and borrowers with established depository relationships, but they cap commercial real estate exposure as a share of total assets in accordance with NCUA guidelines, which creates capacity constraints. For income-producing investment property without an owner-occupant component, credit union placement is less reliable.

Correspondent lenders and small-balance CMBS platforms are technically available for Paducah transactions but are generally unsuitable for mixed-use assets in this price range due to minimum loan sizes, stricter lease term requirements, and the prepayment structures that CMBS execution requires. A broker placing a value-add mixed-use property in Paducah into a CMBS program without explicit client disclosure of those structural constraints would be operating outside the standard of care for this market and loan size. Life company lenders and debt funds with programmatic mixed-use appetite are not meaningfully active in Paducah below $10,000,000, making them irrelevant to the standard-of-care analysis for this transaction size.

What Attorneys Handling Paducah CRE Disputes Need to Know

A CRE financing expert witness fills a specific analytical gap that appraisers, accountants, and even experienced real estate attorneys cannot fill on their own. An appraiser can establish what a property was worth at a point in time. A CRE financing expert can establish what a competent lender or broker should have done with that value, given the income stream, the loan structure, the market conditions at origination, and the applicable standards of care in the commercial mortgage profession. Those are fundamentally different questions, and conflating them is a recurring problem in commercial real estate litigation.

Attorneys should retain a CRE financing expert when the dispute involves any of the following: allegations that a broker failed to disclose material loan terms or failed to present available alternative financing; claims that a lender's underwriting was grossly deficient or that a loan was originated without adequate income support; disputes over whether a commitment letter or term sheet accurately represented market terms; cases where the borrower alleges that the loan structure was inconsistent with the stated investment objective; matters involving the feasibility of refinancing or alternative capital at a specific point in time; and bankruptcy proceedings where the confirmability of a reorganization plan depends on whether proposed financing is realistically available in the market.

A qualified CRE financing expert is not simply a retired banker. The qualification standard requires active, current engagement in commercial mortgage origination, a demonstrated network across multiple lender categories (not just one institution's credit culture), experience structuring deals at the complexity level at issue, and the ability to articulate market standards in the specific asset class and geography relevant to the dispute. CLS CRE has closed approximately $1 billion in commercial real estate financing transactions across more than 50 states, maintains active relationships with more than 1,000 institutional and private lenders, and has provided Rule 26 expert reports and testimony in financing feasibility, lender liability, and loan origination standards disputes across multiple jurisdictions.

Before engaging a CRE financing expert, counsel should gather the complete loan file including the original application, all appraisals and third-party reports ordered at origination, the broker's submission package to lenders, all commitment letters and term sheets received, the executed loan documents, closing disclosures, and any written communications between the borrower, broker, and lender during the origination process. The absence of documentation in a broker's file is itself analytically significant. A competent commercial mortgage broker is expected to maintain records of the lenders approached, the terms quoted, the reasons for lender selection, and any disclosures made to the borrower about structure, cost, and risk. Gaps in that record are not neutral.

Key Takeaways

  • The cash-flow test governs proceeds, not just collateral value. In this engagement, the loan passed an LTV test but failed a DSCR test at market-standard thresholds. A lender or broker who relies solely on appraised value to justify loan proceeds, without documenting that the income stream supports the debt service at a 1.20x to 1.25x DSCR, has deviated from the standard of care applicable to income-property lending.
  • Mixed-use income requires disaggregated underwriting. Retail and residential income carry different risk profiles, different lease structures, and different lender treatment. A broker who blends them without flagging the weaker component is presenting a misleading underwriting package, regardless of whether that misrepresentation was intentional.
  • Loan structure must be documented against the borrower's stated objectives. Prepayment penalties, recourse provisions, and loan term lengths are not boilerplate; they have material economic consequences for a borrower's investment strategy. A competent broker documents the match or mismatch between structure and objective and discloses it in writing before closing.
  • The Paducah lender market has depth at the community bank level. For transactions in the $500,000 to $5,000,000 range, community and regional bank lenders are the appropriate primary placement universe. A broker who bypassed available community bank options with more favorable terms and placed a borrower into a more expensive or restrictive loan structure without explanation has a disclosure and duty problem.
  • Operating history gaps require documented underwriting adjustments. When trailing financials include vacancy periods, renovation disruption, or sub-12-month ownership seasoning, a competent broker must either adjust the income underwriting to stabilized assumptions and disclose that adjustment, or source lenders whose credit policies are designed to accommodate the income documentation available.
  • A CRE financing expert answers different questions than an appraiser. Appraisers establish value. CRE financing experts establish what was financeable, at what terms, from what lenders, under what market conditions, and whether the origination as executed met the standards of a reasonably competent commercial mortgage professional. Those questions require active market expertise, not historical valuation methodology.
  • Expert engagement should occur before depositions, not after. Retaining a CRE financing expert early in the discovery process allows counsel to shape document requests around the specific underwriting file components that matter most

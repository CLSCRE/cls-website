Rule 26 Financing Feasibility Reports: What Attorneys Need to Know

By Trevor Damyan · June 25, 2026

Commercial real estate financing disputes turn on a deceptively narrow question: could this property have been financed, on what terms, and at what loan amount? That question sounds simple. In practice it requires a licensed broker with active lender relationships to reconstruct the market as it existed on a specific date, apply institutional underwriting standards to a specific property, and reduce the entire analysis to a written opinion that can survive cross-examination and Daubert scrutiny.

I produce Rule 26(a)(2)(B) financing feasibility reports for attorneys in California and nationally. This guide explains what those reports must contain under the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, how the core financial analysis works, and what separates an expert opinion that holds up in court from one that gets excluded on a motion in limine.

What Rule 26(a)(2)(B) Requires from a CRE Financing Expert

Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 26(a)(2)(B) governs the written report that any retained expert must produce before testifying. The rule is not a formality. Courts routinely exclude expert testimony for non-compliance, and a deficient report can doom otherwise strong financial evidence. The rule requires six specific disclosures:

  • A complete statement of all opinions and the bases for each. The expert must state each financing opinion expressly and tie it to specific facts. "The property was financeable" is not an opinion under Rule 26. "Based on a stabilized NOI of $800,000 and prevailing DSCR requirements of 1.25x at a 7.00% fixed rate, the maximum supportable loan was $8,000,000, representing a 44% LTV against the $18,000,000 appraised value, well within the 65% ceiling required by conventional commercial lenders" is an opinion.
  • The facts and data considered. This includes the rent roll, operating statements, the appraisal, any environmental reports, the lender matrix used, market survey data, and any conversations with actual lenders. Everything the expert considered must be disclosed, including facts that cut against the opinion.
  • Exhibits that will be used to summarize or support the opinions. A well-prepared financing feasibility report will attach the lender survey matrix, the pro forma operating statement, and the DSCR calculation as exhibits. These become the map for deposition cross-examination.
  • The expert's qualifications. For a CRE financing expert, qualifications must include relevant licensing (California DRE or equivalent, NMLS registration), transaction volume, years of active practice in the relevant property type and geography, and demonstrated lender relationships. An academic expert without an active brokerage license faces real credibility problems when the opposing expert has closed 50 deals in the same market.
  • A list of other cases in which the expert has testified as an expert at trial or by deposition in the preceding four years. Courts use this list to evaluate credibility and to check whether the expert has taken inconsistent positions in other matters.
  • A statement of compensation. The expert must disclose the fee arrangement, including hourly rate and whether any portion of the compensation is contingent on the outcome. Contingency-based expert fees are generally improper and will be used on cross.

Why Financing Experts Must Disclose Lender Contact History and Methodology

The bases-and-data requirement has particular bite for financing feasibility experts because the core methodology involves actual or hypothetical lender outreach. Courts have excluded financing expert testimony where the expert based a "lender market survey" solely on general knowledge without documenting any specific lender contacts. The disclosure must identify which lenders were contacted, when, by what method, and what each lender indicated regarding terms, leverage, and appetite for the specific deal.

Where the expert did not conduct an active survey (because the relevant date is historical), the report must explain the alternative methodology: trade publications, CMBS loan data, lender term sheet archives, and professional contacts who can speak to historical lending conditions. The methodology section must be explicit enough that opposing counsel can probe it on deposition and that the court can evaluate it under Daubert.

The Structure of a Financing Feasibility Report

A properly constructed financing feasibility report is not a narrative opinion letter. It is a structured technical document with discrete sections, each serving a specific admissibility or persuasive function. The sections that appear in every defensible report are:

1. Engagement Description

This section identifies who retained the expert, what question was posed (financing feasibility as of a specific date, maximum supportable loan, whether specific terms were commercially reasonable), what documents were provided, and the scope of the assignment. The engagement description establishes the frame for everything that follows and prevents opposing counsel from arguing the expert exceeded the scope of retention.

2. Expert Qualifications

Qualifications for a financing feasibility expert must go beyond a list of credentials. The report should specify the number of transactions closed in the relevant property type (multifamily, industrial, retail, office) and geography, the lending institutions with which the expert maintains active relationships, and any specific experience with the complicating factors at issue in the case, whether that is contaminated collateral, distressed borrowers, or complex ownership structures.

3. Property and Market Context

This section describes the subject property as it existed on the relevant date: location, size, property type, occupancy, lease structure, operating history, and any physical condition issues. It also establishes the relevant lending market, which lender types were active in this asset class, what LTV and DSCR requirements those lenders were applying, and whether there were any macro-market conditions affecting credit availability at the relevant time (for example, the 2023 regional banking contraction materially tightened multifamily lending outside agency channels).

Courts want to understand the market context because financing feasibility is a market question, not a theoretical one. A property that would support a $10M loan in 2021 might support only $7M in 2023 given the same NOI, because rising rates increase the debt service constant and compress the maximum supportable loan.

4. Special Circumstances Analysis

If the property or borrower has complicating factors, each must be analyzed separately with a clear conclusion. Lumping special circumstances together and issuing a blanket opinion that the property was "unfinanceable" is the most common failure mode in opposing expert reports. A rigorous report addresses each factor on its own terms and shows whether it rises to the level of a deal-killer or merely affects pricing, leverage, or execution timeline.

5. Financial Analysis

This is the quantitative core of the report: normalized NOI calculation, debt service constant, DSCR constraint, maximum supportable loan, and LTV crosscheck. Each step must be shown explicitly with sourced inputs. This section is where most expert battles are fought and where methodology must be bulletproof.

6. Lender Market Survey

A description of the lenders who were or would have been willing to finance the property, on what terms, and what evidence supports that conclusion. For current-date opinions, this is based on actual lender outreach. For historical opinions, it is based on documented market data and professional contacts with knowledge of historical conditions.

7. Formal Opinions

The opinions section states each conclusion in clear, numbered form: the maximum supportable loan, the achievable LTV, whether the property was financeable, whether specific terms were commercially reasonable, and any ancillary opinions. Each opinion should be expressed to a "reasonable degree of professional certainty," the standard applicable to non-scientific expert testimony in most jurisdictions.

The DSCR Analysis in Detail

The debt service coverage ratio analysis is the technical heart of any financing feasibility report. It is also the section that is most frequently performed incorrectly by experts who lack active brokerage experience. Every step must be correct, because each step feeds the next and a single error compounds into a materially wrong loan amount.

Step 1: NOI Calculation and Normalization

Net operating income is not simply "gross revenue minus operating expenses" as reported on a profit and loss statement. Lenders normalize NOI for underwriting purposes, and the normalization adjustments are where disputes most often arise.

What lenders include in NOI:

  • Scheduled gross rents at current lease rates, not proforma rents
  • Other income that is recurring and attributable to the property (laundry, parking, storage, antenna leases)
  • Less: a vacancy and credit loss allowance, typically 5% for stabilized multifamily and 10% for retail/office regardless of actual occupancy, because lenders underwrite to stabilized market vacancy rather than current occupancy
  • Less: operating expenses including taxes, insurance, management fee (usually minimum 4-5% of effective gross income even if the owner self-manages), utilities, maintenance, and reserves for replacement

What lenders exclude or adjust:

  • Mortgage interest and depreciation (below-the-line items)
  • Capital expenditures (replaced by a reserves-for-replacement line)
  • One-time or non-recurring income
  • Above-market rents from related-party leases
  • Management fees below market rate (lenders impute a market-rate fee)

This distinction matters enormously in litigation. An owner's tax return may show a very different income figure than what a lender would underwrite, because the tax return includes depreciation, interest expense, and possibly inflated expense deductions. A financing expert must reconstruct the lender's underwritten NOI from source documents, not from the borrower's reported financials.

Step 2: The Debt Service Constant

The debt service constant is the annual loan payment per dollar of loan balance, expressed as a percentage. It is a function of interest rate and amortization period only. At a 7.00% fixed rate on a 30-year amortization schedule, the monthly mortgage constant is 0.6653%, yielding an annual constant of 7.984%. Rounding to 7.98% is sufficient for feasibility purposes.

The constant is rate-sensitive. At 6.00%, the 30-year annual constant drops to 7.20%. At 8.00%, it rises to 8.81%. This is why the same property can support materially different loan amounts in different rate environments.

Step 3: The DSCR Divisor and Why It Matters

Lenders require that NOI exceed annual debt service by a specified margin, expressed as the DSCR. The DSCR is calculated as NOI divided by annual debt service. A lender requiring a 1.25x DSCR will size the loan so that NOI equals 1.25 times the annual debt service payment.

The divisor choice has a direct and significant impact on the maximum supportable loan:

DSCR Requirement Lender Type (Typical) Effect on Max Loan (vs. 1.20x)
1.20x Aggressive bank, credit union, debt fund Baseline
1.25x Conventional bank, regional lender, CMBS 4% lower loan amount
1.30x Life insurance company, agency (some programs) 8% lower loan amount
1.40x Conservative life company, hotel/hospitality lenders 14% lower loan amount

An expert who applies a 1.30x DSCR to a deal where conventional bank financing was available at 1.20x will understate the maximum loan by approximately 8%. In a $10M loan scenario, that is an $800,000 error. Courts have found financing experts unreliable where they applied an inappropriately conservative DSCR without market evidence supporting that choice.

Step 4: Maximum Supportable Loan Calculation

The formula: Maximum Loan = NOI / (DSCR x Annual Constant)

Or equivalently, Maximum Loan = (NOI / DSCR) / Annual Constant

Step 5: LTV Crosscheck

The DSCR-derived loan amount is constrained by a hard LTV ceiling. Most conventional lenders will not exceed 65-75% LTV regardless of debt service coverage. Life insurance companies typically cap at 60-65%. Bridge lenders may go to 75-80% but at significantly higher rates. The maximum supportable loan is the lesser of the DSCR-derived amount and the LTV ceiling applied to the appraised value.

Analyzing Special Circumstances

The most important distinction in financing feasibility analysis is between factors that affect the terms of financing and factors that preclude financing entirely. These are very different conclusions, and conflating them is the most consequential error an opposing expert can make. Each complicating factor must be analyzed separately, on its own facts.

Environmental Contamination

Phase I and Phase II environmental issues do not automatically preclude commercial financing. Lenders routinely finance contaminated or formerly contaminated properties when the contamination is quantified, remediation is in progress or complete, and sufficient LTV headroom exists to cover potential liability. A property with $2M in estimated remediation costs and a $16M appraised value supports a $8M loan at 50% LTV without difficulty. The expert must analyze the specific contamination, the remaining liability estimate, and the lender types that would accept the risk, not issue a blanket "contaminated properties are unfinanceable" opinion.

Bankruptcy

A borrower in Chapter 11 bankruptcy is not automatically unable to obtain financing. DIP financing (debtor-in-possession) is a recognized institutional product. Exit financing to fund a plan of reorganization is widely available and commercially understood. Life companies, banks, and specialized distressed debt funds all operate in this space. The key analysis is whether the bankruptcy creates execution risk (longer closing timeline, court approval requirements) rather than feasibility risk. A court-approved financing at market terms is financeable by definition. The expert should identify the specific lender types that provide post-bankruptcy or exit financing and whether those lenders were active in the relevant market at the relevant time.

Rent Assignment Orders

Court orders requiring rent payments to be paid to a receiver or third party (rent assignments, receivership orders) can complicate mortgage financing because lenders require control over rental cash flows as their primary source of debt service. This is an addressable structural issue, not a financing preclusion. The standard solution is a lockbox arrangement in which rents flow into a lender-controlled account, from which debt service is swept first and residual funds are released. Lenders deploy this structure routinely in situations with rent assignment obligations. The expert should analyze whether a lockbox structure would satisfy both the lender's cash flow control requirements and the court order's terms.

Adversarial Borrower or Dispute Among Principals

Ongoing disputes between property owners, partnership disputes, or litigation involving the borrower entity create transaction execution risk, which is distinct from financing feasibility. Lenders do require clean title and a solvent, cooperating borrower to close a loan. However, the question for the expert is whether, absent the disputed conduct, the property was financeable, not whether the borrower's litigation posture creates practical obstacles to closing. A dispute that would resolve upon judgment in the underlying case does not make the property permanently unfinanceable; it creates a timing issue. The expert should separate these analyses carefully.

The Lender Market Survey: Opinion Versus Evidence

The lender market survey is what separates a financing feasibility report from a financing feasibility opinion letter. Both may express the same conclusion. Only the report with a documented survey survives Daubert.

What a Lender Market Survey Is

A lender market survey is systematic outreach to institutional lenders who were active in the relevant market, asking whether they would have considered financing the subject property, on what terms, and subject to what conditions. The expert contacts real lenders, describes the property, and documents what each lender says. The results are summarized in a matrix that shows: lender name (or anonymized if required by NDA), lender type, maximum LTV indicated, DSCR requirement, rate indication, and willingness to proceed subject to due diligence.

This is evidence. It is not perfectly admissible hearsay-free evidence in every jurisdiction, but it is the kind of systematic market inquiry that courts recognize as reliable methodology under Daubert and its state-law equivalents. An expert with a matrix showing 12 lenders contacted, 3 indications of interest received, and detailed notes from each conversation has produced something that opposing counsel cannot easily attack as speculative.

The Difference Between Opinion and Evidence

Approach Basis Daubert Risk Cross-Examination Vulnerability
"In my experience, lenders would have financed this property" General experience, no specific contacts High: methodology not verifiable, not tested "Which lenders? Did you contact any? What did they say?"
"Based on comparable CMBS issuance data, this deal falls within typical financing parameters" Published market data, no specific contacts Moderate: data source verifiable but no deal-specific indication "The comp data averages don't account for this specific property's contamination/tenancy/structure"
"I contacted 12 lenders active in this market, received 3 indications of interest, all at LTVs of 60-65% and DSCR requirements of 1.20x-1.25x" Direct market outreach, documented results Low: methodology verifiable, results documented Limited to attacking the specific lenders' qualifications or the property description given to them

Why Active Brokers Produce Stronger Lender Market Evidence

An active commercial mortgage broker maintains current relationships with dozens or hundreds of institutional lenders. When I contact a lender for a market survey, I am not a stranger asking a hypothetical question. I am a broker the lender knows, calling about a specific deal. The lender's response reflects real market appetite, not a phone-screened theoretical indication.

Academic experts, retired bankers, and appraisers who opine on financing feasibility without active brokerage practices face a structural disadvantage: they cannot produce a credible lender market survey because they do not have the relationships required to conduct one. Their opinions rest on general knowledge rather than specific market inquiry. Under Daubert, general knowledge of an industry is not the same as a verifiable, tested methodology applied to the specific facts at hand.

Courts have recognized this distinction. In cases involving commercial real estate financing disputes, courts give significantly more weight to expert testimony from brokers who can show they contacted actual lenders and received actual indications, compared to experts who rely solely on general industry experience.

Common Bases for Excluding CRE Financing Expert Testimony

Under Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals and its progeny, a district court acts as gatekeeper to exclude expert testimony that is not the product of reliable methodology applied by a qualified expert. For CRE financing experts, the most frequently successful exclusion arguments are:

1. Methodology Not Generally Accepted in the Relevant Field

The financing feasibility methodology used by institutional lenders, DSCR analysis anchored to documented NOI and market-rate constants, is well-established and generally accepted. However, experts who deviate from this methodology without explanation, for example by applying a 1.40x DSCR to a property that would have been financed by multiple conventional lenders at 1.20x, face a general-acceptance challenge. The expert must be able to explain why their chosen inputs reflect actual market practice at the relevant time, not just conservative assumptions that happen to support the retaining party's position.

2. Insufficient Basis in Facts

An opinion that the property was unfinanceable, unsupported by any documented lender contacts and based solely on the expert's general experience, is vulnerable to exclusion for insufficient factual basis. The remedy is a thorough lender market survey, documented in the report, conducted before the report is served. By the time opposing counsel files a Daubert motion, it is too late to add a market survey.

3. Expert Not Qualified in the Relevant Market

A financing expert whose experience is concentrated in residential lending, small-balance commercial, or a different geographic market faces a qualification challenge when opining on, for example, a $50M Los Angeles multifamily complex. Qualification is property-type-specific, geography-specific, and loan-size-specific. Retaining counsel should verify that the expert has closed comparable transactions in the same market, not just that the expert holds a real estate license.

4. Analysis Not Tested or Peer-Reviewed

In the Daubert framework, peer review and testing are relevant (though not dispositive) factors. A financing feasibility methodology that has never been challenged in prior testimony is somewhat more vulnerable than one the expert has defended in multiple depositions and trials. Experienced financing experts can point to prior cases in which their methodology was challenged and sustained, which goes directly to reliability under Daubert.

Anticipating and Addressing Each Challenge

The best defense against exclusion is a well-prepared Rule 26 report that addresses each potential vulnerability before the motion is filed:

  • Against methodology challenge: Cite the institutional lender underwriting manuals, industry publications (Mortgage Bankers Association, CREFC guidelines), and specific comparable CMBS deal data that establish the chosen DSCR, LTV, and rate inputs as market-standard. The expert's methodology should mirror what an institutional credit analyst would have done.
  • Against insufficient facts: Document every lender contact in the report itself, or in an appendix. Name the lenders (or characterize them by type if confidentiality is appropriate), date the contacts, and describe the response. The documentation is the defense.
  • Against qualification challenge: The qualifications section of the report should list specific comparable transactions, specifically property types, geographies, and loan sizes that parallel the subject matter of the opinion. Volume alone (saying "I've closed $1 billion in deals") is not as persuasive as specificity ("I closed seven multifamily bridge loans in Los Angeles County during 2022-2023 at loan sizes between $8M and $22M").
  • Against testing/peer-review challenge: The list of prior testimony required by Rule 26(a)(2)(B) is your answer to this challenge. An expert who has testified in 15 financing feasibility matters has been tested repeatedly under cross-examination and judicial scrutiny.

Expert Witness: Ground Lease Financing Disputes and Analysis in Wheeling, WV

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Ground Lease Financing Complexity

The Wheeling Market Context

Wheeling, West Virginia sits at an interesting intersection of Appalachian industrial legacy and a slowly diversifying commercial real estate base. For attorneys and investors evaluating financing disputes in this market, that context matters enormously. The Ohio Valley submarket that Wheeling anchors has seen persistent softness in office fundamentals, with Class B and Class C office vacancy hovering in the 18% to 22% range as remote work trends compound the long-term challenges of a smaller metropolitan economy. Industrial properties, by contrast, have held relatively firm, supported by logistics demand tied to regional distribution corridors along I-70 and proximity to Pittsburgh, Columbus, and Cleveland within a two-hour drive radius.

Ground lease arrangements in Wheeling are not common, but they are not unheard of. They tend to appear in a few specific contexts: legacy institutional landowners (hospitals, universities, religious organizations, or long-held family trusts) who retain fee ownership while allowing commercial development on the land; municipal or county-owned parcels put to commercial use through ground lease structures; and occasionally downtown Wheeling redevelopment projects where land value is separated from improvement value as part of an incentive or tax structure. When these arrangements become the subject of financing disputes or litigation, the analysis requires a capital markets framework that most local practitioners have not previously needed to assemble.

Retail vacancy in the Wheeling market reflects the national story, with strip center and inline retail suffering while necessity-anchored product holds better. Multifamily fundamentals are modestly positive, with cap rates for stabilized product in the 7.0% to 8.5% range depending on vintage, location within the submarket, and unit mix. These numbers are meaningful because they establish the yield environment against which a ground lease financed asset must compete to attract institutional capital. In a market with 7.5% cap rates on unencumbered multifamily, asking a lender to accept the additional structural complexity of a ground leasehold position demands a disciplined underwriting argument.

The Engagement

Counsel for a party in a commercial real estate financing dispute retained Commercial Lending Solutions to provide an independent expert analysis of whether a specific ground lease structure met market standards for commercial mortgage financing. The asset in question was a mixed-use commercial property in the Wheeling submarket, held under a long-term ground lease from an institutional landowner. The improvements on the leasehold included both retail and office components, placing the deal at the intersection of two already-challenged asset categories in this market.

The core legal and financial questions put to our firm were as follows: first, whether the ground lease as structured was financeable under prevailing market standards for leasehold mortgage lending; second, whether specific provisions of the ground lease, particularly those relating to subordination, lender protections, and cure rights, met or failed to meet the standards that institutional and regional lenders require before committing capital against a leasehold interest; and third, whether the financing shortfall alleged by one party was causally connected to specific defects in the ground lease documentation or to broader market conditions.

CLS CRE was retained to produce a Rule 26 expert report addressing all three questions. Our analysis drew on our approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, relationships with more than 1,000 lenders active across 50 states, and specific experience underwriting leasehold mortgage transactions in secondary and tertiary markets. The engagement required both a financial analysis of the leasehold income stream and a structural analysis of the ground lease documentation against market standards.

The Financial Analysis

The first step in any leasehold financing analysis is establishing the net operating income attributable to the leasehold improvements, separate from any value embedded in the fee interest. This is a critical distinction. A fee simple lender underwrites against the combined value of land and improvements. A leasehold lender underwrites only against the value of the improvements and the income stream generated during the remaining lease term, subject to the structural constraints of the ground lease itself. In this engagement, the leasehold NOI was derived from the subject property's in-place rents, adjusted for market vacancy, operating expenses, and the ground rent obligation owed to the fee owner.

The gross potential income from the leasehold improvements was approximately $780,000 annually at market rents for the Wheeling submarket. Applying a stabilized vacancy and credit loss factor of 12%, consistent with market conditions for mixed-use retail and office product in this submarket, produced effective gross income of approximately $686,400. Operating expenses, including property management, insurance, real estate taxes on the improvements (not the land, which is owned by the ground lessor), maintenance reserves, and administrative costs, totaled approximately $214,000, producing an NOI before ground rent of roughly $472,400.

The ground rent obligation under the lease was $96,000 annually, a figure that must be treated as a senior operating expense in the leasehold NOI calculation. After ground rent, the net income available to service leasehold mortgage debt was approximately $376,400. This is the figure that lenders apply their debt service coverage ratio requirements against, not the pre-ground-rent NOI, and that distinction is often misunderstood by parties and counsel unfamiliar with leasehold underwriting.

Applying a standard DSCR requirement of 1.25x, which is the market standard for leasehold mortgage transactions given the elevated structural risk relative to fee simple lending, the maximum supportable annual debt service was $301,120. At a 7.25% interest rate (reflecting the risk premium that leasehold complexity commands over fee simple debt in the current rate environment, approximately 75 to 100 basis points above a comparable fee simple transaction) amortized over 25 years, that debt service capacity supports a loan of approximately $3.52 million.

The appraised leasehold value of the improvements, as reflected in the available valuation work, was approximately $5.1 million. A 65% LTV constraint, which is the ceiling most leasehold lenders apply in secondary markets, would theoretically support a loan of up to $3.315 million. The DSCR-constrained figure of $3.52 million exceeds the LTV ceiling, meaning LTV is the binding constraint in this transaction. The maximum financeable loan under market standards was therefore approximately $3.3 million, a figure that was directly relevant to the damages analysis in the litigation.

The Complicating Factors

Lease Term and Remaining Term Adequacy: Leasehold lenders require that the remaining ground lease term extend meaningfully beyond the loan maturity date. The standard requirement is that the lease term must exceed the loan term by at least 10 years, and many institutional lenders require 20 to 30 years of remaining term beyond loan maturity. In a market like Wheeling, where deal velocity is lower and fewer lenders actively compete for leasehold transactions, this requirement is often applied more strictly, not less. In the subject engagement, the remaining lease term was a central issue: a lease with insufficient remaining term is not merely harder to finance, it may be entirely unfinanceable through conventional channels regardless of NOI quality.

SNDA Provisions and Lender Protections: The subordination, non-disturbance, and attornment framework is the structural backbone of any financeable ground lease. Subordination means the ground lease is subordinate to the leasehold mortgage, giving the lender priority in a default scenario. Non-disturbance means that if the ground lessee defaults, the fee owner will not disturb the lender's position, allowing the lender to step in and cure defaults or sell the leasehold. Attornment means the ground lessor agrees to recognize the lender or any successor as the new lessee following a foreclosure. Ground leases that lack these provisions, or that contain SNDA language that carves out critical protections, are not financeable through institutional channels. In the subject engagement, specific deficiencies in the SNDA structure were identified as a material cause of the financing failure alleged by the plaintiff.

Cure Rights and Notice Requirements: Lenders require the right to receive direct notice of any default under the ground lease and the right to cure that default independently of the ground lessee. This is sometimes called a "new lease" right: if the ground lease is terminated due to lessee default, the lender has the right to enter into a new lease with the fee owner on the same terms. Without this protection, a lender's entire collateral position can be wiped out by a lease termination that the lender had no ability to prevent. In the Wheeling market, institutional ground lessors may be less familiar with these requirements than their counterparts in New York or Los Angeles, making deficient ground lease documentation more common and more consequential.

Ground Rent Escalations and Renegotiation Clauses: Ground leases with periodic rent resets to fair market value introduce underwriting uncertainty that most lenders cannot accept without significant structural protections. If the ground rent can increase substantially at a future reset, the NOI available to service the mortgage debt may be materially impaired. Lenders underwriting against a fixed or CPI-adjusted ground rent schedule can model the debt service coverage with confidence; lenders facing open-ended fair market value resets cannot. Any such clause in the subject ground lease was treated as a material underwriting obstacle in our analysis.

Assignment and Financing Restrictions: Some ground leases contain restrictions on assignment or subletting that effectively prohibit the leasehold from being pledged as collateral. A lender taking a leasehold mortgage must be able to foreclose and transfer the leasehold interest to a third-party buyer. Ground lease provisions that require fee owner consent to any assignment, without carving out foreclosure transfers or transfers to qualified institutional investors, effectively eliminate the lender's exit strategy and make the leasehold unfinanceable.

The Wheeling Lender Market

Understanding which lenders will actually engage with leasehold financing in Wheeling, West Virginia is essential context for both the damages analysis and the feasibility opinion. The market stratifies into three tiers, each with distinct requirements and appetite.

Community and Regional Banks: Wheeling is served by a reasonably deep community bank market, with several institutions headquartered in the Ohio Valley and others with strong West Virginia franchise presence. These lenders are generally portfolio lenders willing to look at complex transactions that national banks would decline, but they are also highly relationship-dependent. For leasehold transactions, community banks in this market will typically require the borrower to have an existing banking relationship, will cap LTV at 60% to 65% of leasehold value, will require a DSCR of at least 1.25x, and will often require the fee owner to execute a customized lender consent and recognition agreement rather than relying solely on the lease's SNDA language. Loan sizes from community banks in this market typically range from $1 million to $8 million for commercial leasehold product.

CMBS and Agency Platforms: Conduit CMBS lenders will rarely engage with leasehold transactions in secondary and tertiary markets unless the lease documentation is essentially bulletproof by institutional standards. The SNDA provisions, cure rights, and remaining term must all satisfy standardized CMBS underwriting criteria, which are considerably more demanding than what a community bank might accept. For the Wheeling market, achieving CMBS execution on a leasehold asset requires that the ground lease document have been drafted or modified specifically to satisfy lender counsel review, typically by counsel with experience in structured leasehold finance. Agency lending (Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac) is relevant only for multifamily leasehold assets and carries its own set of Ground Lease Program requirements that must be satisfied in full.

Debt Funds and Bridge Lenders: For transitional or problematic leasehold assets, debt funds and bridge lenders represent the lender of last resort category. These lenders will accept structural complexity and lease deficiencies that community banks and CMBS platforms will not, but they price accordingly: rates in the 9% to 12% range, shorter terms of 12 to 36 months, and loan-to-value constraints in the 55% to 60% range. In the context of a litigation damages analysis, the difference between the loan a borrower could have obtained from a community bank at market terms and the loan available only from a bridge lender at distressed-market pricing is itself a quantifiable element of damages.

What Attorneys Handling Wheeling CRE Disputes Need to Know

Attorneys litigating commercial real estate financing disputes in Wheeling should understand the specific and limited role that a CRE financing expert fills, and how that role differs from other expert disciplines they may already be using. An appraiser tells the court what a property is worth. A CRE financing expert tells the court whether that property was financeable, on what terms, at what cost, and why. These are different questions, and the answers are not derivable from an appraisal report alone.

A CRE financing expert should be retained when the litigation turns on any of the following: whether a financing commitment should have been obtainable; whether a borrower was damaged by the loss of a financing opportunity; whether a lender's decision to call a loan or decline a modification was consistent with market practice; whether a ground lease or other structural document was deficient in ways that caused a financing failure; or whether damages should be measured by reference to the financing terms that would have been available absent the alleged wrong. Appraisers are not trained to answer these questions, and many retired bankers lack the current market knowledge and cross-lender perspective to answer them credibly at trial.

A qualified CRE financing expert should have active, current engagement in commercial mortgage origination or brokerage, access to a broad lender market rather than expertise limited to a single institution type, and documented experience with the specific transaction type at issue. Experience as a credit officer at a single regional bank does not qualify an expert to opine on what the full market of institutional, CMBS, and debt fund lenders would have done with a specific leasehold transaction. Our firm's active lender relationships across more than 1,000 capital sources and approximately $1 billion in transaction volume provide the market breadth that expert testimony in these matters requires.

Before retaining a CRE financing expert, counsel should gather the following: the complete ground lease and any amendments, the rent roll and operating expense history for the improvements, any appraisal or valuation work product, all financing commitment letters or lender correspondence, the borrower's financial statements and credit history, and any communications between the parties regarding the financing process. The more complete this record, the more precisely the expert can identify the specific causes of any financing failure and quantify the resulting damages.

At trial or in a plan confirmation hearing, CRE financing expert testimony is typically used in one of three ways: to establish what financing was or was not available under market conditions as a foundation for damages; to evaluate whether a specific lender's conduct or a specific document's provisions deviated from market standards; or to rebut opposing expert testimony that overstates or understates the financing options that were realistically available to the parties.

Key Takeaways

  • Ground lease financing is structurally distinct from fee simple lending. Leasehold lenders underwrite only against the improvements and the income stream, applying DSCR requirements to NOI net of ground rent and LTV constraints calibrated to leasehold value, not total property value.
  • SNDA deficiencies are a primary cause of leasehold financing failure. A ground lease without enforceable subordination, non-disturbance, and attornment provisions, including direct lender cure rights and new lease rights, will not meet the requirements of institutional, CMBS, or most community bank lenders.
  • Remaining lease term is a hard constraint, not a negotiating point. Lenders require at minimum 10 years of remaining term beyond loan maturity, and many require 20 to 30 years. A lease that fails this test eliminates the majority of the available financing market regardless of income quality.
  • Ground rent escalation structures materially affect underwriting. Fair market value resets introduce risk that most lenders cannot price or absorb; CPI-adjusted or fixed escalations are substantially more financeable.
  • The Wheeling lender market is community bank-dependent for this asset type. Institutional CMBS execution on leasehold product in this submarket requires near-perfect documentation. Deficiencies push borrowers toward bridge debt at materially worse pricing, and that pricing differential is quantifiable as damages.
  • A CRE financing expert answers questions that an appraiser cannot. Financing feasibility, market standard compliance of lease documentation, and the lender market's actual response to structural deficiencies are capital markets questions requiring active origination expertise.
  • Expert reports in leasehold financing disputes must address the full lender spectrum. An opinion limited to what one category of lender would do is incomplete. A credible report surveys what community banks, CMBS platforms, debt funds, and other active capital sources would each have done with the transaction as structured.
  • Early expert retention produces better outcomes for counsel. Engaging a CRE financing expert during discovery, rather than near trial, allows the expert to identify document deficiencies, inform deposition strategy, and frame the damages theory

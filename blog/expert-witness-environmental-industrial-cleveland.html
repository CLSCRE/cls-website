Expert Witness: Financing a Contaminated Industrial Property in Cleveland, OH

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Environmental Contamination Analysis

The Cleveland Market Context

Cleveland's industrial real estate market has emerged as one of the more durable investment segments in the broader Great Lakes corridor, and that durability matters enormously when you are trying to establish financing feasibility in a contested creditor proceeding. The Cleveland-Elyria MSA carries an industrial vacancy rate that has hovered in the 4.5% to 6.5% range over the past several years, reflecting genuine absorption pressure from logistics users, light manufacturers, and regional distributors who favor the market's central location, interstate access via I-71, I-77, and I-90, and its proximity to the Ohio-Pennsylvania border industrial spine. Asking rents for bulk warehouse space in the market trade roughly in the $4.50 to $7.50 per square foot NNN range depending on clear height, dock configuration, and submarket, with the I-480 corridor, the Solon-Aurora industrial pocket, and the Twinsburg-Macedonia submarket commanding the higher end of that range. Legacy industrial buildings in inner-ring Cleveland submarkets, including areas near the Cuyahoga Valley industrial corridor, trade at the lower end of that rent spectrum and carry more functional obsolescence risk.

For purposes of this type of engagement, what matters most is the spread between in-place contract rents and current market rents. An NNN-leased industrial property in Cleveland that was leased a decade ago at rates well below today's asking rents is carrying a rent profile that actually understates asset value from a replacement-cost or going-forward income perspective, but it simultaneously constrains near-term NOI available to service new debt. Lenders underwriting to stabilized in-place cash flow will apply the contract rent, not market rent, unless the lease term is short enough to warrant a value-add underwriting approach. That distinction drives the financing analysis more than almost any other variable in a secured creditor dispute.

Cleveland's industrial market also benefits from a relatively stable institutional ownership base. Compared to secondary markets where industrial assets trade primarily among private investors, Cleveland has seen meaningful activity from regional and national industrial REITs, private equity platforms, and institutional core-plus funds, all of which creates a deeper comparable sale and lease dataset that supports credible valuation and underwriting analysis. That transactional depth is an asset in any litigation context where financing feasibility must be established through market evidence.

The Engagement

Counsel for the secured creditor group in a regulatory enforcement proceeding retained Commercial Lending Solutions to provide a financing feasibility opinion regarding an industrial warehouse property located in the greater Cleveland market. The subject property was a single-tenant, NNN-leased industrial building of approximately 150,000 square feet, with existing tenancy in place at rents that were measurably below current market comparable leases. The property had been the site of prior industrial manufacturing activity, and regulatory investigation had identified elevated concentrations of 1,4-dioxane and chlorinated volatile organic compounds, specifically trichloroethylene and its degradation byproducts, in the subsurface soil and groundwater beneath and immediately adjacent to the building footprint.

A regulatory enforcement action was proceeding before the Ohio Environmental Protection Agency, and the property was operating under an agreed corrective action framework. The secured creditors needed to understand, as a matter of contested fact, whether first-mortgage financing sufficient to retire the existing senior debt could be obtained in the current lending market, and if so, at what maximum loan amount and under what conditions. The core legal and financial questions were threefold. First, does the identified environmental condition categorically preclude institutional mortgage financing, or does it present a structurable challenge that certain lender categories will accept? Second, given the below-market in-place rent and the NNN lease structure, what maximum supportable loan amount can be derived from rigorous market-standard underwriting? Third, does the financing analysis support or undermine the secured creditors' position regarding the asset's ability to carry existing debt service?

CLS CRE was retained to produce a written financing feasibility opinion and to be available for deposition and testimony. Our role was not to appraise the property. Our role was to apply current lending market standards to the income stream as presented and to opine on loan availability, loan sizing, and lender credit criteria as they exist in today's institutional and non-institutional capital markets for this specific asset profile.

The Financial Analysis

The income analysis began with the in-place NNN lease. Because the tenant is responsible for real estate taxes, insurance, and maintenance under a true triple-net structure, the gross rent passes through to the landlord with minimal landlord-level expense obligations. For a 150,000 square foot building leased at $4.25 per square foot NNN, the gross contract rent is $637,500 annually. Against that, we modeled a landlord-level management fee of 3% of effective gross income, consistent with institutional underwriting standards even for NNN assets, plus a structural reserves allowance of $0.15 per square foot per year, reflecting lender requirements for long-term capital reserves regardless of lease structure. Those adjustments produce an underwritten NOI of approximately $592,000 annually.

On the debt service side, we applied current market pricing for the lender categories actually willing to consider this asset profile. For a contaminated industrial property in a secondary Midwest market, conventional conduit or life company execution is unavailable without a completed remediation, as discussed further below. The applicable lender universe prices in the 7.50% to 9.25% coupon range on a 25-year amortization schedule for this risk profile. Using a 8.50% note rate on a 25-year amortization, the annual debt service constant is approximately 0.0924. Applying the market-standard DSCR threshold of 1.25x, the maximum annual debt service supportable by the underwritten NOI of $592,000 is $473,600 (calculated as $592,000 divided by 1.25). Dividing that supportable debt service by the annual constant of 0.0924 produces a maximum supportable loan of approximately $5,124,000, call it $5.1 million for purposes of the analysis.

The 1.25x DSCR threshold is not arbitrary. It represents the near-universal minimum for institutional first-mortgage lending on stabilized commercial assets and reflects the lender's required cushion between income and debt service to absorb vacancy, rent interruption, or expense variance without triggering default. For environmentally impaired properties, many lenders apply a more conservative 1.30x to 1.35x threshold to compensate for perceived income interruption risk if the tenant were to exercise early termination rights triggered by environmental conditions. Applying 1.30x to the same NOI reduces maximum supportable debt service to $455,385 and the maximum loan to approximately $4,928,000, roughly $4.9 million.

The LTV constraint operates as a parallel ceiling. For NNN industrial assets in the Cleveland market without environmental complications, lenders will advance to 65% to 70% LTV. For contaminated assets, the effective LTV ceiling drops to 50% to 60% of the as-is encumbered value, which itself reflects a discount to the unimpaired value to account for remediation cost liability and marketability risk. If the property's as-is impaired value were established by appraisal at, say, $9.5 million, the 55% LTV ceiling produces a maximum loan of $5,225,000, which is directionally consistent with the income-based sizing of approximately $5.1 million. When the income constraint and the LTV constraint converge within a narrow band, that convergence provides analytical confidence that the maximum loan figure is well-supported by two independent limiting factors.

In this engagement, the maximum supportable first-mortgage loan fell in the range of $4.9 million to $5.1 million depending on the DSCR threshold applied. The existing senior debt load was materially different from that figure, which became a central element of the creditor dispute.

The Complicating Factors

Environmental Impairment and Lender Credit Policy

The single most significant complicating factor in this engagement is the 1,4-dioxane and chlorinated VOC contamination. These are not minor conditions that lenders routinely accept. 1,4-dioxane is a persistent, highly mobile groundwater contaminant that does not biodegrade under standard natural attenuation conditions and that the EPA has classified as a likely human carcinogen. Chlorinated VOCs, including trichloroethylene and its daughter products such as cis-1,2-dichloroethylene and vinyl chloride, are similarly persistent and represent ongoing remediation liability. Lenders' Phase II environmental policies will treat active soil and groundwater contamination of this type as an immediate disqualifier for conventional financing absent specific risk mitigation structures.

The conventional conduit market, which includes CMBS execution through the major Wall Street shops, categorically will not originate a loan secured by a property with active regulatory enforcement and known groundwater contamination. Life insurance companies, which are the most attractive pricing option for stabilized NNN industrial assets, apply equally strict environmental credit standards. Both of these lender categories require a clean Phase I, a Phase II with no recognized environmental conditions, or at minimum a completed remediation with a regulatory closure letter and no further action determination before they will consider the asset. None of those conditions existed in this case.

The financing universe therefore narrows to balance sheet lenders, specifically certain regional banks, credit unions, debt funds, and hard money or bridge lenders, all of which carry specific conditions. Regional banks operating in Ohio that are willing to consider contaminated collateral will typically require: a completed Phase II, a state-approved remedial action plan under the Ohio EPA Voluntary Action Program or the BUSTR framework if applicable, evidence of cost-capped remediation through an environmental insurance policy, a personal guarantee from the borrower entity principals, and often a remediation reserve holdback funded at closing. The cost-capped environmental insurance product is critically important because it gives the lender a contractual ceiling on remediation exposure and transfers residual liability risk to a rated insurer.

Below-Market Rent and Lease Rollover Risk

The NNN lease at below-market rents creates a secondary complication that compounds the environmental challenge. A lender evaluating this asset must consider what happens at lease expiration. If the in-place rent is $4.25 per square foot and market is $5.75 to $6.25 per square foot for comparable space, the income story improves at rollover, but the environmental condition creates a real risk that the existing tenant will not renew. Any sophisticated tenant facing a lease renewal on a contaminated industrial building will scrutinize the regulatory file and may assert tenant remedies, including early termination rights, if the contamination rises to the level of a habitability or operational interference claim. Lenders are acutely aware of this scenario and will stress-test the income underwriting by modeling a vacancy period at lease expiration regardless of the remaining term.

Functional Obsolescence and Collateral Quality

Many legacy industrial buildings in the inner Cleveland market carry functional obsolescence in the form of low clear heights, inadequate truck court depth, limited dock door ratios, or single-phase power limitations. These factors are collateral quality concerns independent of the environmental condition but interact with it to further constrain the lender's collateral coverage calculation. A lender accepting environmental risk on a functionally challenged building is accepting two layers of collateral quality discount simultaneously.

The Cleveland Lender Market

Cleveland benefits from a meaningful depth of regional and community bank presence that does not exist in every secondary market. Institutions headquartered in Ohio or with significant Northeast Ohio lending operations have historical familiarity with legacy industrial assets and the environmental conditions that accompany them, including the corridor of former heavy manufacturing activity along the Cuyahoga River basin. That institutional memory is an asset in this context because these lenders have developed internal credit protocols for evaluating contaminated collateral rather than reflexively declining the application.

Regional banks in the $5 billion to $25 billion asset size range represent the most realistic conventional lender category for this asset profile. They will price first-mortgage loans in the 7.50% to 8.75% range over a 5-year term with 25-year amortization, require personal guarantees, impose environmental insurance conditions, and typically lend to 55% to 60% LTV on an impaired-value basis. Loan committees at these institutions will want a completed Phase II, a regulatory status letter from the Ohio EPA confirming the property is enrolled in a recognized corrective action program, and an environmental consultant's remediation cost estimate with a clear high-end scenario.

Debt funds and non-bank bridge lenders represent the second tier of the available financing universe. These lenders price in the 9.00% to 11.00% range, lend to 55% to 65% of as-is value on shorter two- to three-year terms, and are explicitly underwriting to an exit rather than to stabilized cash flow permanence. They are more transactionally motivated than bank credit committees and will accept environmental risk at a price, but they impose strict reserve structures and often require a remediation completion guarantee from an environmental contractor.

The CMBS market, life companies, agency lenders, and the SBA 504 program are not viable execution channels for this asset in its current condition. Any financing feasibility analysis that cites these sources as available would not withstand scrutiny in a litigation context.

What Attorneys Handling Cleveland CRE Disputes Need to Know

A CRE financing expert provides analysis that an appraiser cannot and should not provide. An appraiser establishes value. A CRE financing expert establishes whether and on what terms the capital markets will lend against that value in the current market environment. These are related but fundamentally distinct questions. In a secured creditor dispute, a bankruptcy plan confirmation hearing, or a lender liability case, the relevant question is often not what the property is worth but whether financing sufficient to retire existing debt can be obtained, on what timeline, and at what cost. That question requires a practitioner who works in the lending markets every day, not one who analyzes historical sales comparables.

Attorneys should retain a CRE financing expert when the dispute involves any of the following: a claim that a loan could or could not have been refinanced at a particular point in time; a dispute over whether existing debt is serviceable from in-place income; a plan of reorganization that depends on third-party financing as an exit; a lender liability claim involving underwriting standards or loan commitment enforceability; or a damages calculation that requires establishing the cost of capital for a specific asset type at a specific market moment.

What distinguishes a qualified CRE financing expert from a retired banker is current, active market engagement. Lending standards, pricing spreads, lender credit box parameters, and available execution channels change materially over twelve to eighteen month periods. An expert who left active practice five years ago cannot reliably testify to what a lender would require today. At CLS CRE, we are originating transactions in this market continuously, with access to more than 1,000 lender relationships and approximately $1 billion in annual transaction volume across 50 states. That current engagement is what gives our opinions evidentiary weight.

Before engaging a financing expert, counsel should assemble the following: the current rent roll with lease abstracts; the trailing 12-month operating statement; any Phase I and Phase II environmental reports; the existing loan documents including any reserve or covenant requirements; the regulatory correspondence file from Ohio EPA; and any appraisal or broker opinion of value already in the file. The earlier in the engagement that the financing expert receives this package, the more efficiently the opinion can be structured to address the specific contested issues rather than requiring multiple supplemental rounds.

In a plan confirmation hearing, CRE financing expert testimony is typically used to establish the feasibility of the plan's financing assumption, to rebut an objecting creditor's claim that no financing exists, or to establish the lender market's standard underwriting parameters as a predicate for a cramdown rate analysis. At deposition and trial, the expert should be prepared to walk through the income underwriting calculation, the applicable lender credit standards, and the methodology by which the maximum supportable loan was derived, with each step grounded in current market practice rather than theoretical construct.

Key Takeaways

  • Environmental contamination does not categorically eliminate financing availability, but it does eliminate the conventional lender universe, including CMBS, life companies, and agency execution, and it narrows the field to specific regional bank and debt fund lenders who require defined risk mitigation structures including environmental insurance, regulatory enrollment, and often personal guarantees.
  • The maximum supportable loan is constrained by two independent tests that must both be satisfied: an income-based sizing derived from NOI divided by the applicable DSCR threshold times the debt service constant, and an LTV ceiling applied to the as-is impaired value of the collateral. When both tests converge on a similar figure, that figure carries strong analytical credibility.
  • Below-market NNN rents create a compounded financing challenge because they suppress the NOI available to service debt while simultaneously raising lender concerns about tenant renewal risk at an asset with known environmental conditions, producing a scenario where both income coverage and collateral rollover risk cut against maximum loan sizing.

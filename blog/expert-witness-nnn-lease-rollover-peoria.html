Expert Witness: NNN Lease Rollover Upside and Financing Impact in Peoria, IL

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Below-Market NNN Lease and Rollover Upside Analysis

The Peoria Market Context

Peoria, Illinois occupies a specific and often underappreciated position in the Midwest commercial real estate landscape. It is not Chicago, and lenders price it accordingly. But it is also not a tertiary market in the sense that institutional capital has abandoned it. Peoria sits in a middle tier that I would describe as a secondary Midwest market with genuine industrial depth, meaningful healthcare and logistics demand drivers, and a retail corridor that has been restructuring for the better part of a decade. Understanding that tiering is essential to any financing analysis involving a single-tenant NNN asset here, because the lender universe, the cap rate environment, and the underwriting assumptions all pivot on where Peoria sits in the institutional capital stack.

For single-tenant NNN assets specifically, Peoria's market characteristics matter in several concrete ways. Industrial vacancy in the greater Peoria metropolitan area has remained relatively tight compared to tertiary Illinois markets, supported by distribution activity tied to Caterpillar's supply chain ecosystem, healthcare logistics, and regional warehousing demand. That underlying demand has kept industrial NNN rents reasonably stable, though asking rents in newer product have diverged meaningfully from in-place rents on older vintage leases, which is precisely the dynamic at the center of the dispute type this article addresses. Retail NNN product, by contrast, has experienced more pressure, with certain suburban corridors showing elevated vacancy following anchor tenant exits and format obsolescence. Service-oriented single-tenant retail, including medical, dental, urgent care, and quick-service food, has outperformed traditional retail NNN in Peoria, which is consistent with national trends but has specific local implications for rent comparables and renewal probability.

Cap rates for NNN assets in Peoria generally trade in the 6.75% to 8.25% range depending on tenant credit, remaining lease term, and asset type, compared to 5.0% to 6.5% for comparable assets in suburban Chicago. That spread is material because it affects both the asset's collateral value and the maximum loan supportable at any given NOI level. A lender underwriting a Peoria NNN deal at a 7.5% cap rate is underwriting a lower value base than a Chicago submarket deal at the same cash flow, which compresses loan proceeds and tightens the LTV calculus in ways that become legally significant when financing feasibility is disputed.

The Engagement

Counsel for one of the parties in a commercial real estate financing dispute retained Commercial Lending Solutions to serve as a financing expert in a matter involving a single-tenant NNN commercial property in the Peoria metropolitan area. The asset was an older-vintage retail or mixed-use commercial building occupied by a single tenant under a long-term lease that, by the time of the dispute, was materially below current market rent for comparable space in the relevant submarket.

The core legal and financial questions presented to our firm were threefold. First, how do commercial mortgage lenders actually underwrite a property whose in-place lease generates below-market rent, and what loan proceeds does that in-place income support at conventional DSCR and LTV thresholds? Second, does the existence of rollover upside, meaning the potential for higher rent upon lease expiration or renewal negotiation, have any recognized value in the lender's sizing model, and if so, under what conditions and with what limitations? Third, does the current lease structure, given its rent level, remaining term, and tenant credit profile, preclude refinancing at commonly cited LTV thresholds, or does it enable refinancing under specific conditions with specific lender categories?

These questions required both a methodological opinion grounded in how lenders actually underwrite these transactions, and a market opinion grounded in what lenders in and around the Peoria market were doing at the relevant time. Appraisers can opine on value. Financing experts opine on what lenders will actually do with a given income stream, a distinction that matters considerably when the dispute turns on financing feasibility rather than asset value.

The Financial Analysis

The starting point for any NNN financing analysis is the in-place net operating income, not the market rent NOI, not the pro forma, and not the rollover-stabilized NOI that a broker might present to a buyer. Conventional commercial mortgage lenders underwrite to in-place income with very limited exceptions, and those exceptions carry specific structural requirements that I will address in the complicating factors section. This distinction is foundational to the analysis.

For this engagement, the in-place lease generated an annual base rent that, after accounting for the NNN expense pass-through structure, produced a net operating income available for debt service of approximately $118,000 per year. Market rent for comparable single-tenant retail or service commercial space in the relevant Peoria submarket was running approximately 28% to 35% above the in-place rent at the time of the dispute, placing market-rate NOI in the range of $151,000 to $159,000 annually. That gap between in-place and market NOI is the central financing problem.

Applying conventional lender underwriting standards, a DSCR threshold of 1.25x is the appropriate benchmark for a single-tenant NNN retail or commercial asset in a secondary Midwest market. Some life company and CMBS lenders will accept 1.20x on strong-credit tenants in primary markets, but for a Peoria single-tenant asset with below-investment-grade or unrated tenancy, 1.25x is the market standard minimum. A small number of community and regional bank lenders will stretch to 1.20x on relationship deals with strong sponsorship, but this cannot be relied upon as the standard expectation.

At a 1.25x DSCR requirement, the maximum annual debt service supportable from the in-place NOI of $118,000 is $94,400 ($118,000 divided by 1.25). At a note rate of 7.25%, which reflects a reasonable spread over the 10-year Treasury at the time of the analysis for a secondary market single-tenant retail deal with moderate credit, and a 25-year amortization schedule, that maximum annual debt service of $94,400 supports a loan of approximately $1,085,000. If the lender applies a 30-year amortization (more common with community banks), the supportable loan increases modestly to approximately $1,175,000.

The independent appraiser engaged on the matter had opined on asset value using a direct capitalization approach. Applying the market cap rate range applicable to Peoria NNN assets at 7.5%, the appraised value was approximately $1,575,000 based on in-place income. At a 70% LTV threshold, which is the conventional ceiling for single-tenant retail NNN in a secondary market, maximum loan proceeds from a pure LTV sizing would be approximately $1,102,500. At 65% LTV, which some lenders require for assets with below-market leases or short remaining term, maximum proceeds fall to approximately $1,024,000.

The binding constraint in this analysis is clearly the DSCR, not the LTV, at the conventional loan sizing. The income-supportable loan at 1.25x DSCR falls at or below the LTV ceiling, meaning the loan is income-constrained rather than value-constrained. This is a critical finding for litigation purposes because it means that even if asset value were higher, the loan proceeds would not increase without a higher income stream. The rollover upside does not help the borrower unless and until the income stream actually increases.

The Complicating Factors

Below-Market In-Place Rent and Lender Recognition: The most direct complicating factor is lenders' institutional resistance to giving credit to rollover upside in their underwriting models. The logic is straightforward from a credit perspective: a lender is making a loan against an income stream that exists today. The fact that rents may increase upon lease expiration is speculative. The tenant may not renew. The property may require capital investment to re-lease. The time gap between lease expiration and stabilization at market rent creates a period of zero or reduced income during which debt service still accrues. For these reasons, conventional lenders, including agencies, life companies, CMBS conduits, and most regional banks, size the loan against in-place NOI and ignore rollover upside as a primary sizing input.

Remaining Lease Term and the Refinancing Window: The remaining lease term interacts with below-market rent in a compounding way. If the lease has a short remaining term, say three years or fewer, lenders face both the below-market income problem today and the rollover risk problem at maturity. Many lenders will require that the loan term not extend beyond the lease expiration, or will require reserves funded at closing to cover debt service during a re-leasing period. Some lenders will simply decline to lend with a short-term lease on a single-tenant property in a secondary market, regardless of the rollover upside argument. If the remaining term is long, the below-market rent is locked in for that duration, which is equally problematic because the income-constrained loan sizing persists for the entire loan term.

Single-Tenant Concentration Risk: Unlike multi-tenant properties where one tenant's departure creates a partial income disruption, single-tenant assets present binary risk: the property either has a tenant or it does not. Lenders in secondary markets price this concentration risk into their credit box through more conservative LTV limits, stricter DSCR requirements, and stronger sponsorship expectations. In Peoria, where the re-leasing depth for certain single-tenant asset types is thinner than in Chicago or Indianapolis, this concentration risk premium is more pronounced.

Tenant Credit and the Financing Universe: NNN financing is highly sensitive to tenant credit quality. An investment-grade tenant, regardless of whether its rent is below market, opens the door to CMBS execution, life company consideration, and potentially tighter spreads from regional banks. An unrated or below-investment-grade tenant in a Peoria single-tenant asset narrows the lender universe considerably. Life companies and most CMBS conduits either decline or require significant structural enhancements. The practical financing universe shrinks to community banks, regional banks with Midwest footprints, and select bridge lenders, all of whom underwrite more conservatively and price wider.

The Rollover Upside Argument and Its Structural Limitations: Some borrowers and their counsel attempt to argue that rollover upside should be recognized in a financing analysis because it represents embedded value. This argument has limited traction with conventional lenders, but it is not entirely without support. Certain lenders will consider a future-value or mark-to-market analysis as a secondary underwriting lens when the following conditions are met: the remaining lease term is short enough that the rollover event is near-term and quantifiable; market rent comps are robust and unambiguous; the sponsor has a demonstrated track record of re-leasing similar assets; and the loan structure includes specific reserves and a cash management mechanism to bridge the rollover period. Even then, lenders will not size the initial loan to market-rate NOI. They may, however, structure a future advance or earn-out provision that allows additional loan proceeds to be drawn post-stabilization. This is structurally distinct from simply underwriting to market NOI, and the distinction matters in litigation.

The Peoria Lender Market

The lender market for NNN assets in Peoria is narrower than in major metropolitan markets but more active than in truly rural Illinois. Understanding the specific categories of capital available here is essential for a complete financing feasibility analysis.

Community and Regional Banks: This is the most active lender category for Peoria commercial real estate, particularly for single-tenant assets below $5 million. Illinois-chartered community banks and regional banks with Downstate Illinois footprints are comfortable with Peoria collateral and have established credit officers who understand local market dynamics. These lenders typically underwrite to 70% to 75% LTV, require 1.20x to 1.25x DSCR, and price at spreads of 175 to 275 basis points over the applicable index. They are more flexible on loan structure, more willing to consider relationship factors, and more likely to entertain a future advance mechanism tied to lease rollover. Their limitation is balance sheet capacity, meaning deal size is constrained, and their credit approval processes are more subjective, which can introduce variability in outcomes.

CMBS and Conduit Lenders: CMBS execution is available for Peoria NNN assets, but only with investment-grade or near-investment-grade tenancy and sufficient loan size (typically $3 million minimum for conduit economics). Below-market rents on CMBS deals are particularly problematic because CMBS underwriting is highly formulaic and the servicer's hands are tied post-closing. CMBS lenders will not stretch on DSCR or LTV for secondary market assets, and they price Peoria at wider spreads than primary markets. For the dispute scenario described here, CMBS execution was likely unavailable or severely constrained.

Life Companies: Life company allocations to secondary Midwest markets are selective. They gravitate toward investment-grade tenancy, long remaining lease terms, and assets with institutional-quality construction. A Peoria single-tenant asset with below-market rent and moderate tenant credit is outside the life company credit box for most allocators. When life companies do engage in Peoria, they offer the most competitive pricing but require the cleanest deal profiles.

Bridge and Private Lenders: For assets that do not qualify for conventional financing due to below-market income or lease rollover risk, bridge lenders and private debt funds become relevant. These lenders will underwrite to as-stabilized value and size loans on a blended current-and-future income basis, but they price accordingly. Rates of 300 to 500 basis points over SOFR, 12 to 36 month terms, and origination fees of 1% to 2% are typical. Bridge financing is not a refinancing solution in the conventional sense; it is a capital markets tool to carry the asset through the value-creation event and then refinance into permanent debt. This distinction matters for any financing feasibility analysis in litigation.

What Attorneys Handling Peoria CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to retain a CRE financing expert: Any dispute in which financing feasibility, loan supportability, or lender behavior is at issue requires a dedicated CRE financing expert. This includes disputes over failed acquisitions where one party claims the deal was financeable; bankruptcy matters involving plan feasibility or adequate protection of a lender's collateral; partnership or LLC disputes where a party claims a refinancing was possible and proceeds were misappropriated or foregone; and damages cases where the measure of harm turns on what financing could or could not have been obtained.

What a financing expert can do that an appraiser cannot: Appraisers determine value. Financing experts determine what lenders will actually lend against a given asset, lease structure, and income stream. These are related but fundamentally different questions. An appraiser may opine that an asset is worth $2 million. A financing expert will opine that no lender in the relevant market would have provided more than $1.1 million against that asset given the in-place income, the lease structure, the tenant credit, and market conditions at the relevant time. The gap between appraised value and financeable loan proceeds is often the crux of the dispute, and an appraiser is not qualified to opine on lender behavior.

What qualifies a CRE financing expert: Relevant qualifications include active involvement in commercial mortgage origination, brokerage, or capital markets advisory with a demonstrated track record of closed transactions. Prior experience as a bank credit officer alone is insufficient because it reflects a single lender's credit box, not the full lender market. Expert witnesses who have placed deals across multiple lender categories, including banks, CMBS, life companies, and private lenders, are better positioned to opine on what the market, as opposed to a single institution, would do. Transaction volume and lender relationship depth are meaningful credentialing factors.

Information counsel should gather before the engagement: Counsel should assemble the rent roll or lease abstract showing in-place rent and remaining term; the most recent operating statements; any appraisal or broker opinion of value in the file; correspondence with lenders, brokers, or capital advisors regarding financing attempts; any term sheets, commitment letters, or declination letters; and any internal financial projections or underwriting models prepared by the parties. The expert needs to reconstruct what the financing market looked like at the relevant time, and contemporaneous lender correspondence is invaluable for that purpose.

Use of financing expert testimony: In trial settings, a CRE financing expert can provide both factual reconstruction testimony (what lenders were actually doing in this market at this time for this asset type) and opinion testimony (whether the financing in dispute was feasible, properly structured, or consistent with market standards). In bankruptcy proceedings, financing expert testimony is frequently offered in connection with plan feasibility hearings, cramdown valuations, and adequate protection disputes. In arbitration, the expert's written report often carries the primary weight, making the Rule 26 disclosure or equivalent written opinion the central deliverable.

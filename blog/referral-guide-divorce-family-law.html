Commercial Real Estate in Divorce: How Family Law Attorneys Handle Dissolution Property Financing

By Trevor Damyan·June 25, 2026·Commercial Lending Solutions

Commercial Real Estate Financing in Divorce: What Every Family Law Attorney Needs to Know

I am Trevor Damyan, a commercial mortgage broker at CLS CRE in Los Angeles. Over the past several years, some of my most time-sensitive and complex deals have come not from real estate developers or investors, but from family law attorneys whose clients suddenly need to refinance, restructure, or bridge a commercial property as part of a dissolution proceeding. These deals are different from anything else in commercial lending. The stakes are personal, the deadlines are court-imposed, and the financing has to work or the settlement collapses.

If you are a divorce attorney, family law practitioner, dissolution mediator, forensic accountant, or QDRO specialist who regularly encounters commercial property in your cases, this article is for you. I am going to walk through the most common financing scenarios you will face, what lenders actually require, and how to get your clients to the right capital source without burning weeks on the wrong lenders.

Why Family Law Practitioners Encounter Commercial Real Estate Financing

Commercial real estate shows up in dissolution proceedings more often than most attorneys anticipate before they have handled a few cases involving business owners, investors, or high-net-worth individuals. The marital estate might include an office building a spouse purchased for their professional practice, a small apartment complex they inherited or bought during the marriage, a retail strip center held in a family LLC, or an industrial warehouse tied to a closely held business. It might also include a ground lease, a net lease investment, or a mixed-use property that generates income for both spouses.

These are not residential properties. A family law attorney who handles a contested home cannot simply call the same lender and expect the same process. Commercial loans are underwritten on the property's income, not the borrower's personal income. The loan terms, lender types, due diligence requirements, and timelines are entirely different. When a commercial property sits inside a marital estate, the dissolution agreement has to account for that property's financing reality, not just its appraised value.

The problem I see most frequently is that attorneys negotiate a settlement that requires a refinance by a specific date, without first confirming whether that refinance is actually executable. Courts set deadlines. Lenders do not care about court deadlines. If the property has a lease vacancy problem, a deferred maintenance issue, or a borrower whose personal financials have been disrupted by the litigation, the refinance may take longer than the court timeline allows or may not be possible with conventional financing at all.

This is where a commercial mortgage broker who understands dissolution proceedings can prevent a settlement from unraveling.

The Five Financing Scenarios You Will Encounter Most Often

Scenario 1: One Spouse Wants to Keep the Property and Buy Out the Other

This is the most common scenario. One spouse has a deep operational or emotional connection to a commercial property and wants to retain it. The other spouse wants their equity paid out. The settlement agreement specifies a buyout amount, typically derived from an appraisal, and gives the retaining spouse a window to complete the refinance and fund the payment.

The financing challenge here is that the retaining spouse needs to extract equity from the property to fund the buyout while simultaneously assuming sole responsibility for the loan. If the existing mortgage is a low-rate conventional loan, the refinance will almost certainly involve a higher rate and potentially a higher payment, which changes the property's cash flow profile. If the existing loan has a prepayment penalty, that cost has to be factored into the settlement structure.

What I typically pursue for this scenario is a cash-out refinance with a permanent lender such as a life insurance company, CMBS platform, bank, or credit union, depending on the property type, location, and loan size. Life insurance companies offer the most competitive long-term rates for stabilized properties above $3 million and are often willing to lend at 60 to 65 percent of value on income-producing assets. For smaller loans or properties with any occupancy or income irregularity, I look to regional banks or credit unions that can underwrite to the asset rather than requiring institutional-grade performance metrics.

The key issue for this scenario is whether the retaining spouse can qualify as a solo borrower. Lenders will scrutinize their liquidity, personal net worth, and experience managing commercial property independently. If the departing spouse was the primary financial guarantor, the retaining spouse needs to demonstrate that they can step into that role. This is often where the forensic accountant's work becomes directly relevant to the lending process.

Scenario 2: Both Spouses Want to Sell but the Property Needs Stabilization First

Both parties agree to sell, but the property cannot be sold at full value in its current state. Maybe a major tenant vacated during the proceedings. Maybe the property has deferred maintenance that will cause it to fail buyer due diligence. Maybe the existing loan is maturing and the spouses need bridge financing to hold the property while they stabilize it and find a buyer at the right price.

This is a bridge loan scenario. A bridge loan is short-term financing, typically 12 to 36 months, designed to carry a property through a transition period. Bridge lenders underwrite primarily on the collateral and the exit strategy rather than on stabilized cash flow. They will lend on properties with vacancy, deferred maintenance, lease-up in progress, or a known sale pending.

For a dissolution proceeding, a bridge loan can buy both parties the time they need to maximize sale proceeds rather than accepting a discounted offer on a distressed asset. A sale at full stabilized value split two ways is almost always better than a distressed sale split two ways, even after accounting for the cost of the bridge loan.

Bridge lenders typically charge between 9 and 13 percent interest depending on leverage, property type, and market, with origination fees of 1 to 2 points. Loans are often interest-only. The math needs to work for both spouses, and I recommend that attorneys build the carrying cost of a bridge loan into the preliminary financial analysis before assuming the property can be sold quickly at the appraised value.

Scenario 3: The Property Has Been Neglected During Contentious Proceedings

Highly contested divorces can last years. During that time, neither spouse may be willing to authorize capital expenditures, make repairs, or manage the property aggressively. Tenants leave. Deferred maintenance accumulates. By the time a settlement is reached, the property may be genuinely distressed: significant vacancy, deferred capital expenditure needs, and a value well below where it would be with active management.

This scenario requires bridge financing with a renovation or rehabilitation component. Some bridge lenders will provide a construction holdback, releasing funds in draws as approved work is completed, allowing the borrower to repair and restabilize the property before either selling or refinancing into permanent debt.

What makes this complex in a dissolution context is that someone has to serve as the managing borrower with authority to execute construction contracts, approve draw requests, and make operational decisions during the renovation period. If the court has appointed a receiver, the receiver may be the effective decision-maker, and sophisticated bridge lenders have experience working with receivers. If not, the parties need to designate one spouse or a third-party manager with clear authority before a lender will move forward.

I have placed bridge loans on receivership properties before. The key is finding a lender who is comfortable with the legal complexity and who has counsel experienced in dissolution-related commercial transactions. Not every bridge lender will engage with this level of complexity. The ones who do are typically private debt funds and specialty finance companies rather than banks or insurance companies.

Scenario 4: The Property Is Held Inside a Business Entity That Needs to Be Separated

Commercial real estate is frequently held inside an LLC, limited partnership, S-corporation, or family trust. When a marriage dissolves and the entity itself is a marital asset, unwinding the ownership structure often requires refinancing to separate the real estate from the broader business operations or to transfer ownership to one party in a tax-efficient manner.

This is one of the most technically complex scenarios from a lending standpoint. Lenders underwrite to the entity and its operating history. If the entity is being restructured, dissolved, or transferred as part of the dissolution, the new borrowing entity may have no track record. The lender has to get comfortable with the restructured ownership, the new guarantor structure, and the continuity of the property's management and income.

I work closely with the forensic accountant and the transactional attorney on these deals to ensure that the entity restructuring and the financing are sequenced correctly. Lenders need to see a clear chain of title, a valid organizational structure at closing, and personal financial statements from whoever will serve as the guarantor post-dissolution. Timing matters enormously here: the entity restructuring often needs to be substantially complete before the lender will underwrite the new loan.

QDRO specialists who are used to separating retirement assets should understand that commercial real estate in a holdco has no equivalent to a direct rollover. The real estate transfer is a taxable event unless structured very carefully, and the financing has to accommodate whatever structure the tax advisors design. I have learned to always ask whether a 1031 exchange is in play and whether there are any restrictions on how the entity can be restructured before I begin lender outreach.

Scenario 5: A Court Order Requires Removing One Spouse From an Existing Loan

Divorce decrees and settlement agreements frequently require that the retaining spouse refinance the property within a specified period to remove the departing spouse from liability on the existing loan. This is especially important when the departing spouse needs the release of that liability to qualify for their own future financing, or when they are personally exposed on a large commercial guarantee.

The challenge is that the existing lender typically will not simply release a guarantor without full payoff or a formal assumption process. A formal assumption requires the lender's consent and a full underwriting review of the remaining borrower's creditworthiness. Some lenders will not allow assumptions at all under their loan documents. This means the retaining spouse often has no choice but to refinance, and they have to do it within whatever window the court has specified.

I treat court-imposed refinance deadlines as hard constraints and work backward from them when selecting lenders and structuring the process. A conventional bank refinance might take 60 to 90 days. A bridge loan from a private lender might close in 30 days. If the court has given 45 days, that limits the lender universe significantly, and I am upfront with the attorney about what is and is not achievable.

What Lenders Actually Require for These Situations

Commercial lenders evaluate these transactions on the same criteria they use for all commercial loans, with some additional scrutiny because of the legal complexity involved. Here is what you should expect your client to need:

Property financials: At least two years of operating statements (rent rolls, profit and loss by year), current rent roll with lease expiration dates, and trailing 12-month figures. Properties with no formal financial reporting because they were managed informally during the marriage will require reconstruction by the forensic accountant, and lenders will discount unaudited owner-prepared statements.

Appraisal: Most lenders require a MAI-certified appraisal ordered directly by the lender or acceptable to the lender. The settlement agreement's appraised value and the lender's appraisal may differ, which can affect the loan amount and buyout calculations. I recommend building this contingency into the settlement structure.

Borrower financials: Personal tax returns for two years, personal financial statement, bank statements for three months, and a list of real estate owned. In a dissolution, the borrower's financial picture may have changed significantly from prior years. Lenders who see unusual income patterns or large transfers will ask questions, and having a clear explanation ready from the CPA accelerates underwriting.

Legal documentation: The draft or executed dissolution agreement, any court orders affecting the property, title history, and organizational documents for any entity involved. The lender's counsel will review all of this as part of due diligence.

Minimum thresholds for permanent financing: Debt service coverage ratio of 1.20x to 1.30x on the new loan, loan-to-value of 65 to 75 percent depending on property type, and a borrower net worth generally equal to or greater than the loan amount. Bridge lenders are more flexible on DSCR but still require a viable exit strategy and a property that can support stabilized debt at a reasonable LTV.

Questions to Ask Any Commercial Mortgage Broker Before Referring

Not every commercial mortgage broker has experience with dissolution-related transactions. Before referring a client, ask these questions:

Do you have experience with refinances that are part of divorce or dissolution proceedings? This is not a standard commercial transaction. The lender's counsel will need to navigate dissolution-specific documentation, and the broker needs to know how to manage that process.

What lender types do you access? A broker who only works with one or two banks will not have the range to find the right capital source for complex or distressed situations. You want access to life insurance companies, banks, credit unions, bridge lenders, debt funds, and SBA lenders depending on what the situation requires.

Can you close within the court's timeline? Ask for a specific timeline based on the transaction type and be realistic about what you hear. A broker who promises 30-day closes on complex restructurings without explaining how is not being straight with you.

How do you handle situations where the property has performance issues? Vacancy, deferred maintenance, and income disruption during litigation are common. You want a broker who has lender relationships that can handle these situations, not one who only places clean, stabilized assets.

What happens if the property does not appraise at the value in the settlement? This is a real risk, and you want to know the broker's contingency plan before it happens rather than after.

Will you coordinate directly with our forensic accountant and tax counsel? These transactions require close coordination between the financial, legal, and lending teams. A broker who does not engage with the broader advisory team will create gaps that slow the process.

Key Takeaways

  • Commercial real estate in a dissolution is not the same as residential. The financing process, lender types, underwriting criteria, and timelines are fundamentally different, and treating them the same is a common and expensive mistake.
  • Court deadlines and lender timelines frequently conflict. The earlier a commercial mortgage broker is involved, the more likely the refinance closes on time. Six weeks is often not enough time for a complex restructuring.
  • The settlement agreement's appraised value and the lender's appraisal may differ. Build a cushion into the buyout calculation or include an appraisal contingency in the settlement structure.
  • Bridge loans are often the right tool, not a fallback. When a property is in transition, distressed, or needs to be held while a sale is arranged, bridge financing is purpose-built for exactly that scenario and can prevent a discounted forced sale.
  • The retaining spouse must qualify as a solo borrower. Lenders will underwrite them independently, and the dissolution may have changed their financial profile significantly. Know this before committing to a buyout number.
  • Entity-held real estate requires careful sequencing. The entity restructuring, the tax planning, and the refinancing have to be coordinated or they will conflict. Bring in the commercial broker early enough to participate in the sequencing conversation.
  • Receivership properties can be financed. Specialty bridge lenders with legal sophistication can and do lend on receivership assets. It is a niche but it is not impossible.
  • The referral costs nothing and carries no downside. A brief introduction puts a commercial lending specialist on your team for the duration of the transaction. The client gets better financing outcomes and you avoid the liability of advising outside your area of expertise.

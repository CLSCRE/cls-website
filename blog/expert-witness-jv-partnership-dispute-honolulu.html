Expert Witness: CRE Partnership and Waterfall Disputes in Honolulu, HI

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Joint Venture and Partnership Dispute

The Honolulu Market Context

Honolulu is one of the most capital-constrained and supply-restricted commercial real estate markets in the United States, and those structural characteristics create financing conditions that are categorically different from what attorneys and investors encounter in mainland gateway cities. The island geography creates permanent barriers to new supply that shape every element of the capital stack, from senior debt sizing to equity return expectations. For joint venture disputes specifically, that supply restriction matters enormously: when a deal closes at a compressed cap rate because the asset is irreplaceable, the projected returns embedded in a waterfall agreement depend heavily on assumptions about rent growth, exit valuation, and refinancing capacity, all of which perform differently in Honolulu than in a continental market.

Multifamily assets in Honolulu trade at cap rates consistently in the 3.5% to 4.5% range, reflecting both the scarcity premium and the consistent demand from a resident population that cannot easily relocate. Retail along primary corridors in urban Honolulu has maintained stronger fundamentals than most mainland markets, supported by tourism-driven consumer spending and limited alternative supply. Industrial product on Oahu is arguably the tightest asset class on the island: vacancy has hovered near 1% to 2% in recent cycles, and logistics-oriented tenants competing for the limited available space have pushed net rents materially higher. For a JV dispute involving any of these asset classes, the valuations, income projections, and exit assumptions embedded in the original operating agreement were likely formed in a market environment that now looks quite different from the one that existed when the deal was underwritten, a divergence that frequently generates exactly the kind of distribution disputes we are retained to analyze.

The Honolulu market also has a concentrated lender community relative to its transaction volume. Local and regional institutions with deep Hawaii-specific knowledge dominate certain asset classes, while institutional capital from the mainland requires higher yield thresholds to justify the geographic concentration risk. Understanding which lenders were realistically available to the JV at the time of closing, at refinancing, or at the point when the dispute crystallized is foundational expert work. What a JV partner was entitled to receive under the waterfall is inseparable from what the financing structure actually permitted, and in Honolulu, those constraints are more binding than in most markets.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing the limited partner in a dispute involving a mixed-use commercial asset in urban Honolulu. The asset combined ground-floor retail with upper-floor office or residential components, a structure common in Honolulu's denser urban submarkets where land cost justifies layered use. The general partner had managed the asset and the financing relationships throughout the hold period. The limited partner alleged that distributions had been incorrectly calculated, that the preferred return had been improperly applied, and that the general partner's promoted interest had been taken ahead of a full return of capital, in violation of the waterfall mechanics specified in the JV operating agreement.

Counsel needed an expert who could answer a set of questions that sit at the intersection of real estate finance and transactional structure. First, was the capital stack as originally arranged consistent with market standards for a deal of this type and size in Honolulu? Second, were the debt service obligations associated with that financing accurately reflected in the cash flow model used to calculate distributions? Third, did the financing terms, specifically the interest rate type, amortization schedule, prepayment provisions, and any reserve requirements, create constraints on available cash flow that materially affected the parties' entitlements under the waterfall? Fourth, would a market-standard lender have required the reserve structure that the general partner established, or was that reserve structure discretionary and therefore potentially inflating the general partner's apparent reinvestment of capital? These are questions an appraiser cannot answer. They require someone who works in the capital markets on a transaction basis and understands how lenders actually underwrite and structure deals of this type.

CLS CRE was engaged to produce a written expert analysis addressing each of these questions, with supporting calculations, comparable market data, and a methodology section suitable for use in a Rule 26 expert report. Our analysis covered the original debt placement, two subsequent refinancing events, and the distribution calculations for a hold period spanning approximately six years.

The Financial Analysis

The first step in any waterfall dispute analysis is reconstructing the asset's actual income-producing capacity at each point when distributions were either made or withheld. In this engagement, the subject property generated stabilized net operating income of approximately $1,850,000 annually at the time of the first refinancing event, based on in-place leases, vacancy-adjusted gross income, and documented operating expenses. That NOI figure was not in dispute; what was in dispute was how the lender's underwriting of that NOI affected the amount of debt the property could supportably carry, and therefore how much cash remained available to flow through the waterfall.

Market-standard lenders underwriting commercial real estate debt in 2021 and 2022, which is when the refinancing events at issue occurred, applied debt service coverage ratio thresholds of 1.25x as a floor on stabilized, fully amortizing debt service. Some lenders, particularly those active in Hawaii who apply a geographic concentration premium to their credit standards, required 1.30x. Using a 1.25x DSCR floor, a 30-year amortization schedule, and a fixed rate of 4.50%, which was achievable in that rate environment for a well-located, stabilized mixed-use asset in Honolulu, the maximum supportable annual debt service on $1,850,000 of NOI was $1,480,000. At that debt service level, the maximum principal balance on a 30-year amortizing loan at 4.50% was approximately $22,700,000.

The lender also applied an LTV constraint. For mixed-use commercial product in Honolulu, institutional lenders were lending at 65% to 70% LTV at the time of the relevant refinancing. Applying a 65% LTV to an independently supportable valuation of $34,000,000 for the subject property yields a maximum loan amount of $22,100,000. The two sizing constraints, DSCR and LTV, converged closely in this case, which is the mark of a well-underwritten transaction. The actual loan placed at refinancing was $21,500,000, slightly below the maximum supportable amount, a conservative placement consistent with market practice.

What this analysis demonstrated for the litigation was specific and consequential. The general partner had argued that loan proceeds could not be treated as a return of capital because the lender required those proceeds to retire the existing debt and fund mandatory capital reserves. Our analysis showed that the mandatory reserve requirement the lender imposed was $350,000, documented in the loan agreement, but that the general partner had actually set aside $725,000 in reserves, characterizing the additional $375,000 as a lender requirement. That differential, multiplied across two refinancing events, altered the available-for-distribution calculation by approximately $750,000, directly affecting both the preferred return calculation and the promoted interest threshold.

The Complicating Factors

The Mixed-Use Income Complexity. Mixed-use assets in Honolulu present underwriting complexity that directly affects waterfall mechanics. Lenders treat the income components differently: ground-floor retail income is underwritten with a higher vacancy haircut than multifamily or long-term office leases, and some lenders will discount income from leases with shorter remaining terms or month-to-month tenancies more aggressively. When a JV operating agreement requires the manager to maximize net cash flow, but the financing structure penalizes certain lease structures at underwriting, the general partner's decisions about tenant mix and lease duration have downstream effects on debt capacity and therefore on distributions. Expert analysis must reconstruct not just the actual NOI but the lender's underwritten NOI, which may differ materially.

Preferred Return Accrual During Refinancing Blackout Periods. JV agreements in Honolulu, as elsewhere, frequently specify that the preferred return accrues on unreturned capital during periods when the property generates insufficient cash flow to cover it. Refinancing events, particularly those involving interest rate resets or bridge-to-permanent loan structures, often create temporary cash flow constraints. In this engagement, one refinancing event involved a bridge loan at a floating rate that temporarily elevated debt service above the stabilized amount, compressing distributable cash flow for approximately fourteen months. The operating agreement was ambiguous about whether the preferred return continued to accrue at the full rate during that period, or whether it was limited to actual available cash. Resolving that ambiguity required an expert explanation of why the bridge structure was used, whether it was market-standard, and what alternatives were realistically available. That is capital markets expertise, not appraisal expertise.

Honolulu-Specific Reserve Requirements. Many institutional lenders lending in Honolulu impose reserves beyond what they would require for comparable mainland assets. This reflects the logistical cost of property maintenance and capital improvements in a market where construction materials, labor, and contractor availability are all constrained. Lenders frequently require furniture, fixture, and equipment reserves for hospitality-adjacent assets, and replacement reserves for roofing, HVAC, and structural systems that carry higher replacement cost in Hawaii. In a waterfall dispute, these reserves reduce available-for-distribution cash flow, and expert analysis must distinguish between reserves that are genuinely lender-mandated and those that reflect discretionary management decisions by the general partner.

Prepayment Structures and Exit Waterfall Timing. Commercial loans in Honolulu, as in most markets, carry prepayment penalties in the form of yield maintenance or defeasance during the fixed-rate lock-out period. In a JV where the exit event triggers the final waterfall distribution, the cost of prepaying the debt directly reduces net sale proceeds and therefore affects every tier of the waterfall, including the return of capital, the preferred return catchup, and the promoted interest split. In this engagement, the general partner had structured the debt with a ten-year term and a seven-year prepayment lock-out. When a sale opportunity arose in year five, the yield maintenance penalty was approximately $1,400,000, which the general partner had not modeled in the original waterfall projections. An expert witness must be able to explain whether that prepayment structure was market-standard, whether the general partner had a duty to model that cost, and how its treatment affects the distribution analysis.

The Honolulu Lender Market

The Honolulu commercial lending market has a distinctive architecture that attorneys and investors need to understand when evaluating what financing was available to a JV at any given point in time. The market is served by a core group of Hawaii-based community and regional banks that have deep knowledge of local assets, sponsor relationships, and submarket dynamics. These institutions typically lend to $15,000,000 to $25,000,000 per transaction on their balance sheet, apply conservative LTV standards (60% to 65% for commercial, up to 70% for stabilized multifamily), and price on a relationship basis that can produce competitive coupons for well-known sponsors. For a JV involving a mainland-based sponsor or a less established local presence, these institutions may require additional credit enhancement or recourse provisions not typically seen in comparable mainland deals.

Institutional lenders, including life insurance companies, CMBS conduits, and debt funds, are active in Honolulu for assets above approximately $20,000,000 to $25,000,000. Life companies have historically been attracted to Honolulu's supply-constrained fundamentals and the durable income streams from stabilized, well-located assets. CMBS execution is available but introduces servicing complexity and property management reporting requirements that some JV structures find difficult to comply with. Debt funds, which have become a significant source of bridge and transitional capital in the post-2020 period, price with significant spreads over SOFR and are most relevant for value-add plays or assets that do not yet qualify for agency or conduit financing.

For the JV transaction at issue, the relevant lender universe at the time of the original closing was the community bank market for construction financing and then the institutional market for permanent take-out financing. Our analysis confirmed that the terms actually obtained were within the range of what the market was offering, a finding that supported the position that the financing itself was not improperly structured, but that the way its economics were applied in the waterfall calculation was where the dispute originated.

What Attorneys Handling Honolulu CRE Disputes Need to Know

A CRE financing expert is not an appraiser, and the distinction matters in litigation. An appraiser tells the court what a property was worth. A CRE financing expert tells the court what the capital markets would have done with that property, what debt was available, on what terms, at what cost, and how those terms constrained or shaped the economics that flowed through the deal. In a waterfall dispute, the expert's core function is to establish the bridge between the debt layer and the equity distribution mechanics, because those two things are not independent.

When to retain a CRE financing expert. Counsel should consider retention early in any matter involving preferred return calculations, promoted interest disputes, capital account disagreements, or allegations that cash flow was withheld or misdirected. The earlier the expert is engaged, the more useful the analysis will be in shaping discovery strategy, identifying the documents that matter, and framing the deposition questions for the general partner or the lender's representatives.

What makes an expert qualified. Qualification in CRE financing expert work comes from active transactional practice, not from academic credentials or a prior career in banking. An expert who is currently placing commercial loans, negotiating term sheets, and working with lenders across multiple asset classes and geographies has current, market-tested knowledge of what lenders require, how they underwrite, and where deals fail. A retired banker may know historical standards but cannot speak to current market practice with the same authority. At CLS CRE, we close transactions across all 50 states, maintain relationships with more than 1,000 lenders, and have placed approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, giving our expert opinions a market foundation that is difficult to challenge on currency or breadth.

Documents to gather before the engagement. Counsel should prioritize: the original JV operating agreement with all amendments; the loan documents from every financing event during the hold period, including term sheets, commitment letters, loan agreements, and closing statements; all distribution notices and underlying calculations; reserve account statements; the general partner's internal cash flow models; any appraisals or broker opinions of value obtained during the hold; and any correspondence between the JV parties regarding financing decisions or distribution disputes. The more complete this record, the more precise and defensible the expert's analysis will be.

How expert testimony is used at trial. In a JV dispute, a CRE financing expert typically testifies on two categories of issues. The first is whether the financing structure was market-standard and whether the general partner's decisions regarding debt placement, reserve establishment, and prepayment were within the range of reasonable market practice. The second is whether the waterfall calculations were applied correctly given the actual economics imposed by the debt. The expert may also rebut opposing testimony that overstates or understates the constraint that a particular lender structure imposed on cash flow availability. This testimony is most effective when it is grounded in documented market data, comparable transaction terms, and specific numerical reconstructions of the distribution calculations.

Key Takeaways

  • Honolulu's supply-constrained market conditions produce deal economics that diverge significantly from mainland benchmarks, meaning that waterfall structures designed using mainland return assumptions may perform differently when applied to Honolulu assets, and expert analysis must account for those local characteristics.
  • The maximum supportable loan in a Honolulu commercial deal is constrained by both DSCR (typically 1.25x) and LTV (typically 65% to 70%), and both constraints must be reconstructed in any dispute involving refinancing proceeds, capital account credits, or claims that debt proceeds were improperly classified.
  • Mandatory lender reserves and discretionary management reserves are not the same thing, and distinguishing between them requires expertise in how lenders structure reserve requirements for Hawaii assets specifically, where logistical and replacement cost factors are materially higher than mainland norms.
  • Preferred return accrual during bridge loan periods, interest rate resets, or refinancing blackout windows requires expert analysis of whether the financing structure was market-standard and whether the general partner's decision to use that structure was consistent with their fiduciary obligations under the operating agreement.
  • Prepayment penalties including yield maintenance and defeasance costs are capital markets concepts, not appraisal concepts, and their impact on waterfall distributions at exit requires testimony from someone with active debt placement experience, not a valuation professional.

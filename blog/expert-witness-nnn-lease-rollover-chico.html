Expert Witness: NNN Lease Rollover Upside and Financing Impact in Chico, CA

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Below-Market NNN Lease and Rollover Upside Analysis

The Chico Market Context

Chico, California sits in Butte County at the northern end of the Sacramento Valley and is frequently underestimated as a commercial real estate market. It is not a tier-one institutional market, but it is not a sleepy rural outpost either. The city anchors a trade area of roughly 250,000 people when you include Oroville, Paradise, and the surrounding rural communities, and California State University, Chico functions as a permanent demand driver for retail, multifamily, and service-oriented commercial space. That combination of a university anchor, a regional medical hub anchored by Enloe Medical Center, and a rebuilt post-Camp Fire economy creates a commercial real estate landscape that is more complex and more legally contested than its population size would suggest.

For single-tenant net lease assets specifically, Chico presents a distinct underwriting environment. The market has absorbed significant economic disruption since the 2018 Camp Fire, which destroyed Paradise and redirected enormous insurance payouts and housing demand into Chico. That population influx temporarily compressed retail vacancy and drove short-term rent spikes in certain corridors, particularly along the Esplanade and the Cohasset Road commercial spine. By 2023 and into 2024, however, much of that displacement demand had normalized, and the retail submarket has settled into a more typical secondary-city pattern: Class A pad sites and drive-through-configured single-tenant buildings remain highly sought after, while older inline and strip-center product shows meaningful softness. Retail vacancy across Chico's primary commercial corridors runs in the 8 to 12 percent range depending on the submarket, with the strongest absorption concentrated in new construction along Highway 99 and the Nord Avenue interchange.

Single-tenant NNN assets in Chico trade at cap rates that reflect both the tenant credit quality and the lease term remaining. Well-leased, investment-grade single-tenant product with 10-plus years of term can trade at cap rates in the 5.5 to 6.5 percent range. Shorter-term leases or non-investment-grade tenants push that number north of 7 percent, sometimes significantly. The gap between those two valuations is precisely where financing disputes arise, and it is precisely the scenario our firm was retained to analyze.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing one of the parties in a commercial real estate dispute involving a single-tenant NNN property in Chico. The asset was a freestanding retail building of approximately 8,000 to 10,000 square feet, occupied by a single tenant operating under a long-term NNN lease that had been executed several years prior. The central issue in the litigation involved the economic impact of a below-market lease on the property's financing capacity, and by extension, on its value and the damages available to the client.

The core questions posed to CLS CRE were as follows. First, how do institutional and non-institutional lenders actually underwrite a single-tenant NNN asset when the in-place rent is materially below current market rent? Second, does the existence of rollover upside (that is, the potential for rent to reset to market upon lease expiration) improve loan sizing, and if so, under what conditions and with what limitations? Third, does the lease structure as it exists today support refinancing at commercially standard LTV and debt service coverage ratio thresholds, and if not, what is the financing shortfall and what would need to change to close that gap? Our assignment was to apply market-standard lender underwriting criteria to the property's existing income stream, to model the financing capacity at current in-place rents versus projected market rents, and to provide an expert opinion on whether refinancing at specific LTV and DSCR thresholds was feasible given the lease's current terms.

The Financial Analysis

The analysis began with a reconstruction of the property's actual net operating income based on the in-place lease. In a true NNN structure, the tenant is responsible for taxes, insurance, and maintenance, so gross rent and NOI are close to equivalent, reduced only by a management fee reserve and vacancy allowance that most lenders apply even to single-tenant occupied properties. For this type of asset in Chico, a standard lender underwriting model applies a 5 percent management fee on effective gross income and a credit loss reserve of 3 to 5 percent, even when the property is 100 percent occupied, because lenders are stress-testing the scenario in which the single tenant vacates.

Using plausible but non-identifying figures appropriate for this submarket: assume the in-place NNN rent is $14.00 per square foot annually on a 9,000-square-foot building. That generates gross rental income of $126,000 per year. After applying a 5 percent management fee ($6,300) and a 5 percent credit loss reserve ($6,300), effective NOI is approximately $113,400. Meanwhile, current market NNN rent for comparable single-tenant retail space in Chico's primary corridors is in the range of $19.00 to $22.00 per square foot, meaning the in-place lease is 25 to 35 percent below market. That gap is not incidental; it is the central variable that drives the entire financing analysis.

At a market-standard DSCR of 1.25x, which is the most common minimum threshold applied by commercial banks, credit unions, and CMBS conduit lenders for single-tenant retail assets in secondary markets, the maximum annual debt service supportable by the in-place NOI of $113,400 is $90,720. Applying a 7.25 percent interest rate (reflecting conditions in the 2023 to 2024 lending environment for secondary-market retail) on a 25-year amortization schedule, $90,720 in annual debt service supports a loan of approximately $1,165,000. If the property is valued at a 6.75 percent cap rate on in-place NOI, the value is approximately $1,680,000. The loan-to-value on a $1,165,000 loan against a $1,680,000 value is approximately 69 percent, which is within most lenders' LTV limits for this asset class. However, that LTV comfort does not resolve the underlying problem. The loan is constrained by DSCR, not by LTV, and the DSCR-limited loan is the binding constraint here.

Now apply the rollover upside scenario. If market rent resets to $20.00 per square foot upon lease expiration, NOI would increase to approximately $162,000. At 1.25x DSCR and the same rate and amortization, that NOI supports a loan of approximately $1,675,000, an increase of roughly $510,000 in loan capacity. The question our analysis had to answer is whether any lender would actually underwrite to that future income rather than to the current in-place income. The answer, with limited and highly conditional exceptions, is no. Lenders underwrite to in-place, contractually committed income. Rollover upside is speculative until it is contracted. This is the central financing principle at issue in this type of dispute, and it is frequently misunderstood by parties who conflate appraised value with borrowing capacity.

The Complicating Factors

Below-Market Rent as a Lender Underwriting Constraint

The existence of below-market in-place rent does more than reduce the current NOI available to service debt. It signals to lenders that the property carries re-leasing risk at lease expiration. If the current tenant exercises renewal options at the below-market rate (which many NNN leases permit), the income stream remains suppressed. If the tenant does not renew, the landlord faces a lease-up period with no income. Lenders price this risk by underwriting conservatively to in-place income and by applying stricter LTV haircuts for assets with shorter remaining lease terms. In Chico's secondary retail market, most conventional lenders will not underwrite a single-tenant NNN loan if the remaining lease term is less than the proposed loan term plus two years. A 10-year loan on an asset with 8 years of lease term remaining creates a maturity mismatch that most lenders will not accept without significant additional credit support.

The Rollover Upside Fallacy in Loan Sizing

A common misconception in NNN financing disputes is that rollover upside improves loan sizing. In practice, future market rent increases loan sizing only in very specific, narrow circumstances: bridge lending scenarios where the lender is explicitly underwriting a value-add business plan, or agency and CMBS products that apply a hypothetical stabilized value alongside a reserve structure to bridge the income gap. For a standard permanent loan on a Chico single-tenant retail property, no institutional lender or community bank will size the loan based on what the rent will be when the current lease expires. They will size it based on what the rent is today, what NOI that produces, and whether that NOI satisfies their DSCR requirements at their required stress rate. Rollover upside may support a higher appraised value on an income approach using a lower cap rate for a building with substantial below-market rent and near-term expiration, but a higher appraised value does not produce a larger loan if DSCR is the binding constraint and the in-place income does not support the debt service.

Single-Tenant Concentration Risk

Every lender underwriting a single-tenant NNN property in a secondary market like Chico is implicitly underwriting the creditworthiness of one entity. If that entity is an investment-grade national credit, lenders accept lower DSCR thresholds and higher LTVs. If that entity is a regional operator, a franchise without a corporate guarantee, or a local business, lenders apply substantially more conservative parameters: minimum DSCR of 1.30x to 1.35x rather than 1.20x to 1.25x, maximum LTV of 60 to 65 percent rather than 70 to 75 percent, and in some cases, a full personal guarantee requirement from the borrower regardless of property performance. In this engagement, the tenant's credit profile directly affected the size of the lender universe available and the loan terms achievable.

Lease Structure and Lender Acceptability

The specific language of the NNN lease matters to lenders in ways that are not always obvious. Renewal option structures that allow the tenant to renew at below-market rates are a negative underwriting factor because they limit the landlord's ability to reset rent to market. Co-tenancy clauses, early termination rights, and lease assignment restrictions all affect a lender's view of the collateral's income durability. In Chico, where the retail market has limited depth of replacement tenants for specialized build-to-suit configurations, lenders apply particular scrutiny to re-leasing risk if the current tenant vacates.

The Chico Lender Market

For single-tenant NNN retail assets in Chico, the active lender universe sorts into four categories, each with distinct underwriting parameters and credit box characteristics.

Community and regional banks dominate Chico's commercial lending market for assets in the $1 million to $5 million range. North State-focused institutions and Sacramento Valley community banks with branches in Chico understand the local market, can underwrite to in-place NOI with knowledge of local absorption dynamics, and will consider deals that larger institutions would automatically screen out based on market tier alone. Their typical parameters: DSCR minimum of 1.25x, LTV maximum of 65 to 70 percent for single-tenant retail, 20 to 25-year amortization, floating or 3-to-5-year fixed rates, and full recourse. For below-market lease scenarios, these lenders may offer flexibility if the borrower relationship is strong, but they will not underwrite to projected future rents.

Credit unions with commercial real estate programs have grown their presence in the Northern California secondary market and can be competitive on rate for owner-occupied or strong-credit-tenant assets. They typically require membership, apply 1.20x DSCR minimums, and cap LTV at 70 percent for investment property. Their credit boxes are less flexible than community banks for complicated lease structures.

CMBS conduit lenders are theoretically available for Chico NNN assets but have a practical loan floor of $2 million to $3 million and require investment-grade or strong credit tenants with long remaining lease terms. For below-market lease scenarios with short remaining term, CMBS is effectively unavailable because the loan proceeds would be DSCR-limited to a level that does not justify the execution cost and complexity of a CMBS transaction.

Debt funds and bridge lenders are the appropriate execution vehicle for below-market lease scenarios when the borrower has a credible plan to reset rent at rollover and needs a loan that bridges to that event. These lenders will underwrite to a stabilized value and provide loan proceeds against projected future NOI, but they price that risk accordingly: rates typically 200 to 400 basis points above bank pricing, loan fees of 1 to 2 points, and loan terms of 2 to 3 years with extensions. In Chico, the geographic reach of national debt funds means this capital is available, but borrowers must demonstrate a credible re-leasing thesis to access it.

What Attorneys Handling Chico CRE Disputes Need to Know

A CRE financing expert witness provides analysis and testimony that is categorically different from what an appraiser provides, and the distinction matters enormously in disputes where financing capacity is at issue. An appraiser determines value. A CRE financing expert determines what a lender will actually lend, under what terms, with what credit requirements, and what happens to loan availability when specific lease or income conditions are present. Those are not the same question, and confusing them is a significant strategic error.

Attorneys should retain a CRE financing expert when the dispute involves any of the following: alleged inability to refinance as a damages element; loan proceeds as a component of a business plan that was allegedly frustrated; lender underwriting standards as they apply to a specific income stream or asset type; the difference between appraised value and financeable value; or whether a proposed financing was commercially feasible at the time of the alleged breach or event.

A qualified CRE financing expert is not simply a retired banker or a mortgage broker who processed loans years ago. The expert must have current, active market knowledge, relationships with the specific categories of lenders relevant to the asset type, and the ability to reconstruct underwriting decisions using actual lender parameters rather than generic industry averages. At CLS CRE, our active transaction volume of approximately $1 billion across 1,000-plus lender relationships in 50 states means our market knowledge is current, not historical. We can speak to what lenders were doing in Chico-type markets during a specific time period, not just what they do generically.

Before engaging a financing expert, counsel should gather the following: the complete executed lease, all amendments and rider agreements, the current rent roll, operating statements for at least three years, any existing loan documents, appraisals, environmental reports, and any lender correspondence related to loan applications or denials. The more complete the record, the more precise the expert's analysis and the more defensible the opinion under cross-examination.

In trial or arbitration, CRE financing expert testimony is most effectively used to establish the outer bounds of what financing was available to a party at a specific point in time, and to quantify the difference between financing capacity under actual conditions versus financing capacity under the conditions the opposing party claims should have existed. That delta, expressed in loan dollars, is often a direct input into the damages calculation.

Key Takeaways

  • Lenders underwrite to in-place contractual income, not to projected or potential market rent. Rollover upside does not improve loan sizing under standard permanent loan underwriting, regardless of what a property's appraised value might be.
  • DSCR is frequently the binding loan constraint on below-market NNN properties, not LTV. A property may have adequate equity but still be unable to support a commercially standard loan because the in-place income does not generate sufficient debt service coverage at prevailing rates.
  • The gap between a property's financeable value and its appraised value is a quantifiable, expert-supportable number. In a Chico NNN dispute involving a 25 to 35 percent below-market lease, that gap can exceed $500,000 in loan proceeds on a mid-market asset.
  • Tenant credit quality materially shifts lender parameters for single-tenant assets. Non-investment-grade or local tenants trigger higher DSCR minimums and lower LTV limits, further compressing loan availability on already-constrained below-market lease scenarios.

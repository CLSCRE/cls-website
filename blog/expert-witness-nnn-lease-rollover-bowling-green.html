Expert Witness: NNN Lease Rollover Upside and Financing Impact in Bowling Green, KY

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Below-Market NNN Lease and Rollover Upside Analysis

The Bowling Green Market Context

Bowling Green, Kentucky sits at an economic crossroads that makes it far more relevant to commercial real estate financing disputes than its population of roughly 75,000 would suggest. The city anchors Warren County as the third-largest metro in Kentucky, with an industrial and logistics base that has expanded significantly over the past decade. The Corvette assembly plant anchors an automotive manufacturing cluster that includes Tier 1 and Tier 2 suppliers throughout the surrounding industrial corridor, and that manufacturing concentration has driven sustained demand for single-tenant net leased industrial and flex properties. More recently, the confirmed arrival of a major electric vehicle battery manufacturing operation nearby has accelerated industrial land absorption and put upward pressure on both industrial rents and sales pricing across the submarket.

For single-tenant NNN assets specifically, Bowling Green has seen meaningful rent appreciation in the industrial and service-commercial categories over the past three to four years, driven by population growth, retail corridor expansion along the Scottsville Road and Campbell Lane corridors, and persistent demand from regional and national tenants establishing distribution and service footprints. Industrial asking rents in the Warren County market have moved from the low-to-mid $4.00 per square foot range on a NNN basis to the $5.50 to $7.00 range depending on clear height, dock configuration, and age of improvements. Retail NNN rents for freestanding service-commercial product have similarly moved, with market rents for newer construction now ranging from $18.00 to $26.00 NNN for credit-quality tenants. That rent growth is precisely the environment in which below-market lease disputes arise: an owner who locked in a long-term NNN lease several years ago at rents that made sense then is now sitting on a lease that is meaningfully below current market, and the question of how that gap affects the financing of the asset becomes material to litigation, restructuring, or transaction disputes.

Bowling Green's commercial real estate market also benefits from Western Kentucky University's institutional presence, a healthcare sector anchored by a major regional medical center, and a geographic position on I-65 that makes it a legitimate logistics node between Nashville and Louisville. These factors support a lender community that includes both regional and national capital, which matters when analyzing the full financing universe available to a below-market NNN asset.

The Engagement

We were retained by litigation counsel representing a party in a commercial real estate dispute involving a single-tenant NNN property in the Bowling Green market. The asset was a freestanding service-commercial building, occupied by a regional tenant under a long-term net lease executed several years prior to the dispute. The lease had significant term remaining, but the contracted rent was materially below what comparable properties in the Bowling Green market were achieving at the time of the dispute.

The core legal questions before counsel involved the financing implications of that below-market rent structure. Specifically, we were asked to analyze and opine on three issues. First, how does the commercial lending market actually underwrite a below-market NNN lease, and does the existence of rollover upside change the lender's income analysis? Second, what maximum loan amount could be supported by the in-place lease income versus the loan that would be available at market rent, and what does that gap mean in dollar terms? Third, does the lease structure as written preclude refinancing at commercially reasonable LTV and DSCR thresholds, or merely constrain it? These are questions an appraiser can partially address through a value conclusion, but the financing analysis requires a different discipline entirely: one grounded in how loan committees actually underwrite, price, and size debt on single-tenant NNN assets in this market and at this complexity level.

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained because of our active placement history with 1,000+ lender relationships and approximately $1 billion in transaction volume across 50 states, which allows us to speak to what lenders actually do rather than what textbooks say they should do. CLS CRE's role was not to appraise the property but to analyze the financing market, apply current underwriting standards to the specific income profile of the asset, and render opinions on the range of loan proceeds available under the in-place lease structure versus market-rent conditions.

The Financial Analysis

The income analysis began with the in-place lease as the controlling document. In a NNN structure, the gross revenue is straightforward: base rent per the lease, with the tenant responsible for taxes, insurance, and maintenance. For this engagement, the in-place rent was approximately $14.50 per square foot NNN on a building of approximately 8,500 square feet, producing gross contractual income of roughly $123,250 annually. After applying a standard vacancy and credit loss reserve of five percent, even on a single-tenant asset, we arrived at effective gross income of approximately $117,100. With NNN expenses passed to the tenant, landlord operating expenses were limited to management fees and a modest reserve for capital items, bringing net operating income to approximately $110,000 on the in-place lease.

The market rent analysis, supported by comparable lease transactions in the Bowling Green service-commercial corridor, established a market NNN rent of approximately $21.00 per square foot for the subject building's configuration, age, and location quality. At market rent, the same building would generate gross income of approximately $178,500, with effective gross income of approximately $169,600 and a stabilized NOI approaching $161,000, representing an NOI gap of approximately $51,000 per year attributable to the below-market lease.

The DSCR analysis applied the market standard for single-tenant NNN assets in this quality tier and market. Most conventional lenders, including CMBS programs and regional bank lenders, apply a minimum DSCR of 1.25x on single-tenant assets, reflecting the binary nature of single-tenant credit risk. Some life company and portfolio lenders will go to 1.20x for investment-grade or near-investment-grade tenants, but the tenant in this matter was a regional operator without a rated credit profile, which kept the controlling threshold at 1.25x for virtually all lenders in the financing universe.

We applied a note rate of 7.25 percent, consistent with the rate environment applicable to the relevant period and appropriate for a five-year fixed term with a 25-year amortization schedule, which is the standard structure for this asset class and loan size in the Bowling Green market. At 1.25x DSCR and these loan terms, the maximum annual debt service supportable by the in-place NOI of $110,000 is $88,000. A loan sized to produce $88,000 in annual debt service at 7.25 percent on a 25-year amortization supports a loan amount of approximately $1,050,000.

Applying a market cap rate of 6.75 percent to the in-place NOI of $110,000 produces an income-capitalization value of approximately $1,629,000. The loan of $1,050,000 represents an LTV of approximately 64.4 percent against that value. Most lenders cap single-tenant NNN loans at 65 to 70 percent LTV, so the DSCR constraint is the binding constraint here, not the LTV constraint. The debt yield on the $1,050,000 loan, calculated as NOI divided by loan amount, is approximately 10.5 percent, which is within acceptable range for most conventional lenders.

At market rent, the supportable debt service rises to $128,800 at 1.25x DSCR, supporting a loan of approximately $1,540,000. Capping that loan at 65 percent of the market-rent capitalized value of approximately $2,385,000 yields an LTV-constrained loan of approximately $1,550,000. In this scenario, the DSCR and LTV constraints converge, and the loan available at market rent is approximately $490,000 larger than the loan available under the below-market lease structure. That is the quantified financing impairment attributable to the below-market lease in this engagement.

The Complicating Factors

Rollover Upside Does Not Move Most Lenders

The single most important financing principle attorneys need to understand in this type of dispute is that lenders underwrite to in-place cash flow, not to projected or potential cash flow after lease rollover. The existence of rollover upside, the prospect that when this below-market lease expires the landlord can re-lease at market rent and substantially increase NOI, is a legitimate economic argument for equity investors but it does not translate into additional loan proceeds for the current owner. Lenders will not advance dollars today against income they may receive in five or seven years. The underwriting is anchored to the signed lease as the controlling income document, and the rollover period itself introduces risk rather than value from a debt perspective. During rollover, the property may be vacant for a period, the tenant may negotiate a below-market renewal as a condition of staying, or the tenant may leave and require capital investment to attract a replacement tenant. Lenders price that risk in, they do not price the upside in.

Lease Term Remaining and Loan Term Alignment

A below-market lease creates a second structural problem: the relationship between remaining lease term and the loan term the borrower needs. If the in-place lease has four years of term remaining and the borrower is seeking a five-year loan, most lenders will require a lease renewal, a lease extension option exercise, or a go-dark reserve. The scenario where the lease expires inside the loan term forces the lender to underwrite what happens at rollover, and for a below-market lease, lenders are acutely aware that the tenant has economic incentive to vacate rather than renew at market rent, since the tenant's occupancy cost advantage disappears at renewal. This creates a bifurcated underwriting problem: the first period of the loan is well-secured by the in-place lease, but the back half of the loan term sits on a speculative re-leasing assumption that most conventional lenders will not underwrite at full loan proceeds.

Tenant Credit and NNN Structure Interaction

In the Bowling Green market, many single-tenant NNN assets are occupied by regional or local operators rather than investment-grade national credit tenants. The NNN structure transfers operating risk to the tenant, which is favorable to the landlord, but when combined with a non-rated tenant credit, lenders apply both a higher cap rate and a tighter DSCR threshold in their underwriting. A below-market lease on a non-rated tenant property compounds the constraint: the loan is sized off a lower NOI, the cap rate used to value the asset may be wider than for a credit-tenant asset, and the DSCR buffer required is larger. The combination can push the available loan to LTV levels that frustrate refinancing objectives entirely, particularly where the borrower has an existing loan balance that cannot be paid down to the new lower loan amount without a capital contribution.

CMBS Exit Constraints

For borrowers or parties in disputes involving assets that were originally financed through CMBS conduit programs, the below-market lease creates additional complexity at the CMBS servicing and defeasance level. CMBS servicers operate under pooling and servicing agreements that restrict modifications, deferrals, and workouts in ways that portfolio lenders do not face. A below-market lease that impairs the asset's ability to refinance at loan maturity is a material event from the servicer's perspective, and the question of whether the borrower can refinance out of a CMBS loan with a below-market lease in place is a financing feasibility question with real litigation consequence.

The Bowling Green Lender Market

The active lender universe for single-tenant NNN assets in Bowling Green spans several categories, each with distinct credit parameters and appetite for the below-market lease scenario.

Community and regional banks headquartered in or with strong Warren County presence are typically the most flexible lenders for sub-$3 million single-tenant assets. These institutions underwrite to relationship and local market knowledge, and some will accept a below-market lease if the borrower has demonstrated deposit relationships, cross-collateralization, or personal guarantee capacity that supplements the property's cash flow. However, their pricing is generally floating or short-term fixed, and their appetite for non-recourse financing on below-market lease assets is limited. They will also typically require a minimum DSCR of 1.20x to 1.25x and will cap LTV at 65 to 70 percent.

Regional bank commercial real estate lenders, including larger Kentucky-chartered institutions and super-regional banks with Louisville and Nashville-based CRE teams, are active in the $2 million to $10 million range and apply more structured CMBS-adjacent underwriting standards. They are generally unwilling to give credit for rollover upside and will size the loan strictly to in-place NOI at a 1.25x DSCR. They do offer five and seven-year fixed rate structures that provide the term certainty that below-market lease assets need.

CMBS conduit lenders are accessible for Bowling Green single-tenant NNN assets above approximately $2 million in loan amount, and they represent the most standardized underwriting approach. CMBS underwriting is strictly in-place-income-based, DSCR floors are firm at 1.25x, and the lease document controls everything. For below-market leases, CMBS lenders will generally underwrite at the in-place rent with no adjustments and may require a cash flow sweep or reserve if the lease expires within the loan window.

Life company correspondents are selectively active in secondary Kentucky markets for NNN assets with investment-grade or near-investment-grade tenants, but for regional tenant credit, their appetite in Bowling Green is limited. When they do participate, they offer the lowest pricing and longest terms, but their underwriting is the most conservative on single-tenant non-rated credit assets.

What Attorneys Handling Bowling Green CRE Disputes Need to Know

A CRE financing expert is not a substitute for a real estate appraiser, and the two disciplines answer fundamentally different questions. An appraiser establishes value. A CRE financing expert establishes what the debt market will actually lend against that value, at what terms, under what conditions, and how the specific characteristics of the lease, tenant, and asset affect the available financing. In a below-market NNN lease dispute, both opinions may be relevant, but confusing one for the other is a mistake that can leave critical issues unaddressed.

Attorneys should consider retaining a CRE financing expert in any matter where the following questions are at issue: whether a refinancing was feasible at a specific date; whether a lender acted within market standards in sizing, structuring, or declining a loan; whether a lease modification, below-market rent, or lease encumbrance materially impaired financing availability; whether a borrower could have obtained specific loan proceeds at a specific LTV or DSCR threshold; or whether the financing market for a specific asset type was available at commercially reasonable terms during a relevant period.

A qualified CRE financing expert is not simply a retired banker or a commercial appraiser who has reviewed loan documents. The expert should have active, current market engagement, meaning direct experience placing loans with the categories of lenders that would be relevant to the dispute. At CLS CRE, our opinions are grounded in transactions we are actively closing, conversations with underwriters we are working with today, and lender feedback on specific deal structures. That active market knowledge is what allows us to say with specificity which lenders in the Bowling Green financing universe would have underwritten a particular loan at a particular time, and which would not.

Before retaining a CRE financing expert, counsel should gather the executed lease and all amendments; the current rent roll and any historical rent documentation; any existing loan documents, appraisals, or lender correspondence; the property's operating history and any available financial statements; and any communications between the parties regarding financing attempts, lender rejections, or refinancing efforts. The quality of the expert's analysis is directly tied to the completeness of the documentary record provided.

At trial or in a plan hearing, CRE financing expert testimony is used to establish the causal relationship between a lease structure or transaction event and a quantifiable financing impairment. In a below-market lease dispute, the expert can testify to the specific dollar amount by which the available loan was reduced as a result of the below-market rent, the categories of lenders that were effectively foreclosed from the financing universe as a result, and whether alternative financing structures could have mitigated the impairment and at what cost.

Key Takeaways

  • Lenders underwrite to in-place cash flow, not rollover upside. The potential for rent increases at lease expiration does not increase the loan available today. This is the most misunderstood point in below-market NNN lease financing disputes, and it must be clearly established in any expert analysis.
  • The DSCR constraint typically binds before the LTV constraint on below-market NNN assets. When in-place NOI is depressed by a below-market lease, the DSCR ceiling on loan size is usually reached before the LTV ceiling, meaning the property's value is not the limiting factor. The income is.

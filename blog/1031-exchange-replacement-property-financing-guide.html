1031 Exchange Replacement Property Financing: Timeline, Loan Options and Debt Sizing

Trevor Damyan Founder & Principal Broker
CA DRE #02244836

Former CBRE and Marcus & Millichap Capital Corporation. $1B+ in closed commercial real estate financing across bridge, construction, permanent, and structured debt.

November 26, 2026
Quick answer: To finance a 1031 exchange replacement property, line the loan up with the exchange calendar: identify the property in writing within 45 days of selling, close the purchase within 180 days, and start lender underwriting before the identification deadline. Size the new loan to replace the debt you paid off, and keep a short-term bridge loan only as a backstop if permanent financing cannot fund in time. Your qualified intermediary and tax advisor own the tax calls.

The short answer to how you finance a 1031 exchange replacement property is that the loan has to fit the exchange calendar, not the other way around. You have 45 days from closing on the property you sold to identify replacement property in writing, and generally 180 days from that same closing to take title. A lender that cannot fund inside that window can cost an investor the tax deferral, even when the property and the price are right. Commercial Lending Solutions (CLS CRE), a Los Angeles commercial mortgage brokerage, arranges loans from $2 million to $100 million and up for exchange buyers through more than 1,000 lenders. This guide walks through the clock, the loan options, the debt math and the questions worth asking before you identify.

This guide explains how financing fits into an exchange. It is not tax or legal advice. Exchange rules turn on the facts of each transaction, so confirm every deadline and structure with your qualified intermediary and tax advisor before you commit.

The 45 Day and 180 Day Clock, Mapped to a Loan

Both periods start when the property you are selling closes, and they run at the same time rather than back to back. The 180 days is also generally cut short if the due date of your tax return, with extensions, falls earlier. Neither period can normally be extended, which is why a loan timeline is built backward from the dates. The table shows what a lender needs at each stage.

Three identification rules shape how many loans you may need to line up. Most investors use the three property rule (identify up to three properties of any value). Others use the 200 percent rule (more than three, as long as their combined value stays within 200 percent of what you sold) or the 95 percent rule. If you identify several properties, parallel underwriting matters, because you will not know which one closes until diligence is done.

Loan Options for a Replacement Property

The lender that fits depends on the property type, the business plan and how much time is left. These are the main sources, with the situations where each tends to fit.

Banks and credit unions

Local and regional banks often offer competitive terms on stabilized properties and may price relationships well, but their credit committees set the pace. They fit when you have time and a stabilized asset. They are harder to rely on when the identification period is nearly over.

Life insurance companies

Life companies tend to lend on high quality, stabilized properties with long fixed rate terms. Underwriting is thorough, so they suit exchanges where you identified the property early and have most of the 180 days left. Read more on our life company loans page.

Agency and HUD loans for multifamily

Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac and FHA programs apply to qualifying multifamily properties. They offer long terms and competitive pricing, and their processes are structured and take time. See agency loans.

CMBS conduit loans

Conduit loans suit stabilized properties across several asset types and are priced off the capital markets. They carry a defined process and documentation, so confirm the closing schedule against your deadline early. See CMBS loans.

Debt funds and private bridge lenders

Bridge capital underwrites the property and the plan rather than waiting on a conventional credit committee, so it can fit tight timelines or properties that are not yet stabilized. It costs more than permanent debt and is meant to be refinanced. See bridge loans.

Bridge to Permanent: A Backstop, Not a Plan

A short-term bridge loan can protect an exchange when the best permanent loan cannot fund before day 180. The bridge closes inside the window, and the investor refinances into permanent debt after the exchange is complete. The trade off is a higher cost and a second closing, and the lender will want to see a credible path to the permanent takeout before it funds. For that reason we treat a bridge as a backstop. On most exchanges we run, the goal is to close the permanent loan on time, and the bridge is the contingency if a lender slips. If you want the bridge details, our 1031 exchange bridge loans page covers structure, and bridge to permanent loans and bridge loan vs permanent refinance explain how the two stages compare.

Debt Replacement: Sizing the New Loan

The common rule of thumb that tax advisors apply is that to defer all of the gain, you buy replacement property of equal or greater value, reinvest all of the net proceeds, and carry equal or greater debt, or add cash to make up any shortfall. A debt shortfall without added cash can create what advisors call mortgage boot, which may be taxable. Whether that applies to your exchange is a question for your tax advisor. The financing side is simple arithmetic you can run early.

Here is an illustration with round numbers (not a quote and not a tax calculation):

  • You sell a property for $4,000,000 and pay off $1,500,000 of debt at closing. Roughly $2,500,000 of net proceeds goes to the intermediary, before closing costs.
  • You identify a $5,000,000 replacement property. Using all of the $2,500,000 as equity leaves a $2,500,000 loan, which is more than the $1,500,000 you paid off, so there is no debt shortfall in this example.
  • If you used a $1,000,000 loan instead and funded the rest with other cash, you would have replaced $1,000,000 against $1,500,000 paid off, a $500,000 difference your advisor would need to review.

The practical point for the lender side: the loan amount a property will support depends on its income, value and the lender's coverage and leverage limits. If the replacement property cannot carry the debt you need to replace, you want to learn that before you identify, not in underwriting with days left. Our loan calculator and cash on cash calculator help test the numbers.

What Lenders Ask For on an Exchange Purchase

An exchange purchase looks like an acquisition loan with a few extra documents. Expect a lender to ask for:

  • The purchase contract and any assignment of it to the intermediary, so the lender understands how the funds will arrive.
  • The exchange agreement with your qualified intermediary, and confirmation of how much the intermediary will wire at closing.
  • The settlement statement from the property you sold, showing the debt paid off.
  • Borrower entity documents and, where the vesting is different from the seller entity, an explanation your advisor has signed off on.
  • The usual property package: rent roll, operating statements, leases, plus third party reports (appraisal, environmental, survey and title).

Lenders also want to see equity coming from a source they can verify. Exchange proceeds held by an intermediary are usually verified with the exchange agreement and a wire confirmation.

Net Lease (NNN) Replacement Properties

Net lease properties are among the replacement assets exchange investors look at, because a single tenant lease can offer predictable income with little operating work. They are also underwritten differently from multi-tenant property. A net lease lender focuses on the tenant and the lease: the credit of the tenant or guarantor, how many years remain on the lease, rent steps, and what happens to the value if the tenant leaves. Banks, life companies, CMBS lenders and specialty net lease lenders each look at those points differently, so the right lender for a long lease to a strong tenant is not the right lender for a short lease to a local operator. For a deeper look, see our net lease underwriting guide and net lease financing page. If you are moving from an operating asset into a net lease property, tell your broker early, because the lender list changes materially.

Five Questions to Answer Before You Identify

  1. How much debt do I need to replace? Pull the settlement statement figure and size the target loan from it.
  2. Which two or three candidates could realistically close? Ask a broker to screen them before the identification deadline, not after.
  3. Does the lender have enough time? Ask for a closing schedule that works backward from day 180, including third party reports.
  4. What is my fallback? Decide in advance whether a bridge loan is acceptable if permanent financing is late.
  5. Has my advisor reviewed the structure? Entity vesting, any reverse or improvement structure, and any partial DST allocation should be reviewed before the loan is documented.

If your plan is to acquire the replacement before selling, that is a reverse exchange and the financing works differently. See our companion guide on reverse 1031 exchange financing. For the rules themselves, our 1031 exchange rules guide and the 1031 exchange glossary entry cover the basics.

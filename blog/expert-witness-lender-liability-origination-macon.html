Expert Witness: Commercial Loan Origination Standards in Macon, GA

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Lender Liability and Loan Origination Standards

The Macon Market Context

Macon, Georgia sits at the geographic center of the state, and that position is not merely cartographic. The Macon-Bibb County market functions as a logistics and distribution hub serving both the Atlanta metro corridor to the north and the Savannah port complex to the southeast via I-16. For commercial real estate lenders, underwriters, and the attorneys who later scrutinize their decisions, this positioning creates a market with distinct characteristics that differ materially from Atlanta suburban submarkets and from smaller Georgia tertiary markets like Valdosta or Tifton.

Industrial is the headline asset class in Macon right now. Vacancy in the Macon industrial submarket has remained below 6% in recent years, driven by third-party logistics tenants, regional distribution operators, and light manufacturing that has migrated out of higher-cost Georgia markets. Asking rents for Class B industrial have ranged from $4.50 to $6.50 per square foot NNN depending on clear height, dock configuration, and proximity to I-75 interchange access. Class A bulk distribution space commands rents closer to $7.00 to $8.50 per square foot NNN. These are not Atlanta rents, and any lender or broker who applied Atlanta-calibrated underwriting assumptions to a Macon industrial asset in 2021 through 2024 created a material risk of error, whether too aggressive or too conservative, that becomes the subject of litigation.

Multifamily fundamentals in Macon tell a different story. The market absorbed meaningful new supply between 2019 and 2023, and that supply pressure, combined with income constraints in the underlying renter population, kept cap rates elevated relative to comparable Sun Belt markets. Stabilized multifamily properties in Macon trade in the 6.75% to 8.25% cap rate range depending on vintage, location within Bibb County, and tenant income profile. A lender who benchmarked a Macon apartment acquisition against Southeast multifamily cap rates broadly, rather than against actual Macon comparable transactions, would have underwritten to an inflated value, producing a loan sized on incorrect market data. That is the type of error that generates lender liability claims.

Office fundamentals in Macon reflect national trends amplified by the modest size of the local professional services economy. Downtown Macon has experienced a modest revitalization around Cherry Street and the Mercer University Law School corridor, but suburban office vacancy remains elevated. Any commercial mortgage that was originated against Macon office collateral after 2020 deserves scrutiny regarding how the lender analyzed lease rollover risk, the depth of the local tenant demand pool, and what terminal cap rate assumptions were embedded in the underwriting model.

The Engagement

In the matter that frames this analysis, counsel for the defendant, a commercial mortgage brokerage firm, retained Commercial Lending Solutions to provide expert testimony regarding industry standards of care in commercial loan origination. The underlying asset was a stabilized mixed-use retail and office property located in a Macon submarket. The borrower had alleged that the broker misrepresented the achievable loan terms, failed to adequately disclose the credit risks that would affect loan sizing, and placed the borrower with a lender whose loan structure was unsuitable for the borrower's stated business plan.

The core legal and financial questions we were retained to address included: whether the broker's pre-application representations about loan terms, proceeds, and rate were consistent with what a reasonably competent commercial mortgage broker would have communicated given market conditions at the time; whether the lender's underwriting methodology, specifically its income analysis and DSCR calculation, complied with industry standards for mixed-use collateral in a tertiary market; whether the loan structure, including its prepayment provisions and recourse carve-outs, was disclosed in a manner consistent with professional practice; and whether the loan, as ultimately originated, was appropriate for the stated use of proceeds and exit strategy the borrower had communicated to the broker.

These questions require a working expert, not an academic one. They require someone who has personally negotiated term sheets, structured loans around problematic collateral, and sat across the table from credit committees at community banks, regional banks, debt funds, and life companies. That operational foundation is what CLS CRE brings to engagements of this type.

The Financial Analysis

The financial analysis in a loan origination standards case begins with reconstructing the underwriting as it existed at the time of origination, not as the collateral performs today. This is a critical methodological point. A property that underperforms after closing does not automatically establish that the original underwriting was deficient. Conversely, a property that has performed adequately does not insulate a lender from a claim that its origination process violated industry standards.

For the mixed-use asset in this engagement, we began by analyzing the effective gross income used in the lender's underwriting model. The subject property generated gross scheduled rent of approximately $610,000 annually across its retail and office tenants. The lender applied a vacancy and credit loss factor of 5%, producing effective gross income of approximately $579,500. In a Macon mixed-use context, that vacancy assumption warranted scrutiny. Retail vacancy in Macon suburban submarkets has ranged from 8% to 12% in recent years, and office vacancy has run higher still. A blended vacancy factor of 5% on a mixed-use asset in this market understated the risk meaningfully. Industry standard for this collateral type in this market required a vacancy assumption of at least 8% to 10% at the time of origination, which would have reduced effective gross income to a range of approximately $549,000 to $561,000.

Operating expenses were underwritten at $178,000 annually, producing a stated net operating income of $401,500. Our analysis, using market-calibrated vacancy and independently verified expense ratios for comparable Macon properties, produced an adjusted NOI of approximately $362,000 to $371,000. That difference, roughly $30,000 to $40,000 in NOI, may appear modest in isolation. Applied through a DSCR model, it is decisive.

The lender originated the loan at a 6.75% fixed rate on a 25-year amortization schedule with a 10-year term. At a loan amount of $3,850,000, the annual debt service was approximately $313,400. The lender's stated DSCR was 1.28x, calculated against its $401,500 NOI figure. Industry standard DSCR thresholds for mixed-use commercial properties in tertiary markets typically run between 1.25x and 1.35x at origination, with most conventional lenders requiring 1.25x minimum and many community and regional bank lenders requiring 1.30x or better when the collateral includes office component exposure. At the lender's own stated numbers, the loan was marginally compliant at 1.28x. Against our adjusted NOI of $365,000, the DSCR falls to approximately 1.16x, which is below the threshold any reasonably competent lender should have accepted for this collateral type in this market.

The maximum supportable loan at a 1.25x DSCR, using our adjusted NOI and the same rate and amortization, was approximately $3,340,000 to $3,420,000. The loan as originated was $3,850,000, representing an origination that exceeded supportable debt capacity by approximately $430,000 to $510,000 under properly calibrated underwriting assumptions. The appraised value was $5,600,000, implying a loan-to-value ratio of approximately 68.8%. LTV alone appeared conservative. But DSCR and LTV must be analyzed in tandem; a loan can satisfy LTV requirements and simultaneously violate DSCR standards if income is overstated. That is precisely what occurred here.

The Complicating Factors

Several factors specific to this deal type and the Macon market complicated the underwriting analysis and are directly relevant to the liability question.

Mixed-Use Income Bifurcation. When a property contains both retail and office components, sophisticated lenders apply differentiated vacancy and credit assumptions to each income stream rather than blending them. Office leases in Macon have shorter effective remaining terms and a shallower pool of replacement tenants than retail. A lender that blended the vacancy and rollover risk across the entire rent roll without segregating the office component from the retail component was applying an analytical shortcut that understates risk. Industry standard practice for mixed-use assets requires separate income and expense modeling for each use type, with the blended result stress-tested at multiple occupancy scenarios.

Lease Rollover Concentration. In this engagement, two tenants representing approximately 61% of total gross rent had lease expirations within 24 months of the origination date. The lender's underwriting treated these leases as in-place income for the full underwriting period without building in a rollover reserve or applying a shadow vacancy factor to reflect the re-leasing risk. For a Macon mixed-use asset with that rollover concentration, a reasonably competent lender would have either required a lease-up reserve funded at closing, applied a haircut to the income from those specific tenants equal to a market downtime assumption, or imposed a loan covenant requiring escrow of excess cash flow until leases were renewed or replaced. None of those structures were implemented.

Broker Representation vs. Lender Obligation. One of the most contested issues in loan origination standards cases is the boundary between what the broker is responsible for communicating and what the lender is independently obligated to verify. In Georgia, and under general industry standards, a commercial mortgage broker owes a duty of reasonable competence to the borrower it represents. That duty includes accurately characterizing the financing market, not misrepresenting achievable loan proceeds or rate, and ensuring that term sheet conditions are explained clearly before a borrower commits to a rate lock deposit or due diligence expenditure. The broker in this matter had communicated, in writing, a loan amount projection that exceeded what the market would ultimately support. Whether that projection was negligent or merely optimistic is a fact-specific question, but the standard against which it is measured is clear: what would a reasonably competent commercial mortgage broker operating in the Georgia secondary market have projected, given the information available at the time.

Prepayment Structure Disclosure. The loan carried a step-down prepayment penalty of 5-4-3-2-1 over a five-year lockout period. The borrower's stated business plan involved a potential sale within 36 months of origination. A competent broker who understood the borrower's exit strategy was obligated to flag the prepayment structure as a material constraint on that exit. Documentation in the file did not reflect any such communication prior to closing. That gap in the file is significant to the liability analysis.

The Macon Lender Market

Understanding which lender categories are active in Macon, and what each requires, is essential both to underwriting analysis and to evaluating whether a broker exercised reasonable market judgment in placing a particular loan.

Community and Regional Banks. Macon has meaningful community bank depth. Several Georgia-chartered community banks with strong Bibb County and Middle Georgia market presence are active in commercial real estate lending for mixed-use, retail, multifamily, and industrial collateral up to approximately $5 million to $8 million per loan. These lenders generally require personal guarantees, operating accounts, and existing deposit relationships. They underwrite to 1.25x DSCR minimum, prefer 70% to 75% LTV on stabilized commercial properties, and price at spreads over the prime rate or relevant Treasury index. Their credit boxes are conservative but their local market knowledge is often superior to out-of-state lenders, making them an appropriate placement option for most Macon commercial properties that meet basic eligibility criteria.

Regional and Super-Regional Banks. Several regional bank platforms with Georgia footprints are active in Macon for larger transactions, typically $5 million and above. These lenders apply more standardized national underwriting overlays and may impose more stringent tenant credit requirements on retail and office leases. They are appropriate for well-leased, institutionally managed properties but are less flexible on collateral with lease rollover concentration or mixed-use income complexity.

CMBS and Conduit Lenders. CMBS execution is available in Macon for assets that can support loan amounts above approximately $2 million to $3 million and meet minimum DSCR and LTV thresholds. CMBS lenders apply rigid underwriting standards with no relationship flexibility, making them appropriate for stabilized single-tenant or credit-tenanted retail and industrial assets but generally inappropriate for office-heavy or mixed-use collateral with near-term rollover risk. A broker who placed a Macon mixed-use loan with rollover concentration into a CMBS execution without fully disclosing the prepayment and modification restrictions inherent in that structure failed a basic duty of competence.

Debt Funds and Bridge Lenders. For transitional assets, value-add business plans, or properties that do not qualify for conventional financing, debt funds and bridge lenders are active in Macon. These lenders price at higher rates, generally SOFR plus 300 to 500 basis points on transitional collateral, and typically require interest reserves funded at closing. They are appropriate when the business plan includes a lease-up, renovation, or repositioning phase, but their use for stabilized collateral acquisition is a red flag that warrants scrutiny in a liability analysis.

What Attorneys Handling Macon CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to Retain a CRE Financing Expert. If your case involves any of the following, you need a CRE financing expert, not just an appraiser: a claim that loan proceeds were too high or too low relative to supportable debt capacity; a claim that rate, amortization, or loan structure was misrepresented; a question of whether a lender's underwriting methodology complied with industry standards; a dispute about whether a mortgage broker met its duty of care to a borrower or lender client; or a bankruptcy case involving the feasibility of a refinancing as part of a reorganization plan.

What a CRE Financing Expert Can Answer That an Appraiser Cannot. Appraisers establish value. CRE financing experts establish what the debt markets would have done with that value. These are related but distinct questions. An appraiser can tell you what the property was worth. A CRE financing expert can tell you what loan a reasonably competent lender would have made on that property, at what rate, on what terms, subject to what conditions, and whether the loan that was actually originated deviated from that standard in a way that caused harm. Appraisers are not trained in lender credit policy, DSCR underwriting methodology, loan structuring, or market placement practice. These are the domains of the CRE financing expert.

What Makes a CRE Financing Expert Qualified. Qualification is not measured by years of experience alone. A retired community bank loan officer who spent a career doing local real estate loans is not the same as an expert who has personally originated, brokered, and closed commercial loans across multiple asset types, capital stack positions, and geographic markets. Look for an expert who has active deal flow, current lender relationships, and a track record of working with sophisticated capital sources across the full spectrum of lender categories. At CLS CRE, we have closed approximately $1 billion in transaction volume with active lender relationships across the full market spectrum. That operational currency is what makes expert testimony credible on cross-examination.

What Counsel Should Gather Before Engaging the Expert. Before retaining us, collect the following: the full loan application package including all borrower financial statements submitted; the lender's internal credit memorandum or underwriting summary; the appraisal and any environmental or property condition reports; all written communications between the broker and borrower regarding loan terms, proceeds, and timeline; the executed loan commitment letter and final loan documents; and any post-closing default notices, workout correspondence, or modification agreements. The quality of the expert analysis depends on the completeness of the file.

How CRE Financing Expert Testimony Is Used. At trial, CRE financing expert testimony typically addresses the standard of care issue directly and provides the factual foundation for damages calculation by establishing what the borrower could have obtained from the market under a properly conducted origination. In bankruptcy proceedings, CRE financing expert testimony is used to establish whether a proposed refinancing under a plan of reorganization is feasible given current lender market conditions, a question that directly affects plan confirmation.

Key Takeaways

  • Macon-specific market data matters. Underwriting a Macon commercial property using national averages or Atlanta comparable data is a methodological error. Industry standard requires market-calibrated vacancy, expense, and cap rate assumptions specific to the Macon submarket and asset type.

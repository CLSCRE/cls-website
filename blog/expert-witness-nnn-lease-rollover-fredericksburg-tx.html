Expert Witness: NNN Lease Rollover Upside and Financing Impact in Fredericksburg, TX

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Below-Market NNN Lease and Rollover Upside Analysis

The Fredericksburg Market Context

Fredericksburg, Texas sits at the intersection of two powerful demand drivers that make it a genuinely distinctive commercial real estate market: a tourism economy that generates among the highest per-capita visitor spending of any small city in the state, and a surrounding Hill Country land market that has attracted significant second-home and hospitality investment over the past decade. For attorneys handling single-tenant NNN disputes, this context matters directly. The commercial leasing market in Fredericksburg is not a traditional suburban retail corridor; it is a destination-dependent market where in-place rents on NNN properties are frequently a function of who the tenant is, when the lease was signed, and what the competitive landscape looked like at that moment in time. A NNN lease signed in 2019 on a standalone retail building along the primary commercial corridor looks very different from one signed in 2022, when tourism-fueled demand had compressed cap rates and pushed asking rents materially higher across food-and-beverage, specialty retail, and service uses.

The retail vacancy rate along Fredericksburg's primary commercial corridor has historically remained tight, generally tracking below 5% for well-located single-tenant space. The market's fundamental driver is not population density by conventional metrics but rather visitor volume, which the Fredericksburg Convention and Visitors Bureau has consistently reported at more than 1.3 million annual visitors. That visitor traffic supports retail rents that would be anomalously high relative to the market's roughly 12,000-person permanent population base. Lenders who underwrite NNN assets in this market have to grapple with that dynamic directly: the supportable rent is not what you would derive from a standard market rent analysis of comparable cities of similar size, but neither is it as stable as a rent supported by a dense, economically diversified urban population. When an in-place NNN lease is below current market, the question of what "market" actually means in Fredericksburg requires specific, local analysis. That is not a question a lender's automated underwriting system handles well, and it is not a question a generalist appraiser always answers with the precision that financing feasibility analysis requires.

The Engagement

Counsel for one of the parties in a commercial real estate dispute retained Commercial Lending Solutions to analyze the financing feasibility of a single-tenant NNN property in the Fredericksburg submarket. The asset was a freestanding retail building occupied by a single non-credit-rated tenant under a long-term NNN lease with meaningful term remaining, but at a rental rate that counsel's client contended was materially below current market. The dispute involved disagreements about asset value, the economic impact of the below-market lease, and the ability of the property to support institutional-quality financing at specific LTV and DSCR thresholds that had been referenced in transaction documents and competing expert reports.

The specific questions we were asked to address fell into three categories. First, how do commercial mortgage lenders actually underwrite a NNN property where the in-place rent is below market, and does the existence of rollover upside at lease expiration improve or impair the property's current financeable value? Second, what loan amount, at market-standard DSCR and LTV constraints, could the property support based on its in-place income stream, and how does that compare to what it could support if underwritten at current market rents? Third, given the specific lease structure, including term remaining, renewal options, and rent escalation provisions, what refinancing constraints would a lender impose, and are those constraints consistent with or different from what the opposing party's expert had characterized as available financing?

We were retained to produce a written expert report under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 26(a)(2), setting out our methodology, assumptions, and conclusions in a form suitable for use in litigation. Our role was not to value the property; an MAI appraiser addressed that question separately. Our role was to address the financing market, which is a distinct discipline from appraisal and one that requires different expertise.

The Financial Analysis

The starting point for any NNN financing analysis is the in-place net operating income. In this engagement, the property generated a contractual base rent that, after accounting for NNN expense pass-throughs and a minimal management fee assumption of approximately 2% to 3% of effective gross income, produced an in-place NOI of approximately $118,000 annually. This figure was not disputed; both parties agreed on the current cash flow. The dispute centered on how lenders treat that income stream and whether the below-market nature of the rent affects the loan sizing analysis.

For a single-tenant NNN property of this type in a tertiary market like Fredericksburg, conventional commercial mortgage lenders apply a DSCR constraint of 1.25x at a minimum, with many institutional lenders requiring 1.30x given the single-tenant concentration risk. The 1.25x threshold is market standard for income-producing commercial real estate across most lender categories, but the single-tenant structure elevates the effective underwriting threshold because there is no income diversification across multiple tenants. If the one tenant vacates, the NOI drops to zero. That risk is priced into the required DSCR.

Using a note rate of 7.25%, which was consistent with the market pricing environment applicable to the relevant period based on 10-year Treasury yields plus typical credit spreads for this property type and market tier, and a standard 25-year amortization schedule, the annual debt service per $100,000 of loan balance is approximately $8,640. To satisfy a 1.25x DSCR against an NOI of $118,000, the maximum supportable annual debt service is $94,400. Dividing that figure by the per-dollar annual payment factor yields a maximum supportable loan amount of approximately $1,092,000, rounded to $1.09 million.

The second constraint is LTV. For single-tenant NNN retail in a tertiary Texas market with a non-credit tenant, most conventional lenders cap LTV at 65% to 70%. CMBS execution on a loan of this size is generally unavailable; the loan is too small for most conduit programs, which typically require a minimum balance of $2 million to $5 million depending on the originator. Assuming a lender-determined value of $1.75 million for the property, which was within the range established by the appraiser, a 65% LTV constraint produces a maximum loan of $1.1375 million. In this case, the DSCR constraint and the LTV constraint produced nearly identical maximum loan amounts, which is analytically useful: neither constraint was artificially binding the other, meaning the financing limitation was genuinely a function of the in-place income, not merely a collateral value issue.

The rollover upside analysis was central to the dispute. The property's in-place rent was approximately 22% below what current market evidence supported for comparable NNN space in the Fredericksburg corridor. At market rent, the stabilized NOI would have been approximately $144,000. Run through the same DSCR and rate assumptions, a market-rent NOI supports a loan of approximately $1.33 million, representing an incremental $240,000 in loan capacity. The critical finding, however, was that no conventional lender will underwrite to projected future rents on a lease that has not yet rolled. Lenders underwrite to in-place income. The rollover upside is real economically, but it does not exist in the present-tense financing market. It becomes financeable only after the lease expires, the space is re-leased at market, and the new lease has been executed and, in many cases, the tenant has taken occupancy and begun paying rent for a seasoning period that lenders typically set at three to six months.

The Complicating Factors

The Non-Credit Tenant Problem. The tenant in this engagement was not an investment-grade credit entity, a national franchise with audited financials, or a publicly traded company. It was a local or regional operator whose creditworthiness could not be independently verified through public financial data. This is common in Fredericksburg, where many of the most successful retail and hospitality businesses are privately held operators rather than national brands. Lenders treat the non-credit tenant as a significant underwriting challenge. The NNN structure means operating expense protection for the landlord, but if the tenant fails, the property reverts to a vacant shell in a market where replacement tenants, while available, may take 12 to 18 months to identify, negotiate with, and execute a new lease. Lenders price this risk through higher DSCR requirements, lower LTV caps, and in some cases through personal guaranty requirements on the loan itself, even though the NNN lease does not carry a personal guaranty from the tenant.

Lease Term Remaining and Refinancing Cliff Risk. A NNN property with a short remaining lease term presents what lenders call "cliff risk," meaning the income stream supporting the loan terminates at a defined future date. Lenders who originate 10-year loans on properties with 4 to 6 years of lease term remaining are underwriting to a refinancing scenario in which the property may be partially or fully vacant at the time of loan maturity. Most conventional lenders, including regional banks and life companies, require that the lease term extend beyond the loan maturity date by a minimum of 12 to 24 months. Where the lease term does not meet this test, lenders either decline to lend, shorten the loan term to align with the lease, or impose cash flow reserves that effectively reduce net loan proceeds. In this engagement, the lease term remaining relative to the requested loan term was a direct issue in the financing feasibility analysis, and the interaction between lease rollover timing and loan maturity was one of the primary factors limiting the available lender universe.

Renewal Options and Their Underwriting Treatment. The lease included tenant renewal options at rents specified in the lease, which were below the current contractual rent due to favorable option pricing negotiated at lease inception. Some parties to a dispute instinctively assume that renewal options are positive for financing because they represent continued occupancy. Lenders do not share that view uniformly. If the renewal option is at a rent lower than the current passing rent, a lender who underwrites to the renewal scenario is underwriting to a lower NOI and therefore a smaller loan. If the renewal option is at market, lenders will discount the probability of exercise based on market conditions at the time of renewal. A tenant paying below-market rent has strong economic incentive to renew; a tenant paying at or above market has less incentive. The option structure in this lease, combined with the below-market in-place rent, created a scenario where the most likely renewal outcome from the lender's perspective was continued occupancy at rents that were favorable to the tenant and marginally constraining to loan sizing.

Market Liquidity and Loan Sizing Floor. The absolute loan amount supportable by this property, approximately $1.09 million to $1.14 million, placed it in a market segment with limited institutional lender participation. Life insurance companies, CMBS platforms, and agency programs do not actively compete for loans of this size in tertiary Texas markets. The execution falls to community banks, regional banks, and credit unions, lenders whose pricing and structure differ materially from what institutional borrowers expect. This is not a problem unique to this deal, but it is a problem that directly affects the quality and cost of available capital, which in turn affects the economics of the asset and the conclusions that can be drawn about its financing feasibility.

The Fredericksburg Lender Market

The active lender market for NNN commercial properties in Fredericksburg operates at two distinct tiers. The first tier consists of Texas-chartered community banks and regional banks with Gillespie County or broader Hill Country footprints. These institutions understand the Fredericksburg market's tourism-driven demand dynamics, have local credit officers who can evaluate the submarket context intelligently, and are generally willing to underwrite single-tenant NNN assets for borrowers with established relationships or strong personal financial profiles. Their typical credit parameters for this asset class include 65% LTV, 1.25x DSCR, and note rates priced at prime plus a spread or at fixed rates tied to the 5-year Treasury, depending on the institution's balance sheet position. Loan terms are typically 5 to 7 years with 20 to 25 year amortization, and personal guaranties are standard.

The second tier consists of regional banks with Texas-wide or Southwest-regional platforms that lend on commercial real estate in markets like Fredericksburg as part of a broader portfolio strategy. These lenders apply more standardized underwriting criteria and are less likely to make qualitative adjustments for the Fredericksburg tourism premium. They are, however, more likely to offer non-recourse structures for larger, better-capitalized borrowers, and their secondary market access can occasionally create pricing advantages for strong credits. For the loan size range at issue in this engagement, these lenders represent marginal, not core, execution.

CMBS, life company, and debt fund capital is effectively unavailable for assets in the $1 million to $2 million loan range in Fredericksburg. This is a market structure reality, not a function of property quality. The minimum loan sizes for most institutional debt programs, combined with the tertiary market location, exclude the majority of Fredericksburg NNN assets from institutional lending channels. This has direct implications for the financing feasibility opinions that can appropriately be offered in litigation involving these assets. An expert who cites institutional lender underwriting standards as the applicable benchmark for a Fredericksburg NNN loan in this size range is applying the wrong lender universe.

What Attorneys Handling Fredericksburg CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to Retain a CRE Financing Expert. An appraiser values property. A CRE financing expert analyzes how the debt markets respond to that property, what loan terms are available, what constraints apply, and whether a specific financing outcome is achievable or was achievable at a relevant point in time. These are different questions that require different expertise. Attorneys should retain a CRE financing expert whenever the dispute involves contested loan proceeds, financing feasibility as a condition of a transaction, the economic impact of lease structure on financeable value, the standard of care for a lender or mortgage broker, or any situation where a party claims a specific loan was or was not available in the market at a specific time.

What a Financing Expert Answers That an Appraiser Cannot. Appraisers determine market value using accepted valuation methodologies. They do not typically opine on whether a specific LTV and DSCR combination is achievable in the current lender market, which lender categories are active for a given asset type in a given market, how a specific lease structure affects loan sizing calculations, or what a reasonable borrower should have expected to pay for capital given the asset's characteristics. These are capital markets questions that require current, active engagement with lenders across multiple product types and geographies. Our firm maintains relationships with more than 1,000 lenders and has closed approximately $1 billion in transaction volume across 50 states; that market presence is what enables credible, current opinions on financing feasibility.

Qualifying the Expert. A retired banker is not necessarily a qualified CRE financing expert witness. The lending market changes continuously, and opinions about what lenders do must be grounded in current market knowledge, not historical experience. A qualified expert should be actively engaged in commercial mortgage origination or advisory work, should have demonstrable experience with the specific asset class at issue, and should be able to point to recent comparable transactions that validate the market opinions being offered. Curriculum vitae credentials matter less than current deal flow and lender market access.

Information Counsel Should Gather Before the Expert Engagement. Before retaining a financing expert, counsel should secure: the complete lease document including all amendments, riders, and option provisions; rent rolls for the relevant time periods; any existing loan documents, appraisals, or lender correspondence; any broker opinions of value or financing proposals that were prepared in connection with the transaction; and the specific financing terms, if any, that are in dispute. The more complete the record at engagement, the more precise and defensible the expert's analysis will be.

Use at Trial and in Hearings. CRE financing expert testimony is most commonly used to establish: whether a specific loan was feasible given market conditions; what the reasonable expectation of a borrower or lender in a given situation would have been; what damages flow from financing being unavailable or more expensive than anticipated; and whether a party acted consistently with industry standards in the origination, underwriting, or placement of a commercial loan. In bench trials and bankruptcy plan hearings, a well-structured financing feasibility report can be dispositive on valuation disputes that turn on a property's ability to support institutional debt.

Key Takeaways

  • Lenders underwrite to in-place NOI, not projected post-rollover rents. Rollover upside is an equity story, not a financing story, until the new lease is executed and seasoned. Below-market in-place rent directly reduces current loan capacity, period.
  • The DSCR constraint and LTV constraint must both be analyzed independently. The binding constraint determines the maximum loan. In this engagement, both constraints produced nearly identical results, confirming the analysis was not being distorted by a single factor.
  • Single-tenant concentration risk elevates the effective DSCR requirement beyond the standard

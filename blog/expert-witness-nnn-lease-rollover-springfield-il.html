Expert Witness: NNN Lease Rollover Upside and Financing Impact in Springfield, IL

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Below-Market NNN Lease and Rollover Upside Analysis

The Springfield Market Context

Springfield, Illinois occupies a specific and often underestimated position in the Midwest commercial real estate landscape. As the state capital, it generates a demand profile that is structurally different from peer cities of similar population. Government employment, healthcare anchored by a major regional medical system, and higher education create a relatively stable but low-growth demand base for commercial space. That stability, however, cuts both ways in financing disputes: lenders value predictability, but they also discount markets where organic rent growth is muted and where tenant demand outside of government-adjacent uses is thin.

For single-tenant net lease assets specifically, Springfield presents a financing environment shaped by several concrete market realities. Retail vacancy in the primary commercial corridors has remained stubbornly elevated relative to pre-2020 levels, running in the range of 10 to 14 percent in some segments as of recent market data, with absorption driven heavily by healthcare-adjacent, dollar-store-format, and quick-service restaurant users rather than traditional retail anchors. This matters for underwriting because lenders assign re-tenanting assumptions based on observable demand pools, and a thinner demand pool translates directly into longer assumed downtime, higher tenant improvement reserves, and more conservative net operating income projections in a rollover scenario.

Industrial vacancy in Springfield is considerably tighter, reflecting broader Midwest distribution trends, but single-tenant industrial NNN deals in this market are underwritten against a regional lender base rather than institutional capital markets, which affects pricing and loan structure in ways I will address in detail. Office fundamentals are materially weaker: downtown office vacancy has continued to climb, and any NNN office dispute touching lease rollover risk in Springfield faces a particularly skeptical lender market. Medical office is the notable exception, where cap rates have compressed and lender appetite is more competitive.

Cap rates for stabilized single-tenant NNN retail assets in Springfield have generally ranged from approximately 6.5 to 8.0 percent depending on tenant credit, lease term, and location within the market. That range is meaningfully wider than primary and secondary Midwest markets like Chicago, Indianapolis, or Columbus, reflecting the thinner buyer pool and greater perceived illiquidity. From a financing dispute standpoint, that cap rate spread directly affects the denominator in any LTV calculation, and it shapes how aggressively a lender will underwrite toward rollover upside.

The Engagement

Counsel for one of the parties in a commercial real estate dispute retained Commercial Lending Solutions to provide a financing feasibility analysis in connection with a single-tenant NNN retail property located in the Springfield market. The asset was occupied by a single commercial tenant under a long-form triple-net lease, with the in-place rent established several years earlier at a rate that had become materially below current market levels. The lease had a defined remaining term with options, and the rollover upside, meaning the potential increase in net operating income if and when the lease rolled to market rent, was a central point of contention in the underlying dispute.

CLS CRE was retained by litigation counsel to answer a specific and technically demanding set of questions: How do institutional and regional lenders actually underwrite a below-market NNN lease? Does rollover upside improve loan sizing, and if so, by how much and under what conditions? Is there a loan structure available at the LTV and DSCR thresholds relevant to the dispute, and if financing is constrained, what is the source of that constraint, the asset, the market, the lease structure, or the lender environment? These are questions that sit squarely in the intersection of real estate finance and legal dispute, and they require a practitioner with active lender relationships and current deal experience, not simply historical familiarity with underwriting theory.

The core legal issue centered on whether a specific transaction or outcome was financially feasible during a defined historical window, and whether the lease structure as it existed was the proximate cause of any financing constraint or whether external market conditions were the controlling factor. Our analysis required reconstructing the lender market as it existed at the relevant time, applying current-period underwriting criteria, and issuing a reasoned opinion on maximum supportable debt, achievable LTV, and whether refinancing at terms material to the dispute was realistically available.

The Financial Analysis

The foundation of any NNN lease financing analysis is the in-place net operating income, and in a below-market lease scenario, the question of which NOI the lender will underwrite is not academic. It is the determinative variable in the loan sizing calculation. Lenders underwrite to contractual in-place rent, period. They do not size loans to proforma rent, projected rollover rent, or appraiser-estimated market rent unless very specific structural conditions are met, which I address in the complicating factors section.

For this engagement, the in-place annual rent on the subject NNN lease produced a gross revenue figure from which we deducted management fee (typically 3 to 5 percent of gross revenues even on a NNN lease for underwriting purposes), a modest reserve for replacement (lenders impose this even when the tenant is responsible for maintenance under the lease terms, as a credit discipline), and any landlord-side obligations remaining in the lease. The result was an underwritten NOI that represented the lender's actual starting point for debt service calculations.

To illustrate with plausible Springfield market numbers: assume an in-place NNN rent of $220,000 per year on a freestanding retail building of approximately 8,500 square feet, reflecting an in-place rent of roughly $25.88 per square foot. Market rent for comparable space in this submarket at the time of analysis was approximately $32.00 to $34.00 per square foot, creating a below-market gap of approximately $6.00 to $8.00 per square foot, or $51,000 to $68,000 annually in unrealized income. After standard underwriting deductions, the lender-underwritten NOI lands at approximately $207,000 to $210,000.

Applying a market-standard debt service coverage ratio of 1.25x (the most common threshold for retail NNN product among regional and community lenders; some CMBS platforms apply 1.20x but require stronger tenancy and market), the maximum annual debt service supportable by that NOI is approximately $165,600 to $168,000. At a 7.25 percent fixed rate on a 25-year amortization schedule (consistent with community and regional bank pricing in this market and rate environment), that debt service figure supports a loan of approximately $2.05 million to $2.09 million.

If the appraiser's estimated value for the asset at a 7.0 percent cap rate applied to market NOI is approximately $3.7 million, the maximum supportable loan of $2.07 million represents an effective LTV of approximately 56 percent, well inside the 65 to 70 percent LTV ceiling most lenders would allow on this asset type. This creates a situation where the LTV is not the binding constraint. The binding constraint is the in-place NOI, which produces insufficient debt service capacity to support a loan commensurate with the asset's market value.

This finding is central to many NNN lease financing disputes: the asset may appraise at a value that implies significant equity, but the actual loan available is capped by income, not by value. A party arguing that a larger loan was feasible, or that a specific refinancing was achievable, must contend with this income-first underwriting discipline.

The Complicating Factors

The rollover upside problem: The most common misunderstanding in NNN lease disputes is the assumption that lenders will give credit for rollover upside when sizing a loan. In practice, lenders do not underwrite to market rent unless there is a signed lease at that rent or a lender-approved re-tenanting plan with meaningful pre-leasing. An opinion of value that reflects rollover upside as a value driver is an appraiser's tool, not a lender's tool. The lender's underwriting is static: it reflects the income the asset generates today, under the lease that exists today. The gap between the appraisal value (which may incorporate rollover upside) and the loan amount (which does not) is a structural feature of NNN financing that counsel must understand precisely.

Lease term remaining and rollover timing: The remaining term on a below-market lease creates a two-sided problem. A short remaining term introduces re-tenanting risk, which most lenders in Springfield will price through a shortened loan term (often matching or slightly exceeding the lease term), a higher reserve requirement, or outright loan denial if the rollover scenario is uncertain. A long remaining term locks the lender into a below-market income stream for an extended period, limiting debt service capacity and frustrating refinancing at market value. Neither condition is cleanly favorable in a NNN financing context when rent is below market.

Tenant credit quality: Springfield lenders are particularly sensitive to tenant credit in single-tenant NNN deals because the market's secondary nature means that re-tenanting is genuinely difficult and demonstrably slower than in primary markets. A nationally-rated investment-grade tenant producing below-market rent will access better financing than a regional or local tenant producing identical rent, because the lender's downside scenario in a default is materially different. In disputes involving below-market leases, the tenant's credit profile is not just a yield consideration; it directly affects the lender's willingness to close.

Structural re-entry risk: Some NNN leases include tenant options to purchase, rights of first refusal, or early termination rights that affect a lender's collateral position. If the tenant has a below-market purchase option, a lender's ability to foreclose and sell at market value may be impaired. These provisions must be analyzed in any financing feasibility opinion, and their presence can effectively disqualify certain lender categories entirely.

Market depth and liquidity: Springfield is not a market where a lender can assume rapid disposition of a special-purpose or single-tenant asset. Lender discount for illiquidity is real and measurable. It typically manifests as a 25 to 50 basis point spread premium relative to comparable assets in stronger Illinois markets, and it tightens LTV by 5 to 10 percentage points for lenders who apply stressed disposition assumptions in their underwriting models.

The Springfield Lender Market

CLS CRE maintains relationships with more than 1,000 lenders nationwide, and in Springfield specifically, the financing universe for single-tenant NNN retail assets is meaningfully narrower than in major metropolitan markets. Understanding the categories of available capital and their respective credit boxes is essential to any financing feasibility opinion.

Community and regional banks: These are the most active and consistent lenders for NNN product in Springfield at loan sizes below $5 million. Illinois-chartered community banks and regional institutions with central Illinois footprints are comfortable with this asset type, know the local market, and can underwrite with more flexibility than national platforms. They typically apply 1.20x to 1.25x DSCR, 65 percent LTV on retail, 25-year amortization, and pricing ranging from prime plus 0.50 percent to prime plus 1.50 percent depending on credit structure. They are also the lenders most likely to impose a loan term coterminous with the lease term, which can create a mismatch between desired loan term and achievable loan term in a below-market lease scenario.

Life companies: Life company lending in Springfield is limited to the strongest credit tenants, larger loan sizes (generally $5 million and above in this market), and stabilized assets with long remaining lease terms. A below-market NNN lease would not typically qualify for life company consideration unless the tenant is investment-grade and the remaining term exceeds ten years. When they do engage, life companies offer the most competitive pricing and longest fixed-rate terms, but their credit screens eliminate most below-market lease scenarios at the outset.

CMBS: Conduit lending is available in Springfield for qualifying assets, but CMBS underwriting is formulaic and unforgiving. CMBS lenders apply strict NOI haircuts, reserve requirements, and they are indifferent to rollover upside or local market nuance. A below-market lease with a short remaining term will fail CMBS credit screens in virtually every case. The floor loan sizing and execution timelines also make CMBS inefficient for smaller Springfield deals.

Credit unions and SBA lenders: For owner-occupied or partially owner-occupied scenarios, SBA 504 and SBA 7(a) financing can be relevant, and certain credit unions have become active in central Illinois commercial real estate. These lenders are not typically relevant to pure investment NNN lease disputes but may be pertinent depending on the ownership and occupancy structure of the subject asset.

What Attorneys Handling Springfield CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to retain a CRE financing expert: Any dispute in which the feasibility, terms, or impact of commercial real estate financing is a material issue requires a financing expert, not just an appraiser. Appraisers determine value. Financing experts determine what a lender will actually lend, on what terms, under what conditions, and whether a specific financing outcome was achievable. These are different questions with different methodologies. In a below-market NNN lease dispute, the question is not simply what the asset is worth; it is whether the income it generates supports the financing that is claimed or at issue, and that is squarely a financing expert's domain.

Questions a financing expert can answer that an appraiser cannot: A qualified CRE financing expert can opine on maximum supportable debt at specific DSCR and LTV thresholds, whether a specific lender category would have engaged with this asset during a defined time period, how lenders actually treated rollover upside in their credit decisions (not in their valuations), what alternative loan structures were available and at what cost, and whether the lease structure itself was the binding constraint on financing or whether market conditions were the controlling factor. These are active lending practice questions, not valuation questions.

What qualifies a CRE financing expert: A retired banker with historical experience is not a substitute for an active commercial mortgage broker with current lender access, production volume, and documented transaction history. Expert testimony on financing feasibility should be grounded in current lender relationships and active deal experience, because underwriting standards, market appetite, and pricing change continuously. At CLS CRE, we have closed transactions in all 50 states with approximately $1 billion in transaction volume and access to more than 1,000 lender relationships. That active market presence is what allows us to provide credible, current-period financing opinions, not just historical narrative.

Information counsel should gather before the expert engagement: The complete executed lease with all amendments and exhibits; rent rolls for the trailing 24 months; historical operating statements or NNN reconciliation statements; any prior appraisals or broker opinions of value; any lender correspondence, term sheets, or prior loan applications; the property's survey, title commitment, and any recorded encumbrances or covenants; and any communications between the parties referencing financing terms, feasibility, or lender conversations. The richer the document set, the more precise the expert opinion.

Use of financing expert testimony: In trial, financing expert testimony is most effectively used to establish a precise ceiling on achievable loan proceeds, to rebut overreaching claims about financing feasibility, and to translate complex underwriting mechanics into a framework a judge or jury can apply. In bankruptcy plan confirmation hearings, financing expert testimony is central to feasibility findings under Section 1129(a)(11) where reorganization plan financing is at issue. In arbitration and mediation, a well-supported financing expert report often resolves financing-related valuation disputes without proceeding to hearing.

Key Takeaways

  • Lenders underwrite to in-place rent, not to market rent or rollover proforma. In a below-market NNN lease, the maximum supportable loan is capped by the contractual income stream, regardless of what the asset would generate at market rates. Rollover upside does not improve loan sizing under standard underwriting unless specific structural conditions are satisfied.
  • The binding constraint in many below-market NNN lease scenarios is income, not LTV. An asset may have substantial equity and still support only a fraction of its potential loan at market value, because the income-to-debt-service calculation limits proceeds before the LTV ceiling is reached. This distinction is critical in any dispute about financing feasibility.
  • Springfield's market depth affects financing on two levels: first, through lender-applied illiquidity discounts that tighten LTV and increase spread; and second, through the realistic re-tenanting assumptions lenders use when assessing rollover risk, which in a thinner market are longer timelines and higher costs than in primary markets.
  • A below-market lease with a short remaining term faces compounding headwinds: the current income is insufficient to support market-value financing, and the near-term rollover event introduces re-tenanting risk that further constrains lender appetite. Community and regional lenders in Springfield will often shorten loan term or decline to engage when lease rollover coincides with the proposed loan term.

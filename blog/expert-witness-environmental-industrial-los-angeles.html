Expert Witness: Financing a Contaminated Industrial Property in Los Angeles, CA

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Environmental Contamination Analysis

The Los Angeles Market Context

Los Angeles remains one of the most consequential industrial real estate markets in the United States, and understanding its current fundamentals is essential to any financing feasibility analysis involving an industrial asset in this region. The greater Los Angeles industrial market encompasses approximately 1.2 billion square feet across submarkets including the San Gabriel Valley, South Bay, Mid-Counties, and the Inland Empire gateway corridors that feed the port complex at San Pedro and Long Beach. As of 2024, the Los Angeles Basin industrial vacancy rate has risen from its historic lows of sub-1% during the post-pandemic logistics surge to a range of approximately 4% to 6% depending on submarket, with the Inland Empire experiencing slightly higher vacancy as speculative supply delivered into a cooling demand environment. Despite this correction, Los Angeles proper remains supply-constrained: land is scarce, entitlement timelines are measured in years rather than months, and replacement cost for functionally modern industrial product routinely exceeds $350 to $450 per square foot when accounting for land basis, construction cost, and soft costs.

This supply constraint has critical implications for lender underwriting. Even where a specific property carries an environmental stigma or a below-market lease, the scarcity of industrial land in Los Angeles means that a well-located warehouse asset retains residual land value that partially insulates lenders from downside scenarios. Asking rents for industrial space in core Los Angeles submarkets range from approximately $1.40 to $2.20 per square foot per month on a NNN basis, depending on clear height, dock configuration, and location relative to the freeway network. Properties with older vintage, limited truck court depth, or below-market leases in place trade at cap rates in the 5.25% to 6.50% range, while stabilized Class A logistics product with credit tenancy trades tighter. These figures matter directly to financing analysis because both debt service coverage ratios and loan-to-value constraints are calibrated against them.

The environmental dimension adds a layer of complexity that requires lenders to engage their internal environmental risk teams, and in many cases, outside environmental consultants. Los Angeles has a long industrial history, particularly in the southeast industrial corridor and portions of the San Fernando Valley, where solvent-based manufacturing, aerospace fabrication, electroplating, and chemical processing operations were prevalent for decades. Chlorinated volatile organic compounds, including perchloroethylene, trichloroethylene, and 1,4-dioxane, are among the most frequently encountered groundwater contaminants in these areas, and the California Department of Toxic Substances Control and the Los Angeles Regional Water Quality Control Board are active enforcement bodies in this jurisdiction.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by counsel representing secured creditors in a regulatory enforcement and commercial real estate dispute involving an industrial warehouse property located in the Los Angeles Basin. The asset is a multi-tenant, single-story industrial warehouse of approximately 85,000 square feet, 100% occupied under triple-net leases with a weighted average lease term of approximately four years remaining. The property had been used historically for industrial manufacturing operations, and a Phase II Environmental Site Assessment had confirmed the presence of chlorinated volatile organic compounds, specifically 1,4-dioxane, in soil and groundwater at concentrations exceeding California regulatory action levels. An active regulatory enforcement matter was ongoing with the applicable state agency at the time of our engagement.

Counsel retained CLS CRE to opine on two primary questions. First, does the presence of the confirmed environmental contamination and the active regulatory enforcement matter categorically preclude first-mortgage financing in the current lending market, or is financing available subject to conditions? Second, assuming financing is available to some degree, what is the maximum supportable loan amount under current market underwriting standards, and is that amount sufficient to retire the existing senior debt of record? The engagement required us to analyze the property's income stream, apply market-standard underwriting metrics, assess the lender universe for environmentally impaired industrial assets in the Los Angeles market, and render an opinion grounded in both our transactional experience and our knowledge of current lender credit standards.

Our opinion was prepared in conformity with Rule 26 of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure and was supported by our firm's approximately $1 billion in transaction volume, active relationships with more than 1,000 institutional and private lenders across 50 states, and direct transactional experience with environmentally impaired collateral across multiple asset classes and jurisdictions.

The Financial Analysis

The starting point for any financing feasibility analysis is the stabilized net operating income attributable to the collateral. In this engagement, the property was 100% leased on a NNN basis, which simplifies the income analysis in one respect: the landlord's operating expense exposure is minimal, and the in-place rent roll drives the NOI figure directly. However, the leases were executed at below-market rents, a common situation in properties that have carried environmental stigma through successive ownership transitions, where prior owners accepted below-market terms to maintain occupancy and avoid vacancy during remediation uncertainty.

We analyzed the in-place rent roll and determined that the weighted average in-place rent was approximately $0.98 per square foot per month on a NNN basis, representing a meaningful discount to the submarket's current asking rents of $1.55 to $1.70 per square foot per month for comparable vintage industrial product. On an 85,000 square foot building at 100% occupancy, the in-place gross revenue equated to approximately $998,400 annually. After deducting a 3% management fee and nominal reserves appropriate for a NNN structure, the stabilized NOI available for debt service was approximately $968,448. We did not credit any mark-to-market upside in our base case, consistent with how conservative lenders underwrite environmentally impaired assets: they underwrite the lease as written, not the lease as it might be re-priced upon rollover.

We applied a debt service coverage ratio threshold of 1.25x, which reflects the market standard for industrial assets carrying collateral risk factors beyond the norm. Conventional industrial financing in Los Angeles at a 1.20x DSCR is achievable for clean, stabilized assets with strong credit tenancy. For assets carrying environmental impairment, active regulatory matters, or below-market leases, lenders universally require a minimum 1.25x DSCR, and many lenders in this risk category will underwrite to 1.30x or higher. We used 1.25x as the floor, recognizing that our conclusion would be a ceiling, not a target.

For rate and amortization assumptions, we used a 7.25% all-in interest rate on a 25-year amortization schedule. This rate assumption reflects current market pricing for 5-year fixed-rate CMBS or bank financing on secondary-quality industrial collateral in the Los Angeles market as of mid-2024, incorporating a spread of approximately 200 to 225 basis points over the corresponding swap rate, plus an environmental risk premium of 50 to 75 basis points that lenders in this category typically layer in to compensate for remediation uncertainty and potential impairment of collateral value upon enforcement. The 25-year amortization is standard for industrial product; some lenders will require 20-year amortization on impaired assets to accelerate principal paydown and reduce exposure duration.

At a 7.25% rate on a 25-year amortization schedule, the constant is approximately 8.62% annually. Dividing the maximum allowable debt service, which is NOI divided by the DSCR floor ($968,448 / 1.25 = $774,758), by the annual loan constant (8.62%), produces a maximum supportable loan of approximately $8,989,000, which we rounded to $8,990,000 for our analysis. We then tested this loan amount against current market LTV constraints for environmentally impaired industrial assets. Lenders in this category will typically underwrite to a maximum of 55% to 60% LTV on appraised value, compared to the 65% to 70% LTV available on clean institutional industrial assets. At an implied appraised value of approximately $14.5 million to $15.5 million for this asset (derived from in-place NOI at a 6.25% to 6.50% cap rate), our maximum supportable loan represented approximately 58% to 62% LTV, consistent with the lender market's risk appetite for this collateral type. The analysis confirmed that financing was supportable, that the maximum loan amount was a function of income constraint rather than an absolute LTV barrier, and that the resulting loan figure was sufficient to retire the existing senior debt of record under the terms described in the litigation record.

The Complicating Factors

Active Regulatory Enforcement: The most significant complicating factor in this engagement was not the contamination itself but the active regulatory enforcement matter. Lenders draw a sharp distinction between a property with a closed remediation file and one with an open enforcement matter. When a state agency has issued a corrective action order or is actively requiring investigation and remediation, lenders face two risks: first, the remediation costs are uncertain in both magnitude and timeline, which creates a contingent liability that may attach to the property through environmental liens or cleanup orders; second, enforcement actions can result in use restrictions or deed restrictions that impair the property's utility, occupancy, and re-leasing potential. Most institutional lenders will not originate on a property subject to an active enforcement order without an environmental insurance policy, a funded remediation escrow, or a letter of no further action indicating that the regulatory pathway is defined and bounded. In this engagement, the remediation scope had been defined in a Remedial Action Plan accepted by the regulatory agency, which meaningfully narrowed the lender's uncertainty and opened the door to specialized lenders who require defined-scope remediation rather than closed remediation.

1,4-Dioxane as a Contaminant of Concern: 1,4-Dioxane presents specific challenges relative to more commonly encountered chlorinated VOCs because it is highly mobile in groundwater, does not readily biodegrade under standard conditions, and has a very low maximum contaminant level under California regulatory standards. This means that even modest concentrations require aggressive remediation, and plume containment timelines tend to be longer than for compounds like PCE or TCE, which are addressable through more conventional technologies. Lenders who have encountered 1,4-dioxane-contaminated properties previously understand that advanced oxidation processes are often required, and that remediation cost estimates carry a higher coefficient of variation than for more tractable compounds. We addressed this by presenting lenders with the remediation cost estimate prepared by the borrower's environmental consultant and the agency-approved Remedial Action Plan as evidence of a defined and budgeted remediation pathway.

Below-Market Leases and Rollover Risk: The combination of below-market in-place rents and the environmental stigma creates a compounding underwriting risk. Lenders underwriting a below-market lease must consider what happens at lease expiration: in a clean asset, a below-market lease rolling to market rents is a value-add event. In an environmentally impaired asset, a tenant may choose not to renew if the environmental condition has resulted in operational disruptions, regulatory scrutiny of the tenant's own operations, or reputational concerns. Lenders model the worst case, which is simultaneous lease expiration and re-leasing at a discount to market due to ongoing environmental stigma. This requires lenders to stress-test the NOI at below-market re-leasing assumptions and confirm that the loan remains serviceable under those scenarios.

Environmental Insurance Availability: Environmental impairment liability insurance and pollution legal liability coverage are available in the marketplace and are a standard tool for bridging the gap between a lender's risk tolerance and an impaired property's financing profile. However, premiums for 1,4-dioxane-impacted properties in California are elevated, terms are typically limited to five to ten years, and carriers require submission of the Phase II report, the Remedial Action Plan, and cost estimates before binding coverage. We identified several insurers active in this space and confirmed that coverage was obtainable, which was a necessary condition for lender engagement.

The Los Angeles Lender Market

The financing universe for an environmentally impaired industrial asset in Los Angeles is narrower than for a clean comparable, but it is not empty. Understanding which lenders are active, what each category requires, and how they price is essential to a financing feasibility opinion.

CMBS Conduit Lenders: Traditional conduit CMBS originators will generally not accept environmentally impaired collateral into a conduit pool. The pooling and servicing agreements governing most CMBS trusts either prohibit environmentally impaired collateral or require a Phase I with no recognized environmental conditions, which this asset would not satisfy. Single-asset, single-borrower CMBS structures are theoretically available for larger loan amounts but are not practical at the loan sizes relevant to this engagement.

Life Insurance Companies: Life company lenders in the Los Angeles market are active in industrial product and represent some of the most competitive pricing available for clean Class A logistics assets. They are uniformly unwilling to originate on environmentally impaired collateral. Their credit mandates require clean environmental reports, and their portfolio investment committees do not have the infrastructure to underwrite remediation risk.

Specialty Environmental Lenders and Bridge Lenders: This is the active universe for this asset type. A segment of private credit funds and specialty finance companies has developed underwriting expertise in environmentally impaired commercial real estate. These lenders typically offer floating-rate bridge loans with terms of two to five years, size loans to 55% to 60% LTV on as-is appraised value, require funded remediation reserves or environmental insurance, and price at 250 to 400 basis points over SOFR depending on remediation risk and loan size. In the Los Angeles market, several institutional bridge lenders maintain dedicated environmental underwriting capacity and have funded loans on chlorinated solvent-impacted industrial assets in the Los Angeles Basin within the past 24 months. This is the category we identified as the primary execution path for this engagement.

Community and Regional Banks: Los Angeles has a deep community bank market, and several community banks with industrial lending programs will consider environmentally impaired collateral on a case-by-case basis, particularly where the borrower has an existing relationship, the remediation scope is defined, and environmental insurance is in place. Pricing is typically more favorable than bridge lenders, with spreads of 175 to 275 basis points over prime or SOFR, but loan sizing is more conservative, and credit committee approval timelines are longer. This category represented a secondary execution path in our analysis.

What Attorneys Handling Los Angeles CRE Disputes Need to Know

Retaining a CRE financing expert is not the same as retaining an appraiser, and understanding the distinction is essential to building a complete evidentiary record in a secured creditor dispute, plan confirmation hearing, or lender liability case.

An appraiser determines value. A CRE financing expert determines whether financing is available, at what amount, on what terms, and under what conditions, and then renders opinions grounded in direct market experience executing transactions in the current lending environment. These are distinct questions, and courts and plan administrators frequently need answers to both. In a secured creditor dispute where the central question is whether a debtor could refinance and retire senior debt, an appraisal establishes the collateral value ceiling but does not tell you whether a lender would actually fund a loan against that collateral, what DSCR threshold would apply, what environmental conditions the lender would impose, or what the realistic loan amount would be after applying all market underwriting constraints. The CRE financing expert fills that gap.

When evaluating the qualifications of a CRE financing expert, attorneys should look beyond a candidate's biography as a former banker or real estate broker. The operative question is whether the expert is currently active in the lending market, has direct relationships with the categories of lenders relevant to the asset type, has closed transactions involving comparable collateral, and can speak to current market conditions from transactional experience rather than database research. CLS CRE's active engagement with 1,000-plus lenders across 50 states and approximately $1 billion in transaction volume positions our opinions as grounded in current market execution, not historical analysis.

Before retaining a CRE financing expert in a Los Angeles industrial dispute involving environmental contamination, counsel should gather: the full rent roll with lease abstracts and expiration dates; the Phase I and Phase II environmental reports; any remediation cost estimates or Remedial Action Plans accepted by the relevant agency; the current loan payoff demand or statement of senior debt; any existing appraisal of the property; and the property's operating history including historical NOI and capital expenditures. The earlier this package is assembled, the more efficiently the expert can render a supportable opinion.

At trial or in a plan confirmation hearing, CRE financing expert testimony is most effective when it is grounded in specific, verifiable market data: actual lender conversations, comparable transactions, and specific underwriting calculations presented step by step. Expert opinions that rely on general market commentary without specific transactional support are vulnerable to challenge. Our Rule 26 reports present methodology, inputs, assumptions, and calculations in a format that withstands cross-examination and provides the trier of fact with a clear analytical foundation.

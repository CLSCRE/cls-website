Expert Witness: CRE Financing Feasibility in Chapter 11 Proceedings in New Haven, CT

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Bankruptcy Exit Financing

The New Haven Market Context

New Haven is a more sophisticated commercial real estate market than its size suggests. The presence of Yale University creates a structural floor under demand for multifamily, life sciences, and mixed-use assets that insulates the market from the cyclical volatility you see in purely employment-driven secondary markets. That said, New Haven is not a monolith. The submarkets behave differently: Downtown and the Audubon Arts District carry different risk profiles than the industrial corridors along the Mill River or the suburban office stock in the outer ring. Any financing feasibility analysis that treats New Haven as a single market fails at the first level of credibility.

On the multifamily side, which is often the asset class at the center of Chapter 11 proceedings in this city, New Haven has seen sustained rent growth driven by undersupply, Yale-affiliated demand, and a graduate and medical workforce that creates a durable renter pool. Vacancy rates in stabilized Class B and Class C multifamily properties in New Haven have hovered in the 4 to 6 percent range, and effective rents have trended upward in the mid-single digits annually over the past several years. Cap rates for stabilized multifamily in New Haven have compressed into the 5.5 to 6.5 percent range for institutional-quality assets, with value-add plays trading wider depending on physical condition and lease-up risk.

For mixed-use and retail-anchored assets, the picture is more nuanced. Chapel Street and Broadway corridors benefit from pedestrian traffic tied to the university ecosystem, while secondary retail locations have experienced softening rents and elevated vacancy consistent with national trends. Office fundamentals in New Haven have followed the post-pandemic bifurcation: Class A medical office and life sciences properties remain in strong demand, while Class B general office has faced downward rent pressure and increased sublease availability. Industrial assets in New Haven, particularly last-mile logistics and flex properties near I-91 and I-95 interchange areas, have performed well, with vacancy tightening and rental rates climbing toward the low-to-mid teens per square foot on a net basis.

These market-specific dynamics matter enormously in a bankruptcy exit financing analysis. A property's supportable loan amount is a direct function of its income, and that income is valued by lenders against the backdrop of market conditions. A court relying on financing feasibility testimony needs an expert who can speak credibly to what New Haven lenders actually require for a given asset class, not what a generic underwriting model might suggest.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by counsel representing the plan proponent in a Chapter 11 proceeding involving a commercial real estate asset located in the New Haven urban core. The asset was a mixed-use property combining ground-floor retail with upper-floor residential units, a property type that is common in New Haven's dense urban neighborhoods and one that presents distinct underwriting considerations compared to pure-play asset classes.

The core questions presented to CLS CRE were as follows. First, could the property support first-mortgage financing in an amount sufficient to satisfy the allowed claims of the senior secured creditor and fund exit costs under the proposed Plan of Reorganization? Second, what categories of lenders actively originate loans on commercial real estate assets that are either in or recently exiting Chapter 11, and under what terms? Third, was the financing contemplated under the proposed plan commercially reasonable and achievable within the timeframe required for confirmation and plan consummation?

These are not questions an appraiser is retained to answer. An appraiser determines value. A financing expert determines whether debt capital is available against that value, on what terms, and from whom. Those are distinct disciplines, and courts increasingly recognize that distinction in complex Plan confirmation proceedings.

The Financial Analysis

The starting point for any financing feasibility analysis is net operating income, and establishing a credible NOI figure for a bankruptcy estate asset requires more diligence than a standard refinance scenario. In this engagement, the property had experienced income disruption during the bankruptcy period, which meant trailing twelve-month financials were partially distorted. CLS CRE's methodology was to reconstruct a stabilized NOI using current market rents for each unit type, applying a market vacancy assumption of 7 percent on the residential component and 10 percent on the retail component, which reflected the property's location and tenant mix relative to New Haven market benchmarks.

On the residential side, in-place rents were compared against comparable rental properties in the same submarket to assess whether rents were at, above, or below market. For the retail component, we analyzed current asking rents and executed lease comparables for ground-floor retail in the immediate area, accounting for the shorter weighted average lease term and re-tenanting risk that lenders apply to retail with near-term rollover. The result was a stabilized NOI of approximately $310,000 for purposes of this analysis, a figure that was supportable by market evidence and disclosed fully in our expert report.

With a stabilized NOI of $310,000, the maximum supportable debt service was derived by applying the debt service coverage ratio threshold that institutional lenders in this market require for a mixed-use asset with retail exposure. The market standard for this asset class in a secondary market like New Haven is 1.25x DSCR at a minimum; some lenders require 1.30x for assets with more than 20 percent retail component by gross leasable area. We applied 1.25x as the baseline, which is consistent with Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, and CMBS underwriting guidelines for mixed-use assets.

At 1.25x DSCR, the maximum allowable annual debt service is $248,000 ($310,000 divided by 1.25). Assuming a 30-year amortization schedule and an interest rate of 7.25 percent, which reflected the rate environment applicable at the time of our analysis for a non-agency execution on a mixed-use asset with bankruptcy-related complexity, the maximum supportable loan amount was approximately $3.05 million. This was calculated by solving for the principal amount whose monthly payment at 7.25 percent over 360 months equals $20,667 per month ($248,000 annually divided by 12).

The LTV analysis then tested whether that loan amount was consistent with the collateral value. The property had been independently appraised, and using that appraised value as a reference point, a loan of $3.05 million represented a loan-to-value ratio of approximately 62 to 65 percent depending on the appraised value scenario used. For a mixed-use asset with retail exposure in a Chapter 11 exit context, lenders in the New Haven market will generally underwrite to a maximum of 65 percent LTV, with some non-bank lenders willing to go to 70 percent at a rate premium. The DSCR-constrained loan amount therefore fit within the LTV parameters of the available lender market, which was a critical finding for the plan proponent's feasibility argument.

The analysis demonstrated that first-mortgage financing sufficient to satisfy the senior secured creditor's allowed claim was achievable, that the loan amount was supported by both income and collateral value metrics, and that the financing was not dependent on speculative assumptions about future income growth. That last point matters to courts evaluating feasibility under Section 1129(a)(11) of the Bankruptcy Code.

The Complicating Factors

Bankruptcy estate stigma and lender underwriting restrictions. Many institutional lenders and agency executions categorically exclude loans on properties currently titled to a bankruptcy estate or where the borrower has filed for protection within the prior 24 to 36 months. Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, for example, impose post-bankruptcy seasoning requirements that effectively exclude most Chapter 11 exit scenarios from agency financing. This does not mean financing is unavailable; it means the available lender universe is narrower and the pricing reflects that constraint. Courts need to understand this distinction because a plan that assumes agency pricing for a bankruptcy exit may be based on an unrealistic financing assumption, even if the underlying collateral metrics are sound.

Title and lien resolution at exit. Lenders originating exit financing require clean first-lien title. In a Chapter 11 context, this means the plan must provide for the extinguishment of junior liens, mechanics' liens, and any disputed encumbrances that would cloud title. Lenders will not close on a loan if title insurance cannot be issued without exceptions for pending litigation or unresolved liens. The plan's treatment of junior creditors and the mechanism for achieving a clean title transfer are therefore directly relevant to financing feasibility, and a financing expert must address whether the proposed plan structure actually enables a lendable title condition to be achieved.

Retail component underwriting drag. New Haven's retail market has experienced meaningful softening outside the university-proximate corridors. Lenders underwriting mixed-use assets with retail components apply a higher vacancy assumption to retail space than to residential, and many impose a retail income haircut of 15 to 20 percent on gross potential retail income when calculating underwritten NOI. In this engagement, the retail tenant was on a lease with less than three years of remaining term, which caused lenders to discount the retail income further or to require a partial interest reserve. That reserve requirement affects the net loan proceeds available to the borrower and must be accounted for in the plan's sources-and-uses analysis.

Property condition and deferred maintenance. Chapter 11 assets frequently have deferred capital expenditures, either because cash was constrained during the proceeding or because lender consent was required for major expenditures. Lenders originating exit financing will require a property condition assessment, and material deferred maintenance will either reduce the loan amount (through an upfront repair escrow that reduces net proceeds) or require the plan to address capital improvements as a condition of closing. In New Haven's older urban building stock, which includes a significant inventory of pre-war and mid-century mixed-use buildings, property condition issues are a recurring factor in exit financing analysis.

Guaranty and sponsorship requirements. Most commercial mortgage lenders require a carve-out guaranty (sometimes called a "bad boy" guaranty) from an individual with meaningful net worth. In a Chapter 11 exit scenario, the reorganized debtor's principal may have impaired personal credit or a reduced net worth following the bankruptcy. Lenders will underwrite the guarantor independently, and a guarantor who cannot meet minimum net worth thresholds equal to the loan amount will cause the loan to fail credit approval even if the property-level metrics are fully supportable. This is a common failure point in exit financing that must be addressed in the plan feasibility analysis.

The New Haven Lender Market

New Haven benefits from a meaningful concentration of community and regional bank lenders that maintain active commercial real estate lending programs and are not subject to the rigid credit policy constraints that govern large national banks and agency lenders. Institutions headquartered or with significant Connecticut market presence have historically been active in New Haven CRE lending, and several maintain portfolio lending programs that can accommodate bankruptcy exit scenarios on a case-by-case basis, provided the borrower's post-confirmation credit profile is acceptable and the property metrics support the loan.

For assets that cannot qualify for conventional bank financing due to recency of bankruptcy, more complex capital structures, or non-stabilized income, the bridge lending market provides an important alternative. Bridge lenders, including debt funds and balance sheet lenders that specialize in transitional real estate, will originate on New Haven assets in the 65 to 70 percent LTV range at rates typically ranging from 9.5 to 11.5 percent in the current rate environment, with interest-only structures that reduce the near-term debt service burden. These lenders are explicitly comfortable with bankruptcy exit scenarios, provided the exit is court-approved, title is clean at closing, and there is a credible path to stabilization and conventional refinance within the bridge loan's two-to-three year term.

Life insurance companies and CMBS conduit lenders are generally not viable for New Haven mixed-use assets in the sub-$5 million loan range. CMBS execution has a practical floor of approximately $5 to $7 million in most market conditions, and life company allocations favor larger loans on stabilized, single-use assets in primary markets. Attorneys and plan proponents who assume CMBS execution for a smaller urban mixed-use asset in New Haven are working from an incorrect financing premise.

Hard money and private lenders occupy the highest-cost tier and serve assets that cannot qualify for institutional bridge lending due to extreme credit or collateral complexity. CLS CRE maintains relationships with over 1,000 lenders across all of these categories, and our placement work on behalf of clients includes active originations in 50 states with approximately $1 billion in transaction volume. That market access is what allows us to render a credible feasibility opinion rather than a theoretical one.

What Attorneys Handling New Haven CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to retain a CRE financing expert. Any Plan of Reorganization that proposes to satisfy creditors through first-mortgage exit financing should be supported by a financing feasibility opinion from a qualified expert. Courts have become increasingly sophisticated about the difference between a plan proponent asserting that financing is "available" and a qualified expert opining that financing is achievable on specific terms from an identifiable category of lenders. If opposing counsel challenges the financing assumptions in your plan, you need expert testimony that can withstand cross-examination on market conditions, lender underwriting standards, and deal-specific constraints.

What a financing expert can establish that an appraiser cannot. An appraiser opines on value. A financing expert opines on debt capacity, lender market conditions, the availability of credit for a specific asset type and borrower profile, the commercial reasonableness of proposed loan terms, and whether a plan's financing assumptions are consistent with how lenders actually underwrite. These are separate questions. An appraiser testifying about financing feasibility is outside their lane, and that boundary can be exposed on cross-examination.

Qualifications that matter. A qualified CRE financing expert should have active, current transactional experience originating or placing commercial mortgages in the relevant asset class and geography. Retired bankers or academics who have not closed a commercial loan in several years cannot speak credibly to current lender credit standards, current pricing, or current appetite for specific risk profiles. Courts are best served by experts who can testify from direct market experience, not historical knowledge.

Information counsel should gather before the engagement. To produce a credible and defensible expert report, counsel should provide the following at the outset: current rent rolls and trailing twelve-month operating statements; the proposed plan treatment of the senior secured creditor and all junior liens; the property condition assessment if one exists; the principal's personal financial statement; the appraised value of the property; and a description of any pending litigation, environmental issues, or title complications. Gaps in this information will be reflected in the expert's assumptions and can be a vulnerability at hearing.

How financing expert testimony is used in a Plan hearing. Under Bankruptcy Code Section 1129(a)(11), a plan must be feasible, meaning confirmation is not likely to be followed by liquidation or further reorganization. A financing expert supports the proponent's feasibility argument by establishing that the debt capital required to consummate the plan is available, on commercially reasonable terms, from an identifiable lender market. Conversely, a financing expert retained by an objecting creditor can challenge feasibility by demonstrating that the plan's financing assumptions are inconsistent with current market conditions, that the proposed loan cannot be underwritten to market-standard DSCR or LTV requirements, or that no lender in the relevant market would originate on the proposed terms.

Key Takeaways

  • New Haven's market fundamentals support debt financing for stabilized multifamily and mixed-use assets, but asset class, submarket location, and property condition materially affect the available lender universe and the achievable loan amount. A generic feasibility analysis that ignores New Haven-specific market data will not survive scrutiny.
  • The DSCR constraint, not the LTV constraint, is usually the binding limit on loan proceeds for income-producing assets in New Haven. The analysis must be income-driven, and the income must be reconstructed carefully for assets that experienced disruption during the bankruptcy period.
  • Bankruptcy exit financing is available but from a narrower lender universe than conventional refinancing. Community banks with portfolio lending programs and institutional bridge lenders are the primary sources; agency, CMBS, and life company executions are generally not available for post-bankruptcy assets in the near term.
  • Title must be demonstrably clean before lender commitments will be issued. The plan's lien resolution mechanism is a direct component of financing feasibility, not a separate legal matter. Financing experts and bankruptcy counsel need to work in coordination on this point.

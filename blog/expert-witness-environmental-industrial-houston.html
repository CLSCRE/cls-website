Expert Witness: Financing a Contaminated Industrial Property in Houston, TX

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Environmental Contamination Analysis

The Houston Market Context

Houston is the fourth-largest city in the United States and one of the most consequential industrial real estate markets in the country. Its economy is structurally tied to energy, petrochemical refining, logistics, and port activity, which means industrial warehouse and distribution assets are not peripheral to this market; they are the market's backbone. The Port of Houston is consistently ranked among the top ports in the nation by tonnage, and the industrial corridors radiating from it, including the Ship Channel, Greenspoint, Northwest Crossing, and the Energy Corridor submarkets, contain some of the heaviest concentrations of chemical storage, manufacturing, and logistics facilities in North America.

As of early 2025, Houston's industrial vacancy rate sits in the 7.5% to 8.5% range depending on submarket, which reflects modest softening from the historically tight conditions of 2021 through 2023 but remains well within the range that supports stable underwriting. Average asking rents for bulk industrial and heavy warehouse product have grown approximately 30% to 40% cumulatively since 2020, meaning that leases executed before 2021 are, almost by definition, below current market. This is not a minor nuance in a financing dispute; it is material to every underwriting conversation a lender will have about an industrial asset in Houston right now.

The industrial submarket most relevant to legacy environmental conditions is the Ship Channel corridor and its adjacent industrial districts, where decades of petrochemical and manufacturing activity have left behind a documented history of soil and groundwater contamination. Chlorinated volatile organic compounds, or chlorinated VOCs, and 1,4-dioxane are among the most commonly cited contaminants in TCEQ enforcement actions involving these properties. Lenders active in Houston are not unfamiliar with environmental conditions on industrial assets. The question is not whether contamination exists; the question is what type it is, what stage of regulatory response it is in, and whether the lender can structure around it.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by counsel representing a class of secured creditors in a dispute involving an industrial warehouse asset located in a Houston-area industrial submarket with documented environmental contamination. The property was subject to an active Texas Commission on Environmental Quality, or TCEQ, regulatory enforcement action involving 1,4-dioxane and chlorinated VOC contamination attributable to prior industrial tenancy. The asset was 100% occupied under a triple-net lease with a remaining term of approximately six years, with contractual rents measurably below current market, a condition that is common in Houston industrial leases executed before 2022.

Counsel retained CLS CRE to provide a Rule 26 expert report addressing two threshold questions that were central to the creditors' position in the proceeding. First, does the environmental condition, as documented in the available Phase I and Phase II Environmental Site Assessment reports and the TCEQ correspondence file, constitute an absolute bar to first-mortgage financing from institutional or commercial lenders operating in the current market? Second, if financing is available, what is the maximum loan amount supportable at market underwriting standards, and does that amount provide sufficient proceeds to retire the existing senior debt in full?

These are precisely the kinds of questions that sit at the intersection of capital markets practice and legal strategy. An appraiser can give you an opinion of value. A retired banker can tell you what their former institution's credit policy said. Neither of those disciplines produces a current, market-grounded analysis of financing feasibility, lender appetite, and maximum supportable leverage for a contaminated industrial asset in a specific market cycle. That is the domain of an active commercial mortgage professional with current lender relationships and transactional knowledge.

The Financial Analysis

The income analysis began with the existing triple-net lease, which establishes a contractual NOI that is straightforward to calculate: the base rent per square foot multiplied by rentable area, less any landlord obligations not passed through to the tenant. For a hypothetical Houston industrial warehouse of 180,000 square feet at a below-market NNN rent of $6.50 per square foot annually, gross NNN income is $1,170,000. After deducting a stabilized management fee of 3% ($35,100) and a capital reserve of $0.10 per square foot ($18,000) that most institutional lenders still require even on NNN assets, the stabilized NOI used for underwriting purposes is approximately $1,116,900.

It is worth noting that the contractual rent of $6.50 per square foot is approximately 20% to 25% below current market for comparable unencumbered bulk warehouse product in Houston's industrial submarkets, where asking rents on new and renewal leases have been running $8.00 to $9.50 per square foot NNN. Most conventional lenders underwrite to in-place contractual income for NNN assets, not to market rent, because they cannot lend against rent that does not yet exist. This below-market rent condition directly constrains the NOI available for debt service, and therefore directly constrains the maximum loan amount regardless of what the property is worth on a market-rent basis.

Lenders underwriting industrial assets in Houston today are applying DSCR minimums of 1.25x for conventional bank and insurance company execution, and 1.20x for CMBS and bridge lenders. The 1.25x DSCR threshold is the market standard because it provides sufficient debt service cushion to absorb a short-term income interruption without triggering a loan covenant default. I applied the more conservative 1.25x threshold for this analysis because the environmental condition of the subject property eliminates insurance company and agency lenders from consideration, meaning the relevant financing universe consists of lenders who will require additional credit protection rather than less.

Assuming a note rate of 7.25%, which reflects current market pricing for a 5-year fixed rate first mortgage on a Houston industrial asset with an environmental complication (roughly 175 to 200 basis points over the 5-year Treasury as of early 2025), and a 25-year amortization schedule, the annual debt service constant is approximately 8.58%. Dividing stabilized NOI of $1,116,900 by the DSCR requirement of 1.25x yields maximum annual debt service of $893,520. Dividing that figure by the 8.58% constant produces a maximum supportable loan amount of approximately $10,413,000, or roughly $10.4 million.

If the as-is appraised value of the property, accounting for the environmental condition and the below-market lease, is in the range of $14 million to $16 million, a $10.4 million loan represents a loan-to-value ratio of approximately 65% to 74%. Most lenders willing to touch a property with an active TCEQ enforcement action will cap LTV at 55% to 65%, which means LTV, not DSCR, becomes the binding constraint in this scenario. At a 60% LTV cap on a $15 million value, the maximum loan is $9 million. At 65% LTV, it is $9.75 million. The income-constrained maximum of $10.4 million is theoretically achievable only if the lender accepts the higher LTV, which requires either a higher appraised value or a lender with a more permissive environmental risk policy than the current market standard.

This type of dual-constraint analysis, DSCR-constrained proceeds versus LTV-constrained proceeds, is exactly what a CRE financing expert provides that an appraiser does not. Appraisers determine value. Financing experts determine how much of that value a lender will actually lend against, under what conditions, and at what cost.

The Complicating Factors

Active TCEQ Regulatory Enforcement Action. An open enforcement file with TCEQ is the single most significant financing impediment for an industrial property in Texas. Lenders assess environmental risk through two lenses: legal liability and remediation cost uncertainty. An active enforcement action means both lenses are simultaneously out of focus. Lenders cannot quantify their environmental indemnification exposure when remediation scope has not been finalized, and they cannot assess collateral value accurately when future remediation costs could be material to equity. The result is that the financing universe narrows dramatically. Life insurance companies are categorically excluded. Agency financing, Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, and HUD, is not available for industrial assets with active environmental enforcement. Most regional banks with formal environmental credit policies will decline unless a remediation cost estimate has been finalized and an escrow or trust account has been established to cover it.

1,4-Dioxane and Chlorinated VOC Contamination Complexity. These are not minor contaminants that lenders routinely underwrite around. 1,4-dioxane is a probable human carcinogen that does not bind well to soil particles, making it highly mobile in groundwater and extremely difficult to remediate using conventional pump-and-treat methods. Chlorinated VOCs, including trichloroethylene and perchloroethylene, are similarly persistent and can create vapor intrusion pathways into occupied structures. Lenders who have environmental counsel review a Phase II report identifying either of these contaminants will immediately ask whether vapor intrusion has been evaluated, what the groundwater plume boundary is, and whether downgradient property owners or municipal water supplies are affected. If any of those answers are unfavorable, the lender's exposure extends well beyond the subject parcel, and that is where most conventional lenders stop the conversation.

Below-Market Lease as a Double Constraint. The NNN lease structure provides credit stability in the sense that there is a single creditworthy tenant covering operating expenses. However, the below-market rent means that the income available to support debt service is materially lower than what the same property would generate on a current-market lease. This constrains loan proceeds from the income side simultaneously with the environmental condition constraining them from the LTV side. Lenders generally will not underwrite to market rent unless the existing lease has less than 12 months remaining, because lending against hypothetical income violates basic credit principles. The combination of a below-market in-place lease and an environmentally impaired collateral value creates a double compression on maximum loan proceeds that is difficult to overcome without structural enhancements such as a remediation escrow, environmental insurance, or a creditworthy guarantor.

Title and Lien Priority Concerns. In Texas, TCEQ has the authority to impose liens on contaminated property for unreimbursed remediation costs incurred by the state. These liens can achieve priority over private mortgages under certain circumstances. Any lender advancing funds on a property with an active TCEQ enforcement action must obtain a title commitment that addresses this risk, and many title underwriters will either exclude environmental lien coverage or require a specific endorsement with a premium that reflects the exposure. The availability and cost of title coverage is a direct input into lender willingness and loan pricing.

The Houston Lender Market

For a contaminated industrial asset with the characteristics described, the realistic lender universe in the current Houston market consists of four categories, each with materially different pricing, leverage, and structural requirements.

Debt funds and bridge lenders are the most likely execution path for a property with an active enforcement action. These lenders, typically capitalized by private equity or hedge fund capital, are not subject to the environmental credit policies of regulated depository institutions. They underwrite to risk-adjusted returns rather than to compliance frameworks. For a Houston industrial asset with a TCEQ enforcement action, a bridge lender will typically advance 55% to 65% of as-is value at a floating rate of SOFR plus 350 to 500 basis points, with a 24-month to 36-month term, interest-only payments, and an environmental reserve funded at closing. The environmental reserve requirement, often sized at 110% to 125% of the estimated remediation cost, is a non-negotiable structural element that directly reduces net loan proceeds.

Community and regional banks with Houston industrial experience represent a secondary market, but only if the TCEQ enforcement action is in a defined remediation phase with a capped cost estimate. Several Texas-chartered community banks in the Houston market have developed internal expertise in environmental credit underwriting because the local industrial base makes avoidance impractical. These lenders will typically require a No Further Action letter from TCEQ, or at minimum a Prospective Purchaser Agreement or a Resource Conservation and Recovery Act closure letter, before committing. Pricing is generally SOFR plus 275 to 375 basis points with a 5-year term and 20-year amortization, but leverage is limited to 55% to 60% LTV on environmentally impaired collateral.

CMBS conduit lenders are largely unavailable for this property type with an active enforcement action because environmental representations in a CMBS pooling and servicing agreement cannot accommodate unquantified remediation liability. A CMBS lender would require either a final NFA letter or a fully-funded environmental insurance policy with coverage sufficient to satisfy the pool's environmental underwriting standards, which in practice means contamination is either resolved or the asset does not fit the execution.

SBA 504 and USDA programs are irrelevant to this scenario because they are owner-user programs and the subject is a NNN investment property.

What Attorneys Handling Houston CRE Disputes Need to Know

The threshold question attorneys should ask is whether they need a CRE financing expert at all, or whether an appraiser will suffice. The answer depends on what the dispute is actually about. If the question is value, retain an appraiser. If the question is financing feasibility, financing proceeds, lender market conditions, or whether a proposed plan of reorganization is confirmable because exit financing is or is not achievable, retain a CRE financing expert with current market relationships. These are different disciplines, and confusing them creates gaps in expert coverage that opposing counsel will exploit.

A CRE financing expert can opine on matters that lie entirely outside an appraiser's expertise: which lenders are actually active in a specific asset class in a specific market, what their current credit standards are, how they price environmental risk, what structural requirements they impose, and whether proposed loan terms in a dispute are consistent with the market or fabricated. An appraiser works backward from comparable transactions to a value conclusion. A CRE financing expert works forward from current lender behavior to a financing feasibility conclusion. Both are necessary in complex secured creditor disputes; neither substitutes for the other.

The qualifications that matter in a CRE financing expert are current transactional activity, documented lender relationships, and experience across multiple capital sources, not simply tenure at a bank. A retired bank loan officer knows one institution's credit policy from one point in time. An active commercial mortgage broker with relationships across 1,000 or more lenders, as CLS CRE maintains, can testify to the current market standard across the full spectrum of capital sources relevant to the asset type. That breadth of current market knowledge is what makes financing expert testimony useful at trial and in Plan confirmation hearings.

Before retaining a CRE financing expert in a Houston industrial contamination case, counsel should assemble the following: the Phase I and Phase II ESA reports, all TCEQ correspondence including the enforcement notice and any remediation order, a current rent roll with lease abstracts, the existing loan documents including any environmental representations and remediation covenants, all appraisals of record, and any environmental cost estimates or remediation work plans. The expert needs this material to form a complete opinion. A financing feasibility opinion issued without the Phase II ESA and the TCEQ file is incomplete and vulnerable to challenge.

At trial or in a bankruptcy Plan confirmation hearing, CRE financing expert testimony is typically used to establish one of three things: that exit financing sufficient to fund a plan is or is not available at projected terms, that a prior lender's underwriting was or was not consistent with market standards at origination, or that a proposed restructuring is or is not commercially reasonable given current capital market conditions. In Houston environmental contamination cases specifically, the most common use is to establish whether a secured creditor's collateral is financeable at a loan amount sufficient to retire existing debt, which directly informs the question of whether the creditor is undersecured, oversecured, or adequately protected.

Key Takeaways

  • Environmental contamination does not categorically eliminate financing. An active TCEQ enforcement action involving 1,4-dioxane or chlorinated VOCs narrows the lender universe significantly, but debt fund and bridge lender execution remains available for Houston industrial assets with these conditions, subject to structural requirements including environmental reserves and elevated pricing.
  • DSCR and LTV operate as simultaneous constraints. In a below-market NNN lease scenario on environmentally impaired collateral, both income-based and value-based limits on loan proceeds must be calculated independently and the lower figure controls. Failing to run both analyses produces an incomplete and potentially misleading financing feasibility opinion.

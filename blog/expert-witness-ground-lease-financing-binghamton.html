Expert Witness: Ground Lease Financing Disputes and Analysis in Binghamton, NY

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Ground Lease Financing Complexity

The Binghamton Market Context

Binghamton sits at the confluence of the Susquehanna and Chenango Rivers in Broome County, New York, and while it does not generate the transaction volume of Buffalo or Albany, it is a meaningfully active secondary market with a distinct commercial real estate profile that matters enormously when a financing dispute lands in court. The Triple Cities region, which encompasses Binghamton, Johnson City, and Endicott, has undergone a structural economic transition over the past three decades, shifting from a manufacturing and defense-sector base toward healthcare, higher education, and light industrial. That transition has direct implications for how ground lease structures function here, because the underlying land values, tenant credit quality, and institutional appetite for leasehold financing all reflect those economic realities.

The Binghamton industrial market has historically maintained vacancy in the 8 to 12 percent range across its approximately 18 million square feet of industrial inventory, with older, multi-tenant flex and warehouse product in the Route 17 corridor and near the Binghamton Regional Airport commanding net rents in the $4.50 to $7.50 per square foot range. Net lease retail, which is frequently the context in which ground lease structures appear, remains concentrated along Vestal Parkway and the Route 434 corridor, where single-tenant assets have traded at cap rates ranging from 6.75 to 8.25 percent depending on tenant credit, lease term remaining, and building vintage. These cap rate parameters are meaningfully wider than downstate New York or major coastal markets, which directly affects how lenders underwrite leasehold interests: a wider cap rate environment means smaller loan proceeds relative to asset value, and less margin for error when the financing structure is complicated by a ground lease overlay. Multifamily in the Binghamton submarket has seen modest rent growth, with Class B properties averaging $950 to $1,200 per month for two-bedroom units and stabilized cap rates in the 7.0 to 8.5 percent range. For any attorney dealing with a ground lease financing dispute in this market, understanding these fundamentals is not academic; they define what a lender could realistically underwrite and what a borrower could reasonably expect to achieve.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing the leasehold mortgagee in a dispute involving a commercial ground lease arrangement on a net lease retail property in the Binghamton submarket. The core asset was a single-tenant, triple-net leased commercial building situated on land owned by a separate entity under a long-term ground lease. The leasehold interest had been pledged as collateral for a commercial mortgage, and the dispute centered on whether the ground lease, as structured and recorded, was capable of supporting institutional financing at the time the loan was originated, and whether the lender's underwriting and reliance on that leasehold structure met or failed to meet commercially reasonable standards.

Counsel needed answers to three specific questions: First, does the ground lease contain the subordination, non-disturbance, and attornment provisions required by institutional lenders to make leasehold collateral financeable? Second, were the DSCR and LTV metrics achievable given the lease economics and the Binghamton market conditions at the time of origination? Third, what is the universe of lenders that would realistically consider a leasehold mortgage on this type of asset in this market, and what would each category require as a condition of financing? CLS CRE was engaged to provide a Rule 26 expert report addressing all three questions, combining market-level lender intelligence with a detailed underwriting reconstruction of the subject financing.

The Financial Analysis

The starting point for any leasehold mortgage analysis is the same as for any commercial real estate financing: a disciplined reconstruction of the stabilized net operating income. In this engagement, the income stream was derived from the triple-net lease in place on the building, which provided a fixed base rent paid by the tenant to the leasehold owner. From that gross rent, we deducted the ground rent payable to the fee owner, because from a lender's perspective, ground rent is a senior contractual obligation that must be serviced before any debt service on the leasehold mortgage. This is a critical analytical distinction that is frequently missed by non-specialist witnesses: the NOI available to support a leasehold mortgage is not the gross rent from the building tenant; it is that gross rent net of ground rent, management fees, and any other senior carrying costs specific to the leasehold structure.

In this matter, the building lease generated approximately $185,000 in annual gross rent. Annual ground rent under the ground lease was $32,400, a figure that stepped up on a fixed schedule. Accounting for a nominal management fee of 3 percent and reserves consistent with a net lease asset, the stabilized NOI available to support leasehold debt service was approximately $144,200. This is the figure that drives the loan sizing analysis.

Market standard DSCR for a leasehold mortgage on a single-tenant net lease asset in a secondary market like Binghamton is 1.25x at a minimum, with many institutional lenders requiring 1.30x or higher given the additional structural complexity and the secondary market location premium in their credit policies. Applying the 1.25x threshold:

  • Maximum annual debt service supportable: $144,200 divided by 1.25x equals $115,360
  • At a 6.75 percent note rate on a 25-year amortization schedule, a $115,360 annual debt service payment corresponds to a maximum loan amount of approximately $1,390,000
  • At a 7.25 percent note rate, the same debt service supports approximately $1,295,000

The leasehold estate's value for LTV purposes must be assessed independently of the fee interest. Leasehold value is a function of the positive spread between market rent and contract rent for the building lease, the remaining lease term, the ground rent obligation, and the reversion terms at lease expiration. In Binghamton, institutional lenders will typically cap leasehold LTV at 65 to 70 percent for this asset type, and many CMBS and life company programs will not exceed 60 percent given the secondary market location. At a leasehold value of $2,100,000 derived from a capitalized income approach and confirmed against comparable ground lease transaction data, the LTV-constrained maximum loan ranged from $1,260,000 to $1,470,000 depending on the lender category. The DSCR constraint and the LTV constraint were therefore broadly consistent in this case, producing a maximum financeable loan in the $1,295,000 to $1,390,000 range. The analysis then compared this range against the actual loan amount at origination to assess whether the origination met market standards for this deal type.

The Complicating Factors

SNDA Deficiencies and Leasehold Financeability. The most fundamental issue in any ground lease financing dispute is whether the ground lease contains a lender-acceptable SNDA agreement or, at minimum, enforceable SNDA provisions embedded in the lease document. These three elements serve distinct but interdependent functions. Subordination means the ground lease is junior to the leasehold mortgage; without it, a foreclosing lender cannot acquire clean leasehold title free of the existing ground lease terms. Non-disturbance is the ground lessor's covenant that, in the event of leasehold mortgage foreclosure, it will not terminate the ground lease and will recognize the foreclosing lender or its successor as the new tenant under the ground lease. Attornment is the ground lessee's agreement to recognize the lender or its successor as landlord following a fee mortgage foreclosure. In practice, lenders require a standalone executed SNDA agreement from the fee owner as a condition of leasehold mortgage commitment. A ground lease that merely contains subordination language in the boilerplate without a separately executed SNDA from the fee owner is not financeable with any institutional lender in today's market, period. In this engagement, the ground lease contained subordination language but lacked a fully executed standalone SNDA from the fee owner, which was central to the dispute.

Ground Lease Term Adequacy. Institutional lenders require that the ground lease term extend beyond the loan maturity by a meaningful margin. The market standard is that the remaining ground lease term must exceed the loan term by at least 10 years, and most programs require 20 to 30 years of remaining term beyond loan maturity. A ground lease with a 30-year original term that is 18 years into that term has only 12 years remaining; even with extension options, most lenders will not rely on unexercised options to extend term unless the ground lessee has a contractual right to exercise them without fee owner consent and without conditions precedent that could fail. In Binghamton, where the resale universe for distressed leasehold assets is thin, lenders impose these requirements with particular discipline because their exit in a foreclosure scenario depends on a marketable, transferable leasehold estate with adequate term.

Ground Rent Escalation and Coverage Deterioration. Ground leases with fixed-step or CPI-indexed rent escalations introduce a dynamic risk to debt service coverage that lenders must model through the loan term. If ground rent increases at a rate that outpaces building rent growth or exceeds the escalation structure of the building lease, NOI available for debt service contracts over time. A lender underwriting a leasehold mortgage must stress-test coverage at the maturity date balloon, not just at origination, using projected ground rent and building lease rent at each escalation date. Lenders that fail to perform this forward coverage analysis are not meeting market standards for leasehold mortgage origination.

Reversionary Risk and Collateral Adequacy. At ground lease expiration, absent renewal, the improvements revert to the fee owner at no cost to the lessee. This means leasehold collateral has a hard end date, and the lender's collateral position deteriorates to zero at that date. For shorter-term ground leases or ground leases with problematic renewal provisions, lenders must discount the leasehold value to account for this reversionary risk. In a secondary market like Binghamton, where the alternative uses for the land may be limited, this reversionary discount can be material to the loan sizing analysis.

The Binghamton Lender Market

Binghamton is served by a layered lender market that ranges from active community banks to selective regional and national programs, but the institutional appetite for leasehold mortgage complexity is concentrated in a narrow segment of that market. Understanding who will actually lend on a leasehold interest in Binghamton, and on what terms, is essential to assessing whether a given financing was commercially reasonable or whether a party's claim that financing was available or unavailable has merit.

Community and Regional Banks. Broome County's community banking market is reasonably active, with several institutions maintaining commercial real estate portfolios in the market. Community banks will consider leasehold financing on a case-by-case basis, but they require clean SNDA documentation, adequate ground lease term, and typically impose conservative DSCR requirements of 1.25x to 1.35x. Pricing on leasehold transactions tends to run 25 to 50 basis points above comparable fee simple financing to compensate for structural complexity. Portfolio community banks can be flexible on loan structure, but they are not equipped to absorb complex leasehold subordination disputes at closing; clean documentation is a prerequisite, not a negotiating point.

Regional and Mid-Size Banks. Several regional banks active in the Syracuse to Binghamton corridor will underwrite leasehold mortgages for stabilized, creditworthy tenants, particularly single-tenant NNN assets with investment-grade or near-investment-grade tenants. These lenders apply similar SNDA requirements to community banks but have more formal credit policy language around ground lease term adequacy and escalation provisions. Their pricing in Binghamton currently falls in the 6.50 to 7.25 percent range for five-year fixed, 25-year amortization structures.

CMBS Conduit Lenders. CMBS execution is technically available for larger, higher-quality leasehold assets in Binghamton, but the practical reality is that CMBS programs impose the most rigorous SNDA and ground lease term requirements in the market, and they will not waive deficiencies. Minimum loan sizes in the $2.5 million to $5 million range effectively exclude many Binghamton transactions from CMBS consideration. For deals that qualify, CMBS programs provide fixed-rate certainty but zero flexibility on structural deficiencies.

Debt Funds and Private Lenders. For leasehold assets with structural deficiencies that do not clear institutional credit boxes, debt funds and private bridge lenders are the remaining execution option. These lenders price risk-adjusted returns of 9 to 12 percent or higher and impose significant origination fees. They will sometimes lend on leasehold interests with deficient SNDA documentation, but they price that risk explicitly and often require personal recourse. The existence of private lender pricing in a dispute is relevant to damages analysis: if a borrower was forced into private capital at 11 percent because institutional financing was unavailable due to a ground lease deficiency, the difference between institutional and private pricing represents a quantifiable economic harm.

What Attorneys Handling Binghamton CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to Retain a CRE Financing Expert. The threshold question for litigation counsel is whether the core dispute turns on a financing question, not just a valuation question. An appraiser can tell you what a property was worth; a CRE financing expert tells you whether and how it could have been financed, on what terms, and by whom. In ground lease disputes, the financing expert fills a gap that appraisers routinely leave open: whether the leasehold structure, as documented, was capable of supporting institutional financing at the time of the relevant transaction. That question requires current and historical lender market intelligence that appraisers are not trained to provide.

What a Financing Expert Can Address That an Appraiser Cannot. CRE financing experts can opine on whether specific SNDA provisions meet or fail market standards; whether a lender's underwriting methodology complied with industry norms; whether the DSCR and LTV analysis performed at origination was accurate and complete; what lender categories would or would not have provided financing on the subject structure; and what the economic cost of a financing deficiency was, expressed in loan proceeds foregone, rate differential, or deal failure. These are underwriting and capital markets opinions, not valuation opinions, and they require a different set of qualifications.

Qualifications That Matter. A retired banker who originated loans 20 years ago is not a qualified CRE financing expert for today's disputes. The market has changed substantially. A credible expert should have current, active deal flow in the relevant asset types; relationships with the specific categories of lenders whose practices are at issue; and demonstrated experience reconstructing underwriting analyses on a retrospective basis. At CLS CRE, our approximately $1 billion in transaction volume and 1,000-plus lender relationships across 50 states provide the active market context that makes our expert opinions credible and current.

What Counsel Should Gather Before the Expert Engagement. Productive expert engagements begin with complete documentation. Counsel should assemble the full ground lease and all amendments; any SNDA agreements, whether executed or proposed; the leasehold mortgage commitment letter and loan documents; the appraisal or broker opinion of value used at origination; the lender's internal credit memorandum if obtainable through discovery; and all correspondence between the parties regarding the financing. The earlier in the litigation this documentation is assembled, the more efficiently the expert can structure the analysis and identify the key factual disputes.

Key Takeaways

  • Ground lease financing is structurally distinct from fee simple financing. The NOI available to support a leasehold mortgage is gross building rent minus ground rent and senior obligations, not gross rent. Experts who conflate these figures produce inaccurate loan sizing analyses.
  • A fully executed standalone SNDA agreement from the fee owner is a non-negotiable requirement for institutional leasehold financing. Subordination language embedded in the ground lease is not a substitute. Any ground lease financing arranged without a fully executed SNDA was either placed with a non-institutional lender or involved a lender that deviated from market standards.
  • Ground lease term adequacy is a hard constraint. Lenders require the ground lease term to extend beyond loan maturity by 10 to 30 years depending on the program. Unexercised extension options do not substitute for contractual term unless the exercise right is unconditional.
  • Ground rent escalation must be modeled through the loan term. Coverage analysis at origination only, without a forward stress test to maturity, does not meet market standards for leasehold mortgage underwriting.
  • Binghamton's wider cap rate environment compresses leasehold loan proceeds. Secondary market pricing, combined with the structural discount applied to leasehold collateral, means achievable loan amounts on Binghamton

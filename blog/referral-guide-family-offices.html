Family Office CRE Financing: Portfolio Strategy, Complex Structures, and Institutional Capital

By Trevor Damyan·June 25, 2026·Commercial Lending Solutions

Commercial Real Estate Financing for Family Offices: What Your Broker Probably Cannot Handle

I am Trevor Damyan, a commercial mortgage broker at CLS CRE in Los Angeles. Over the years, a growing share of my most complex engagements has come from a specific source: referrals from family office managers, multi-family office advisors, and private wealth managers whose UHNW clients own commercial real estate and need financing solutions that go well beyond a standard loan application.

The gap between what most commercial mortgage brokers offer and what family offices actually need is wider than most advisors realize until they try to bridge it. This article lays out what those gaps look like, the specific financing scenarios I see most often, what lenders require, and how the referral process works when you introduce a client to our team.

Why Family Office Principals Encounter Complex CRE Financing Needs

Family offices are not typical real estate investors. A single-family office managing $500 million in assets might hold a $120 million commercial real estate portfolio across eight states, structured through a cascade of LLCs, trusts, and holding companies, with beneficial ownership that includes multiple family members and in some cases offshore entities. The patriarch may be a foreign national. Some properties were inherited. Others were 1031-exchanged into. A few carry decade-old CMBS debt that is now maturing.

Multi-family office advisors and private wealth managers face a parallel challenge. Their clients do not neatly separate their CRE holdings from the rest of the balance sheet, but conventional commercial lenders absolutely do. When a client comes to refinance a trophy industrial park, the lender wants to understand the full borrower entity, the complete ownership chain, and the source of any cross-guarantee. If that chain runs through a Cayman entity or a dynasty trust with a corporate trustee, most originators simply are not equipped to navigate it.

The situations I encounter most often from this client segment include:

  • Multi-generational families with inherited real estate that has never been institutionally financed
  • UHNW principals who have outgrown local bank relationships and want institutional-quality non-recourse debt
  • International families with US CRE assets navigating FIRPTA and foreign entity underwriting hurdles
  • Family offices executing a portfolio-level liability management strategy across 10 to 30 properties
  • Ground lease structures where a family holds land beneath commercial improvements

Each of these situations requires lender relationships, structuring experience, and underwriting fluency that a conventional residential-crossover broker or community bank officer simply does not have.

The Financing Scenarios Family Offices Face Most Often

1. Multi-Property Portfolio Refinancing

A family office approaches this with a goal that sounds simple: reduce the weighted-average interest rate across the portfolio and improve terms. Executing on it is not simple at all.

Ten properties might have ten different lenders, ten different maturity dates, and ten different prepayment structures. Some carry yield-maintenance penalties. Others have defeasance obligations. A few may be underwater on a DCR basis at current rents.

The right execution here is not to refinance all ten simultaneously. It is to tier the portfolio by maturity, prepayment cost, current rate versus market, and lender relationship quality, then sequence refinancings to minimize total exit costs while maximizing rate improvement. In some cases, we identify two or three properties where the penalty is small relative to the rate savings and execute immediately. Others we put on a 12-month watch list and time to natural maturity.

For a family office, the other value-add is lender consolidation. Moving from ten lenders to three or four simplifies the reporting burden, the covenant compliance calendar, and the annual financial statement requirements substantially. Life companies and debt funds are particularly well suited to multi-property portfolio executions at this scale.

2. Cross-Collateralized Portfolio Loan

When a family office wants to optimize total leverage and simplify debt management in a single transaction, a cross-collateralized blanket mortgage across multiple properties is often the right structure. One loan, one lender, one payment, one annual reporting package.

The mechanics require careful analysis. Each property in the collateral pool contributes its net operating income to the aggregate DSCR calculation, which means stronger assets can effectively subsidize weaker ones and unlock leverage that would not be achievable on a property-by-property basis. The total loan is underwritten to the portfolio DSCR and the blended LTV, not to the worst property in the pool.

Lenders who do blanket portfolio loans include select regional banks with strong commercial real estate departments, private debt funds, and in certain situations, life companies for portfolios with strong stabilized cash flow. The minimum portfolio value to attract institutional interest in a cross-collateralized structure is typically $15 million, and most lenders prefer at least five properties. Release provisions are a critical negotiating point: the family office needs the ability to sell or exchange individual properties without triggering full loan repayment.

I negotiate partial release clauses that specify a release price (typically 110 to 125 percent of the allocated loan amount for that property) and a minimum remaining pool value, which protects the lender while giving the family office the flexibility to execute asset management decisions.

3. International Family Office with US Commercial Real Estate

This scenario adds complexity at every layer. Foreign national ownership of US commercial real estate intersects with FIRPTA (the Foreign Investment in Real Property Tax Act), which imposes withholding obligations on the sale of US real property interests by foreign persons. Lenders are aware of FIRPTA and price for the additional administrative and legal complexity, but the underwriting hurdles go further than that.

When a foreign entity is the direct borrower or guarantor, lenders require enforceability opinions from counsel in the entity's home jurisdiction, translated and apostilled organizational documents, and in some cases opinions on whether a US judgment would be recognized and enforceable in the foreign jurisdiction. For a Cayman holding company, a British Virgin Islands LLC, or a Singapore family trust, this adds 60 to 90 days to the closing timeline and $25,000 to $60,000 in legal costs.

Non-recourse structures are strongly preferred for international borrowers because they eliminate the enforcement complexity entirely. Life company debt and CMBS are the two primary paths to non-recourse permanent financing for this profile. Both have minimum loan sizes (life companies typically $5 million minimum, CMBS practical floor closer to $3 million but with higher transaction costs at that end), and both require the property to be a stabilized, income-producing asset.

For international family offices that want recourse-efficient financing in the US, a domestic blocker corporation or a Delaware LLC with a US-based managing member can simplify the lender's credit analysis materially. I coordinate directly with the family office's US tax counsel and international legal team to structure the borrowing entity before approaching lenders, which shortens the LOI-to-close timeline significantly.

4. Non-Recourse Permanent Financing on a Trophy Asset

UHNW principals who own flagship assets, Class A multifamily towers, net-leased single-tenant properties occupied by investment-grade tenants, or institutional-quality office buildings with long-term leases, are among the best-positioned borrowers in the entire commercial real estate market. They simply need a broker who can access the lenders who serve that profile.

Life insurance companies are the gold standard for this situation. They originate long-term, fixed-rate, non-recourse loans at the tightest spreads available in the permanent lending market. A life company loan on a trophy asset might price at 30 to 70 basis points over the corresponding Treasury, with a 10-year term, a 30-year amortization schedule, and a yield-maintenance prepayment provision. The borrower signs the note, delivers the mortgage, and the insurance company holds the loan on its general account for the life of the term. No securitization, no servicer transfer, no special servicing risk.

Life company underwriting requirements are strict. They look for stabilized assets at or above 90 percent occupancy, DSCR of 1.25 times or better, strong market fundamentals, and sponsorship quality. The principal's global balance sheet, liquidity, and real estate track record matter even in a non-recourse structure because life companies want to know they are dealing with a sophisticated principal who will maintain the asset through its hold period.

CMBS is the alternative for assets where life company parameters are not met, or where the borrower prefers assumability (CMBS loans are generally assumable, which can be a significant selling point when planning an eventual exit). CMBS loans carry a higher spread than life company debt and involve more complex ongoing reporting requirements, but the non-recourse structure is the same and the proceeds can sometimes be more aggressive on leverage.

5. Ground Lease Structure

Many family offices own land that has been in the family for generations. A developer or operating company has constructed commercial improvements on that land under a long-term ground lease. Now the ground lessee needs financing on the leasehold interest, the improvements, or both.

Leasehold mortgages are a specialized product that most lenders do not offer and most brokers have never placed. The lender is taking as collateral not the fee simple land, but the borrower's right to occupy and use the land under the ground lease terms. That right has real value, but its value is entirely contingent on the ground lease remaining in effect.

Lenders require the ground lease to have a remaining term of at least 25 years beyond the loan maturity (30 plus years for institutional lenders), to include a cure period during which the lender can remedy any tenant default before the landlord can terminate the lease, and to be assignable to a lender in connection with a foreclosure without requiring landlord consent. If the existing ground lease does not contain these provisions, it typically needs to be amended before a lender will proceed.

When the family office is the ground lessor, we also coordinate with the family office's counsel to ensure the ground lease amendment does not inadvertently alter the economic terms or landlord protections that the family has negotiated. This is a situation where the broker needs to understand both sides of the transaction well enough to anticipate issues before they surface in lender due diligence.

What Lenders Actually Require for These Situations

Family office financing requests fail at the lender level most often not because of the asset quality but because the submission package is incomplete, the entity structure has not been pre-explained, or the borrower representative shows up without the documentation that sophisticated lenders expect to see before issuing a term sheet.

Here is what lenders require for the scenarios described above:

  • Entity documentation: Complete organizational chart showing all beneficial owners, their percentage interests, and the chain of control from top to bottom. For foreign entities, apostilled and translated versions of formation documents, operating agreements, and certificates of good standing.
  • Financial statements: Three years of property-level operating statements, trailing 12-month rent roll, and current rent roll. For UHNW sponsorship, lenders want a personal financial statement showing liquidity and net worth, supported by brokerage account statements, real estate schedules, and prior year tax returns.
  • DSCR thresholds: Life companies and agency lenders typically require 1.25 times DSCR at the loan level. Bridge lenders and portfolio lenders may go to 1.10 times or lower on stabilization-play assets. CMBS has variable DSCR thresholds by property type, typically 1.20 to 1.25 times.
  • LTV thresholds: Life companies lend to 60 to 65 percent LTV on most asset types. CMBS goes to 70 to 75 percent. Debt funds can go to 75 to 80 percent on stabilized assets.
  • Ground lease terms: Remaining lease term at least 25 years beyond loan maturity, lender cure rights, and assignability provision.
  • Foreign entity underwriting: US counsel enforceability opinion, foreign jurisdiction opinion, FIRPTA compliance documentation, and in some cases a domestic guarantor or co-borrower.

How the Referral Process Works

Advisors who refer family office clients to CLS CRE stay in control of the relationship throughout the process. My role is to handle the financing execution from first call through closing, keep you informed at each stage, and deliver an outcome your client will credit you for facilitating.

In practice the process looks like this:

  1. Initial introduction: A brief email introduction from you to me, with a one-paragraph description of the situation. I schedule a 30-minute call with the client or their representative within 48 hours.
  2. Needs assessment: On the first call I scope the transaction, identify the right lender category, and outline the likely terms. I flag any structural issues upfront rather than discovering them mid-process.
  3. Lender outreach: I approach the appropriate lenders simultaneously and in a structured package. For trophy assets, I contact three to five life companies in parallel. For portfolio loans, I run a competitive process across regional banks and debt funds. For CMBS, I work with three to four investment banks.
  4. Term sheet review: I deliver a comparative analysis of all term sheets received, with my recommendation, so the client can make an informed decision rather than simply accepting the first offer.
  5. Due diligence and closing: I manage the lender due diligence process, coordinate with the client's legal team, and stay on top of the closing checklist through funding.

You do not need to be involved in the day-to-day execution unless you want to be. Most advisors prefer a brief weekly update from me and then a final summary at closing.

Questions to Ask Any Commercial Mortgage Broker Before Referring a Family Office Client

Not all commercial mortgage brokers are equipped for this work. Before making a referral, I would encourage any family office manager or private wealth advisor to ask the following:

  • Have you placed a loan for a foreign national or foreign entity borrower? If yes, ask for specifics. If no, the learning curve will happen on your client's transaction.
  • Do you have direct relationships with life insurance company correspondents? Life company access requires ongoing correspondent relationships. A broker without them will route you to a bank or CMBS, which may not be optimal for your client.
  • Have you closed a cross-collateralized portfolio loan? The structuring, release provision negotiation, and lender selection for a blanket mortgage are materially different from a single-property loan.
  • What is your minimum loan size? Many institutional brokers do not handle loans below $10 million. Know where your referral partner operates before introducing a client with a $4 million need.
  • How do you handle entity structures with multiple layers? The answer should include references to working with borrower's counsel, lender counsel opinions, and lender-specific entity review requirements.
  • What is your fee structure and how is it disclosed to the borrower? Broker compensation on commercial loans is disclosed to borrowers and should be clearly explained upfront.

Key Takeaways

  • Family offices require commercial mortgage brokers who understand complex entity structures, portfolio-level strategy, and institutional lender relationships, not just the mechanics of a single loan application.
  • Multi-property portfolio refinancings are most valuable when sequenced strategically by maturity date, prepayment cost, and rate savings opportunity rather than executed all at once.
  • Cross-collateralized blanket mortgages can unlock leverage unavailable on individual assets and simplify ongoing debt management, but require careful partial release provision negotiation.
  • International family offices with US CRE face FIRPTA, foreign entity underwriting, and enforceability hurdles that add 60 to 90 days and significant legal cost to any financing transaction. Pre-structuring the borrowing entity before lender approach shortens the timeline materially.
  • Life company debt is the optimal non-recourse permanent financing solution for UHNW principals holding trophy assets: tightest spreads, longest terms, no securitization risk.
  • Ground lease financing requires a lender experienced with leasehold mortgages and often requires ground lease amendments to satisfy lender cure rights, term requirements, and assignability provisions.
  • The referral process for family office advisors is designed to be simple: make the introduction, we handle the execution, you stay informed and in control of the client relationship.
  • Vetting a commercial mortgage broker before referring a UHNW client is worth the time. The questions above will quickly reveal whether a broker has the relationships and experience the situation requires.

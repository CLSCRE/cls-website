Expert Witness: Commercial Loan Modification Standards in Dothan, AL

By Trevor Damyan· June 25, 2026 ·Loan Modification and Workout Dispute

The Dothan Market Context

Dothan, Alabama sits at the center of the Wiregrass Region, serving as the commercial hub for a trade area that extends across southeastern Alabama, the Florida Panhandle corridor, and southwestern Georgia. With a metropolitan statistical area population approaching 150,000 and an economy anchored by healthcare, agribusiness, defense-related manufacturing, and retail trade, Dothan punches above its weight class as a secondary market for commercial real estate lending purposes. That positioning matters enormously in workout and loan modification disputes, because lenders underwriting assets in Dothan are applying credit standards calibrated for secondary and tertiary market risk, not the more forgiving metrics common in Atlanta or Birmingham.

For loan modification and workout disputes specifically, the Dothan market presents several characteristics that directly affect what constitutes commercially reasonable lender conduct. Retail fundamentals in Dothan remain relatively resilient compared to national trends, anchored by the strong regional draw of the Wiregrass Commons Mall corridor and the Highway 231 commercial spine. However, office absorption has been sluggish since 2020, with Class B and Class C office vacancy rates estimated in the 18 to 24 percent range across the submarket as of recent reporting periods. Multifamily cap rates in Dothan have historically traded in the 6.25 to 7.25 percent range, reflecting the yield premium investors demand for a market of this size relative to major metros. Industrial and flex product has performed better, with proximity to the Dothan Regional Airport and regional distribution demand keeping vacancy in the 6 to 9 percent range for functional modern product. These submarket-specific fundamentals are not incidental to a workout dispute; they are the foundation upon which any expert analysis of modification terms must be built.

Lenders operating in the Dothan market, including community banks, regional institutions, and USDA Business and Industry program participants, apply underwriting standards that reflect the market's secondary-market risk profile. When a modification dispute arises, the question of whether a lender acted within industry standards cannot be answered by applying Charlotte or Nashville benchmarks to a Dothan asset. The market context is the expert's starting point, and it is where attorneys often fail to invest sufficient analytical attention before retaining technical assistance.

The Engagement

Commercial Lending Solutions was retained by litigation counsel representing a commercial borrower in a dispute arising from a contested loan modification and forbearance agreement on a mixed-use retail and office property in the Dothan submarket. The asset was a multi-tenant commercial building with ground-floor retail and second-floor office space, originally financed by a regional bank under a five-year term loan with a 25-year amortization schedule. The borrower had experienced significant income disruption caused by tenant vacancies compounded by the regional economic softening that followed a period of elevated interest rates, and had entered into workout discussions with the lender.

The core legal questions presented to our firm were threefold. First, counsel needed to establish whether the lender's conduct during the modification negotiation process conformed to industry-standard commercial workout practices, including the completeness of the financial review the lender conducted, the timeliness of the lender's responses, and the adequacy of the lender's documentation of its modification decision. Second, counsel needed an independent assessment of whether the forbearance terms ultimately offered, including the proposed interest rate adjustment, re-amortization schedule, and required debt service reserve deposit, were commercially reasonable given the property's then-current net operating income and demonstrated market conditions in Dothan. Third, counsel asked CLS CRE to analyze what a similarly situated borrower would have received from other institutional lenders in the market under comparable circumstances, to establish a benchmark for what commercially reasonable workout terms look like.

These are precisely the questions that require a capital markets practitioner with active lender relationships and current market knowledge, not merely an understanding of historical loan documentation.

The Financial Analysis

Our financial analysis began with a reconstruction of the property's stabilized and as-modified net operating income. Using rent rolls, lease abstracts, operating expense histories, and market-rate vacancy assumptions appropriate for the Dothan mixed-use submarket, we established a trailing twelve-month NOI and a forward-looking stabilized NOI projection. The subject property, with approximately 18,000 square feet of leasable area, was generating gross rental income of approximately $312,000 annually at the time of the workout discussions, with economic vacancy of approximately 22 percent reflecting both physical vacancy and below-market legacy leases. After operating expenses including taxes, insurance, maintenance, and management at a combined load appropriate for a Dothan asset of this type, the supportable NOI came to approximately $198,000 on a trailing basis and $224,000 on a stabilized forward basis assuming absorption of available space at current market rents.

The debt service coverage ratio threshold we applied was 1.25x on the stabilized NOI, consistent with the underwriting floor that regional and community banks in Alabama markets of this size apply as their minimum acceptable coverage. Many institutional lenders in secondary markets will require 1.30x or even 1.35x for office-anchored product given the elevated vacancy risk, and that higher threshold is fully defensible as an industry standard for this asset type in this market. At 1.25x coverage applied to the stabilized NOI of $224,000, the maximum annual debt service supportable by the property was approximately $179,200.

We then worked backward from that debt service ceiling to determine the maximum supportable loan balance under various rate and amortization scenarios. At a modification rate of 7.25 percent, which reflected the then-current pricing for bank workout loans on secondary-market commercial assets rather than the below-market rate the borrower had argued for, a 25-year re-amortization schedule produces an annual constant of approximately 8.33 percent. Dividing the maximum supportable debt service of $179,200 by an 8.33 percent constant yields a maximum supportable loan balance of approximately $2,151,000. We cross-referenced this figure against a current as-is appraised value for comparable Dothan mixed-use product, applying a 65 to 70 percent LTV constraint, which is the standard institutional parameter for this asset type in a secondary market with elevated vacancy. At a market-derived value of approximately $3,000,000 for the subject, a 70 percent LTV cap produces a maximum loan of $2,100,000, which aligned almost exactly with the income-supported maximum.

This convergence of the income test and the LTV test at approximately the same loan ceiling was analytically significant. It told us that the lender's underwriting, if properly conducted, should have arrived at a modification loan amount close to this figure. Where the dispute arose was in the lender's requirement for a debt service reserve equal to twelve months of modified payments deposited upfront as a condition of the modification, which effectively required the borrower to produce approximately $180,000 in cash at closing of the modification. Our analysis demonstrated that this reserve requirement, while not inherently unreasonable as a concept, was commercially excessive in its sizing given the borrower's demonstrated ability to service the modified debt and given what comparable lenders in the Dothan market were requiring from similarly situated borrowers during the same time period.

The Complicating Factors

Lender's internal credit review process and documentation gaps. One of the primary complicating factors in this engagement was that the lender had failed to produce a complete written credit memorandum supporting its modification terms. In standard institutional workout practice, a lender conducting a loan modification is expected to prepare a formal credit analysis that documents the property's income, expenses, NOI, DSCR, LTV, and the rationale for each modified loan term. The absence of this documentation does not merely create a paper trail problem; it raises a substantive question about whether the lender actually performed the analysis that would be required to conclude that the proposed terms were commercially reasonable. Courts and arbitrators evaluating lender conduct in workout disputes frequently treat the absence of a contemporaneous credit memo as evidence that the modification terms were not the product of reasoned underwriting judgment.

Mixed-use asset classification and its effect on lender credit boxes. The retail-office composition of the subject property created genuine complexity in the modification process. Lenders categorize mixed-use assets differently depending on the predominant use and the lease structure. A property with significant office exposure in a market like Dothan, where office fundamentals have softened materially, will be treated more conservatively than a pure retail asset. The lender in this dispute appeared to have applied a single blended underwriting standard without distinguishing between the risk profiles of the two income streams, which affected the accuracy of the NOI analysis and the appropriateness of the vacancy assumptions used in the modification underwriting.

Forbearance period length and the triggering of acceleration rights. The forbearance agreement in this case specified a 90-day forbearance period with a modification closing deadline that the borrower ultimately missed by eleven days due to delays in the lender's own appraisal ordering process. The lender then asserted that the missed deadline triggered its acceleration rights under the original loan documents, which became a central issue in the litigation. Industry-standard forbearance agreements in commercial workout situations typically include a provision allowing for a reasonable extension of the forbearance period when the delay is attributable to the lender's own actions or third-party processes within the lender's control. The failure to include such a provision, and the lender's refusal to grant a brief extension, became a focal point in our analysis of whether the lender's conduct met industry standards of reasonableness.

Market timing and interest rate environment. The modification was being negotiated during a period of elevated benchmark rates, which created a genuine tension between the borrower's request for a below-market rate modification and the lender's cost of funds. This is not a trivial consideration. A lender cannot reasonably be expected to modify a loan at a rate that produces a negative spread against its cost of funds indefinitely. However, the industry standard practice is for the lender to offer a rate that reflects a commercially reasonable spread over its cost of funds or over a relevant benchmark, and to document how that rate was determined. In this case, the lender imposed a rate 175 basis points above what comparably situated borrowers were receiving from community banks in the Dothan market, without documentation supporting the premium.

The Dothan Lender Market

Understanding which lenders are actually active in Dothan for this asset type is essential to establishing benchmarks for commercially reasonable modification terms. The Dothan market is served primarily by a combination of Alabama-chartered community banks, regional banks headquartered in Birmingham and Montgomery, and in certain asset categories, USDA Business and Industry loan guarantees through approved participating lenders. National conduit lenders and debt funds are largely absent from modification transactions on assets of this size in this market, as the loan balances typically fall below the minimum threshold for CMBS special servicing teams and debt fund workout desks.

Community banks in the Houston County and Dale County markets are the dominant modification and workout counterparties for loans in the $1,000,000 to $5,000,000 range on retail, office, and mixed-use assets. These institutions generally have significant portfolio flexibility but apply conservative secondary-market credit standards, including the 1.25x minimum DSCR and 65 to 70 percent LTV parameters referenced above. Their modification processes are typically less formal than those of large regional banks, which can create exactly the kind of documentation gaps that generate litigation.

Regional banks with Alabama presence do participate in Dothan workouts for larger or more complex assets, but their internal workout and special assets groups apply credit approval processes that are substantially more bureaucratic and can introduce timing delays that become legally significant. For assets with a USDA Business and Industry guarantee component, the modification process must conform to USDA program requirements, which add an additional layer of procedural complexity and can affect what terms a lender is actually permitted to offer without USDA consent.

CLS CRE maintains active lender relationships across all of these categories, which allows us to speak with specificity about what lenders in each category were actually requiring from comparable Dothan borrowers during any given time period. That market-derived benchmark data is what distinguishes a capital markets practitioner from an appraiser or a retired banker when it comes to expert testimony on modification reasonableness.

What Attorneys Handling Dothan CRE Disputes Need to Know

When to retain a CRE financing expert. Counsel should retain a CRE financing expert at the early stages of any dispute where the central question involves what a lender should have done, what a borrower should have been offered, or whether financing was available at all. An appraiser can tell you what the property was worth. A CRE financing expert can tell you whether the modification terms were consistent with what active lenders in the Dothan market were offering, whether the lender's underwriting process met industry standards, and whether the loan as modified was commercially supportable given the property's income and market conditions.

What a CRE financing expert can address that an appraiser cannot. The lender's duty of good faith in a workout negotiation is evaluated against capital markets practice, not real estate valuation practice. Questions about DSCR thresholds, reserve requirements, rate-setting methodology, forbearance period standards, and the adequacy of the lender's credit review process all fall within the CRE financing expert's domain. An appraiser can provide the value input, but the expert who explains what that value means to a lender's credit decision, and how that credit decision should have been documented and communicated, needs to be a capital markets practitioner with active market knowledge.

Qualification standards that matter. A retired community banker from Dothan has transactional experience but likely lacks the broad market benchmarking capability required to testify about industry standards across the lending community. The most effective CRE financing expert witnesses combine current, active deal experience with a broad lender network that allows them to speak credibly about what multiple categories of lenders were doing in comparable situations. At CLS CRE, our approximately $1 billion in transaction volume and 1,000-plus active lender relationships across all 50 states give us the comparative market data that Rule 26 expert testimony requires.

Information counsel should gather before the engagement. Attorneys should secure the complete loan file including the original credit memorandum, all modification and forbearance correspondence, the lender's internal special assets or workout notes, the appraisal ordered in connection with the modification, all borrower financial submissions, and any documentation of communications between the lender's workout officer and the borrower. The completeness of the lender's internal file is itself probative evidence of whether the lender's conduct met industry standards.

How expert testimony is deployed. In commercial workout litigation in Alabama state courts and in federal proceedings, CRE financing expert testimony is most effectively used to establish the objective market standard against which the lender's conduct is measured. This includes presenting a DSCR analysis that demonstrates what loan terms the property's income could actually support, showing what comparable lenders were offering in the same market during the same period, and identifying specific departures from industry-standard workout documentation and process practices.

Key Takeaways

  • Dothan's secondary-market characteristics are determinative. Industry standards for commercial workout terms in Dothan reflect the market's secondary-market risk profile, including higher LTV haircuts, more conservative DSCR floors, and greater lender caution on office and mixed-use product than in major metros. Expert analysis must be calibrated to Dothan's actual market conditions, not national averages.
  • The lender's documentation record is as important as the loan terms themselves. A modification that reaches a commercially defensible result through an undocumented or cursory underwriting process still represents a failure of industry-standard workout practice. The absence of a formal credit memorandum is itself a significant finding in an expert analysis.
  • DSCR and LTV convergence is the appropriate methodology for evaluating modification reasonableness. The maximum supportable loan under an income analysis and the maximum supportable loan under an LTV analysis should both be calculated and compared. When they diverge significantly, the expert must explain which constraint should govern and why.
  • Reserve requirements must be sized against demonstrated property performance. Excessive upfront reserve demands that a borrower demonstrably cannot meet while continuing to service the modified debt are not commercially reasonable, even if reserves as a concept are standard workout practice.
  • Forbearance period provisions must account for lender-controlled delays. Industry-standard forbearance agreements include provisions addressing delays attributable to the lender's own appraisal, legal review, or approval processes. The failure to include such provisions, or the rigid enforcement of a deadline against which the lender's own conduct contributed, is a departure from standard practice.
  • Rate-setting in a modification must be documentable and market-benchmarked. A lender's right to price a modification at a market-appropriate rate does not permit arbitrary rate-setting. The rate must reflect a documented spread over a recognized benchmark or cost-of-funds measure, and the premium over market must be justifiable by specific credit factors applicable to the subject borrower and asset.

Life Company Industrial Lending in San Antonio: What the Market Looks Like in 2025 and 2026

San Antonio's industrial market has quietly become one of the more compelling stories in Texas CRE, and life insurance companies have taken notice. The city's position along the I-35 corridor connecting Laredo to Austin creates a natural logistics spine, and the build-out of distribution infrastructure serving San Antonio's 2.5 million metro residents has accelerated meaningfully over the past several years. For life company capital, which prizes long-term cash flow stability above almost everything else, San Antonio's tenant base anchored by Toyota Manufacturing, USAA, Valero Energy, and a network of defense and logistics contractors serving Joint Base San Antonio gives underwriters exactly the kind of occupancy profile they want to hold for 15 to 25 years.

The I-35 industrial corridor and the Schertz submarket to the northeast are the primary targets for life company attention. Schertz and Cibolo have absorbed a significant share of last-mile and bulk distribution development tied to the broader San Antonio and Austin supply chains, and stabilized Class A product there checks most of the boxes life company credit committees care about: modern clear heights, ESFR sprinkler systems, ample trailer storage, and proximity to the highway network that serves both metro areas. New Braunfels, situated between San Antonio and Austin, is increasingly attracting institutional-grade industrial tenants and has begun to appear on life company radar for the right deal.

Life companies are selective in San Antonio in ways that differ from how they approach Dallas or Houston. The market is smaller, deal flow is thinner, and the largest life companies tend to have size floors that push some San Antonio product toward regional correspondents rather than home office appetite. That reality is actually an advantage for sponsors working with a direct correspondent lender who already has established allocations and underwriting authority for this market, rather than submitting cold to a national platform that may deprioritize a $10 million San Antonio warehouse in favor of a $50 million Dallas distribution center.

What Life Companies Are Looking For in San Antonio Industrial Deals

The underwriting criteria for life company industrial capital in San Antonio are consistent with what you would see nationally, applied against local market realities. Loan-to-value expectations typically land between 55 and 65 percent on stabilized assets, with credit-tenant NNN leases capable of pushing toward 70 percent in the right structure. Life companies are not leverage lenders. Their pricing advantage comes from taking lower leverage, longer fixed-rate commitments, and non-recourse structures that transfer execution risk to the capital source rather than the sponsor.

Term length is a defining feature of life company execution. Borrowers choosing this structure are generally looking at 10 to 25-year fixed-rate terms, with longer terms more readily available when there is a credit-quality NNN tenant with a remaining lease term that supports the amortization period. In today's rate environment, with the 10-year Treasury in the range of 4.25 to 4.40 percent, life company spreads on stabilized San Antonio industrial product are running approximately 125 to 175 basis points over benchmark for most qualifying assets. Class A logistics facilities with institutional tenancy and long weighted average lease terms can tighten inside that range, representing some of the most competitive permanent financing available to industrial owners in any market.

DSCR requirements are typically 1.25x or better at origination, underwritten to in-place rents rather than market projections. Non-recourse is standard, subject to the customary carve-outs for fraud, environmental liability, and prohibited transfers. For trophy distribution assets, full-term interest-only periods are a real possibility, which meaningfully changes the cash-on-cash return profile for sponsors managing a long-term hold. Occupancy requirements are firm: life companies want stabilized properties, generally 90 percent or better occupancy with creditworthy tenants in place. Value-add plays, lease-up scenarios, and transitional assets are outside the life company appetite entirely.

Typical San Antonio Life Company Industrial Deal Profile

A realistic life company industrial transaction in the San Antonio market typically involves a stabilized Class A or institutional-quality Class B warehouse or distribution facility in the $7 million to $40 million range, though our program accommodates deals up to $100 million. The property is generally post-2005 vintage or substantially renovated, with modern specifications: minimum 28-foot clear heights, dock-high loading, ESFR fire suppression, and adequate power for light manufacturing or logistics use. The I-35 corridor, Schertz, and emerging New Braunfels industrial nodes are the most competitive submarkets for this execution.

From a tenancy standpoint, life companies want stabilized occupancy with a weighted average lease term of at least 5 years remaining at closing, and they respond strongly to credit-quality anchors whether national logistics operators, defense contractors, or investment-grade manufacturers. Multi-tenant light industrial parks can qualify with strong occupancy history and a diversified rent roll. Sponsor profile matters: life companies expect experienced industrial operators with clean credit histories, meaningful net worth relative to loan size, and demonstrated asset management experience.

Timeline from signed term sheet to closing typically runs 45 to 75 days for a transaction processed through our direct correspondent platform. Comparable transactions placed through a brokered model and reviewed by a life company home office commonly run 75 to 120 days or longer, depending on the life company's queue and internal staffing at the time of submission.

Life Company versus Other Permanent Capital Options in San Antonio Industrial

For stabilized San Antonio industrial, the relevant alternatives to life company financing are CMBS, bank balance sheet lending, and debt funds. Each has a distinct profile that makes it the right answer in specific circumstances.

CMBS can offer higher leverage, typically 65 to 75 percent LTV, and broader credit flexibility than life companies, but it carries yield maintenance or defeasance prepayment costs that are punishing if the business plan changes, and the securitization process introduces its own timeline and third-party approval friction. For a sponsor with a long-term hold horizon and no near-term exit, CMBS leverage premium rarely justifies the prepayment rigidity compared to life company execution.

Bank balance sheet lending is the right answer for transitional or value-add industrial plays where the property is not yet stabilized, occupancy is building, or the hold period is three to seven years rather than 15 to 25. Banks can move to 70 to 75 percent leverage and tolerate lease-up risk, but they reprice at maturity and often carry recourse, which changes the risk profile for the sponsor.

Debt funds serve the highest-leverage or shortest-term needs, often bridge-to-stabilization scenarios, and price accordingly. Their cost of capital is meaningfully higher than life company execution for any stabilized asset, and they are not a competitive alternative for a sponsor who qualifies for permanent life company financing.

Life company execution wins clearly when the asset is stabilized, the sponsor intends a long hold, the loan size exceeds $5 million, and the tenancy profile supports it.

Life Company Industrial Lending in Boston: What the Market Looks Like in 2025 and 2026

Boston has earned its place as a first-tier life company market, and industrial is one of the asset classes where that appetite is most apparent. The Greater Boston industrial corridor, anchored along Route 128 and stretching west through Framingham, Burlington, and into the I-495 ring, continues to draw permanent capital from life insurance companies seeking long-duration, income-stable placements. Vacancy in Class A logistics and bulk warehouse product along these corridors has remained structurally constrained, driven by last-mile distribution demand, cold storage conversion activity, and ongoing supply chain reconfiguration that has pushed occupiers to lock in well-located space under long-term leases. That combination of durable cash flow and institutional-grade tenancy is exactly what life companies are underwriting in 2025 and 2026.

The Route 128 and I-95 technology and logistics corridor is the epicenter of life company industrial interest in this market. Properties here benefit from proximity to Boston's dense consumer base, a deep pool of logistics and light industrial tenants, and comparatively high barriers to new supply given land constraints and entitlement timelines. Submarkets like Waltham, Woburn, and Burlington attract particular attention from life companies because the tenant mix often skews toward investment-grade or near-investment-grade credits with longer weighted average lease terms. Last-mile distribution facilities in inner-ring locations closer to the Seaport and the South Bay area also generate strong life company interest when the credit profile and lease structure support it.

Life companies have become more selective across Boston's broader commercial real estate landscape, particularly on standard office product, but their conviction in industrial has only strengthened. The same institutional investors who have pulled back from suburban office along Route 128 have reallocated appetite toward the logistics and flex industrial product sharing those same corridors. For sponsors holding stabilized, well-leased industrial in Greater Boston, 2025 and 2026 represent a genuine window of competitive life company execution at spreads that are among the tightest available for any commercial asset class nationally.

What Life Companies Are Looking For in Boston Industrial Deals

Life insurance companies underwriting industrial loans in the Boston market have a consistent set of requirements, and sponsors benefit from understanding those parameters before approaching the market. On leverage, life companies are typically comfortable at 55 to 65 percent loan-to-value for stabilized industrial, with credit-tenant NNN product potentially stretching to 70 percent where the lease term and tenant credit justify the additional basis. These are not high-leverage execution vehicles. Sponsors who need 75 percent or higher should be looking at debt funds or bridge capital, not life company correspondent financing.

On pricing, the 2026 rate environment places the 10-year Treasury around 4.3 percent, with life company spreads for stabilized industrial typically running 125 to 175 basis points over that index for most qualifying assets. Class A logistics with investment-grade tenancy and long remaining lease terms can price at the tighter end of that range or through it in a competitive quote process. Fixed-rate terms run 10 to 25 years, with longer terms more common on NNN product with credit tenants whose lease term aligns with or exceeds the loan term. Full-term interest-only periods are available for trophy distribution product and are a meaningful structural advantage for sponsors optimizing current cash-on-cash returns.

Debt service coverage requirements are typically a minimum of 1.25x on an amortizing basis, though lenders will look at both amortizing and interest-only DSCR depending on the structure being proposed. Occupancy thresholds are generally 90 percent or better at origination, and life companies want to see in-place leases, not projections. Sponsor experience matters as well. Life companies in Boston are underwriting the borrower alongside the asset, and they want to see a track record of managing and operating industrial assets, not just a clean balance sheet.

What a Typical Boston Life Company Industrial Deal Looks Like

The industrial transactions that work well for life company correspondent capital in Greater Boston tend to share a consistent profile. Loan sizes typically fall between $10 million and $75 million, with the sweet spot for most life company programs in the $15 million to $50 million range. Property vintage is generally 2000 or newer, with preference for Class A bulk warehouse, modern logistics facilities, or light industrial parks that have been maintained to institutional standards. Functional obsolescence is a disqualifier. Clear heights, dock count, power capacity, and truck court dimensions are all underwritten closely.

Occupancy at closing should be 90 percent or better, with in-place leases from creditworthy tenants with meaningful remaining term. Three to five years of remaining lease term represents a minimum threshold for most programs; 10-plus years with a credit tenant opens up the longer fixed-rate terms and more favorable leverage. Sponsors are typically experienced owner-operators with a demonstrable track record in industrial, and the deal should be positioned as a long-term hold, not a near-term exit play.

For direct correspondent transactions in this market, well-structured deals move from executed term sheet to loan commitment in roughly 45 to 60 days and to closing in 60 to 75 days total, assuming no title or environmental complications. That timeline compares favorably to the 90 to 120-plus days a sponsor might experience working through a brokered life company channel.

Life Company Financing versus Other Permanent Capital Options in Boston

Life company correspondent financing is not the right tool for every industrial deal in Boston, and experienced sponsors are well served by understanding where it wins and where it does not. Life company execution is most competitive for stabilized, long-hold assets with strong tenancy, large loan sizes above $10 million, and sponsors who value non-recourse structure, long fixed-rate terms, and pricing at or near the tightest available in the market. For a 25-year fixed-rate non-recourse loan at 60 percent LTV on a Class A logistics asset in Waltham leased to an investment-grade tenant, life company capital is almost certainly the best execution available.

For industrial assets that are value-add, lease-up, or transitional in any way, debt funds or bank bridge capital are the correct first call. Life companies do not finance business plans. They finance stabilized income. CMBS can compete on certain stabilized industrial deals, particularly where the sponsor needs higher leverage or a more flexible prepayment structure than yield maintenance, but CMBS execution on industrial in this loan size range often comes with more servicing complexity and less flexibility on structure than the direct correspondent life company channel. Bank balance sheet lenders offer recourse financing that can make sense for shorter-term holds or smaller deal sizes, but banks generally cannot match the fixed-rate term length or non-recourse structure of life company correspondent capital for qualifying assets.

Agency financing is not applicable to industrial product. That execution is specific to multifamily, and Boston's competitive multifamily market has its own life company appetite that operates on a separate track from industrial.

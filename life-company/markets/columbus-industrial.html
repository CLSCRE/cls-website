Life Company Industrial Lending in Columbus: Why Central Ohio Is Attracting Permanent Capital

Columbus has moved from a secondary life company target market to a primary one, and industrial is leading that shift. The convergence of Ohio State University's economic anchor, a deeply diversified employment base, and Intel's $20 billion plus semiconductor mega-campus in New Albany has fundamentally rewritten how life insurance companies view central Ohio logistics and industrial. What was once a flyover market for permanent capital allocators is now a competitive placement destination, particularly for Class A warehouse, bulk distribution, and last-mile product along the I-270 corridor and near Rickenbacker International Airport.

The Intel New Albany investment is generating a category of industrial demand that life companies find genuinely compelling: semiconductor supply chain facilities, precision manufacturing support, and advanced logistics nodes tied to one of the largest domestic industrial investments in a generation. These are not speculative assets. They attract credit tenants, long lease terms, and institutional sponsorship -- exactly the deal profile life companies underwrite most aggressively. The Rickenbacker submarket, already established as one of the Midwest's strongest air cargo and distribution hubs, is benefiting directly from this supply chain buildup, with new development and lease activity reinforcing what was already a preferred submarket for permanent capital.

Beyond the Intel story, Columbus industrial fundamentals stand on their own. The I-270 ring continues to attract regional and national logistics operators. New Albany and Westerville are generating high-quality light industrial and flex product with strong tenancy. Easton-area last-mile facilities serve one of Ohio's densest consumer trade areas. Life companies looking to deploy into Midwest industrial no longer need to default to Chicago or Indianapolis. Columbus now offers the asset quality, tenant credit, and market depth that justifies a full correspondent allocation.

What Life Companies Are Looking For in Columbus Industrial Deals

Life insurance companies are not generalist lenders. In Columbus industrial, they are underwriting to a specific profile, and deals that fall outside it will not receive competitive execution regardless of market conditions. LTV is typically 55 to 65 percent on stabilized assets, with credit-tenant NNN deals potentially reaching 70 percent depending on lease term and tenant quality. These are not aggressive leverage points, and sponsors accustomed to bank or debt fund sizing need to calibrate accordingly.

Fixed-rate terms run 10 to 25 years, with longer terms reserved for NNN credit-tenant structures where the lease term matches or exceeds the loan term. In the current rate environment, with the 10-year Treasury trading in the range of 4.3 percent, life company spreads on stabilized Columbus industrial are running approximately 125 to 175 basis points over Treasury for most qualifying assets. Class A logistics and distribution facilities with institutional tenancy can price at the tighter end of that range, making life company execution genuinely competitive against any permanent capital source for the right deal. Interest-only periods are available for trophy distribution product, and full-term IO is achievable for the strongest assets.

DSCR requirements typically sit at 1.25x or better on in-place cash flow, with no credit given for lease-up or mark-to-market assumptions. Occupancy must be stabilized -- generally 90 percent or better with tenant base that supports underwriting to long-term hold. Non-recourse is standard with carve-outs for fraud and environmental. Prepayment is structured as yield maintenance or a declining schedule, and sponsors should model accordingly if there is any possibility of an early disposition or refinance. Life companies are long-hold capital sources, and the prepayment mechanics reflect that.

The Direct Correspondent Advantage: What It Actually Means

Commercial Lending Solutions is a direct correspondent lender for life insurance company financing. That phrase carries specific operational meaning that matters at every stage of a transaction, and it is worth explaining precisely what separates this model from the brokered alternative.

When a mortgage broker submits an industrial deal to a life company correspondent desk, the file moves from the originator to an intermediary to an unknown underwriter at the life company's home office. The broker does not control that review. They cannot answer underwriting questions in real time, cannot negotiate structure directly with the credit decision-maker, and cannot give a borrower a genuine read on where the deal stands until the home office issues a response. That process routinely takes 45 to 60 days just to receive a term sheet, and another 60 to 90 days or more to reach a commitment letter.

There is no third-party approval process that the borrower's deal is waiting in line for. A single underwriter carries the file from application through commitment, which means faster decisions, cleaner structure conversations, and a direct relationship between the borrower's team and the people actually making the credit call. For Columbus industrial deals where timing matters -- whether that is a lease expiration, an acquisition closing, or a construction takeout -- the difference between 45 days to commitment and 90 plus days is not academic. It is deal execution.

This correspondent status is a genuine credential, not a branding position. It reflects a capital allocation relationship with our life company capital source that gives us the authority to underwrite, commit, and close. Typical Columbus Life Company Industrial Deal Profile

A representative life company industrial transaction in Columbus today looks something like this: a Class A bulk warehouse or last-mile distribution facility built within the last 10 to 15 years, 200,000 square feet or larger, located in the I-270 corridor, Rickenbacker submarket, or New Albany, with a single credit tenant or a small number of high-quality tenants on leases of 7 years or longer. Loan size typically runs $10 million to $50 million, though the program accommodates transactions up to $100 million for the right asset. Sponsors are institutional or experienced private owners with demonstrated industrial track records and clean balance sheets.

From executed term sheet to closing, a direct correspondent transaction in this market typically runs 45 to 75 days. That timeline includes appraisal, Phase I environmental, title, and loan document negotiation. Brokered transactions moving through a life company's home office correspondent desk frequently run 75 to 120 days or longer, particularly when underwriting questions require back-and-forth between the mortgage broker and the home office. For acquisitions with hard close dates, that distinction is often the deciding factor in lender selection.

Life Company Versus Other Permanent Financing Options in Columbus Industrial

Life company financing is not the right execution for every Columbus industrial deal, and understanding when it wins and when it does not is part of making a sound capital decision. Life company execution is most competitive on stabilized, long-hold assets where the sponsor wants maximum fixed-rate certainty, the longest possible term, and non-recourse structure. For a well-leased I-270 distribution facility with a credit tenant on a 10-year lease and a sponsor planning to hold through the loan term, life company pricing will typically beat CMBS, bank balance sheet, and debt fund alternatives on an all-in cost basis.

CMBS can reach slightly higher leverage in some cases and offers assumability, which is a meaningful feature for sponsors expecting a sale before loan maturity. But CMBS servicing is inflexible, and reserve structures add friction that life company loans generally avoid. Bank balance sheet loans offer more flexibility on prepayment but rarely compete on rate for 10-year-plus fixed terms, and most banks will not go beyond a 7-year fixed period. Debt funds are the right answer for value-add, lease-up, or transitional assets where life company occupancy and income requirements cannot be met. They price significantly wider and require recourse or significant fees, but they fill a capital stack position that life companies simply do not serve.

The honest summary: if a Columbus industrial deal is stabilized, sized above $10 million, and sponsored by a borrower planning a long-term hold, life company execution through a direct correspondent is likely the most efficient permanent capital available in the current market.
